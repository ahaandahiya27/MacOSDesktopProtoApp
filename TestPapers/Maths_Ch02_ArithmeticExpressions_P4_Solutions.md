## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** Innermost first: 8 − 5 = 3, then 2 × 3 = 6, so 15 − 6 = 9. Now 4 × 9 = 36, and 200 − 36 + 6 = 170. Subtracting the trailing +6 instead of adding it gives 158. Adding the product 4 × 9 instead of subtracting it gives 200 + 36 + 6 = 242. Dropping the +6 entirely (treating it as already used) gives 200 − 36 = 164.

**2. (A)** By the distributive idea 56 × 50 − 56 × 2 = 56 × (50 − 2) = 56 × 48, so the two are identical. Believing 56 × 50 − 56 × 2 is bigger comes from seeing the larger first product 56 × 50 and forgetting the full second subtraction; believing 56 × 48 is bigger comes from over-subtracting 56 × 2 twice.

**3. (B)** A minus sign in front of the bracket flips every inner sign: −30 stays −30, +12 becomes... each sign reverses so −(30 − 12 + 8 − 5) = −30 + 12 − 8 + 5. The version 150 − 30 − 12 + 8 − 5 flips only some signs (failing to flip −12 and +8); 150 − 30 + 12 + 8 − 5 leaves +8 unflipped; 150 − 30 − 12 − 8 + 5 flips the wrong pair.

**4. (A)** Factor the common 14: 14 × 100 − 14 × 3 = 14 × (100 − 3) = 14 × 97. Writing 14 × 97 − 3 subtracts the 3 once more after it was already inside the bracket; 14 × 103 adds instead of subtracts; 1400 − 3 distributes only the first term and forgets to multiply the 3 by 14.

**5. (A)** Brackets first: 2 + 4 × 2 = 2 + 8 = 10, and 11 − 2 × 4 = 11 − 8 = 3. The two divisions run left to right: 120 ÷ 10 = 12, then 12 ÷ 2 = 6; and 3 × 3 = 9; finally 6 + 9 = 15. Keeping only the bracket product 3 × 3 and dropping the 6 gives 9. Stopping after 120 ÷ 10 = 12 and never halving gives 12. Grouping the divisions as 120 ÷ (10 ÷ 2) = 24, which then plus 9 — or instead 120 ÷ 10 ÷ 2 = 6 plus a mis-multiplied 3 × (11 − 2) = 27 — gives 33.

**6. (D)** Each × or ÷ chain collapses to one signed term: 4 × 5 = 20 carries the minus in front, giving −20; 36 ÷ 9 = 4 carries its leading plus, giving +4; the standalone 90 and −7 stay. So the terms are 90, −20, 4, −7. Listing 90, −4, 5, 36, 9, −7 splits the products into separate numbers; making the +4 negative ignores its plus sign; making all positive ignores the operation signs.

**7. (C)** Going from 73 − 28 to 75 − 31 the first number rises by 2 but the amount subtracted rises by 3, a net change of 2 − 3 = −1, so 75 − 31 is one less. Hence 73 − 28 is greater. Calling them equal ignores that the subtrahend grew more than the minuend; choosing 75 − 31 assumes the bigger first number always wins.

**8. (C)** Subtracting 24 and then 16 removes 24 + 16 = 40 in total, so 100 − 24 − 16 = 100 − (24 + 16). Writing 100 − (24 − 16) removes only 8; (100 − 24) + 16 adds the 16 back; 100 + (24 − 16) adds instead of subtracts.

**9. (A)** Innermost: 2 × 3 = 6, then 8 + 6 = 14, then 20 − 14 = 6. Now 5 × 6 = 30, and 30 − 4 = 26. Forgetting to subtract the trailing 4 gives 30. Adding the 4 instead of subtracting it gives 34. Computing the inner 8 + 2 × 3 as 8 + 2 = 10 (skipping the × 3) gives 20 − 10 = 10, so 5 × 10 − 4 = 46.

**10. (C)** The common factor 6 pulls out: 6 × 48 + 6 × 52 = 6 × (48 + 52) = 6 × 100 = 600. Getting 100 stops at 48 + 52 and forgets the × 6; 300 multiplies only one of the two terms by 6; 6000 mis-places the zero.

**11. (C)** Reading the expression as signed terms 36, −19, +14, −6, the positives sum to 36 + 14 = 50 and the negatives to 19 + 6 = 25, giving 50 − 25 = 25; this regrouping is allowed because addition is commutative and subtraction is adding a negative. The choice that adds all four as positives (75) drops the minus signs; (36 + 19) − (14 + 6) wrongly moves +14 into the subtracted group; claiming order is fixed denies commutativity.

**12. (C)** The minus before the bracket flips all three inner signs: −(25 − 10 − 5) = −25 + 10 + 5, so the value is 80 − 25 + 10 + 5 = 70. Leaving every sign unchanged gives 40; flipping only two of the three signs gives 60; flipping the leading 25 the wrong way gives 90.

**13. (B)** Write both around 28 × 40: 27 × 41 = (28 − 1)(40 + 1) = 28 × 40 + 28 − 40 − 1 = 28 × 40 − 13, which is 13 less than 28 × 40. So 28 × 40 is greater. Calling them equal assumes shifting 1 from each factor cancels; choosing 27 × 41 trusts the larger-looking second factor.

**14. (A)** Brackets first: 3 + 3 × 2 = 3 + 6 = 9 and 9 − 2 × 3 = 9 − 6 = 3. Then left to right among × and ÷: 72 ÷ 9 = 8, 8 × 4 = 32, and 2 × 3 = 6; finally 32 − 6 = 26. Treating × 4 as part of the divisor, 72 ÷ (9 × 4) = 2 and dropping the rest leaves 2. Computing the second bracket as 9 − 2 = 7 (skipping the × 3) gives 32 − 2 × 7 = 18. Adding the last product instead of subtracting gives 32 + 6 = 38.

**15. (C)** Since 101 = 100 + 1, 23 × 101 = 23 × 100 + 23 × 1 = 2300 + 23 = 2323. Writing 23 × 100 + 1 multiplies only the 100 by 23 and forgets to multiply the 1; doubling to 23 × 100 + 23 × 100 splits 101 as 100 + 100; subtracting 23 treats 101 as 100 − 1.

**16. (D)** Adult cost is 4 × 120, child cost is 3 × 80, and the single coupon subtracts 50 once: 4 × 120 + 3 × 80 − 50. Reading it as 4 + 120 × 3 + 80 − 50 adds the counts to the prices wrongly; (4 + 3) × (120 + 80) charges every person both prices; 50 × 7 deducts the coupon once per ticket.

**17. (B)** Work outward removing brackets with sign flips: 10 − 3 = 7, then 40 − 7 = 33, then 100 − 33 = 67; equivalently the nested minus signs flip to 100 − 40 + 10 − 3 = 67. Keeping all signs negative gives 47; flipping the 3 to plus gives 73; flipping the 10 the wrong way gives 53.

**18. (B)** From the first sum to the second, one part rises by 3 (248 → 251) while the other falls by 2 (176 → 174), a net change of +3 − 2 = +1, so 251 + 174 is one larger. Calling them equal assumes the +3 and −2 cancel; choosing 248 + 176 misreads which side gained more.

**19. (D)** Inner brackets: 7 − 3 = 4 so 2 × 4 = 8 and 4 + 8 = 12, giving 3 × 12 = 36; and 6 − 2 × 2 = 6 − 4 = 2 giving 5 × 2 = 10. Then 36 − 10 = 26. Adding the two products gives 46; subtracting 36 from a smaller piece gives 6; stopping at 3 × 12 = 36 forgets the second product entirely.

**20. (D)** Distributing the 9 over the difference multiplies both inner terms: 9 × (20 − 4) = 9 × 20 − 9 × 4. The form 9 × 20 − 4 multiplies only the 20 by 9; (9 − 20) × 4 versus 9 − 20 × 4 changes which factor the bracket binds; 9 × 20 + 9 × 4 adds instead of subtracts.

**21. (D)** The signed terms are 56, −20, +18, −2. Adding the positives 56 + 18 = 74 and the negatives 20 + 2 = 22 gives 74 − 22 = 52. Dropping the +18 term gives 56 − 20 − 2 = 34; making the −20 positive gives 56 + 20 + 18 − 2 = 92; reading the last term as +2 gives 56 − 20 + 18 + 2 = 56.

**22. (B)** Three successive subtractions remove the total 8 + 9 + 5 = 22, so the expression is 60 − (8 + 9 + 5). Writing 60 − (8 − 9 − 5) removes only 8 − 14 = −6; 60 − (8 + 9 − 5) adds the 5 back; the last option both splits and re-adds a 5.

**23. (B)** Since 34 × 36 = (35 − 1)(35 + 1) = 35 × 35 − 1, the product 34 × 36 is exactly one less than 35 × 35, so 35 × 35 is greater. Calling them equal assumes spreading the factors by 1 keeps the product; choosing 34 × 36 trusts that a larger factor (36) always wins.

**24. (A)** Innermost: 8 − 6 = 2, then 12 ÷ 2 = 6, then 144 ÷ 6 = 24; finally 24 + 2 × 3 = 24 + 6 = 30. Ignoring the inner bracket and dividing left to right, 144 ÷ 12 ÷ 2 = 6, then 6 + 6 = 12. Forgetting the trailing + 2 × 3 leaves the bare 24. Adding 2 before multiplying by 3, (24 + 2) × 3, gives 78.

**25. (D)** Total bottles are 9 crates of 6 each, 9 × 6 = 54, and 4 break, so 9 × 6 − 4 = 50. The form 9 × (6 − 4) wrongly removes 4 bottles from every crate; (9 − 4) × 6 removes whole crates instead of bottles; 9 + 6 − 4 adds crates to bottles.

**26. (B)** Since 93 = 100 − 7, 8 × 93 = 8 × 100 − 8 × 7. Writing 8 × 100 − 7 subtracts only 7 instead of 8 × 7; 8 × 90 + 3 splits 93 as 90 + 3 but forgets to multiply the 3 by 8; 8 × 100 + 8 × 7 treats 93 as 100 + 7.

**27. (B)** A plus before a bracket keeps the inner signs (+30 − 10); a minus before a bracket flips them (−8 + 3). So the result is 50 + 30 − 10 − 8 + 3. Leaving the second bracket's −3 unflipped gives ... − 8 − 3; flipping the first (plus) bracket's signs gives 50 − 30 + 10 ...; flipping the −10 to +10 leaves 50 + 30 + 10 ...

**28. (D)** Collapse each × or ÷ chain to a signed term: 48 ÷ 6 = 8 (so −8), 5 × 4 = 20 (so +20), 12 ÷ 4 = 3 (so −3). Then 100 − 8 + 20 − 3 = 109. Making the +20 term negative gives 100 − 8 − 20 − 3 = 69; making the −8 positive gives 100 + 8 + 20 − 3 = 125; making the −3 positive gives 100 − 8 + 20 + 3 = 115.

**29. (B)** Because 99 = 100 − 1, the distributive idea gives 47 × 100 − 47 × 1 = 47 × 99, so both are identical. Choosing 47 × 100 − 47 as larger is misled by the big 47 × 100; choosing 47 × 99 forgets the subtraction undoes the extra group of 47.

**30. (C)** Work from the deepest bracket: 7 + 1 = 8, then 2 × 8 = 16, then 30 − 16 = 14, then 14 ÷ 2 = 7. Now 4 × 7 = 28, and 28 + 5 = 33. Skipping the ÷ 2 gives 4 × 14 + 5 = 61. Stopping at 4 × 7 = 28 and dropping the + 5 leaves 28. Subtracting the 5 instead of adding it gives 28 − 5 = 23.

**31. (A)** Each hour nets 90 − 40 = 50 and there are 7 hours, so 7 × (90 − 40) = 350; this also equals 7 × 90 − 7 × 40. The form 7 × 90 − 40 subtracts the spending for only one hour; 7 × 90 − 40 × 1 does the same; multiplying the whole bracket by 7 again over-counts the shift.

**32. (B)** Since 35 = 30 + 5, 18 × 35 = 18 × 30 + 18 × 5. Writing 18 × 30 + 5 forgets to multiply the 5 by 18; 18 × 30 × 5 multiplies the parts instead of adding them; the split is certainly possible by distribution.

**33. (C)** The minus flips every sign inside the bracket: −(15 + 6 − 4) = −15 − 6 + 4; the trailing + 5 is outside and unchanged. So it is 90 − 15 − 6 + 4 + 5. Flipping only the first sign gives 90 − 15 + 6 − 4; leaving the −4 unflipped gives ... − 6 − 4; flipping the +6 to keep + and +4 also misreads the inner signs.

**34. (B)** Evaluate each: (6 + 2) × (5 − 1) = 8 × 4 = 32; 6 + 2 × 5 − 1 = 6 + 10 − 1 = 15; 6 × 2 + 5 × 1 = 12 + 5 = 17; 6 × 2 − 5 + 1 = 12 − 5 + 1 = 8. The largest is 32. The trap is reading the bracketed product as if order of operations made it small, or computing 6 + 2 × 5 − 1 left-to-right as (6 + 2) × 5 − 1 = 39.

**35. (C)** From the first to the second, the minuend drops by 3 (502 → 499) and the subtrahend drops by 4 (318 → 314); a smaller subtraction of 4 outweighs the smaller start of 3, net +1, so 499 − 314 is larger by 1. Calling them equal assumes the two drops cancel; choosing 502 − 318 trusts the bigger first number.

**36. (B)** Innermost: 2 × 4 = 8, then 12 + 8 = 20, then 40 − 20 = 20; so 3 × 20 = 60 and 250 − 60 − 8 = 182. Failing to flip the inner bracket sign (writing 40 − 12 + 2 × 4 = 36, so 3 × 36 = 108) gives 250 − 108 − 8 = 134. Forgetting the trailing − 8 gives 190. Dropping the whole product 3 × (…) leaves 250 − 8 = 242.

**37. (D)** Each bus holds 32 + 2 = 34 people and there are 5 buses, so 5 × (32 + 2) = 170; equivalently 5 × 32 + 5 × 2. The form 5 × 32 + 2 counts the teachers on just one bus; 5 + 32 × 2 mixes the counts; (5 + 32) × 2 doubles a meaningless sum.

**38. (C)** The common factor 15 pulls out: 15 × 7 + 15 × 13 = 15 × (7 + 13) = 15 × 20. Adding an extra 15 (15 × 20 + 15) double-counts; 15 × 91 multiplies the 7 and 13 instead of adding them; 15 + 7 + 13 drops the multiplications entirely.

**39. (D)** Removing a bracket after a minus flips each inner sign: −(30 + 25) = −30 − 25, so 120 − (30 + 25) = 120 − 30 − 25. The version with − 30 + 25 fails to flip the +25; 120 − (30 − 25) should become 120 − 30 + 25, not − 25; the last option fails to flip the 30.

**40. (D)** Pairing 19 + 81 = 100 and 36 + 64 = 100 makes two round hundreds totalling 200; this regrouping is allowed because addition is commutative and associative. The pairing (19 + 36) + (81 + 64) gives 55 + 145 — correct total but no round pair; (19 + 64) + (36 + 81) gives 83 + 117 — again no round pair; addition can always be regrouped.

**41. (A)** Order: 6 ÷ 3 = 2 so the bracket is 5 + 2 = 7; the × and ÷ run left to right giving 96 ÷ 8 = 12, 12 × 3 = 36, and 2 × 7 = 14. Then 36 − 14 + 4 = 26. Subtracting the trailing 4 instead of adding it gives 36 − 14 − 4 = 18. Adding the bracket product instead of subtracting gives 36 + 14 + 4 = 54. Grouping the first chain as 96 ÷ (8 × 3) = 4 gives 4 − 14 + 4 = −6.

**42. (C)** Rewrite each as a count of 25s: 25 × 41 is 41 groups; 25 × 42 − 25 × 2 = 25 × 40 (40 groups); 25 × 38 + 25 × 2 = 25 × 40 (40 groups); 25 × 39 + 25 = 25 × 40 (40 groups). So 25 × 41 alone has 41 groups and is greatest, while the other three each reduce to 25 × 40. Picking 25 × 42 − 25 × 2 is the trap of overrating the leading 25 × 42 and undercounting the subtraction.

**43. (B)** Factor the first two terms: 17 × 6 + 17 × 4 = 17 × (6 + 4) = 17 × 10 = 170, then subtract the lone 17 to get 153. Stopping at 170 forgets the final − 17; adding the 17 gives 187; subtracting 17 twice gives 136.

**44. (A)** Every inner sign flips after the minus: −(50 − 30 + 20 − 10) = −50 + 30 − 20 + 10. So the expansion is 200 − 50 + 30 − 20 + 10. Keeping the original inner signs gives 200 − 50 − 30 + 20 − 10; flipping only some terms gives 200 − 50 + 30 + 20 − 10; flipping the leading 50 wrongly gives 200 + 50 − 30 ...

**45. (B)** Three drains of 80 remove 3 × 80 = 240, and 150 is added: 500 − 3 × 80 + 150 = 410. The form 500 − 3 + 80 + 150 treats the count and litres as separate additions; 500 − 3 × (80 + 150) wrongly drains the refill three times; (500 − 3) × 80 multiplies the wrong quantity.

**46. (D)** Within a × and ÷ chain we work left to right: 36 ÷ 3 = 12, then 12 × 2 = 24; the minus in front makes the term −24. Doing 3 × 2 = 6 first (wrongly prioritising ×) then 36 ÷ 6 = 6 gives −6; stopping at 36 ÷ 3 = 12 gives −12; dropping the leading minus gives +24.

**47. (A)** Brackets first: 84 + 36 = 120, 2 + 4 × 2 = 10, and 10 − 2 × 4 = 2. Then 120 ÷ 10 = 12 and 3 × 2 = 6; finally 12 − 6 = 6. Adding the two products instead of subtracting gives 12 + 6 = 18. Computing the second bracket as 10 − 2 = 8 (skipping the × 4) gives 3 × 8 = 24, so 12 − 24 = −12. Dividing only by the 2 (reading the divisor as just 2) gives 120 ÷ 2 = 60, so 60 − 6 = 54.

**48. (A)** In 3 × (20 + 4) the 3 multiplies both 20 and 4, giving an extra 3 × 4 = 12 beyond 3 × 20; in 3 × 20 + 4 the 4 is added only once. So 3 × (20 + 4) is larger by 12 − 4 = 8. Calling them equal is the half-distribution error of multiplying only the first inner term; the two are clearly comparable, so 'cannot be decided' is wrong.

**49. (D)** Each carton has 5 + 3 = 8 pens, so 12 cartons hold 12 × (5 + 3); distributing gives 12 × 5 + 12 × 3 = 96. The form 12 × 5 + 3 multiplies only the red pens by 12; 12 × 5 + 3 is not equal to 12 × (5 + 3); and 12 × 5 × 3 multiplies the colours together rather than adding them.

**50. (C)** Innermost: 12 − 4 = 8, then inside the square bracket 30 − 8 − 6 = 16, and finally 64 − 16 = 48. Treating the square bracket as 30 − 8 + 6 = 28 (mis-flipping the −6) gives 64 − 28 = 36... ; the value 32 comes from 64 − 32 using 30 − 8 + 6 + 4-style slips; 40 comes from 30 − 4 − 6 inside (dropping the 12); 56 comes from 64 − 8 keeping only the inner difference.

**51. (D)** First form the pouches: 84 ÷ 7 = 12, then give away 5, leaving 84 ÷ 7 − 5 = 7. The form 84 ÷ (7 − 5) divides by 2 pencils per pouch; (84 − 5) ÷ 7 removes 5 pencils instead of 5 pouches; 84 ÷ 7 ÷ 5 divides the pouches among 5.

**52. (A)** Each product or quotient becomes one signed term: 4 × 3 = 12 with its leading minus gives −12; 18 ÷ 2 = 9 with its leading plus gives +9; the standalone 60 and −5 remain. So the terms are 60, −12, 9, −5. Splitting into single numbers gives the six-number list; making the +9 negative drops its plus sign; making everything positive ignores all operation signs.

**53. (D)** Inner pieces: 4 + 2 = 6 so 3 × 6 = 18; and 2 × 3 = 6 so 10 − 6 = 4. The big bracket is 18 − 4 = 14, then 2 × 14 = 28, and 5 + 28 = 33. Failing to flip the second sub-bracket (using 18 + 4 = 22) gives 5 + 2 × 22 = 49. Forgetting the outer + 5 leaves 2 × 14 = 28. Reading 3 × (4 + 2) as 3 × 4 + 2 = 14 gives 5 + 2 × (14 − 4) = 25.

**54. (B)** The second equals 64 × (100 − 3) = 64 × 97, which is one group of 64 fewer than 64 × 98. So 64 × 98 is greater by 64. Calling them equal mistakes the subtraction of three 64s for two; choosing the second is misled by the large 64 × 100.

**55. (D)** Since 36 = 40 − 4, 25 × 36 = 25 × 40 − 25 × 4 = 1000 − 100 = 900. Writing 25 × 40 − 4 subtracts only 4 rather than 25 × 4; adding 25 × 4 treats 36 as 44; the arithmetic 1000 − 100 = 800 is a subtraction slip.

**56. (A)** Without brackets 20 − 6 + 2 = 16; to reach 12 we must subtract both the 6 and the 2, i.e. 20 − (6 + 2) = 20 − 8 = 12. The grouping (20 − 6) + 2 gives 16; 20 − (6 − 2) gives 16; bracketing the whole thing changes nothing, still 16.

**57. (C)** Each shirt costs 250 − 30 = 220 and there are 8, so 8 × (250 − 30) = 1760; equivalently 8 × 250 − 8 × 30. The form 8 × 250 − 30 applies the discount once for all eight; 8 × 250 − 30 × 1 does the same; multiplying the bracket by 8 again double-counts the order.

**58. (C)** Collapse each chain: 144 ÷ 12 = 12, 7 × 3 = 21, 50 ÷ 5 = 10, 2 × 2 = 4, with signs +12, +21, −10, −4. Sum: 12 + 21 − 10 − 4 = 19. Treating the −4 as +4 gives 12 + 21 − 10 + 4 = 27; treating the −10 as +10 gives 12 + 21 + 10 − 4 = 39; treating both subtracted terms as added gives 12 + 21 + 10 + 4 = 47.

**59. (A)** Evaluate the original: 40 + 20 = 60, 60 ÷ 6 = 10, 3 × 4 = 12, total 22; so 60 ÷ 6 + 12 matches. Writing 40 + 20 ÷ 6 + 12 drops the bracket and divides only the 20; (40 + 20) ÷ (6 + 3) × 4 absorbs 3 × 4 into the divisor; 60 ÷ (6 + 3 × 4) divides 60 by the whole tail.

**60. (C)** Since 99 = 100 − 1, subtracting 99 is the same as subtracting 100 then adding back 1: 200 − 99 = 200 − 100 + 1 = 101. The form 200 − 100 − 1 subtracts 101; 200 + 100 − 1 adds instead of subtracting; 200 − 90 − 9 − 1 subtracts 100 in total, giving 100, not 101.
