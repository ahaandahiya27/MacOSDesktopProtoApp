Worked Solutions

1. (A) After the Guptas faded, India had many strong regional kingdoms rather than one empire. It was not one empire, kingdom-less, or Arab-ruled.

2. (D) It was a vibrant, creative age (Ellora, Mamallapuram, universities, the rules of zero, Bhakti), not a dark age or single-emperor era.

3. (D) The age produced Ellora, Mamallapuram, Nalanda and Vikramashila, the rules of zero and Bhakti - creativity, not a void.

4. (B) Many regional kingdoms meant political fragmentation, yet culture flourished. It was not unity, collapse, or all-India foreign rule.

5. (D) Harshavardhana ruled from Kannauj (from 606 CE). Vatapi was the Chalukya capital, Kanchi the Pallava capital, and Ellora a Rashtrakuta temple site.

6. (C) Harshavardhana came to power around 606 CE. 1206 is far later, 326 BCE is Alexander's time, and 1947 is independence.

7. (A) After Harsha, no single emperor dominated the north, sparking the Tripartite Struggle among regional powers. It was not one emperor, ruler-less, or Arab-ruled.

8. (A) Xuanzang was a Chinese Buddhist pilgrim who toured India. He was not a Pala king, an Arab general, or a Bhakti saint.

9. (D) A traveller writes from his own viewpoint and may exaggerate, so his account is one source to cross-check. It is neither perfect, worthless, nor the only source.

10. (C) Epigraphy (Greek epi- 'on' + graphein 'to write') studies inscriptions on stone or metal. Coins are numismatics, stars are astronomy, and travelogues are one source among many.

11. (C) A prashasti is a eulogy praising a ruler and may exaggerate, so it needs careful handling. It is not neutral, enemy-written, or about ordinary village life.

12. (D) The Tripartite Struggle was for Kannauj, the symbolic key to the north. Ellora was a temple site, the Tamil south had its own kingdoms, and Sindh was taken by the Arabs.

13. (D) The Palas, Gurjara-Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas fought the Tripartite Struggle. The southern trio, the Chalukya/Pallava/Gupta set, and the ancient dynasties are different.

14. (A) Three great powers fighting for Kannauj shows it was a prized symbol of northern supremacy. It was not worthless, southern, or Arab.

15. (D) A long, indecisive three-way struggle reflects balanced rivalry among strong kingdoms. It was not one empire, peaceful, or foreign-ruled.

16. (D) The Palas ruled Bengal (and the east). The Tamil south, Deccan and Kashmir belonged to other powers.

17. (B) The Palas patronised Buddhist universities such as Nalanda and Vikramashila. Mamallapuram was Pallava, Ellora Rashtrakuta, and the Sindh conquest Arab.

18. (A) A dvarapandita was a scholar-gatekeeper testing students for admission at Vikramashila. A tribute-paying ruler is a samanta, a chariot-temple a ratha, and a village assembly a sabha.

19. (B) Testing entrants through a scholar-gatekeeper shows high academic standards. The universities did admit students, taught valuable subjects, and were centres of learning, not army camps.

20. (B) The Gurjara-Pratiharas defended the north-west (notably against the Arabs). The Tamil south, Ellora and Bengal belonged to other powers.

21. (B) The Gurjara-Pratiharas helped confine the Arabs to a small foothold. They did not invite conquest, peacefully end the struggle, or build Mamallapuram.

22. (A) The Rashtrakutas are linked to Ellora's great rock-cut temple. Mamallapuram is Pallava, Nalanda Pala, and Kannauj the prize of the Tripartite Struggle.

23. (C) The Kailasa temple is monolithic, carved from one mass of rock. It is not paper, digital, or imported brick.

24. (C) Monolithic means from a single block of stone. Brick, wood-and-cloth and underwater construction are different.

25. (D) A monolithic temple shows extraordinary artistic and engineering skill - evidence the age was creative. It is not a sign of no ability, decline, or foreign dependence.

26. (A) The Chalukyas ruled from Vatapi in the Deccan. Kannauj, Bengal and Sindh belonged to other powers.

27. (B) The southern and Deccan powers built great temples and trade networks. Bengal's universities were Pala, Ellora Rashtrakuta, and Sindh's state Arab.

28. (D) The Pallavas of Kanchi built the stone temples of Mamallapuram. Ellora is Rashtrakuta, Nalanda Pala, and Vatapi Chalukya.

29. (D) A ratha (Sanskrit 'chariot') is a chariot-shaped shrine, as at Mamallapuram. A village assembly is a sabha, a subordinate ruler a samanta, and a reservoir a tank.

30. (D) The rathas show Pallava mastery of stone temple art. They are not about paper, the Silk Route, or Bengal.

31. (B) Both are rock-cut wonders, showing the age's skill in carving temples from stone. Paper printing, paper money and steel building are unrelated.

32. (D) The Cholas, Pandyas and Cheras revived in the south. The Tripartite trio ruled the north, and the others are earlier dynasties or invaders.

33. (A) Their revival made the Tamil south a hub of kingdoms, temples and trade once more. It was not empty, Arab-ruled, or Pala-ruled.

34. (A) A samanta was a subordinate ruler (vassal) governing outer regions for a king, giving tribute and soldiers. A poet-saint is an Alvar or Nayanar, a chariot-temple a ratha, and a pilgrim like Xuanzang.

35. (B) Kings ruled through subordinate samantas and self-governing villages, not directly over every village. There were officials, not Arab governors or national elections.

36. (C) A sabha was a village assembly through which villages governed themselves. It was not an emperor, a ratha, or a university.

37. (C) The Uttaramerur inscriptions record how village sabha members were chosen - evidence of local self-rule. They are not about temple-carving, zero, or Sindh.

38. (D) Self-governing village sabhas show genuine local self-rule under the kings. There was government below the king, it was not foreign-only, and there was no national parliament.

39. (C) Power passed down through samantas and sabhas rather than directly from king to villager. It was not direct, Arab-only, or presidential.

40. (A) Land grants gave land to people, communities or temples, creating landholders. They did not seize land, and they were not rathas or poems.

41. (B) A tank (eri) was a man-made reservoir for water storage and irrigation. It was not a chariot, a monastery, or a samanta.

42. (B) Merchant guilds and flourishing towns show a thriving economy. It was not destroyed, barter-only, or Arab-controlled.

43. (C) Land grants, guilds and water tanks reflect a productive, well-managed economy. It was not ruined, trade-free, or landholder-free.

44. (C) The system was flexible: the Palas rose in status and professions like the Kayasthas became jatis. It was not unchanging, jati-free, or single-group.

45. (C) The Kayasthas show a profession becoming a jati over time - evidence of flexibility. It is not about unchanging jatis, foreign rule, or temple-carving.

46. (A) Multiplying jatis and status changes show a complex, changing system. It was not frozen, abolished, or identical everywhere.

47. (C) Some women and queens held real influence. They were not wholly absent, sole rulers of all India, or barred from every role.

48. (D) Bhakti began in the Tamil south. It did not start on the steppe, in Arab Sindh, or only in the Himalayan north.

49. (A) Bhakti (Sanskrit bhaj, 'to be devoted') is personal, loving devotion to a deity, open to everyone. It is not a war, a tax, or epigraphy.

50. (A) Bhakti opened devotion to all through personal love of a deity, rather than only formal Vedic sacrifice. It was not king-only, sacrifice-only, or Arab.

51. (B) The Alvars were the twelve Tamil poet-saints of Vishnu. The Shiva poet-saints are the Nayanars, tribute-payers are samantas, and assemblies are sabhas.

52. (C) The Nayanars were the sixty-three Tamil poet-saints of Shiva. The twelve Vishnu poet-saints are the Alvars; Xuanzang was a pilgrim, not a Nayanar.

53. (A) Brahmagupta gave the rules for calculating with zero. Harshavardhana was a king, Xuanzang a pilgrim, and Kalhana a historian.

54. (B) The Rajatarangini is Kalhana's 12th-century history of Kashmir's dynasties. It is not a maths treatise, a Bhakti poem, or an Arab chronicle.

55. (B) A chronicle (Greek chronos, 'time') records events in time order. A chariot-temple is a ratha, a reservoir a tank, and a subordinate ruler a samanta.

56. (B) The Arabs conquered Sindh in 712 CE. They did not take all of India, the Tamil south, or Bengal.

57. (C) The Arabs took only Sindh, and their rule stayed limited to a small foothold (Sindh and part of Punjab). They did not conquer all India, nor were they absent, nor did they rule to 1947.

58. (C) Indian rulers such as the Gurjara-Pratiharas and Lalitaditya resisted, confining the Arabs. It was not for lack of resistance, an Arab choice, or Chinese help.

59. (B) Being repulsed and then confined shows strong Indian resistance limited the Arab advance. There was resistance, the Arabs did not take everything, and they did attempt expansion.

60. (A) The post-Gupta age was creative, the Arabs took only Sindh, and caste was flexible - correcting all three errors. The other options keep one or more misconceptions.
