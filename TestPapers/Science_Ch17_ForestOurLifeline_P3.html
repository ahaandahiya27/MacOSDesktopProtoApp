Worked Solutions

1. (C) What makes something a forest is being a stable, self-renewing community of many different kinds of organisms arranged in layers, lasting for years — not any one ingredient. A crowd of a single tree species is more like a plantation; one predator alone does not make a forest; and grass alone is just a grassland. Only the multi-species, multi-layered, long-lasting community captures the idea of a forest as a living system.

2. (D) The combined leafy roof made by the tops of many tall trees together is the canopy. The branchy top of a single tree on its own is that tree's crown. So the whole-forest feature is the canopy and the single-tree feature is the crown — the order Riya then Sam matches canopy then crown.

3. (D) Plants growing in the shaded zone below the tallest trees but above the ground make up the understorey, and such plants are adapted to survive on the reduced light filtering down. They are not in the canopy (that is the top roof), the floor (that is the ground itself), and 'crown' names the top of an individual tree, not a forest layer.

4. (A) Decomposers such as fungi and bacteria continually convert the fallen litter into humus, so leaf fall and leaf breakdown roughly balance and no permanent pile builds up. Leaves are not blown away wholesale, trees do not re-absorb shed leaves, and leaves do not vanish instantly on landing — they take time to be broken down.

5. (C) Humus is the nutrient-rich material decomposers make from dead matter, and its key role is enriching the soil so plants can absorb those nutrients again. It is not a source of breathable oxygen, it does not waterproof the soil (it actually helps soil hold and soak up water), and it feeds the soil and plants rather than serving as direct animal food.

6. (A) The grasshopper eats grass, so it is a plant-eating herbivore, and the energy it uses came from the grass — a green producer. It is not a producer (it cannot make its own food), not a carnivore (it eats plants, not the frog), and not a decomposer (it eats living plant matter, it does not break down the dead).

7. (B) Energy is lost at every link of a food chain, so less is available to support life higher up; that is why top carnivores are few while producers are many. The pattern is set by energy supply, not by social habits, not by an inability to reproduce, and certainly not because tigers need little food — large predators in fact need a great deal.

8. (B) In a real forest, organisms have several food sources and several predators, so the simple chains interconnect into a web — that interlinking is what makes the web realistic. A web still has producers, it does not have a fixed organism count, and energy still flows one way from producers upward, not backward.

9. (A) The owl eats warblers (caterpillar → warbler chain) and shrews (beetle → shrew chain), so it links two separate chains. The warbler and shrew are different animals, the owl is a carnivore not a producer, and being at the top does not mean it eats plants directly — it eats other animals.

10. (B) Caterpillars feed the warblers and graze the leaves, so losing them first starves the warblers and relieves grazing pressure on leaves. Owls do not die instantly — they can still eat shrews; the leaves are not protected by caterpillars (the caterpillars ate them); and beetles eat leaves, not caterpillars, so beetles are not directly cut off.

11. (C) Removing the top predator releases its prey (warblers and shrews), which can multiply and then eat more caterpillars and beetles. Owls are carnivores, not producers of leaves; their removal does matter because they controlled the mid-level animals; and owls ate the birds and shrews, not the caterpillars and beetles directly, so those plant-eaters do not rise immediately — they may instead be eaten more.

12. (C) Decomposers recycle dead leaves and animals into humus, releasing nutrients back to the soil for the trees; remove them and the litter accumulates while nutrients stay trapped, starving the soil. Decomposers do not steal food from living trees, trees do need soil nutrients (not sunlight alone), and decomposers act on dead matter, not on healthy living roots.

13. (D) Decomposers are the link that converts dead matter back into humus that the soil reabsorbs, so cutting them out is exactly where the return path breaks. Extra sunlight does not break the loop, herbivores are not what makes humus (decomposers are), and normal rain helps soak nutrients into the soil rather than carrying them all away.

14. (B) The canopy buffers the lower storeys from direct sun, heat and heavy rain; strip it away and shade-adapted plants and floor animals are suddenly exposed to conditions they cannot tolerate. The canopy is not the lower plants' nutrient source (that is the soil and humus), floor animals breathe ordinary forest air, and losing the canopy tends to make the floor hotter and harsher, not freezing.

15. (A) Producers are so called because they make food from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide, supplying the energy every consumer ultimately uses. Being most numerous is a result, not the reason; giving us wood is a human use, not the ecological role; and plants make food from CO2 and sunlight, not by taking in oxygen and dead matter.

16. (D) Only the wild dog is listed as eating two animals — deer and foxes. The fox eats only rabbits (not deer), the deer eats shrubs (a plant, and not rabbits), and the rabbit eats herbs (a plant, and certainly not foxes), so none of those three feeds on two animals.

17. (A) Foxes eat rabbits, so losing the rabbits cuts the fox's food supply most directly. Deer eat shrubs, not herbs, so they do not compete with rabbits; the wild dog eats deer and foxes, not rabbits; and shrubs are eaten by deer, not protected by rabbits — rabbits eat herbs.

18. (A) Decomposers — fungi and bacteria — break dead bodies of any forest organism back into the soil as humus. Deer are plant-eating herbivores and do not feed on carcasses, the Sun does not recycle dead matter into soil, and rabbits eat herbs rather than transporting nutrients to roots.

19. (D) By day, forest plants photosynthesise, absorbing carbon dioxide and releasing oxygen, which is why they are likened to lungs that refresh the air. They are not taking in oxygen and giving out CO2 like animal breathing, they do not function by storing water as mist, and they actively exchange these gases rather than just filtering dust.

20. (C) Tree roots and litter normally bind the soil and slow rainwater so it soaks in; once removed, fast surface runoff strips the unprotected soil. The cause is loss of root grip and slowing, not the Sun making soil float, not any change in the weight of rain, and not trees having 'drunk the soil.'

21. (D) Forest soil with roots and litter acts like a sponge, taking in rain and releasing it slowly to keep streams steady; without that cover, rain runs straight off, flooding then leaving nothing stored. The effect comes from infiltration and slow release, not from trees timing clouds, not from stream depth, and bare land in fact soaks up less, not more, than forested soil.

22. (A) Forests give off water vapour by transpiration and cast shade, raising local humidity and supporting rainfall, so losing them makes the area hotter and drier. Trees do not physically pull clouds down with their trunks, healthy trees do not burn to seed clouds, and they moderate rather than completely absorb the Sun's heat.

23. (B) Living trees absorb carbon dioxide as they photosynthesise; remove many of them and less CO2 is taken up, so it builds up. Oxygen actually falls because there are fewer plants releasing it, the gas at issue is carbon dioxide not nitrogen, and the question is about the gas that rises because trees that used to absorb it are gone.

24. (B) Plants take in carbon dioxide and lock the carbon into their wood and leaves, and as litter breaks down some passes into the soil as humus — so wood, leaves and soil are the main stores. It is not held in the open air (that is the source it came from), not in the streams, and not chiefly in animal fur and feathers.

25. (A) Honey, gum, medicinal herbs and timber all come directly from forest plants and animals. The other lists mix in plastic, petrol, glass and cement — manufactured or mineral products that are not gathered from a forest — so only the all-forest list is fully correct.

26. (C) Forest regrowth is a slow, step-by-step recovery — herbs first, then shrubs, then trees, with soil and the web of species rebuilding over many years; it cannot be rushed into a few months. It is not done in one season, it is not true that it never recovers, and no single animal can make it regrow overnight.

27. (D) Without their predator, deer multiply and overgraze the seedlings and shrubs the forest needs to regenerate, so the whole system suffers — showing how removing one species disturbs many. Extra dung does not instantly create forest, plants and animals are tightly interdependent (not unaffected), and herbivorous deer do not hunt tigers.

28. (B) A forest is a web of interdependent organisms, so altering one species sends effects along its many feeding and sheltering links to others. The opposite ideas — that species are independent, identical, or that only the biggest one matters — all deny the interconnection that actually causes the ripple.

29. (C) What separates a forest from a wheat field is that the forest is a long-lasting community of many different species arranged in layers, while the field is one crop species grown briefly and removed. The difference is not mainly size, fields do contain some animals, and the test is the lasting multi-species community, not the presence of a single tree.

30. (C) The snake eats the small bird, so it is a flesh-eating carnivore at the chain's top, and the energy in it was originally captured by the leaves and passed up the chain. It does not eat the leaves itself (so not a herbivore), it cannot make its own food (so not a producer), and it eats living prey rather than breaking down the dead (so not a decomposer).

31. (D) A mushroom is a fungus, and fungi are decomposers, so that pairing is right. A tiger is a meat-eating carnivore, not a producer; grass is a green producer, not a carnivore; and a fungus decomposes dead matter rather than grazing on plants like a herbivore.

32. (D) The many services flow from the forest being one interconnected living system in which plants, animals, soil and microbes each support the rest. It is not just stored timber, not a set of unrelated trees, and not a man-made structure needing yearly rebuilding — those views miss the working web that produces the services.

33. (A) Forest soil is enriched by decomposers continually making humus from litter, and held in place by roots and leaf cover, whereas a bare hillside loses both the humus supply and the soil itself to runoff. Fertility is not caused by blocking all rain, by hoof-ploughing, or by mere darkness.

34. (C) Removing the forest removes the very things that hold soil, support rainfall, house wildlife and absorb CO2, so erosion, drier and less reliable rain, habitat loss and rising CO2 all follow. The other options reverse reality — deforestation lowers oxygen, makes streams flashier, dries the climate and worsens flooding rather than improving any of these.

35. (A) Tree-top dwellers like monkeys and fruit-eating birds live in the canopy, the high, sunlit layer rich in leaves and fruit. The forest floor is the shadiest layer with the least light, the understorey is the middle layer not the treetops, and these animals live among branches, not buried in the soil.

36. (D) The floor accumulates litter and dead matter and stays damp and shaded, exactly what worms, beetles and fungi need to feed and live. It is the darkest, not the sunniest layer, oxygen is present throughout the forest not just on the floor, and the floor is not freezing — decomposers thrive in mild, moist conditions.

37. (C) The filtered light lets shade-adapted lower plants carry out enough photosynthesis to survive, so it is essential rather than harmful. It is not something to block out, it does feed the plants and not just warm animals, and light does not remove a plant's need for water and soil nutrients — it works together with them.

38. (D) Light is intercepted layer by layer from the top down, so the forest floor gets the least and is where shade plants and decomposers concentrate. The canopy is the topmost, best-lit layer (not shaded by clouds), the layers do not get equal light, and the understorey is the middle layer, not buried in soil.

39. (A) A mould fungus is a decomposer; its job is breaking dead matter into humus that enriches the soil. A deer is a living-plant-eating herbivore, a mango tree is a producer (it makes food, it does not decompose anything), and a hawk is a carnivore that hunts live prey — none of these decomposes dead matter.

40. (C) Both webs start at the grass, a green producer that traps sunlight, so every animal in either web ultimately draws its energy from that producer. Energy does not start at the top predators and flow down, animals cannot make their own energy, and animals get energy by eating, not by feeding directly on humus.

41. (C) Energy shrinks at every link, so the organism farthest from the producer — the hawk at the top — relies on the smallest remaining share. It is not about body size (the grasshopper is near the bottom and gets a larger share), the grass actually captures the most energy as the producer, and the frog in the middle still gets more than the hawk above it.

42. (A) The forest keeps its soil fertile because decomposers continuously recycle nutrients from dead matter back into the soil, replacing what growing plants withdraw. Plants do not create nutrients from sunlight, the nutrients are genuinely taken up and built into tissue (not merely borrowed), and rain does not deliver a daily fresh nutrient supply from the sky.

43. (B) Because one decomposer community recycles the dead from both the pond and the land chains, those chains are linked through the shared cycling of nutrients into a common soil. This shows water and land chains are connected, not separate; decomposers act on both aquatic and terrestrial dead matter; and they are not partitioned chain-by-chain.

44. (B) The fish feed the kingfisher and graze the algae, so losing them starves the kingfisher and lets algae accumulate. The land deer and tiger do not eat fish, so they are not directly hit; the algae are eaten by the fish (the fish are not the algae's food); and the kingfisher cannot increase when its food disappears.

45. (A) By day the forest's plants photosynthesise, drawing in carbon dioxide and giving off oxygen, which keeps the air balanced and fresh. They do not work by turning dust into nitrogen, tall trees do not wall off stale air, and animals breathe out carbon dioxide (not oxygen) — it is the plants that supply the oxygen.

46. (B) Without the forest's sponge of roots and litter, rain runs straight off — giving floods in the wet season and a dry stream later, the opposite of the steady year-round flow before. The trees did not 'drink the stream dry' (they actually steadied it), forests clearly do affect water flow, and bare soil soaks up less, so no lake forms.

47. (D) As decomposers break the fallen trunk down over the years, the nutrients in the wood pass back into the soil for new plants to reuse. They are not lost forever (the cycle reclaims them), they do not travel up into other trees' canopies, and matter cannot be converted into sunlight.

48. (D) Energy starts at the Sun, is captured by green grass (the producer), passes to the plant-eating deer, then to the meat-eating tiger — so Sun → grass → deer → tiger is right. The reversed arrows are wrong because energy does not flow back toward the Sun, the Sun is the source and cannot sit after the grass, and a deer cannot precede the grass it eats.

49. (A) Bina is right: a forest yields products such as timber but, just as importantly, supplies oxygen, soil protection, water recharge and wildlife habitat all together. Aman wrongly reduces it to timber, it is false that forests give nothing useful, and a forest's value extends far beyond being a tiger's home.

50. (C) Conservation means safeguarding the existing forest and restoring lost forest by planting trees. Clearing it for a crop destroys the very services in question, removing decomposers would wreck nutrient recycling and soil fertility, and felling the canopy exposes and harms the lower layers — none of these conserves the forest.

51. (C) Living plants respire and so do take in some oxygen, but during daytime photosynthesis they release far more than they consume, giving a net gain of oxygen to the air. So it is wrong to say they never take any in; equally wrong to say they only take in and never release; and false that they exchange no gases at all.

52. (B) The upper layers intercept the light, so the floor is dim, yet its accumulation of dead matter and moisture feeds vast numbers of decomposers and small ground dwellers — so abundant life and low light go together there. Abundant life does not require the most light, the floor is not secretly the brightest layer, and forest floor organisms do not make their own light.

53. (B) Since the snake eats both mice and frogs, losing the mice tightens its food supply but it can still eat frogs, so it is pressed but not wiped out. The mouse was not its only food (so it does not die at once), the snake did eat mice (so it is affected), and the snake does not eat clover, so more clover would not help it increase.

54. (D) Clover is the only green plant here, so it is the producer that makes the food every other organism in the web depends on. The snake is a top carnivore (it consumes, it does not produce), the frog is a carnivore in the middle, and the caterpillar is a plant-eating herbivore — being the first animal does not make it a producer.

55. (D) A forest's range of layers and habitats houses many species, so safeguarding the forest safeguards the diversity of life that lives there. A forest is not a single-species place, biodiversity arises from the living community rather than from minerals alone, and most forest species cannot simply move to bare land — they depend on the forest.

56. (B) The step-by-step build-up from herbs to shrubs to a layered, humus-rich forest over decades shows the forest is a dynamic system that develops and renews over long time. It is not fixed once bushes appear, it can establish naturally rather than only by human planting, and it certainly cannot reach its full layered form in one season.

57. (B) It is a cycle because the nutrients keep being reused — returned to the soil by decomposers and taken up again — rather than running out at a final link like a one-way chain. It is not about the loop being a literal equal-sized circle, not about how many organisms a chain may hold, and the nutrients are recycled, not destroyed and remade.

58. (A) They earn the title 'recyclers' by converting dead matter and dung into humus and thereby returning nutrients to the soil for plants to use again. They do not photosynthesise food from sunlight (that is the producers' job), they act on dead matter rather than carrying living things underground, and they return nutrients rather than removing them for good.

59. (C) Grass is the producer feeding the zebra, which feeds the lion and hyena, so destroying the grass starves the zebra and then the carnivores too — the whole web is hit. It is not only the zebra (the meat-eaters depend on the zebra, which depends on grass), not only the lion, and the carnivores cannot make their own food, so they are not spared.

60. (B) A forest earns the name 'lifeline' because it does many life-supporting jobs together — giving oxygen, recycling nutrients, holding soil and water, steadying climate and sheltering species. Reducing it to timber, to a place that matters only to its animals, or to a mere stand of trees all ignore the full set of services that make it a lifeline.
