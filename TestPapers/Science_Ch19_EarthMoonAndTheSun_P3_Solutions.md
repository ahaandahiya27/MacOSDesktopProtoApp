## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** The Moon is not a light source; everything we see of it is sunlight bounced off its surface. Cut off the sunlight and there is nothing to reflect, so the Moon would vanish from view. It does not store and re-emit light, it produces no light of its own (unlike a star which makes light by fusion), and there is no Earth-glow strong enough to make it shine on its own — those options assume the Moon is a source of light, which it is not.

**2. (D)** At new Moon the Moon lies roughly between Earth and the Sun, so its lit half points toward the Sun and away from us; the near side is dark, making it nearly invisible. It is NOT in Earth's shadow (that situation is a lunar eclipse, which happens at full Moon), the Moon never stops reflecting, and the Moon does not actually change size — only the visible lit fraction changes.

**3. (B)** After new Moon the lit part we see grows (waxes): a thin crescent first, then half-lit at first quarter, then more than half (gibbous), then fully lit at full Moon. The correct order is new → waxing crescent → first quarter → waxing gibbous → full. The other orders jumble the growing sequence — gibbous (more than half) cannot come before the crescent (less than half), and the lit fraction must increase steadily from new to full.

**4. (D)** Phases come from seeing different amounts of the Moon's sunlit half as it orbits Earth. Earth's shadow does fall on the Moon occasionally — but that event is a lunar eclipse, which happens only a couple of times a year, not the steady monthly cycle of phases. Earth's shadow can reach the Moon (that is why lunar eclipses exist), brightness is irrelevant to the shape, and clouds cannot create the precise repeating phase pattern.

**5. (B)** If the Moon did not spin at all, we would actually get to see all its sides over one orbit. Because its spin period equals its orbit period (about 27.3 days each), it turns to keep the same face pointed at Earth the whole way around. A fast spin would show us all sides too, and Earth's magnetism is far too weak to lock a body the size of the Moon.

**6. (D)** Keeping one face toward Earth actually requires the Moon to turn once per orbit. A Moon that did not spin would keep its face pointed at the same distant direction in space while it circled us, so over one orbit Earth would face every side of it in turn. Reflection does not depend on spin, and the flipping-each-night idea confuses the daily rotation of Earth with the Moon's monthly motion.

**7. (C)** Sound is a vibration that must pass through a material like air, water, or rock to travel; with no air on the Moon, vibrations cannot reach a listener through the empty space between them. (Vibrations could still pass through the solid ground.) Gravity does not block sound, cold does not 'freeze' it, and the Moon's slow spin is irrelevant — the missing ingredient is air.

**8. (A)** On Earth wind and flowing water quickly wipe out footprints. The Moon has no atmosphere (no wind) and no liquid water, so the usual agents of erosion are absent and prints persist for ages. The Moon's gravity is weak, not strong; it has essentially no atmosphere rather than a thick one; and it is not volcanically active in a way that would resurface footprints.

**9. (B)** On Earth the atmosphere acts like a blanket, storing daytime heat and moderating the night, so temperatures stay in a narrow range. The Moon has no such blanket, so sunlit ground gets very hot and shaded ground gets very cold. The Moon is essentially the same distance from the Sun as Earth, it does not generate heat by itself, and its slow spin actually gives long hot days and long cold nights — but the root cause is the missing atmosphere.

**10. (B)** The Moon's gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's, so an astronaut weighs about a sixth as much and the same push sends them roughly six times higher. Gravity there is not the same as Earth's, not six times stronger (which would make jumping harder), and not zero — they still fall back down, just slowly.

**11. (B)** The lit part of the Moon is the half being struck by sunlight, so the bright curve always bulges toward the Sun and the horns of the crescent point away from it. The dark part is simply the unlit far portion, not Earth's shadow; the direction is set by the Sun, not random; and it is not fixed straight up — it tilts to face wherever the Sun is.

**12. (A)** The 'quarter' refers to the fraction of the orbit completed, not the fraction we see lit: about a quarter of the ~29.5-day cycle has passed since new Moon. (The Sun lights half the Moon at all times; we just happen to see half of that lit half — a 'half Moon' shape.) The Moon does not change size, and Earth's shadow is not involved in phases.

**13. (A)** Every part of the Moon, including the far side, receives sunlight as the Moon rotates — the far side simply can never be observed from Earth because of the Moon's locked rotation. Calling it the 'dark side' is misleading: it is the 'far' side, not a permanently unlit one. It is lit directly by the Sun, not just by faint earthshine, and it is not eternally frozen.

**14. (C)** At any instant the Sun can only light the hemisphere pointed toward it; the other hemisphere is in Earth's own night side. As Earth rotates, places move from the lit half to the dark half, so somewhere is always in day and somewhere always in night. The Sun cannot light all of a sphere at once, it never switches off, and the Moon's shadow is tiny and rare — it cannot blanket a whole country's night.

**15. (D)** Earth rotates eastward (west to east), so to us the whole sky — including the Sun — seems to slide the other way, rising in the east and setting in the west. The Sun does not orbit Earth, the Moon does not drag the Sun, and Earth's revolution takes a year, not a day, so it cannot explain the daily east-to-west march of the Sun.

**16. (B)** The Sun is an ordinary, fairly average star, but it is enormously closer to us than any other star, so its light and heat reach us strongly while distant stars appear as faint points. It is not the biggest or hottest star, it is not the only light-and-heat source (every star makes its own), and it is a star, not a burning planet.

**17. (A)** At full Moon, Earth lies between the Sun and Moon, so the Moon's whole near side is sunlit; at new Moon the Moon lies between Earth and the Sun, so its lit side faces away from us. The first distractor swaps the two arrangements, Earth's shadow only touches the Moon at a lunar eclipse (not every full and new Moon), and the Moon always only reflects — it never makes its own light at any phase.

**18. (C)** 27.3 days is the time to circle once relative to the distant stars. But Earth keeps moving around the Sun, so after 27.3 days the Sun–Earth–Moon line is not yet repeated; the Moon needs about two more days of orbiting to catch up and reach full Moon again. The Moon does not stop or change speed for this reason, and Earth's shadow has nothing to do with the length of the phase cycle.

**19. (A)** 'Waning' means decreasing: after full Moon the lit fraction we see shrinks each night through waning gibbous, last quarter, and waning crescent until new Moon. 'Waxing' would mean growing (new toward full). The lit fraction changes gradually over days, not hour to hour, and it certainly does not stay frozen.

**20. (A)** A big object far away can look the same size as a small object close up. The Sun is about 400 times wider but also about 400 times farther, so the two appear nearly equal in the sky — which is why the Moon can just cover the Sun during a solar eclipse. The Moon is genuinely much smaller, the Sun does not change size, and reflection does not magnify an object.

**21. (C)** Phases are simply about how much of a sunlit sphere a viewer can see. From the Moon, Earth is half-lit by the Sun, and depending on the geometry the astronaut sees more or less of that lit half — Earth's phases. Earth does not make its own visible light, the Moon's shadow on Earth is tiny (a solar eclipse), and Earth does not change shape.

**22. (C)** The terminator is the line dividing day from night on the Moon — exactly where the Sun sits on the horizon (rising or setting) for the ground there. It moves as the Moon rotates. It is not the edge of Earth's shadow (that only appears in a lunar eclipse), it is not a permanent fixed split, and the Moon has no light of its own to fade.

**23. (B)** A solar eclipse needs the Moon between Sun and Earth (the new-Moon geometry); a lunar eclipse needs Earth between Sun and Moon (the full-Moon geometry). Only at these phases is a near-straight Sun–Moon–Earth line possible. The Moon never makes its own light, its distance from Earth is not set by phase, and Earth never stops rotating.

**24. (B)** Even inside Earth's shadow, sunlight passing through the thin ring of Earth's atmosphere is bent (refracted) and reddened, then reaches the Moon, giving it a coppery glow. The atmosphere does not make its own light, the Moon is not on fire, and ocean colour is not what is reaching the Moon — it is refracted, reddened sunlight.

**25. (C)** Stars generate their own light (the Sun is a star); the Moon makes none and is visible only because it reflects sunlight. So 'they both shine' hides a key difference. The Moon is far cooler than a star, stars are seen mostly at night (the Sun, a star, rules the day), and the Moon is vastly smaller than stars — so those other statements are false.

**26. (D)** After a waxing crescent the lit part keeps growing; about a week into the cycle it reaches half-lit, which is first quarter. Full Moon comes about two weeks after new Moon (not one), new Moon is when the lit side faces away (the start, not a week later), and a waning crescent is a shrinking sliver near the end of the cycle, not a growing half-disc.

**27. (D)** The Sun is by far the largest — a star vastly bigger than Earth. Earth is larger than the Moon (the Moon is roughly a quarter of Earth's width). So the true order is Sun > Earth > Moon. The other orderings either shrink the Sun below Earth or place the Moon above Earth, both of which are wrong.

**28. (A)** On Earth, wind, rain, rivers, and living things erase craters over time. The Moon lacks atmosphere and liquid water, so erosion is almost absent and old craters survive nearly unchanged. The lunar craters are ancient (not young), they are not freshly remade to look old, and the Moon's gravity is weak — it is the absence of erosion, not strong gravity, that preserves them.

**29. (D)** Earth spins eastward, so the Sun, Moon, and stars all appear to swing from east to west each day and night — that is what makes the Moon drift across the sky in one night. The Moon takes about a month, not a night, to orbit Earth; sunlight does not push it westward; and Earth's orbit around the Sun takes a year. The nightly drift is the spin of Earth.

**30. (C)** A thin crescent occurs when the Moon is angularly near the Sun (only a little of the lit half shows). Being near the Sun, it appears close to it in the sky and follows it down, visible briefly after sunset in the west. The full Moon (not the crescent) sits opposite the Sun and rises at sunset; the crescent is not overhead at midnight; and the sliver is from geometry, not Earth's shadow.

**31. (D)** Liquid water needs the right pressure and temperature; with essentially no atmosphere and huge temperature extremes, water on the Moon's surface cannot remain liquid (it would freeze or escape as vapour). The Moon is at about Earth's distance from the Sun (not specially close), its gravity is weak (not strong), and the claim it lacks any water-forming material is not the reasoned cause — the missing atmosphere and extreme conditions are.

**32. (B)** Full Moon means Earth is between the Sun and Moon, so the Moon sits opposite the Sun in our sky; when the Sun sets in the west the Moon must be rising in the east. A Moon between Earth and Sun would be a new Moon (up in daytime), the Moon makes no light of its own, and Earth never stops spinning.

**33. (A)** The Sun lights one full hemisphere of the Moon at all times; phases arise because, from our angle, we see varying amounts of that always-lit half — none at new Moon, all at full Moon, and parts in between. It is not true that only the Earth-facing side gets sunlight, the far half is never lit by the Moon itself, and the Moon never makes its own light.

**34. (B)** Because the Moon rotates once per orbit, the same near side always points at Earth and the far side always points away — so from Earth we can never see it, though a spacecraft can fly around to view it. The far side is not always dark (it gets sunlight as the Moon rotates), it is not inside Earth's shadow, and it does not permanently face away from the Sun.

**35. (C)** Day and night anywhere come from how fast that body rotates relative to the Sun. The Moon rotates about once a month, so sunlight stays on one spot for roughly two weeks and then darkness for two weeks — a day–night cycle of about a month. It is not 24 hours (that is Earth's spin), not an hour, and not a year.

**36. (B)** Earthshine is sunlight that first bounces off Earth, lands on the Moon's night side, and bounces back to us — a double reflection that faintly lights the dark part. The Moon makes no light of its own, it is not glowing from interior heat, and starlight is far too weak to produce this effect.

**37. (B)** Day and night come from rotation; spin half as fast and each daylight period and each night period would last about twice as long. The length of the year depends on Earth's orbit, not its spin, so it would be unchanged; seasons come from the axial tilt, not the spin rate; and the Sun would still rise in the east as long as Earth still turns west-to-east — just more slowly.

**38. (A)** Gibbous means we see more than half but not all of the lit hemisphere; geometrically the Moon is past first quarter but not yet at full, so most of the sunlit side faces us. A Moon directly between Earth and Sun is new (lit side hidden), the new-to-crescent positions show only a sliver, and Earth's shadow relates to eclipses, not ordinary phases.

**39. (A)** The Sun generates its own light and heat; the Moon makes none and merely reflects a small part of the sunlight it receives, so moonlight is much fainter and not warming. The Moon does not make light independently, moonlight is far weaker than sunlight (reflection loses most of the energy), and it is not stored heat re-emitted at night.

**40. (D)** Phases depend on the angle between the Sun, Moon, and observer. Even with the Moon fixed, Earth keeps moving (rotating and orbiting), so that angle would still change and we would still see the lit fraction vary — phases would continue. The Moon would not lock to one phase, it would keep reflecting sunlight, and it could never begin to make light of its own.

**41. (C)** Distance is the key: the Sun is unimaginably nearer than any other star, so it spreads into a disc and floods us with light, while even larger stars are so far away they shrink to points. The other stars are genuine stars (not reflective rocks), the Sun is not the largest star, and other stars do make their own light — they are just extremely distant.

**42. (A)** Because Earth's axis is tilted, in the polar summer the pole leans sunward and stays in the lit hemisphere even as Earth spins, so the Sun does not set for weeks. Earth never stops rotating, the Sun cannot light a whole sphere at once, and the Moon's faint reflected light has nothing to do with continuous polar daylight.

**43. (C)** The Moon's spin and orbit take about the same time (~27.3 days), so it turns at just the right rate to keep one face pointed at Earth. A much faster spin or a stopped spin would both let us see all sides over a month, and a slower spin than orbit would not keep the face locked either — equality is what produces the single visible face.

**44. (C)** Air resistance is what normally slows a feather more than a hammer. With no atmosphere on the Moon, there is no air resistance, so gravity alone acts and both objects fall together. The Moon's gravity is weak (about one-sixth of Earth's), it is not zero (they do fall), and magnetism does not pull a feather — the missing factor is air.

**45. (C)** Over one ~29.5-day cycle the visible lit fraction grows (waxes) from new to full, then shrinks (wanes) from full back to new — a smooth swelling and shrinking. It does not grow the whole way (it must shrink after full), it is not fully lit the whole time, and the change is gradual over days, not a sudden midnight jump.

**46. (A)** The Sun is a fairly typical star among billions; it dominates our sky only because it is by far the nearest star to us. It is not the smallest or dimmest, it is not a one-of-a-kind object, and it is genuinely a star (a self-luminous ball of gas), not a planet.

**47. (B)** The only relevant difference is air: Earth's air resists the light paper more than the dense coin, while the Moon's lack of atmosphere removes that resistance so both fall together. The Moon's gravity is weaker, not stronger; it is not zero (objects still fall); and the paper's mass does not change between worlds.

**48. (D)** The Sun is a star pouring out its own light, so directly is overwhelming; the Moon merely reflects a small portion of sunlight, so moonlight is gentle. The Moon is not secretly brighter, the Sun does not get its light from the Moon, and the Moon does reflect some light (we can see it), so it does not absorb all sunlight.

**49. (D)** The Sun lights only the hemisphere facing it; Earth's rotation continually moves any location through the sunlit half (day) and the shadowed half (night). The Sun does not orbit us to take turns lighting cities, city lights are unrelated to day and night, and the Moon's shadow is tiny and rare — it cannot give every city its nightly darkness.

**50. (C)** Order in the cycle: new → first quarter → full → last quarter → new. So last quarter falls after full Moon as the lit fraction shrinks (wanes). It does not come before first quarter, it is not at new Moon (which shows no light), and it has nothing to do with a lunar eclipse — it is an ordinary phase.

**51. (B)** A meteor glows because it rams through Earth's air and heats up. The Moon has no atmosphere, so incoming rocks do not burn — they hit the ground and dig craters. The Moon's gravity, though weak, still draws objects in; coldness does not stop burning (friction with air does the heating, and there is no air); and the Moon does not reflect solid rocks away.

**52. (A)** When you turn one way, the scenery appears to slide the opposite way. Earth spins eastward, so sky objects appear to move from east to west and thus rise in the east, set in the west. They do not appear to move west-to-east (that is the spin direction itself), they are not seen as fixed (the sky clearly drifts), and they do not rise in the north.

**53. (A)** Because the Moon rotates once per orbit, its far side faces the Sun for part of each lunar cycle and is sunlit then — it is the 'far' side (never seen from Earth), not a permanently dark one. The far side does receive sunlight, the Moon does rotate (so it has a far side), and it is not trapped in Earth's shadow.

**54. (D)** At most full Moons the slightly tilted lunar orbit carries the Moon just above or below Earth's shadow, so no eclipse occurs; only when the alignment is close enough does the Moon enter the shadow. Earth's shadow does reach the Moon's distance (eclipses do happen), lunar eclipses require full Moon (not new), and the Moon never makes its own light.

**55. (A)** On Earth, air scatters sunlight after sunset, giving a soft twilight; the Moon has no atmosphere to scatter light, so once the Sun is below the horizon, darkness is sudden. The Moon does not have a thick atmosphere, its rotation is slow (long days), and it has no light of its own to switch off.

**56. (B)** Strictly, the Moon does not 'shine' on its own — it reflects the Sun's light to us, which we perceive as brightness. It does not burn gases (that is what a star does), it does not store and later release light, and its glow is not from internal heat. Reflection of sunlight is the accurate description.

**57. (D)** The Moon is our nearest neighbour; the Sun is much farther but is still by far the closest star; every other star is enormously farther still. So nearest-to-farthest is Moon, Sun, next-nearest star. The other orders wrongly put the Sun nearer than the Moon, place a distant star before the Sun, or rank the far star before the Sun.

**58. (C)** Day and night come from a planet turning so different sides face the Sun. If Earth always kept one face sunward, that side would be in endless day and the opposite side in endless night. It would not give normal day–night (that needs the planet to turn relative to the Sun), the Sun still cannot light a whole sphere at once, and being locked in rotation does not stop the orbit.

**59. (C)** Both crescents look alike on a single night, but tracking them over days separates them: a waxing crescent is heading toward full Moon so its lit part grows, while a waning crescent is heading toward new Moon so its lit part shrinks. Temperature does not distinguish phases, the Moon always only reflects sunlight in every phase, and crescents are visible from across the world, not tied to single poles.

**60. (D)** Earth's daytime sky is blue because air molecules scatter sunlight in all directions. The Moon has no atmosphere to scatter light, so sunlight travels straight to the ground and the surrounding sky stays black even with the Sun up. The Moon has essentially no atmosphere (not a dark one), it is not in Earth's shadow at lunar noon, and sunlight clearly does reach it (it is noon there).
