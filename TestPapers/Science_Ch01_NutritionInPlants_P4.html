Instructions: Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Classic traps: treating respiration as switched off in daylight; reading a net gas measurement as if only one process were running; confusing which factor (chlorophyll vs light vs CO2 vs water) a single controlled set-up actually isolates; assuming soil nitrogen is a raw material of the day's photosynthesis or that air nitrogen is directly usable; calling a parasite a saprotroph/symbiont; assuming a positive starch test proves the tissue is green; and mixing up xylem (water, up) with phloem (food, out from leaves). Do not write on this paper — mark answers on your answer sheet.

1. A sealed glass chamber holds one bean leaf in bright, steady light. Over a fixed hour the leaf takes in 18 units of carbon dioxide for one process while a second process inside the same leaf releases 6 units of carbon dioxide. A meter on the chamber wall measures only the NET change of the gas in the surrounding air. What does the meter read for carbon dioxide over that hour?
(A) A net fall of 18 units, because respiration switches off in the light and only photosynthesis runs.(B) A net fall of 12 units, because photosynthesis used up more carbon dioxide than respiration released.(C) A net rise of 6 units, because only the carbon dioxide given out by the leaf reaches the air.(D) No change at all, because the two processes always cancel exactly inside a leaf.

1. (B) Both processes run at once in the lit leaf. Photosynthesis pulls 18 units of CO2 out of the air; respiration puts 6 units back. The meter sees the NET effect on the air: 18 removed minus 6 added = 12 removed, i.e. a net fall of 12 units. The claim that respiration switches off in light is the day-respiration misconception — respiration never stops, so 18 is wrong. The 'net rise of 6' answer counts only the respiratory output and forgets the much larger photosynthetic uptake from the same air. 'No change/always cancel' is only true at the exact compensation point where the two rates happen to be equal; here uptake clearly exceeds release, so the gases do not cancel.

2. (D) A zero NET reading means CO2 taken in by photosynthesis equals CO2 given out by respiration — the compensation point. Lowering light reduces photosynthesis until its uptake rate drops to match the steady respiratory output, giving zero net change while both processes still run. Total darkness does NOT halt both processes: respiration continues, so the meter would show a net RISE, not zero. Maximum brightness drives photosynthesis far above respiration, giving a large net FALL, not balance. Removing chlorophyll stops photosynthesis entirely while respiration continues, again giving a net rise — and it does stop a process, which the question forbids.

3. (C) The carbon of plant food comes from carbon dioxide, which enters the LEAF through stomata (not the roots/soil), is fixed into glucose during photosynthesis, then travels as food through the PHLOEM to storage organs where surplus glucose is stored as starch. The first wrong option mislocates CO2 uptake at the roots and uses xylem (which carries water, not food). The minerals option confuses soil minerals with the carbon source — minerals are needed for health but are not the carbon backbone of glucose. The oxygen option names the wrong gas: oxygen is a PRODUCT released, not the raw material that supplies carbon.

4. (D) The bulk of a plant's dry body is made of glucose/starch and related substances whose carbon comes from carbon dioxide fixed in photosynthesis — so the new dry mass is mostly carbon captured from CO2, not soil minerals. Water is taken in, but it is the source of hydrogen, not the bulk solid dry mass attributed here, and 'turning water directly into solid body' misstates the process. Oxygen is a product RELEASED by photosynthesis, not a building block absorbed to add mass. The student's root-feeding idea is the classic soil-as-food misconception the chapter explicitly overturns: roots supply water and minerals, but food (and thus most dry mass) is made in the leaves from CO2.

5. (A) A controlled experiment isolates one factor by changing ONLY that factor between two otherwise-identical set-ups. To prove carbon dioxide specifically is needed, keep light, water, warmth and the plant identical and differ ONLY in CO2: one leaf's air has its CO2 absorbed by potassium hydroxide while the other, on the same plant, stays in ordinary air. Any difference in the starch test can then be pinned on CO2 alone. Wrapping one leaf in foil changes LIGHT, not CO2. A plant kept two days in the dark differs in light/destarching history, not CO2. A variegated leaf (green vs white) tests CHLOROPHYLL, not carbon dioxide.

'Entire green border regardless of foil' ignores the light requirement on the shaded green. 'Whole uncovered half including white' ignores the chlorophyll requirement on the white tissue. 'No region at all' is wrong because the lit-green tissue still has everything it needs.

A white patch contains no chlorophyll at all, so it cannot make food and tests negative for starch — exactly the difference observed. Calling red pigment 'a kind of chlorophyll' is false; colour alone does not equal chlorophyll. The soil-feeding answer revives the food-from-soil misconception. 'White tissue makes food using white light' is backwards — colourless tissue without chlorophyll cannot photosynthesise no matter the light.

9. (A) Xylem (intact) carries water UP to the leaves, so the leaves keep their water and keep photosynthesising. 10. (A) Glucose is soluble; large amounts dissolved in cells would upset the cell's water balance by drawing in water. Converting it to insoluble starch lets the plant stockpile food compactly without that osmotic disturbance — which is why surplus glucose is stored as starch. Starch cannot be made directly from sunlight; glucose is made first in photosynthesis, then converted. Starch is a solid food store, not a gas the plant breathes out — that confuses it with carbon dioxide. Starch is not a pigment; the green pigment is chlorophyll, a different substance entirely.

11. (B) Boiling the leaf in alcohol is the step that removes the green chlorophyll; skipping it leaves the chlorophyll in place. Iodine reacts with starch directly and needs no alcohol to 'trigger' it; alcohol's only job is to strip the masking chlorophyll so the colour can be seen.

12. (C) The plant is kept in the dark first so its leaves use up their stored starch (destarching). The second pea, moved into light on day 3, starts photosynthesising and survives. So the pair shows: stored food powers early dark growth, but only light lets the seedling make food and survive once the store is gone. Seedlings do not photosynthesise faster in the dark — they cannot photosynthesise at all without light. 31. (A) Transpiration is the loss of water vapour through stomata, which then condenses as droplets in the bag. A living leaf's stomata work, so it transpires and droplets form; a dead leaf's cells no longer regulate or drive this loss, so far fewer droplets appear. The rain idea is excluded — the bag is sealed and identical for both, so any difference is the leaf itself. Xylem carries water inside the plant but does not 'squirt' water out of the surface; loss is as vapour through stomata. The dead-leaf option is backwards: a dead leaf does not MAKE water; it loses less because its transpiration has effectively stopped.

34. (A) Green plants are the great regulators of atmospheric gases. So by the end oxygen has RISEN and carbon dioxide has FALLEN. 'O2 fallen, CO2 risen' describes net respiration (darkness), the opposite case. 'Both risen' is impossible when CO2 is being consumed. Nitrogen is not taken in or released by either process, so it does not fall sharply.

52. (C) A green plant begins a food chain because it captures sunlight to create brand-new food, introducing fresh energy into the chain — it is a producer. A mushroom only breaks down food (dead matter) that already exists; it adds no new energy, so it cannot be the starting point. The green plant is not 'the only thing that respires' — all the organisms respire. The mushroom does not eat the plant to begin the chain. And size has nothing to do with which organism is the producer.

53. (D) A tiger eats only meat, but the deer it eats fed on plants — so the energy in the tiger's food was first captured from sunlight by green plants and passed up the chain. Plants also release the oxygen the tiger needs to release that energy by respiration. That is the deep, universal dependence. Occasional grass-nibbling when ill is trivial and not the reason. Plants do not pass soil minerals 'directly' to the tiger. And plants release most of their oxygen in daylight, not specially at night.

54. (C) Light is the energy source for the algae's photosynthesis. Persistent shade starves them of light, so they make little food and decline (producers fail first). The snails, which depend on the algae for food, then run short and decline too — the impact passes up the chain from the base. 'Snails thrive on softer algae' invents a benefit; less light means less algae, not better food. Algae cannot 'feed on silt' — they are autotrophs that need light, not particles, to make food. 'Algae don't need light' is flatly false; without light photosynthesis stops.

55. (A) Autotrophs make their own food, but not from nothing — they still need raw materials and energy from outside: carbon dioxide, water and especially light. Locked in a dark cupboard a green plant cannot photosynthesise, so it cannot feed itself and will starve once its reserves are gone. 'Need nothing once grown' is exactly the misconception being corrected. The plant cannot survive by eating the cupboard's wood, nor by taking ready-made food from the air — those are not how a green plant feeds.

56. (B) Carbon dioxide is a raw material of photosynthesis. The KOH removes it from one leaf's air, so that leaf cannot make starch and stays brown; only the normal-air leaf makes starch and turns blue-black. The student's error was treating CO2 as something whose removal HELPS, when in fact CO2 is essential and its removal stops food-making. 'Both turn blue-black, light alone is enough' ignores that CO2 is needed too. 'Neither, destarching destroyed the ability' misunderstands destarching, which only empties stored starch and does not disable photosynthesis. 'KOH feeds the leaf' inverts KOH's role — it ABSORBS CO2, it does not provide it.

57. (D) The leaf releases net oxygen by day (photosynthesis exceeds respiration) and takes in net oxygen by night (only respiration). At the two zero-crossing instants around dawn and dusk, the rate of oxygen made by photosynthesis exactly equals the rate of oxygen used by respiration — the compensation point — so the NET exchange is zero while both processes still run. The leaf has not stopped both processes — respiration never stops. It is not 'only respiring' or 'photosynthesis completely stopped' at those instants — those describe the night, which would give a net oxygen UPTAKE, not zero.

58. (A) Saprotrophs such as fungi and bacteria feed on dead leaves, wood and animal remains by breaking them down — and in doing so they release the nutrients locked inside that dead matter back into the soil, where green plants can absorb and reuse them. That recycling is why decomposers matter even though they make no food themselves. They do not photosynthesise or pass on sun-made food (they have no chlorophyll), and they are not the source of plants' oxygen — green plants make their own oxygen in photosynthesis. 'No real importance' ignores their essential role in returning nutrients to the soil.

59. (A) A plant's body is largely made of food (glucose/starch and related substances) whose carbon comes from carbon dioxide fixed in the leaves during photosynthesis — so the great bulk of new solid mass is built from a gas in the air, not solid soil. Roots take up water and dissolved minerals, not whole solid soil that hardens into wood, so the first wrong option misdescribes how roots work. Oxygen is a PRODUCT released by photosynthesis, not a raw material the tree turns into solid wood. The girl's whole-soil idea is the food-from-soil misconception the chapter overturns: the trunk's mass traces mostly to carbon dioxide, with water supplying hydrogen.

60. (B) Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals UPWARD from the roots; phloem carries the food (sugars) made in the leaves AWAY from them, largely downward. So the correct match is water/minerals = xylem/up and food = phloem/away-and-down. The first wrong option swaps the two tissues. 'Both in the xylem' is false — the plant has two distinct transport tissues. The last option reverses both directions (water down, food up). Only this row keeps each tissue with its correct cargo and direction.
