Instructions: Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Classic traps: treating respiration as switched off in daylight; reading a net gas measurement as if only one process were running; confusing the role (chlorophyll vs light vs CO2 vs water) that a single controlled set-up actually isolates; assuming soil nitrogen is a raw material of the day's photosynthesis or that air nitrogen is directly usable; calling a parasite a saprotroph/symbiont; assuming a positive starch test proves the tissue is green; and mixing up xylem (water, up) with phloem (food, out from leaves). Worked Solutions

1. (B) Both processes run at once in the lit leaf. Photosynthesis pulls 18 units of CO2 out of the air; respiration puts 6 units back. The meter sees the NET effect on the air: 18 removed minus 6 added = 12 removed, i.e. a net fall of 12 units. The claim that respiration switches off in light is the day-respiration misconception — respiration never stops, so 18 is wrong. The 'net rise of 6' answer counts only the respiratory output and forgets the much larger photosynthetic uptake from the same air. 'No change/always cancel' is only true at the exact compensation point where the two rates happen to be equal; here uptake clearly exceeds release, so the gases do not cancel.

2. (D) A zero NET reading means CO2 taken in by photosynthesis equals CO2 given out by respiration — the compensation point. Lowering light reduces photosynthesis until its uptake rate drops to match the steady respiratory output, giving zero net change while both processes still run. Total darkness does NOT halt both processes: respiration continues, so the meter would show a net RISE, not zero. Maximum brightness drives photosynthesis far above respiration, giving a large net FALL, not balance. Removing chlorophyll stops photosynthesis entirely while respiration continues, again giving a net rise — and it does stop a process, which the question forbids.

3. (C) The carbon of plant food comes from carbon dioxide, which enters the LEAF through stomata (not the roots/soil), is fixed into glucose during photosynthesis, then travels as food through the PHLOEM to storage organs where surplus glucose is stored as starch. The first wrong option mislocates CO2 uptake at the roots and uses xylem (which carries water, not food). The minerals option confuses soil minerals with the carbon source — minerals are needed for health but are not the carbon backbone of glucose. The oxygen option names the wrong gas: oxygen is a PRODUCT released, not the raw material that supplies carbon.

4. (D) The bulk of a plant's dry body is made of glucose/starch and related substances whose carbon comes from carbon dioxide fixed in photosynthesis — so the new dry mass is mostly carbon captured from CO2, not soil minerals. Water is taken in, but it is the source of hydrogen, not the bulk solid dry mass attributed here, and 'turning water directly into solid body' misstates the process. Oxygen is a product RELEASED by photosynthesis, not a building block absorbed to add mass. The student's root-feeding idea is the classic soil-as-food misconception the chapter explicitly overturns: roots supply water and minerals, but food (and thus most dry mass) is made in the leaves from CO2.

5. (C) Both interventions cut off the carbon dioxide supply, just by different means. Leaf 1 sits in air whose CO2 is chemically removed by KOH; Leaf 2's stomata — the pores through which CO2 enters — are clogged by grease on the lower surface where most stomata lie. Without CO2 neither can make starch, so neither turns blue-black; only the untouched Leaf 3 does. 'All three' ignores that being on a lit plant cannot substitute for the missing raw material. The grease-can't-stop-it option forgets that blocking stomata blocks CO2 entry, the very thing the starch test here depends on. The KOH-supplies-CO2 option inverts KOH's role: it ABSORBS carbon dioxide, it does not release it.

6. (C) Starch forms only where ALL requirements are met. Photosynthesis needs chlorophyll (so white tissue fails) and light (so foil-covered tissue fails). The only region satisfying both is green tissue that was also lit — it alone turns blue-black. Comparing the lit-green region with the lit-white region shows chlorophyll is needed; comparing the lit-green region with the covered-green region shows light is needed — so one leaf demonstrates both. 'Entire green border regardless of foil' ignores the light requirement on the shaded green. 'Whole uncovered half including white' ignores the chlorophyll requirement on the white tissue. 'No region at all' is wrong because the lit-green tissue still has everything it needs.

7. (B) The decisive factor is the presence of chlorophyll, the light-capturing pigment. A red leaf only LOOKS non-green because a red pigment masks the chlorophyll that is still present, so it photosynthesises and grows. A white patch contains no chlorophyll at all, so it cannot make food and tests negative for starch — exactly the difference observed. Calling red pigment 'a kind of chlorophyll' is false; colour alone does not equal chlorophyll. The soil-feeding answer revives the food-from-soil misconception. 'White tissue makes food using white light' is backwards — colourless tissue without chlorophyll cannot photosynthesise no matter the light.

8. (B) Light reaching the plant weakens sharply with distance, and the bubble rate (a measure of photosynthesis) drops in step — strong evidence that light intensity controls the rate of photosynthesis. Only light was varied; water, temperature and the plant were held constant, so those cannot be the cause. Chlorophyll is not destroyed by simply dimming the light; the chlorophyll is intact and works again when the lamp returns. The plant is sitting in water throughout, so it cannot 'run out of water.' The bubbles are oxygen (a product released in light), not carbon dioxide — CO2 is taken in, not bubbled out, during photosynthesis.

9. (A) Xylem (intact) carries water UP to the leaves, so the leaves keep their water and keep photosynthesising. Phloem carries the FOOD made in the leaves DOWN/out; with the ring broken, that food cannot pass the cut and accumulates as a swelling just above the ring. The wilt-and-die option confuses the two tissues — water travels in the untouched xylem, so water supply is fine. 'Grows faster' is baseless; removing phloem hinders, not helps, the plant. The carbon-dioxide option is irrelevant: CO2 enters through stomata on the leaves, which the ringing does not touch.

10. (A) Glucose is soluble; large amounts dissolved in cells would upset the cell's water balance by drawing in water. Converting it to insoluble starch lets the plant stockpile food compactly without that osmotic disturbance — which is why surplus glucose is stored as starch. Starch cannot be made directly from sunlight; glucose is made first in photosynthesis, then converted. Starch is a solid food store, not a gas the plant breathes out — that confuses it with carbon dioxide. Starch is not a pigment; the green pigment is chlorophyll, a different substance entirely.

11. (B) Both discs contained starch; the difference is access. Boiling (water then alcohol) softens the tissue, kills the cells and strips the green, waxy chlorophyll layer so iodine can penetrate and reveal the starch in disc P. The raw disc Q's intact waxy surface blocks iodine, so its real starch goes undetected. 'Boiling created the starch' reverses cause and effect — the question states both discs already had starch. 'Alcohol added starch' is false; alcohol removes chlorophyll, it does not add food. 'Iodine destroys starch in living tissue' is invented chemistry — iodine detects starch by turning blue-black, it does not destroy it.

12. (B) Destarching empties the leaves of pre-existing starch. Then any blue-black result at the end is unambiguously NEW starch made during the experiment, so the comparison between the CO2-free and normal-air leaves fairly tests the effect of carbon dioxide. Without it, leftover starch could give a false positive and ruin the conclusion. Destarching does not 're-green' leaves — colour is unrelated to emptying starch. It does not make CO2 enter more easily; stomata handle CO2 regardless of stored starch. It does not improve iodine 'sticking' — that is the job of the later boiling steps, not destarching.

13. (A) A seed contains a store of food that fuels early growth even without light. That explains the initial pale shoot. But photosynthesis needs light; in the dark, once the seed's food store is exhausted the seedling cannot make more, so it weakens and dies — which the option predicts correctly. Photosynthesis cannot occur in the dark, so 'photosynthesised in the dark' is impossible and would not predict death. The cotton supplies water, not food, so it is not a food source. A seedling is a green-capable autotroph-in-waiting, not a parasite drawing food from cotton — cotton is not a living host and offers no food.

14. (C) Cuscuta dies on a non-living wire but thrives on a living shrub — proof it needs a LIVING host, the mark of a parasite. The mushroom thrives on DEAD wood and harms nothing alive — the mark of a saprotroph that digests dead, decaying matter. Calling Cuscuta a saprotroph contradicts its death on the lifeless wire (it cannot feed on dead matter). Lumping both as parasites ignores that the mushroom needs no living host. The symbiont/autotroph option is doubly wrong: Cuscuta harms its host (not mutual benefit), and the mushroom has no chlorophyll, so it cannot be an autotroph making food in its cap.

15. (C) In a parasitic relationship one partner is harmed; in the lichen, the fungus gains food while the alga gains water, minerals and shelter — a mutually beneficial partnership, i.e. symbiosis, not parasitism. The 'alga harms the fungus' option invents harm where the description states mutual benefit. 'No living organisms, only minerals' is false — both the fungus and the alga are living. The role-swap option is factually wrong: the ALGA (with chlorophyll) makes the food, not the fungus, so it misstates who feeds whom and still misses the key point that both benefit.

16. (A) Only an organism that photosynthesises can pull CO2 out of the air faster than it releases it. The green fern has chlorophyll and in bright light its photosynthesis exceeds its respiration, so CO2 falls in Jar 1. Cuscuta lacks chlorophyll and, with no host, only respires (and starves) — it raises CO2, and it has no host with it here. The mushroom is a saprotroph with no chlorophyll; it cannot make food in light and only respires, raising CO2. The snail is an animal that breathes IN oxygen and breathes OUT carbon dioxide, so it raises CO2, not lowers it.

17. (C) Each uses a different route. The pea cannot use air nitrogen directly; Rhizobium in its nodules fixes atmospheric nitrogen into a usable form. The pitcher plant photosynthesises for energy-food but obtains scarce nitrogen by digesting trapped insects. Cuscuta, lacking chlorophyll, draws all its nutrition — including nitrogen — from a living host. 'All fix nitrogen through their leaves' is false: ordinary plants cannot fix air nitrogen, and Cuscuta is leafless. 'Photosynthesise nitrogen from sunlight' confuses food-making with mineral nutrition — nitrogen is not a product of photosynthesis. 'None needs nitrogen' contradicts the whole point: nitrogen is essential for plant growth.

18. (C) Most plants cannot take up nitrogen gas from the air; usable nitrogen must be supplied through the soil, fixed into a usable form by nitrogen-fixing bacteria (or fertiliser). With poor soil nitrogen, the plant stays pale and stunted despite the air being full of nitrogen gas. The 'different element' option is false — it is the same element nitrogen, just in an unusable gaseous form. The stomata-too-small option invents a size barrier; the real issue is that plants lack the means to use the gas directly, not pore size. 'Tomatoes don't need nitrogen' is false — all plants need nitrogen for healthy green growth.

19. (B) Photosynthesis on a given day needs carbon dioxide, water, light and chlorophyll — NOT soil nitrogen. So as long as plant B's leaf still has some chlorophyll, it can make starch that day and turn blue-black, just like plant A. The long-term paleness and stunting show nitrogen matters for healthy growth and green colour, but the same-day starch test shows it is not a direct raw material of photosynthesis. 'Only A turns blue-black' wrongly treats nitrogen as a photosynthetic raw material. 'Neither' overstates nitrogen's role — a green-ish leaf still photosynthesises. 'Only B, forced to make extra starch' is backwards: nitrogen shortage hampers growth, it does not boost starch-making.

20. (D) An autotroph makes its own food using chlorophyll and light. An organism with NO chlorophyll that takes all its food ready-made from a living host is a parasite, not an autotroph — that directly contradicts the claim. Losing water vapour (transpiration) is normal for almost any leaf and says nothing about how it feeds. Growing faster in more sunlight actually SUPPORTS being an autotroph (more light, more photosynthesis). Releasing oxygen bubbles when lit underwater is a hallmark OF photosynthesis, again supporting the autotroph claim, not disproving it.

21. (D) An autotroph makes its own food. Cuscuta has no chlorophyll and steals ready-made food from a living host — it is a heterotroph (parasite), so listing it as an autotroph is the error. The cactus's green STEMS contain chlorophyll and do photosynthesise (taking over the job of the missing leaves), so (iii) is a true autotroph. Green algae photosynthesise regardless of size, so (ii) is correct — size is irrelevant to being autotrophic. The mango tree makes food in its leaves; it does not 'draw energy from the soil' (that is the soil-as-food misconception), so (i) is a genuine autotroph.

22. (B) Green plants (producers) capture sunlight's energy and store it as food during photosynthesis; every animal in the chain ultimately runs on that captured energy, passed along as one organism eats another. So the original energy source is sunlight. Soil minerals are needed for the plant's health but are not the ENERGY source — the energy comes from light, not minerals. The animals' bodies merely TRANSFER energy that originally came from the plant; they are not the origin. Oxygen is used to release energy from food during respiration, but it is not itself the source of the energy — the food (built from sunlight) is.

23. (A) An 'is eaten by' / energy-flow arrow points from the organism being eaten to the one that eats it, starting at the producer. Energy enters at the green plant and flows up: green plant → grasshopper → frog → snake. The student's chain runs the wrong way. 'Already correct, predator first' inverts the meaning of the arrows. The frog-first jumbled order breaks the actual eating relationships. 'No direction' is false — food chains have a definite direction of energy flow, always beginning with the producer.

24. (B) The algae are the producers feeding the tadpoles directly. Remove the algae and the tadpoles (the next link) immediately lose their food, so their numbers fall first — the most direct consequence. The fish-increase option is wrong: algae and fish do not compete for the same food (the fish eat tadpoles), and losing the base of the chain harms the whole chain, not helps the fish. Tadpoles are animals; they cannot suddenly photosynthesise their own food. The fish are one step further up the chain, so they are affected AFTER the tadpoles decline, not before — the impact travels up from the base.

25. (A) In the light, the terrarium balanced because photosynthesis recycled the CO2 from respiration and respiration recycled the oxygen from photosynthesis. In the dark, photosynthesis stops but respiration continues: the plant keeps taking in oxygen and giving out CO2 with nothing to recycle them, so CO2 rises, oxygen falls, and the plant weakens. 'Nothing changes' assumes the plant halts all activity in the dark — but respiration never stops. 'Oxygen builds up' is impossible: respiration USES oxygen, and there is no photosynthesis to replace it. A green plant does not convert into a saprotroph; it cannot 'live off the soil' for food.

26. (D) In light, the plant's photosynthesis releases oxygen, which feeds the flame and prolongs burning. In the dark there is no photosynthesis, so no oxygen is added; meanwhile the plant respires, consuming oxygen, so it competes with the flame and the flame goes out sooner. 'Burns even longer' wrongly assumes the plant releases oxygen in the dark — photosynthesis needs light. 'Exactly the same' assumes the plant does nothing in the dark, but it respires and uses oxygen. The green-flame answer is nonsense chemistry; chlorophyll does not colour a flame.

27. (B) Two days in the dark stops photosynthesis and lets the leaf use up its stored starch — destarching. The dark disc then tests negative (no starch left), while the lit disc makes fresh starch and tests positive. This is exactly what destarching is for: a clean baseline so any later positive is NEW starch. The dark does not destroy chlorophyll permanently — the leaf makes starch again after 6 hours of light, proving its chlorophyll is intact and working. The dark adds no starch; it removes it, so 'should have tested positive' is wrong. Iodine works fine on any leaf with starch — the dark disc tested negative because it had no starch, not because iodine failed.

28. (A) Stomata are the pores through which carbon dioxide enters the leaf. When they close to conserve water, CO2 supply drops, and since CO2 is a raw material, photosynthesis slows. Sunlight does not enter through the stomata — light passes through the leaf surface regardless of whether the tiny pores are open, so 'sunlight blocked' is wrong. Closing stomata does not destroy chlorophyll; the pigment is unaffected. Root water absorption is not switched off by stomatal closure — closing reduces water LOSS, it does not stop uptake at the roots; in any case the limiting factor for photosynthesis here is the cut-off CO2.

29. (C) Most stomata are on the lower surface; greasing it clogs them, cutting off the CO2 needed for photosynthesis, so leaf 2 makes little starch and barely turns blue-black. A thin coat of grease on the underside does not block the sunlight that reaches the upper surface and chlorophyll. Grease does not dissolve starch — starch is unaffected by an oily film. The iodine-blocking idea is wrong: the boiling steps clean the leaf before testing, and in any case the controlled comparison with the normal leaves points to a real reduction in starch made, not a testing artefact.

30. (D) The shaded leaf loses little water (low transpiration) but still makes starch in light (photosynthesis works) — so the two can vary independently and are distinct processes. The 'required/always together' option is contradicted by the very observation: low transpiration with successful food-making. 'Photosynthesis causes transpiration, so low loss means no food' is disproven because the low-loss leaf DID make starch. They are not the same process under two names — transpiration is water vapour leaving the stomata, photosynthesis is food being made; the data show one can be low while the other proceeds.

31. (A) Transpiration is the loss of water vapour through stomata, which then condenses as droplets in the bag. A living leaf's stomata work, so it transpires and droplets form; a dead leaf's cells no longer regulate or drive this loss, so far fewer droplets appear. The rain idea is excluded — the bag is sealed and identical for both, so any difference is the leaf itself. Xylem carries water inside the plant but does not 'squirt' water out of the surface; loss is as vapour through stomata. The dead-leaf option is backwards: a dead leaf does not MAKE water; it loses less because its transpiration has effectively stopped.

32. (C) Xylem carries water (and dissolved minerals) UP from the roots; phloem carries the FOOD made in the leaves outward, mostly downward, to the rest of the plant. So the two tissues carry different substances in different directions — the student is doubly wrong. 'Nothing wrong, both carry water up' repeats the error. 'Both carry food' is wrong because xylem carries water, not food. The role-swap option ('xylem food up, phloem water down') reverses both tissues' jobs. Only the correct option keeps xylem = water/up and phloem = food/out.

33. (C) The correct word equation is: carbon dioxide + water → (sunlight, chlorophyll) → glucose + oxygen. The teacher's version wrongly listed oxygen as a raw material on the left and water as a product on the right. Swapping them — water in as a raw material, oxygen out as a product — fixes it. Chlorophyll (not soil minerals) is the required pigment condition, so that swap is wrong. The immediate food made is glucose, not starch (starch is the later storage form), so that swap is wrong. Photosynthesis needs sunlight, not darkness, so replacing sunlight with darkness is wrong.

Glucose is indeed the food made, with surplus stored as starch — correct. Carbon dioxide and water are the two raw materials used up — correct. Only the oxygen-as-raw-material claim reverses a product for a reactant.

35. (B) Photosynthesis requires light as its energy source. With CO2, water and chlorophyll present but NO light, no food is made, so the destarched leaf stays starch-free and does not turn blue-black. Chlorophyll captures light but cannot manufacture starch in the dark on its own — light is essential. Warmth is not the energy source for photosynthesis; it cannot substitute for light. Darkness does not alter iodine chemistry — iodine still turns blue-black with starch and shows nothing without it; here there simply is no starch.

36. (D) Light is the energy source for photosynthesis. As light falls, the plant makes less and less food despite ample CO2, water and warmth, so it slows and weakens — light is the limiting factor. Plentiful carbon dioxide does not poison a plant; CO2 is a raw material it uses. Keeping CO2 topped up does not consume chlorophyll — the two are unrelated. A green plant does not transform into a saprotroph feeding on chamber walls; that is not a real mode by which a green plant lives.

37. (B) The same-day starch test is positive for both pots, showing the day's photosynthesis (needing CO2, water, light, chlorophyll) does not directly require soil nitrogen. 'Directly for photosynthesis' is contradicted by pot 2's positive same-day test. 'Nothing at all' ignores the obvious harm to pot 2's growth and colour. Leaves do not 'make their own carbon dioxide' from nitrogen — CO2 enters from the air through stomata.

38. (A) Rhizobium fixes atmospheric nitrogen into a form the plant can use, so the plant GAINS usable nitrogen. In return the plant, a food-maker, GIVES the bacteria food (sugars) and a home (shelter) in its nodules. The first wrong option reverses the exchange — it is the BACTERIA that supply nitrogen and the plant that supplies food, not the other way round. 53. (C) Green plants are producers: they capture sunlight as food that flows up every food chain to all animals (even meat-eaters depend on plant-eaters), and they release the oxygen animals need to release that energy by respiration. That is the deep, universal dependence. Not every animal eats plants directly — carnivores eat other animals — so 'eaten by all of them' is false; the dependence is via the chain. Plants make most oxygen in the LIGHT (day) when they photosynthesise, not at night. Plants do not pass soil minerals straight to animals; the key dependence is on plant-made food and plant-released oxygen.

54. (C) Light is the energy source for the algae's photosynthesis. Persistent shade starves them of light, so they make little food and decline (producers fail first). The snails, which depend on the algae for food, then run short and decline too — the impact passes up the chain from the base. 'Snails thrive on softer algae' invents a benefit; less light means less algae, not better food. Algae cannot 'feed on silt' — they are autotrophs that need light, not particles, to make food. 'Algae don't need light' is flatly false; without light photosynthesis stops.

55. (D) Autotrophs make their own food, but they cannot do so from nothing: they need carbon dioxide, water and light (plus chlorophyll) from their surroundings as raw materials and energy. So 'need nothing at all' is wrong. 'Completely independent' repeats the error. Autotrophs (green plants) do not eat small animals to 'get started' — they photosynthesise. They do not take ready-made food from the soil; that is the food-from-soil misconception. The accurate correction is that they still depend on CO2, water and light.

56. (B) Carbon dioxide is a raw material of photosynthesis. The KOH removes it from one leaf's air, so that leaf cannot make starch and stays brown; only the normal-air leaf makes starch and turns blue-black. The student's error was treating CO2 as something whose removal HELPS, when in fact CO2 is essential and its removal stops food-making. 'Both turn blue-black, light alone is enough' ignores that CO2 is needed too. 'Neither, destarching destroyed the ability' misunderstands destarching, which only empties stored starch and does not disable photosynthesis. 'KOH feeds the leaf' inverts KOH's role — it ABSORBS CO2, it does not provide it.

57. (B) Zero NET oxygen exchange means the oxygen released by photosynthesis exactly matches the oxygen consumed by respiration at that instant — the compensation point, occurring around dawn and dusk as light changes. Both processes are running; their rates are equal. 'Stopped both processes' is wrong — respiration never stops, and at these moments photosynthesis is occurring too. 'Only respiring, using its own oxygen' describes night, not a zero-crossing where photosynthesis is balancing respiration. 'Photosynthesis stopped, respiration full speed' would give a net oxygen UPTAKE, not zero — that is night-time, not the balance point.

58. (D) The chapter's full picture is: green plants are autotrophs making food by photosynthesis; some organisms are heterotrophs feeding in special modes (parasitic like Cuscuta, insectivorous like the pitcher plant, saprotrophic like mushrooms, symbiotic like lichens and Rhizobium); and soil nitrogen is restored by nitrogen-fixing bacteria. Only the first option captures all of this. 'Food only at night' is false — photosynthesis needs light, so it occurs in the day. 'Plants and animals both make food from sunlight' is false — animals cannot photosynthesise. 'Food ready-made from the soil' is the central misconception the chapter overturns.

59. (A) A plant's body is largely made of food (glucose/starch and related substances) whose carbon comes from carbon dioxide fixed in the leaves during photosynthesis — so the great bulk of new solid mass is built from a gas in the air, not solid soil. Roots take up water and dissolved minerals, not whole solid soil that hardens into wood, so the first wrong option misdescribes how roots work. Oxygen is a PRODUCT released by photosynthesis, not a raw material the tree turns into solid wood. The girl's whole-soil idea is the food-from-soil misconception the chapter overturns: the trunk's mass traces mostly to carbon dioxide, with water supplying hydrogen.

60. (A) Water (carrying the dye) moves UP from the roots through the xylem, which is why the dye appears high in the leaves. Food made in the lit leaf (the labelled sugar) travels AWAY from the leaf — here downward toward the roots — through the phloem. So it is xylem for the dye, phloem for the sugar. The first wrong option swaps the two tissues' jobs. 'Xylem for both' is false — the plant has two distinct transport tissues, water in xylem and food in phloem. Stomata are leaf pores for gas exchange and chlorophyll is a pigment, not transport channels, so that option misnames the structures entirely.
