Worked Solutions

1. (C) Sewage is liquid waste water that carries suspended and dissolved impurities together; rainwater off a clean roof is not waste water, a salt-free clear liquid is not sewage, and a dried solid is the opposite of a liquid waste.

2. (A) The chapter lists homes, industry, farms and storm water as combined sources feeding the sewer, so the merger shows multiple sources; restricting it to toilets, claiming the plant makes it, or naming only storm water all contradict that list.

3. (B) Substances that pass the filter yet leave a solid on drying are dissolved inorganic salts like nitrates and phosphates; scum and grit would be caught by the filter, and a microbe would be destroyed by boiling and is not a dried salt residue.

4. (D) Among sewage contaminants only the pathogenic bacteria are alive and able to reproduce and infect; dissolved phosphate, sand and grease are all non-living impurities.

5. (D) Organic matter comes from once-living material such as food, faeces and cooking oil, while a dissolved metal salt is an inorganic industrial impurity and is therefore the odd one out.

6. (A) Nitrates and phosphates act as plant nutrients, so excess of them spurs overgrowth and disturbs the water body; they do not purify the water, become sand, or turn into biogas in a pond.

7. (C) Covering the sewer prevents contact between germ-carrying sewage and the water and surroundings people rely on, which is the sanitation-disease link; sunlight disinfection, open-air aeration and in-pipe biogas are not why sewers are covered.

8. (C) Dirty water leaves the home into the sewer, is then carried to the plant for cleaning, and only the treated outflow goes to a river or recharges groundwater; any order placing the plant or river before the sewer reverses the real path.

9. (B) Bar screens trap only large floating objects such as bags, rags and sticks; dissolved salts, microbes, fine grit, scum, sludge, gas and oil all slip through and are dealt with by later stages.

10. (A) Without screening, large debris jams and wears out the immediate downstream pumps and pipes; it cannot raise dissolved nitrates, cannot purify water, and would hinder rather than boost biogas.

11. (A) Bar screens catch only big objects, so grit, dissolved matter and germs remain after them; the screen neither removes everything nor introduces impurities.

12. (C) Reducing speed in the grit chamber lets dense mineral particles settle by gravity; dissolved salts do not settle there, oil-skimming belongs to the later settling tank, and bacterial feeding belongs to aeration.

13. (D) Removing hard, abrasive grit early protects downstream machinery and keeps it out of the organic sludge stream; grit is not specially poisonous, is not feedstock for biogas, and the settling tank does hold settled sludge.

14. (C) Sand and grit are captured in the dedicated grit chamber, so a sand surge stresses that unit; the digester, aeration tank and bar screen handle organic sludge, dissolved organics and large objects respectively.

15. (B) The bottom scraper handles the heavy sludge that has settled, moving it for removal; skimming scum is the surface arm's job, while air-bubbling and bag-catching belong to the aeration tank and bar screen.

16. (D) Heavy solids sinking to the floor are sludge while light grease floating up is scum; reversing them, naming gas or grit, or calling both manure are all incorrect.

17. (D) Fine suspended solids that pass grit removal settle in the sedimentation tank as sludge; the bar screen only catches large objects, the digester acts on already-settled sludge, and aeration targets dissolved organic matter rather than sieving solids.

18. (C) A jammed floor scraper leaves the settled sludge accumulating with no path to the digester; aeration air, bar-screen action and digester gas are governed by other equipment entirely.

19. (B) Aeration feeds oxygen to aerobic bacteria that eat the leftover organic load; anaerobic bacteria avoid oxygen, cooling-for-settling is not the aim, and large-object removal happened at the bar screen.

20. (B) The bacterial mass from aeration is activated sludge, a portion of which is recycled to the tank to sustain the cleaning bacteria; it is neither mineral grit, floating scum, nor raw incoming sewage.

21. (D) Aerobic bacteria need the supplied oxygen, so cutting the air weakens them and leaves organic waste poorly treated; they do not benefit from losing oxygen, the tank is not a biogas unit, and dissolved organics do not settle by gravity.

22. (C) Vigorous air bubbling is the signature of the aeration tank; quiet sludge-and-scum layering marks the settling tank, bar-catching marks the screen, and a gas-collecting dome marks the digester.

23. (B) Sealing out air lets anaerobic bacteria decompose the sludge and give off biogas; aerobic bacteria need air, a sealed tank admits no sunlight, and plastics were already removed at the screen.

24. (A) Anaerobic digestion of sludge yields biogas, and the leftover digested sludge dried out becomes manure; aeration makes neither gas, screens and grit chambers make no biogas, and scum is not biogas.

25. (A) Biogas-making bacteria are anaerobic, mirroring the sealed sludge digester; aerobic conditions, sunlight-dependence and freezing are not how biogas bacteria operate.

26. (D) The two are reversed: the digester is air-free and uses anaerobic bacteria while the aeration tank is supplied air for aerobic bacteria; the statement is not correct, not half-right, and the two tanks do not share one bacterial type.

27. (A) Chlorination kills surviving disease microbes before discharge; it does not enrich the water with nutrients, settle grit, or make biogas.

28. (C) Treatment strips out most organic matter and solids, so the outlet is much cleaner than the inlet; it is not dirtier, not unchanged, and still not certified drinkable.

29. (C) Treated effluent is cleaner yet not certified germ-free or potable, so it should not be drunk directly; the plant does not re-pollute the water, treated water is not dirtier than raw, and rivers do not self-purify reliably.

30. (C) Large debris goes first at the screen, then grit in its chamber, then sludge and scum in the settling tank, and finally dissolved organics are consumed in aeration; any order that puts grit before debris or aeration before settling is wrong.

31. (D) The mixed inputs show multiple sources, and a hospital's waste is rich in pathogens; sewage is not solely domestic, not made at the plant, and not mere storm water, and hospitals do not chiefly add clean rain, grit, or oxygen.

32. (A) Sludge is routed to the anaerobic digester and the clarified water to the aerobic aeration tank, treated separately; swapping their destinations or merging both streams into one tank misrepresents the process.
