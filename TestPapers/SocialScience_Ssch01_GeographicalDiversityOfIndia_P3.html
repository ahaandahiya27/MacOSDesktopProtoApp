Worked Solutions

1. (B) The middle (Himachal/Lesser Himalaya) belt holds the moderate-climate hill stations and forests; below it sit the geologically youngest, lowest Shivalik foothills built of river gravel, just above the plains. So descending you cross the middle ranges first and the foothills second. Putting the foothills first reverses the descent. The Himadri is the highest, snow-covered range and lies above, not below, the middle belt, so it cannot appear when descending toward the plains.

2. (D) Air grows colder and thinner with height, so the higher Himadri keeps permanent snow and the giant peaks, while the lower middle range is mild enough for hill stations. Saying they are the same height ignores that altitude is exactly what differs. Calling the hill-town range higher contradicts the fact that the snow range tops it. The snow range is not the lower one; its great height is the cause of the year-round snow.

3. (D) The continuing collision keeps squeezing and uplifting the young Himalayas, so they keep rising. The Aravallis are ancient and being worn down, not raised, by the collision. The Northern Plains are a sinking trough being filled with silt, not lifted. The Deccan is a stable old block; the chapter never describes it sliding into the sea.

4. (A) The Himalayas rose when the floor of the old Tethys Sea was crushed and folded upward during the plate collision, lifting marine fossils to great heights. The Arabian Sea is a present-day ocean to the southwest, not a sea that climbed the peaks. Rivers flow downhill and could not carry shells up to the summits. Marine fossils are remains of sea creatures, so they record a former sea, not in-place rock formation.

5. (C) Frozen winter precipitation is locked as snow and glacier ice, then melts in the warm season, feeding rivers like the Ganga so they flow year-round even before the monsoon. The peaks do not deliver winter rain to the plains; the dry-season flow is meltwater, not rain. There is no underground pumping of seawater. The store is ice, not water squeezed from rock.

6. (D) India's outer barriers are the Himalayas to the north, the Thar plus the Arabian Sea to the west, and the surrounding ocean and seas to the south. The Aravallis are an interior range that slows the desert sand but does not form a national outer wall. The other three options are exactly the recognised outer barriers, so they belong on the list.

7. (C) Himalayan rivers blanket the flat plains with deep, fertile, renewable alluvium that is easy to till, supporting intensive farming and dense population. The Deccan's thin soil over hard rock is what limits it, so attributing thin-rock soil to the plains is backwards. Coastal salty soil and seasonally frozen soil describe other regions, not the alluvial Northern Plains.

8. (C) The plains lie in a subsiding trough in front of the rising mountains; rivers have poured eroded Himalayan material into it for ages, so soft sediment runs very deep. Volcanic lava built the Deccan, not the plains. There is no buried ice canyon described. The plains were not formed by lifted seabed mud; their fill is river-borne mountain debris.

9. (D) The newer floodplain silt (khadar) is renewed by each flood with fresh nutrients, so it is more fertile than the older, higher terrace (bhangar). Flooding deposits nutrients rather than only stripping them. The two alluviums are not identical in fertility, which is the whole point of the khadar/bhangar contrast. Fresh river silt is fine and rich, not barren sand and stone.

10. (C) Level, fertile, river-fed plains favour large settlements, intensive farming and easy movement, while broken hilly terrain hinders them, so people concentrate on the plains. The foothills are not uninhabitable from heat. Rainfall is not unique to the plains. Rivers descend through the foothills, so a total lack of rivers is false; the deciding factor is relief and soil, not absence of water.

11. (B) First the collision lifts the mountains; rivers then erode that high rock, carry the silt downstream, and finally deposit it as fertile alluvium on the level plains. The other sequences scramble cause and effect: silt cannot settle before mountains and rivers exist, silt is the product of erosion rather than its cause, and the mountains have not eroded away nor has a sea flooded and lifted the plains.

12. (B) The Aravallis are one of the world's oldest ranges; immense time and erosion have worn them low. Youth would, if anything, mean active uplift like the Himalayas, so 'young' is the opposite of the truth. They are old folded rock, not a heap of soft sand. They have been eroded down, not sunk back below the ground.

13. (C) Rivers run downhill, so their consistent eastward course shows the plateau tilts down to the east. Water cannot be pulled toward a higher sea; the sea is the low end. The western rim (Western Ghats) is the high edge, not the low one, so saying the west is lower is wrong. A consistent direction across many rivers reflects the slope, not coincidence.

14. (B) The plateau's old, hard, stable crust has concentrated minerals and coal over enormous spans of time, making it mineral-rich. Himalayan rivers carry silt, not fresh mineral ores, onto the plains, not onto the high plateau. Tides act on coasts, not on the interior plateau surface. There are no plateau-top glaciers grinding minerals onto the Deccan; its wealth is inherent to its ancient rock.

15. (A) The west-coast wall is the high, nearly continuous Western Ghats, and the east-coast hills are the lower Eastern Ghats, broken where east-flowing rivers cut through. Swapping their heights and locations is the classic Ghats trap. The Himadri and Shivaliks are Himalayan ranges in the north, not coastal walls, so that pairing is out of place geographically.

16. (B) The plateau is a single tilted block: its raised western edge forms the high unbroken Western Ghats, while the rivers running off the tilt toward the east have carved gaps in the lower Eastern Ghats. Both rims are part of the same ancient block, so 'young and still rising' is wrong. The sea did not flatten the eastern rim; rivers cut it. The difference is the tilt and river erosion, not different minerals.

17. (C) The plateau's forests are home to tribal communities such as the Gond and Bhil. Ladakh's herders live in the northern cold desert, not the plateau. Coral-island fishers belong to the Arabian Sea islands, and mangrove dwellers to the eastern delta, both of which are different regions entirely.

18. (B) Long, slow eastward rivers gather and deposit large sediment loads into the calmer Bay of Bengal, building wide deltas; the short, steep west-flowing rivers reach the rough open Arabian Sea quickly with little sediment, so deltas are scarce there. The west coast does get heavy rain. The east coast clearly has major rivers, not only lakes. Sea saltiness is not what determines delta formation; sediment supply and a calm sea do.

19. (A) The branching fan of fertile new land is a delta; the single funnel where fresh and salt water mingle without building much land is an estuary, so the order is delta then estuary. Reversing them mislabels each landform. A lagoon is a shallow coastal water body cut off by a bar, not the branching delta described, so that pairing is wrong too.

20. (B) Chilika and Pulicat are large coastal lakes and lagoons along the east coast, formed where bars and spits partly enclose seawater. They are not coral atolls, which belong to the Arabian Sea island group. They are not high Himalayan glacial lakes, nor are they volcanic crater lakes; their setting is the sea-fringed eastern coast.

21. (A) The Sundarbans mangroves and the Royal Bengal Tiger lie in the Ganga–Brahmaputra delta, which India shares with Bangladesh. Myanmar lies to the east and does not share this delta. The Indus delta is in the northwest with Pakistan but is not the tiger's mangrove home. The Kaveri delta is in the far south and is not shared with Sri Lanka in this way.

22. (A) Short, fast west-flowing rivers reaching the open, rough Arabian Sea deposit little sediment, producing creeks, coves and estuaries rather than broad deltas. Their rivers are short and swift, not the longest and slowest. Rivers do reach the western sea. Sea temperature is not the controlling factor; river length, speed and the open rough sea are.

23. (D) A heavy sediment load delivered slowly into a calm sea settles and builds up, forming a wide delta. An estuary forms instead when a river is short and swift or the sea is rough enough to sweep sediment away. Coral reefs grow in warm clear shallow seas, not at silt-choked river mouths. Sand dunes are wind-built desert features, not river-mouth landforms.

24. (B) When humid monsoon air is pushed up against the hills it cools and releases enormous rainfall (orographic rain), creating the lush valleys and root bridges. The hills are uplands, not the lowest land collecting drainage. Rivers do not evaporate uphill to make rain. Sea spray does not produce record rainfall; rising, cooling moist air does.

25. (A) The low, flat coral islands are Lakshadweep, lying in the Arabian Sea off the southwest coast. The Andaman & Nicobar group is the higher volcanic one in the Bay of Bengal, so naming it as the coral group is wrong. Lakshadweep is not in the Bay of Bengal, and the Andamans are not in the Arabian Sea; the location pairing must match the formation.

26. (A) The contrast follows from origin: coral reefs build the low Lakshadweep in warm shallow seas, while volcanic activity raises the higher Andaman & Nicobar Islands. Age alone does not turn coral into volcanic rock. Both groups receive rain, so rainfall is not the cause. Slight sea-temperature differences do not explain the rock type; the formation process does.

27. (D) Barren Island, India's only active volcano, lies in the Andaman & Nicobar group, confirming its volcanic origin at a plate boundary. The coral Lakshadweep islands have no volcano. The Sundarbans are a mangrove delta, not an island volcano group, and there are no glacial island chains in Himalayan lakes; the volcano marks the Andamans.

28. (D) Coral thrives in warm, clear, shallow water, and Lakshadweep is exactly such a coral island group in the Arabian Sea. The Andamans are volcanic, not coral, so they do not fit. The Sundarbans are muddy mangrove delta, where coral does not build islands, and the Aravallis are an inland hill range, not islands; only Lakshadweep matches the coral conditions.

29. (A) Lakshadweep lies in the Arabian Sea off the western (Kerala) side, and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands lie in the Bay of Bengal to the east. Swapping the two seas misplaces both groups. The two groups are not in the same sea; they sit on opposite sides of the peninsula, one west and one east.

30. (A) Snow- and glacier-fed Himalayan rivers like the Ganga receive meltwater even in the rainless dry season, so they stay full, whereas rain-fed peninsular rivers fall low without rain. The Ganga is long, not short. The Aravallis do not dam the Deccan rivers. Rivers flow to the sea and do not draw water inland from it; the difference is the source of supply.

31. (C) Perennial rivers, fed by melting snow and glaciers, flow throughout the year, while rain-fed rivers depend on the monsoon and dwindle in the dry season. Reversing those flow patterns is wrong. Perennial rivers are not desert features. And the two types clearly differ by season, so claiming identical year-round flow contradicts the definition.

32. (A) The Narmada and Tapti are notable for flowing west into the Arabian Sea, opposite to the usual eastward drainage of the tilted plateau. They do not turn north to the Ganga. Rivers cannot climb uphill into the Ghats. Both reach the sea, so claiming they end inland is false; their distinction is the westward course.

33. (A) The Himalayan river keeps flowing because melting snow and glaciers feed it continuously through the warm months, independent of rain. Lakes storing monsoon rain are not the chapter's explanation for sustained Himalayan flow. A shorter course is not why it stays full, and the Himalayan rivers are in fact long. Tidal seawater does not push upstream to sustain a river; meltwater does.

34. (B) The plateau's thin soil over hard ancient rock and its seasonally varying rain-fed rivers limit intensive farming, in contrast with the plains' deep fertile alluvium and snow-fed water. The Deccan does have rivers and soil, just less favourable ones. It is neither frozen year-round nor below sea level; it is a raised, warm plateau.

35. (D) Deserts are defined by very low rainfall, not by heat, so high, cold, dry Ladakh qualifies as a cold desert. Ladakh is dry, not heavily rained on. Sand dunes are common in hot sandy deserts but are not required for a region to be a desert. Elevation relative to sea level is irrelevant to the definition; rainfall is the key.

36. (C) Ladakh, the cold desert, has high salt lakes, severe winters and high-altitude animals such as the snow leopard. Tall golden dunes, heat-coping water tanks and scorching frost-free sand all describe the hot Thar Desert, not Ladakh. Both are deserts by low rainfall, but their temperature and features differ, so the cold-desert traits are the lakes, cold and snow leopard.

37. (D) The Aravallis form a long ridge on the desert's eastern flank, acting as a barrier to wind-driven sand moving toward cities like Delhi. They are low and worn, not tall and snow-capped. They are folded rock, not loose absorbing sand. And they lie along the eastern edge, not far to the south; their position and ridge form are what block the sand.

38. (B) The Aravalli ridge blocks eastward-moving desert sand, so removing it would let the Thar's sand spread east toward Delhi. The Himalayas rise from plate collision, unaffected by the Aravallis. The Ganga's course is set by the plains' slope, not by these hills. The western coastline is a sea boundary far away and unrelated to the Aravallis.

39. (A) In the dry Thar, water is precious, so people clean with abundant sand and harvest scarce rain in tanks. The practice is born of scarcity, not a belief that sand outcleans water. Cold nights are not the reason in the hot Thar. No rule bans river water; the point is that very little water is available, so it is conserved.

40. (C) Mount Abu is the highest point of the old, low Aravalli range. The Himadri carries giant snow peaks far higher than Mount Abu, so it is not that range's high point. The western coastal wall (Western Ghats) is a separate southern feature, and the Shivaliks are northern foothills; Mount Abu belongs to the Aravallis.

41. (B) A wide latitudinal spread means the southern, lower-latitude parts are warmer and more tropical, while the northern, higher-latitude parts are cooler, giving India varied climate. India does not sit wholly in a cold zone. Being one country does not make temperatures uniform; latitude varies them. India lies entirely north of the Equator, not split across hemispheres.

42. (B) The Tropic of Cancer crossing the middle places the southern half in the tropical zone, contributing to its warmth, while the north is subtropical. India is north of the Equator, not south of it. It is not in any polar zone. The Tropic is a latitude line, not a boundary between the Himalayas and the plains, so that reading is mistaken.

43. (B) A broad longitudinal extent means the eastern parts greet sunrise earlier than the western parts, since the Earth turns west to east. India is large, not small, so sunrise is not simultaneous everywhere. It does not occupy a single narrow longitude. The Earth rotates as one body; the east does not spin faster, the difference comes from longitude.

44. (D) The Tropic of Cancer is the northern limit of the belt where the Sun can be directly overhead. Land south of it lies within the tropics and stays warm all year, while land to its north is subtropical with sharper seasons. Because the line crosses the middle of India, it neatly marks this climatic divide. India is not wholly temperate (its south is tropical); points on the Tropic do receive overhead Sun around the June solstice; and the Tropic is a line of latitude, not a relief boundary between the plains and the mountains.

45. (C) A large latitudinal spread gives varied climates from tropical south to cooler north, and a large longitudinal spread gives differing local sunrise times, so the two extents explain India's climatic variety and time differences. They do not produce one identical climate or remove seasons. The rising Himalayas come from plate collision, unrelated to the country's latitude-longitude extent.

46. (C) The Himalayas block frigid Central Asian winds and trap the rain-bearing monsoon; removing them would let in cold dry winds and reduce rainfall in the north. North India would not become a coral lagoon, which is a tropical sea feature. It would be drier and colder in winter, but the chapter does not predict a heavy-snow polar desert. Mountains clearly shape climate, so 'nothing changes' is wrong.

47. (B) In both places, humid monsoon air is pushed up the slopes, cools, and releases heavy rain, the orographic effect. Neither owes its rain to a neighbouring desert. Neither is built of coral, and rock type does not attract rain. Both are uplands, not below-sea-level basins; rising, cooling moist air is the shared mechanism.

48. (C) As moist winds climb the windward slope they cool and rain heavily there; having lost most moisture, the air gives little rain to the sheltered side, creating a drier zone behind the crest. The dry interior is a consequence of the Ghats, not an unrelated desert. The slope does not freeze the back side by blocking sunlight. Rivers carry water away after it falls, not the rain in the air.

49. (A) Flat, fertile, well-watered plains support large-scale intensive cropping, whereas steep, cold, thin-soiled mountains permit only small terraced plots and herding. Shared rivers do not make their farming identical; relief and climate differ greatly. Cooler mountains do not farm more intensively than the plains. Both regions do support some farming, so 'neither can' is false.

50. (C) Abundant water and fertile alluvial soil let the plains feed dense populations, while the dry Thar's scarce water and poor soil support far fewer people. The Thar is hot, not too cold to inhabit. The plains are not the only flat land; the Deccan and coasts are also flat in places. The desert is sunny, not sunless; water and soil, not sunlight, are the limiting factors.

51. (B) The barriers slowed but did not fully prevent contact, since traders and invaders found passes and sea routes through them. They certainly existed, so denying them is wrong. They restricted people, not just rivers. And they are natural features such as mountains and seas, not human-made walls; their effect was to hinder, not to seal completely.

52. (D) Together the Himalayas, the Thar plus the Arabian Sea, and the encircling oceans limited easy entry, helping India develop a distinct climate and culture. Random scattered hills do not form such a frame. The east-coast deltas alone are far too small a feature to shelter a subcontinent, and the Lakshadweep reefs are tiny and remote; it is the great outer barriers acting together that matter.

53. (A) Ladakh is a high cold desert, the Thar a hot sandy desert, and the Sundarbans a mangrove forest in a river delta, so each is a different type. They are not all hot deserts, since Ladakh is cold and the Sundarbans is a wet delta. They are not all deltas, since the two deserts are dry. Calling the deserts deltas and the delta a desert reverses the facts.

54. (D) The Himalayas are young, actively rising fold mountains that reach great heights, whereas the Aravallis are among the oldest ranges and have been eroded low over vast time. The Aravallis are above the surface, not submerged. The Himalayas are towering mountains, not flat plateaus. The Aravallis were once high folded rock, not soft material that was never tall; age and erosion explain the difference.

55. (C) Rivers flow from the mountains through the plains to the coasts, moving people, trade and ideas across regions and binding them together. They do not give all regions identical weather; climate still varies. They do not connect the mountains to the distant islands. They water and link the land but do not flatten the whole country into one plain; their uniting role is as routes of connection.

56. (D) 'Unity in diversity' expresses that India's many different landscapes, climates and peoples together make one country. 'Survival of the fittest' is about competition, not unity. 'Divide and rule' is a strategy of splitting people, the opposite of unity. 'Out of sight, out of mind' is about forgetting, unrelated to the chapter's theme.

57. (A) Dividing India by relief, rock and water makes it possible to compare areas and explain why climate, farming and ways of life vary across them. The regions are not all alike; that is precisely why they are distinguished. The physical regions follow nature, not royal boundaries. Language regions are a different matter and do not define physical geography.

58. (D) Mountains, plains, desert, plateau, coasts and islands each shape distinct climates, wildlife and lifestyles, while rivers and shared history tie them into one nation, which is the chapter's full message. The land is not the same everywhere, so lives differ. It is not only the Himalayas that matter; every region does. Geography clearly influences where and how people live.

59. (D) India's varied regions, from cold mountains to hot deserts, wet deltas and warm reefs, offer many different habitats, supporting a wide variety of life. The country does not have a single uniform warm climate; its climates differ greatly. Its rich native wildlife is not all imported. Far from one shared habitat, it has many, which is exactly why biodiversity is high.

60. (C) Each animal lives where the region's relief and climate suit it, the snow leopard in cold mountains, the tiger in warm mangroves, coral fish in warm seas, showing geography shapes wildlife. The species are clearly specialised, so they cannot all live anywhere equally. Wildlife depends strongly on geography, and the regions do not share the same animals; their habitats differ.
