1. A trader records four deals from one market day. Deal 1: a sack of millet handed straight across for a roll of jute, no figure named. Deal 2: a bullock given and two silver coins received back as 'change' to even the swap. Deal 3: a pot of curd swapped for a bundle of reeds, then the curd-giver hands over three cowrie shells to make the trade fair. Deal 4: a length of rope traded for a basket of dates, both judged equal by eye. Counting strictly, how many of the four are genuine barter, and on what principle?
   (A) Three are barter (Deals 1, 3 and 4), because cowrie shells are an old natural object and so do not count as money even when used to settle a balance.
   (B) One is barter (Deal 1 only), because any deal in which the two goods are not weighed against each other on a scale cannot count as a true swap.
   (C) All four are barter, because in every case a physical good was eventually handed over by each side.
   (D) Two are barter (Deals 1 and 4), because only there did goods meet goods with nothing acting as a means of payment; in Deals 2 and 3 the coins and the cowries served as money to settle the balance.

2. A teacher says, 'Identify the EARLIEST point at which a swap stops being barter.' A pure goods-for-goods exchange of wheat for cloth then passes through these stages in order: (i) the traders shake hands; (ii) they agree to meet weekly; (iii) one adds a handful of salt that both treat as a means of payment to balance the deal; (iv) the cloth is later resold. The swap first ceases to be barter at stage:
