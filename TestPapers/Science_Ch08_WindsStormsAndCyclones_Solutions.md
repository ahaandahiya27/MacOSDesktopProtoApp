## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** Air is matter: it has mass (weight) and, like any fluid, it presses equally in every direction — up, down and sideways. It does not press only downward or only at sea level.

**2. (C)** Wind is simply moving air, and air always flows from where the pressure is higher to where it is lower. Heating by itself is not wind; the *movement* it sets up is.

**3. (C)** Boiling drives out most air and fills the can with steam; capping it traps low-density hot gas. On cooling, the steam condenses and the trapped gas contracts, so the inside pressure drops well below the outside air pressure, which then crushes the can. (The walls are pushed in by air, not by cold water or shrinking metal.)

**4. (B)** Land heats faster than water under the same Sun. The hot air over the land expands, becomes lighter and rises, lowering the pressure there; cooler, higher-pressure air from over the sea flows in to replace it — a sea breeze.

**5. (D)** The fast air stream you blow between the balloons has lower pressure than the still air on their outer sides. The higher outside pressure pushes the balloons together, so they move toward each other. (The common guess "move apart" is the trap.)

**6. (C)** A thunderstorm's energy comes from warm, moisture-laden air shooting upward while water drops fall back down through it. Lightning is a *result* of this churning, not its cause; cold sinking air would suppress a storm.

**7. (A)** The equator receives far more direct sunlight than the poles. This temperature difference creates pressure differences, which drive large-scale wind currents that carry heat from hot regions toward cold ones.

**8. (C)** When water vapour condenses it releases its stored (latent) heat. That extra warmth makes the rising air even lighter, drops the central pressure further, and pulls in faster wind carrying still more moist air — a self-strengthening loop.

**9. (B)** Fast wind streaming over the curved roof has lower pressure than the still air trapped inside the house. The greater pressure below then pushes the roof upward, so a loosely tied roof can be lifted off.

**10. (A)** The calm, clear, low-pressure centre of a cyclone is the eye. The violent ring around it is the eye wall.

**11. (B)** Air pressure comes from the weight of the air column above you. On a mountain there is less air overhead, so the outside pressure is lower than at the seashore.

**12. (C)** Sun-heated air over a hot field expands, grows less dense (lighter) and is pushed up by the cooler, denser air around it. Hot air rises; it does not sink or sit still.

**13. (A)** The deadliest part of a landfalling cyclone is usually the storm surge — a great heap of sea water driven onto the low coast, drowning huge areas. Lightning, thunder and hail cause far less of the destruction.

**14. (C)** Warm water heats the air inside the bottle; the air expands and, having nowhere else to go, pushes out into the balloon and inflates it. No water enters and the balloon makes no air of its own.

**15. (C)** At night the land loses heat faster than the sea, so now the sea is the warmer surface. Air over the sea rises, lowering pressure there, and cooler air flows out from the land toward the sea — a land breeze.

**16. (B)** The right response to a cyclone alert is to move to a safe place such as a cyclone shelter and follow the authorities' instructions. Going to the beach or toward the sea, or ignoring the warning, is dangerous.

**17. (B)** Light and sound are made together in a lightning stroke, but light travels enormously faster than sound, so the flash reaches our eyes well before the thunder reaches our ears.

**18. (A)** In summer the land heats more than the sea, drawing moist air inland from the sea toward the land; that sea-to-land flow carries the rain of the summer monsoon.

**19. (C)** Blowing across the top of the strip makes the air there move fast, so its pressure drops below the still air underneath. The higher pressure below then lifts the free end up.

**20. (D)** A cyclone is fed by warm, moist air from a warm sea. Once it moves over land it loses that supply of warm moist air, so it weakens. (Buildings and "cold land" are not the main reason.)

**21. (A)** The eye wall is the narrow ring just around the eye, where the winds are fastest and the rain heaviest — the most violent part of the cyclone.

**22. (A)** The fluids and air inside our body push outward with about the same pressure as the atmosphere pushes inward, so the forces balance and we are not crushed. Air pressure is real and strong; gravity does not cancel it.

**23. (C)** Wind speed is measured with an anemometer. (A thermometer measures temperature, a barometer pressure, a rain gauge rainfall.)

**24. (D)** When warm air rises away from a hot region it leaves lower pressure behind. Surrounding higher-pressure air then rushes in to fill that low-pressure region. Cooler air is denser, not lighter, so options B and C are wrong.

**25. (A)** Flood water during a cyclone is usually contaminated with sewage and debris and can spread disease, so people are told not to drink from open flooded sources.

**26. (B)** Wind flows from high pressure to low pressure. Here the sea has the higher pressure and the land the lower, so the daytime breeze blows from the sea to the land.

**27. (C)** Cooling the trapped air shrinks it and lowers its pressure below the outside air pressure, which then presses the bottle's sides inward, denting it. (The fridge does not squeeze it mechanically.)

**28. (C)** Modern early warnings come from weather satellites and radar, which spot and track cyclones a day or two ahead. A sundial, thermometer or pressure cooker cannot do this.

**29. (D)** Lightning is a giant electric spark: charges build up inside towering storm clouds and then jump as a huge discharge. It is not reflected sunlight or burning gas.

**30. (A)** Water heats and cools much more slowly than land, so the nearby sea moderates coastal temperatures and the daily sea/land breezes keep the coast milder than the far interior.

**31. (B)** The fast train drags the air between it and the person, making that air move quickly and so lowering its pressure. The higher pressure on the person's far side then pushes them toward the train — which is why one must stand well back.

**32. (D)** A tornado is generally a much smaller, narrower but extremely violent whirling column of air, while a cyclone is a far larger system. Both spin, so "no spinning" and "same size" are wrong.

**33. (C)** The growing-cyclone loop runs: moist air rises → vapour condenses → heat is released → central pressure falls → faster wind rushes in → it brings still more moist air, repeating the cycle. Rising pressure or sinking cold air would stop a cyclone, not build one.

**34. (D)** In a strong cyclone the eye-wall winds can reach roughly 150 to 250 km/h — far above a gentle breeze.

**35. (B)** The bigger the pressure difference between two regions, the harder the air is pushed from high to low — so a large pressure difference gives a stronger wind.

**36. (C)** An anemometer's cups are spun by the wind: the faster they spin, the higher the wind speed. (It does not measure temperature or rainfall.)

**37. (B)** Both the sea breeze and the land breeze arise because land and water heat up and cool down at different rates, creating the pressure differences that drive the breezes.

**38. (A)** Storms and cyclones are low-pressure systems, so a sharp, sudden fall in air pressure usually warns that a storm is approaching — not that calm, settled weather is on the way.

**39. (D)** A cyclone's very low central pressure and fierce winds pile up a great mound of sea water (the storm surge) that washes far inland, flooding low coasts. It is not slow rainwater or river water, and the sea does not retreat.

**40. (A)** The atmosphere pushes up on the card with a pressure equal to the weight of many metres of air — far more than the weight of the small column of water in the glass — so the card and water are held in place.

**41. (D)** Thunderstorms need plenty of warm, moisture-rich air that can rise quickly. A hot, humid afternoon supplies exactly that. Cold dry or low-humidity air would not build a storm.

**42. (D)** In winter the land cools more than the sea, so winds tend to blow from the land out toward the sea. Because they come off the dry land, the winter winds are mostly dry — the opposite of the summer monsoon.

**43. (B)** A sound cyclone precaution is to store drinking water and keep emergency supplies and a radio ready. Leaving windows wide open, going out to photograph the storm, or sheltering under a lone tall tree are all unsafe.

**44. (D)** Fast wind across the chimney mouth lowers the pressure there. The higher pressure of the still air inside the house then pushes the smoke up and out through the chimney.

**45. (C)** A cyclone begins with the Sun heating the ocean so warm moist air rises (ii); that vapour then condenses, releasing heat and dropping the pressure further (iii); the falling pressure finally makes the wind spiral inward, gaining speed (i). Order: (ii) → (iii) → (i).

**46. (A)** The very low pressure at the centre is exactly what pulls the surrounding higher-pressure air rushing inward, and that inrush is the cyclone's fierce wind. Low pressure drives the wind, it does not stop it.

**47. (C)** From the centre outward a cyclone has a calm, low-pressure eye, then the violent eye wall, then bands of outer spiralling clouds. The eye and edges are not the violent parts.

**48. (B)** Air warmed from below expands, becomes lighter and rises away, leaving lower pressure behind. Warm air is less dense, so it does not raise the pressure.

**49. (A)** The anemometer cups are turned by the wind, so very slow cups mean the wind is light that day. Spin speed does not report temperature, rain or pressure directly.

**50. (C)** The sea breeze needs the land to be warmer than the sea, which only happens by day when the land heats faster under the Sun. At night that condition is gone (it even reverses), so the daytime sea breeze does not blow at night.

**51. (A)** Before a cyclone the sea has violent winds and high waves that can capsize fishing boats, so fishermen are warned to stay ashore. The fish, water temperature and "disappearing sea" are not the reason.

**52. (D)** The fast air inside the dryer's stream has lower pressure than the slower air around it. If the ball drifts to the edge, the higher surrounding pressure pushes it back into the stream, so it hovers and resists being knocked out.

**53. (D)** Far fewer people die in cyclones today mainly because accurate satellite warnings give time to evacuate to shelters. Cyclones are not weaker, the sea still floods, and the coast is still inhabited.

**54. (B)** A thunderstorm's rain forms in order: warm moist air rises and cools → its water vapour builds tall clouds → the drops grow heavy and fall as rain. Rain or lightning cannot come first, and cold sinking air would not start it.

**55. (B)** The Sun's uneven heating of land, sea and atmosphere creates the pressure differences that drive all winds, including the monsoon. The Moon and household fans are not responsible, and ocean currents themselves are ultimately Sun-driven.

**56. (A)** Cooling the bottle in ice water shrinks the trapped air and drops its pressure below the outside air, so the balloon is sucked inward / collapses into the bottle — the reverse of the hot-water case.

**57. (B)** As a cyclone intensifies its central pressure falls even lower, and because air rushes harder toward that deeper low, the winds get faster. (Falling pressure with faster — not slower — wind is the key pairing.)

**58. (C)** A sea breeze needs the land warmer than the sea. If on some afternoon the land is actually cooler than the sea, the required pressure difference is gone or flipped, so the usual sea breeze weakens or even reverses.

**59. (D)** Fast wind over a roof lowers the pressure above it, creating an upward lift that can tear a loose roof off. Strong, well-anchored roofs resist that lift — which is why coastal homes use them.

**60. (B)** As the cyclone passes over, a ship first meets the fierce eye-wall winds, then the sudden calm of the eye, then the fierce eye-wall winds again from the opposite direction as the far wall arrives.
