Worked Solutions

1. (D) The Himalayas are young fold mountains still being squeezed upward by the northward-moving Indian plate, so they gain height; the Aravallis are among the oldest ranges, no longer actively uplifting, so erosion steadily wears them lower. Saying both rise is false because the Aravallis are not being uplifted. Saying both erode is false because the Himalayas are net rising, and harder rock would erode slower not faster. Erosion strips rock away rather than piling it on summits, and the Aravallis lose height by wearing, not by sinking.

2. (A) Coming DOWN from the top you meet the highest snow-and-giant-peak Himadri first, then the middle Himachal belt with hill stations and forests, then the youngest, lowest gravel Shivalik foothills last. Shivalik-first reverses the descent (that is the climbing order). Putting Himadri after Himachal misorders the snow zone, which is the highest. Putting Shivalik before Himachal swaps the foothills above the hill-town belt, which is wrong.

3. (D) The 'water tower' matters most in the dry season: winter snow and glacier ice melt in the warm rainless months and feed rivers like the Ganga so they keep flowing without rain. During the monsoon the plains already get abundant rain, so the stored melt is least decisive then. In the coldest weeks snow accumulates rather than melting, so flow is actually low. Release is not constant; it is seasonal, peaking with summer melt, so 'equally all year' is wrong.

4. (A) The fossils are remains of creatures from the old Tethys Sea whose floor was crushed and folded skyward when India collided with Asia, so they record a former sea lifted to height. Glaciers carve and scour rock; they do not source marine fossils. The Arabian Sea is a present ocean to the southwest and never climbed the peaks. Rivers flow downhill, so they cannot carry shells up to summits; the uplift of a seabed is the explanation.

5. (B) The ranges differ in height, and because air cools and thins as altitude rises, the higher Himadri is snow-bound, the middle Himachal mild, and the low Shivalik warm, giving three climate belts. Age of uplift does not 'freeze in' a climate; present temperature depends on present altitude. Rock type does not control regional temperature. All three ranges receive sunlight; the higher two are cold because of altitude, not darkness.

6. (D) Height difference comes from age and uplift: young fold mountains under active squeezing reach great heights, while an ancient range that stopped rising long ago has eroded low. The Aravallis were once high folded rock, not material too soft to ever rise. They are above ground and worn down, not submerged. The Himalayas are true mountains with sharp peaks, not flat plateaus, so the difference is real, not an illusion of viewing angle.

7. (C) Uplift comes from the ongoing collision; remove that push and the range no longer rises, so erosion gradually lowers it the way ancient ranges like the Aravallis have been worn down. Mountains do not collapse instantly without a cause, and the chapter describes gradual processes. Tall mountains have no self-lifting force; rising depends on the plate push. Snow and rain would still feed rivers, so the range would not simply become a desert.

8. (B) The plains occupy a subsiding foredeep trough that rivers have packed with deep alluvium worn off the rising Himalayas, so soft sediment runs very deep. Cooled lava is what underlies the Deccan, not the plains, so that reverses the regions. The chapter describes no buried ice sheet beneath the plains. The plains' fill is river-borne mountain debris, not lifted sea-mud; their depth comes from continual river deposition into a sinking trough.

9. (C) Khadar is the new, light, very fertile silt laid on the low floodplain by each flood; bhangar is the old, higher, less fertile alluvium often with lime nodules. Calling the low strip bhangar and the high one khadar swaps the two terms. They are not equally fertile, which is the whole point of the contrast. Old bhangar is the LESS fertile one, so calling it more fertile inverts the rule.

10. (B) Level, fertile, river-fed plains favour intensive farming, easy construction and movement, so populations concentrate there, while rough foothills hinder all of these. Rivers do descend through the Shivaliks, so 'no rivers' is false. The foothills are not uninhabitably hot; relief and soil, not heat, drive the choice. Rainfall is not unique to the plains, so that claim is wrong too.

11. (B) Uplift comes first, then erosion produces silt, then rivers transport it, then it is deposited as alluvium; that is the only order where each step has its cause. Silt cannot settle before the mountains and rivers that make it exist. Silt is the product of erosion, not the tool that grinds rock, so that sequence is backwards. The mountains have not eroded away nor has a sea flooded and lifted the plains; that scenario is fiction.

12. (A) The plains' deep, soft, river-renewed alluvium is easy to work and highly fertile, supporting intensive farming. Thin soil over hard rock describes the Deccan, the very region being contrasted, so it is the opposite of the plains. Salty tidal soil belongs to coasts, and frozen ground to high mountains; neither describes the Northern Plains, whose advantage is its renewable alluvium.

13. (D) Floods lay fresh fine silt rich in nutrients on the low floodplain (khadar) they cover, raising its fertility, while the higher bhangar terrace is left untouched. Floods deposit nutrients on the strip they reach rather than washing them out. The fine silt is rich, not barren sand and stone. The flood reaches the low strip, not the higher terrace, so the claim that it raises the terrace and scours the strip reverses what happens.

14. (A) Several great Himalayan rivers have deposited and coalesced their alluvium along one long foredeep trough, building a continuous plain. Volcanic lava built the Deccan, not the plains, so a lava sheet does not apply here. The plain is alluvium from rivers, not a salt layer left by a former sea. Glaciers are confined to high mountains in the chapter and did not sweep the whole lowland flat; rivers and their merging silt made the continuous plain.

15. (B) Rivers run downhill, so a consistent eastward flow plus a higher western rim means the block tilts down to the east, channeling the rivers that way. A westward slope would send the rivers west, contradicting the observed eastward flow. A flat block would not give every major river one consistent direction, so 'coincidence' fails. Water cannot be pulled uphill toward a higher sea; the east is the low end, not the high one.

16. (D) The plateau's old, hard, stable crust has concentrated minerals and coal across enormous time, making it mineral-rich. Himalayan rivers carry silt onto the plains, not fresh ore onto the high plateau. Mineral and coal deposits form over geological ages, not a few lifetimes, so 'young soft rock filling quickly' is wrong. Tides act on coasts, not on the interior plateau surface; the wealth is inherent to ancient rock.

17. (D) The tilt raises the western edge into the high continuous Western Ghats, and the rivers running off the tilt eastward have sliced gaps in the lower Eastern Ghats. Both rims belong to the same ancient block, so 'one young, one ancient' is false. The sea did not flatten the eastern rim; rivers cut it. Mineral type is not the cause; the tilt plus river erosion shapes the two rims.

18. (C) The plateau's forests are home to tribal communities such as the Gond and Bhil. Ladakh's herders live in the northern cold desert, a different region. Coral-island fishers belong to the Lakshadweep group in the Arabian Sea, and mangrove dwellers to the eastern Sundarbans delta; none of these inhabits the central plateau forests.

19. (A) The high, near-continuous west-coast wall is the Western Ghats; the lower hills broken by river gaps on the east are the Eastern Ghats. Swapping their names and locations is the classic Ghats trap. The Himadri and Shivaliks are northern Himalayan ranges, not coastal walls. The Aravallis are an inland northwest range and the Vindhyas a central range, so neither pair names the coastal Ghats.

20. (C) Deep young river-laid alluvium makes the plains a farming region, while ancient hard stable rock makes the plateau a mineral region; each wealth matches its own rock. Swapping the two reverses the geology, since alluvium is the plains' asset and minerals the plateau's. Only the plains have deep alluvium, so 'both for the same reason' is wrong. The sea did not lay minerals and soil across these interior regions; their wealth comes from their own contrasting rock.

21. (B) Long, slow eastward rivers gather and deposit large sediment loads into the calmer Bay of Bengal, building wide deltas, while short, steep west rivers reach the rough open Arabian Sea with little sediment. The west coast does receive heavy monsoon rain, so 'no water' is false. The east coast clearly has major rivers, not only lakes. Sea saltiness does not control delta formation; sediment supply and a calm sea do.

22. (A) The branching fan of new fertile land is a delta; the single funnel where fresh and salt water mingle without building land is an estuary, so the order is delta then estuary. Reversing them mislabels each landform. A lagoon is a shallow water body cut off by a bar, not the branching fan described, so 'lagoon then delta' is wrong. The first mouth builds land, which an estuary does not, so calling both estuaries is false.

23. (D) Chilika and Pulicat are large coastal lakes and lagoons where bars and spits partly enclose seawater along the east coast. Coral atolls belong to the Lakshadweep group in the Arabian Sea, not the east coast. They are not high Himalayan glacial lakes, nor are they volcanic crater lakes on the plateau; their setting is the sea-fringed eastern coast.

24. (C) A heavy sediment load delivered slowly into a calm, sheltered sea settles and accumulates, forming a wide delta. An estuary forms where a river is short and swift or the sea is rough enough to remove sediment; a calm sea does the opposite, so that reasoning is backwards. Coral reefs grow in clear warm shallow seas, not at silt-choked river mouths. Dunes are wind-built desert features, not river-mouth landforms.

25. (A) Short, fast west-flowing rivers reaching the open, rough Arabian Sea deposit little sediment, producing creeks, coves and estuaries rather than broad deltas. Their rivers are short and swift, not the longest and slowest. Rivers do reach the western sea, so 'no rivers' is false. Sea temperature is not the controlling factor; river length, speed and the open rough sea are.

26. (B) The Sundarbans mangroves and the Royal Bengal Tiger lie in the Ganga–Brahmaputra delta, which India shares with Bangladesh. Myanmar lies to the east and does not share this delta. The Indus delta in the northwest borders Pakistan but is not the tiger's mangrove home. The Kaveri delta is in the far south and is not the shared mangrove delta described.

27. (C) The low, flat coral islands are Lakshadweep, lying in the Arabian Sea off the southwest. The Andaman & Nicobar group is the higher volcanic one in the Bay of Bengal, so naming it as coral is wrong. Lakshadweep is not in the Bay of Bengal, and the Andamans are not in the Arabian Sea; the formation must match the location.

28. (D) The contrast follows from origin: coral reefs build the low Lakshadweep in warm shallow seas, while volcanic activity raises the higher Andaman & Nicobar Islands at a plate setting. Age alone does not turn volcanic rock into coral. Both groups receive rain, so rainfall is not the cause. A slight sea-temperature difference does not decide rock type; the formation process does.

29. (A) Barren Island, India's only active volcano, lies in the Andaman & Nicobar group, confirming its volcanic origin at a plate boundary in the Bay of Bengal. The coral Lakshadweep islands have no active volcano. There is no glacial island chain in Himalayan lakes in the chapter. The Sundarbans are a mangrove delta, not a volcanic island group; the volcano marks the Andamans.

30. (B) Coral thrives in warm, clear, shallow water, and Lakshadweep is exactly such a coral group in the Arabian Sea. The Andamans are volcanic, not coral, so they do not fit. The Sundarbans are muddy mangrove delta where coral islands do not build. The Aravallis are an inland hill range, not islands at all; only Lakshadweep matches the coral conditions.

31. (D) Lakshadweep (coral) lies in the Arabian Sea to the west and the Andaman & Nicobar group (volcanic) lies in the Bay of Bengal to the east, so crossing west to east meets Lakshadweep first. Placing the volcanic Andamans in the Arabian Sea swaps both group and sea. Saying Lakshadweep is in the Bay of Bengal again misplaces the seas. The two groups sit on opposite sides of the peninsula, so 'both in the Arabian Sea' is false.

32. (A) Snow- and glacier-fed Himalayan rivers like the Ganga receive meltwater even in the rainless season, so they stay full, while rain-fed peninsular rivers fall low without rain. The Ganga is long, not short, so length is not the reason. The Aravallis do not dam the Deccan rivers, which lie far to the south. Rivers flow to the sea and do not draw water inland from it; the difference is the source of supply.

33. (B) Perennial rivers fed by melting snow and glaciers flow year-round, while rain-fed rivers depend on the monsoon and dwindle afterward. Reversing those patterns is wrong. Perennial rivers are snow-fed mountain rivers, not desert features, so that pairing fails. The two types clearly differ by season, so claiming identical year-round flow contradicts the definition.

34. (C) The Narmada and Tapti are exceptions because they flow west into the Arabian Sea, opposite the usual eastward drainage of the tilted plateau. They do not turn north to the Ganga. Rivers cannot climb uphill into the Ghats. Both reach the sea, so 'end inland' is false; their distinction is the westward course.

35. (A) The Himalayan river keeps flowing because melting snow and glaciers feed it continuously through the warm season, independent of rain. Stored-lake rain is not the chapter's reason for sustained Himalayan flow. A shorter course is not it, and Himalayan rivers are in fact long. Tidal seawater does not push upstream to sustain a freshwater river; meltwater does.

36. (C) Thin soil over hard ancient rock and seasonally varying rain-fed rivers limit intensive farming on the Deccan, in contrast with the plains' deep alluvium and snow-fed steadier water. The Deccan does have rivers and soil, just less favourable ones. It is not frozen year-round; it is a warm plateau. It is a raised plateau, not below sea level, so constant flooding does not apply.

37. (C) A short, swift river with a small sediment load meeting a rough open sea has its sediment swept away, so it forms an estuary rather than building land. A delta needs heavy sediment dropped into a calm sea, the opposite of a swift river in a rough sea, so 'larger delta' is wrong. Coral reefs need clear warm shallow seas, not turbulent river mouths. Lagoons form behind bars and spits, not from a river's speed.

38. (B) Deserts are defined by very low rainfall, not by heat, so high, cold, dry Ladakh qualifies as a cold desert. Ladakh is dry, not heavily rained on, so 'half right' is false. Sand dunes are common in hot deserts but are not required for a region to be a desert. Elevation relative to sea level is irrelevant; rainfall is the key test.

39. (A) Ladakh, the cold desert, has high salt lakes, severe winters and high-altitude animals such as the snow leopard and ibex. Tall golden dunes, heat-coping water tanks and scorching frost-free sand all describe the hot Thar, not Ladakh. Both are deserts by low rainfall, but their temperature and features differ, so the cold-desert traits are the salt lakes, deep cold and snow leopard.

40. (B) The Aravallis form a long ridge on the desert's eastern flank that blocks wind-driven sand, so removing them would let the Thar's sand spread east toward Delhi. The Himalayas rise from plate collision, unrelated to the Aravallis. The Ganga's course follows the plains' eastward slope, not these hills. The western coastline is a distant sea boundary unaffected by the Aravallis.

41. (C) In the dry Thar water is precious, so people clean with abundant sand and store scarce rain in tanks; the practice is born of scarcity. It is not a belief that sand outcleans water. Cold nights are not the reason in the hot Thar. No rule bans river water; the point is that very little water is available, so it is conserved.

42. (D) The Aravallis act as a long ridge along the desert's eastern flank, standing in the path of wind-driven sand. They are low and worn, not tall and snow-capped, so chilling winds is not the mechanism. They are folded rock, not loose absorbing sand. They lie along the eastern edge, not far to the south; their position and ridge form are what block the sand.

43. (D) Mount Abu is the highest point of the old, worn-low Aravalli range. The Himadri carries giant snow peaks far higher than Mount Abu, so it is not that range's high point. The Western Ghats are a separate southern coastal wall, and the Shivaliks are northern Himalayan foothills; Mount Abu belongs to the Aravallis.

44. (D) A wide latitudinal spread makes the lower-latitude south warmer and more tropical and the higher-latitude north cooler, giving varied climate. India does not sit wholly in a cold temperate zone. Being one country does not make temperatures uniform; latitude varies them. India lies entirely north of the Equator, so it is not split across hemispheres.

45. (B) The Tropic of Cancer is the northern edge of the belt where the Sun can be overhead; land to its south is tropical and warm all year, land to its north subtropical with sharper seasons, and the line crosses India's middle. India is not wholly temperate, since its south is tropical. Points on the Tropic do get overhead Sun around the June solstice, so 'never' is false. The Tropic is a latitude line, not a relief boundary between plains and mountains.

46. (C) A broad longitudinal extent means the eastern parts greet sunrise earlier than the western parts, since the Earth turns west to east. India is large, not small, so sunrise is not simultaneous everywhere. It does not occupy a single narrow longitude. The Earth turns as one body; the east does not spin faster, the difference comes from longitude.

47. (A) A large latitudinal spread gives climates from tropical south to cooler north, and a large longitudinal spread gives differing local sunrise times, so the two extents explain India's climatic variety and time differences. They do not produce one identical climate or one sunrise time. India clearly has seasons, so 'no seasons' is wrong. The rising Himalayas come from plate collision, not from the country's extent.

48. (A) Places nearer the Equator receive more direct sunlight, so the low-latitude south is warmer year-round than the high-latitude north; that is the effect of latitude alone. Higher latitude means cooler, not warmest, so the northern edge being warmest is wrong. Lying north of the Equator does not equalise temperature across 29 degrees of latitude. Distance from Himalayan snow is not a latitude effect, and the south is warmer, not colder.

49. (A) The Himalayas block frigid Central Asian winds and trap the rain-bearing monsoon; removing them would let in cold dry winds and reduce northern rainfall. A coral lagoon is a tropical sea feature, not a result of losing mountains. The region would be drier and colder in winter, but the chapter does not predict a heavy-snow polar desert. Mountains clearly shape climate, so 'nothing changes' is false.

50. (D) As moist winds climb the windward slope they cool and rain heavily; having shed their moisture, the air gives little rain to the sheltered side, drying it. The dry interior is a consequence of the Ghats, not an unrelated desert. The slope does not freeze the back by blocking sunlight; the cause is moisture loss. Rivers move water after it falls, not the rain still in the air, so they do not create the dry zone.

51. (B) In both places humid monsoon air is pushed up the slopes, cools, and releases heavy rain, the orographic effect. Neither owes its rain to a neighbouring desert. Neither is built of coral, and rock type does not attract rain. Both are uplands, not below-sea-level basins; rising, cooling moist air is the shared mechanism.

52. (A) Flat, fertile, well-watered plains support large-scale intensive cropping, while steep, cold, thin-soiled mountains permit only small terraces and herding. Shared rivers do not make their farming identical, since relief and climate differ greatly. Cooler mountains do not farm more intensively than the plains; the rugged cold limits them. Both regions do support some farming, so 'neither can' is false.

53. (C) Abundant river water and fertile alluvium let the plains feed dense populations, while the dry Thar's scarce water and poor soil support far fewer people. The Thar is hot, not too cold to inhabit. The plains are not the only flat land; the Deccan and coasts include flat areas. The desert is sunny, not sunless; water and soil, not sunlight, are the limiting factors.

54. (D) The barriers slowed but did not fully prevent contact, since traders and invaders found passes and sea routes through them. They certainly existed, so denying them is wrong. They restricted people's movement, not just rivers. They are natural features such as mountains and seas, not human-made walls; their effect was to hinder, not to seal.

55. (B) Together the Himalayas, the Thar plus the Arabian Sea, and the encircling oceans limited easy entry, helping India develop a distinct climate and culture. Random scattered hills form no such frame. The east-coast deltas alone are far too small a feature to shelter a subcontinent. The Lakshadweep reefs are tiny and remote; it is the great outer barriers acting together that matter.

56. (C) Each animal lives where the region's relief and climate suit it, the snow leopard in cold mountains, the tiger in warm mangroves, coral fish in warm seas, showing geography shapes wildlife. The species are clearly specialised, so they cannot live anywhere equally well. Wildlife depends strongly on geography, and the regions do not share the same animals; their habitats differ.

57. (D) Varied regions, from cold mountains to hot deserts, wet deltas and warm reefs, offer many habitats, supporting a wide variety of life. India does not have one uniform warm climate; its climates differ greatly. Its rich native wildlife is not all imported. Far from one shared habitat, it has many, which is exactly why biodiversity is high.

58. (B) Ladakh is a high cold desert, the Thar a hot sandy desert, and the Sundarbans a mangrove forest in a river delta, so each is a different type. They are not all hot deserts, since Ladakh is cold and the Sundarbans is a wet delta. They are not all deltas, since the two deserts are dry. Calling the deserts deltas and the delta a desert reverses the facts.

59. (C) Rivers flow from the mountains through the plains to the coasts, moving people, trade and ideas across regions and binding them together. They do not give all regions identical weather; climate still varies. They do not connect the mountains to the distant islands. They water and link the land but do not flatten the country into one plain; their uniting role is as routes of connection.

60. (C) Mountains, plains, desert, plateau, coasts and islands each shape distinct climates, wildlife and lifestyles, while rivers and shared history tie them into one nation, the chapter's full message. The land is not the same everywhere, so lives differ. It is not only the Himalayas that matter; every region does. Geography clearly influences where and how people live, so 'no effect' is false.
