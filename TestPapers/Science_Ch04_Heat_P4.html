1. (C) Heat flows from the hotter water to the cooler water until both reach one common temperature. With equal amounts of the same liquid, that common value sits exactly halfway between the two starting temperatures: (20 + 80) ÷ 2 = 50 °C. The hotter portion does not simply impose its 80 °C — it cools as it warms the other half. Adding the two temperatures (giving 100) treats temperature like a quantity that piles up, which it does not. And heat does not settle at the lower value of 20 °C; the cool water warms up while the hot water cools down.

2. (D) When the amounts are unequal, the final temperature is pulled toward the temperature of the larger amount. Here there is three times as much hot water (300 g) as cold water (100 g), so the mix ends up well above the midpoint of 50 °C and much closer to 80 °C. The midpoint of 50 °C would only be right if the amounts were equal. 'Cold water always wins' is false — the side with more substance has the bigger say, and here that is the hot water. Plainly averaging the two temperatures and nudging the result also ignores that the larger hot share controls the outcome.

3. (A) The final temperature is dragged toward whichever side has far more substance. The bucket has overwhelmingly more water than one drop, so the bucket barely changes and stays near 25 °C; the drop's small heat is shared out among a huge mass of water. The midpoint of 62.5 °C would only apply if the two amounts were equal, which they are wildly not. Being the hottest object does not let one drop heat a whole bucket — heat content depends on amount as well as temperature. And adding warm water cannot cool the bucket below 25 °C.

4. (B) Heat flows only when there is a temperature difference, always from hotter to colder. Both blocks are at 40 °C, so there is no difference and no net flow — both simply stay at 40 °C. Having more metal gives block X more total heat, but the same temperature, so it does not push heat into Y. 'More metal means higher temperature' confuses amount of heat with temperature; they are different. And although X stores more heat than Y, heat does not flow without a temperature gap.

5. (C) Heat travels from the hotter body to the cooler one. The tea is hotter than the room, so heat moves out of the tea into the room air; the tea cools and the room warms by a tiny, barely noticeable amount, until the tea matches room temperature. The student's claim reverses this — the room does not warm the tea. There is no separate substance called 'cold' that flows; cooling is just the loss of heat. And size does not set the flow direction — temperature difference does.

6. (C) For the same liquid and the same amount, the heat needed depends on how many degrees the temperature rises, not on the starting value. Both rose by 10 °C, so each absorbed about the same heat. Ending at a higher temperature (Q at 80 °C) does not by itself mean more heat was added during this step. Starting cold does not make P need extra heat for the same 10 °C climb. And heat is exactly what raised each temperature, so saying no heat entered is wrong — heat and temperature change are tightly linked here.

7. (C) Task (i) is at −5 °C, far below the clinical scale's lowest mark of 35 °C, so only the wide-range laboratory thermometer can reach it. Task (ii) needs a reading held in place after the thermometer leaves the body and carried to another room — that is exactly what the clinical thermometer's kink does, plus its scale is spread finely over the body range for tenth-of-a-degree fevers. Using the clinical one for both fails on (i) because −5 °C is off its scale. Using the laboratory one for both fails on (ii) because its kink-free thread would slip as it cooled on the walk to the other room. The last pairing assigns the wrong tool to each task.

8. (A) The kink traps the thread at the highest temperature it has reached. After the first patient it sits at 39.4 °C. The second patient at 38.6 °C is cooler than 39.4 °C, so the liquid does not expand enough to push the trapped thread any higher — it stays near 39.4 °C, giving a falsely high reading. This is precisely why a clinical thermometer must be shaken down before each use. The thermometer does not reset itself; that is the job of the shake. Readings do not add up across uses. And the kink stops the thread from dropping back, so it does not fall to room temperature on its own.

9. (B) A laboratory thermometer must cover about 120 degrees of range, so each small division is comparatively large (around 1 °C), and it cannot distinguish 39.2 °C from 39.3 °C. A clinical thermometer devotes its whole length to roughly 35–42 °C, so it shows tenths of a degree clearly. The range claim is false: 39.2 °C is inside −10 to 110. Lacking a kink only means the reading is not held while carried away — it can still measure body temperature in contact. And the liquid does not freeze anywhere near body temperature.

10. (C) A laboratory thermometer has no kink, so its thread moves freely up and down with temperature; once removed into cooler air the liquid contracts and the reading falls. A clinical thermometer's kink traps the thread, so its reading stays put after removal. It is not true that every thermometer locks its reading — only the kink does that. The kink-free laboratory thermometer is precisely the one that does NOT hold its reading. And neither reading climbs after removal, since the surroundings are cooler than 100 °C.

11. (D) Across 100 °C, A's 100 divisions make each one 100 ÷ 100 = 1 °C; B's 50 divisions make each one 100 ÷ 50 = 2 °C. The thermometer whose smallest division is smaller can be read more finely, so A (1 °C steps) beats B (2 °C steps). Fewer divisions over the same range make each division LARGER, not smaller, so B is coarser. Equal ranges do not guarantee equal fineness — division count decides it. And the computed values are 1 °C and 2 °C, not 0.5 °C and 1 °C.

12. (B) Ten equal divisions in one degree make each division 1 ÷ 10 = 0.1 °C. Seven divisions above 38 means 38 + 7 × 0.1 = 38.7 °C. Reading 38.07 places the decimal one digit too far, as if each division were 0.01 °C. Counting the 7 divisions as whole degrees (38 + 7 = 45) ignores that they are tenths. And 38.35 would require five divisions per degree of 0.05 each, which is not the stated scale.

13. (B) Twenty degrees split into 10 equal divisions makes each division 20 ÷ 10 = 2 °C. Six divisions above 40 means 40 + 6 × 2 = 40 + 12 = 52 °C. Reading 46 wrongly treats each division as 1 °C (40 + 6). Reading 40.6 wrongly treats each division as 0.1 °C. And 60 jumps to the next numbered mark, ignoring that the thread is only 6 of the 10 divisions up.

14. (B) Five degrees over 5 divisions makes each division 1 °C. Yesterday: 10 + 4 = 14 °C. Today: 15 + 2 = 17 °C. The change is 17 − 14 = +3 °C, a rise. Reading today's value as 16 forgets the reading starts from the 15 mark plus 2 = 17, not 14 + 2. Comparing division counts (4 vs 2) and concluding a fall ignores that today's count is measured above a higher numbered mark (15, not 10), so the temperature actually rose. And 5 °C is just the gap between the numbered marks, not the change in the thread's position.

15. (D) Ten divisions per 10 degrees make each division 1 °C. Seven divisions below 0, moving toward colder, gives 0 − 7 = −7 °C. Reading +7 °C ignores that the thread is below zero, in the colder direction. Reading −70 wrongly treats each division as 10 °C. And −17 wrongly starts counting from the −10 mark instead of from 0.

16. (C) Two degrees split into 4 divisions makes each division 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5 °C. Three divisions above 24 means 24 + 3 × 0.5 = 24 + 1.5 = 25.5 °C, so the second student is correct. The first student assumed each division was 0.1 °C, which is only true when there are 10 divisions per degree — not the case here. The two values 24.3 and 25.5 are clearly different numbers. And 27 °C would require each division to be 1 °C with three of them, which contradicts the stated 0.5 °C divisions.

17. (C) Each division is 1 °C, so the readings are 30 + 2 = 32, 30 + 5 = 35, 30 + 8 = 38 °C — a steady rise of 3 °C per minute. Continuing the pattern, 9:03 gives 38 + 3 = 41 °C, which is 1 division above the 40 mark. The thread is not capped at the 9th division above 30; it simply crosses into the next numbered region. The rise is steady (adding 3 each minute), not doubling. And there is no rule forcing the thread to stop on a numbered mark — it lands wherever the temperature is.

18. (D) In a solid, heat travels by conduction: vibrating particles pass energy to their neighbours, so the disturbance reaches near points first and far points later. That is exactly why the wax falls in sequence from the flame end outward — a gradual arrival, not chance. If radiation reached every bead equally and instantly, they would fall together, which they do not. Convection needs a fluid that can flow; the solid rod's particles are locked in place and cannot circulate. And the wax does not make its own heat — it merely melts when conducted heat arrives.

19. (C) The faster the far bead melts, the faster heat reached the end, so the best conductor loses its bead first. Copper (30 s) is fastest, glass (5 min) is in the middle, wood (15 min) is slowest — so the order is copper > glass > wood. Being light does not improve conduction; wood is light yet a poor conductor. Glass conducts far less than copper, so it is not best. And an identical heat source does NOT equalise the results — the material decides how quickly heat passes through, which is the whole point of the experiment.

20. (C) Same shape means the only difference is the material. Copper is a good conductor and quickly delivers heat to its far end; wood is a poor conductor, so heat creeps along it slowly and the far end needs much more time to reach the same temperature. Identical shape does not make the times equal, because the materials conduct at very different rates. Poor conductors heat their far ends MORE slowly, not faster. And wood does conduct heat — just slowly — so it is not 'never.'

21. (B) Both objects are at 10 °C, so neither is actually colder. But the metal knob is a good conductor: it whisks heat away from your warmer hand quickly, cooling the skin fast, so it FEELS colder. Wood is a poor conductor and draws heat slowly, so it feels less cold. The wood is not at a lower temperature — both are 10 °C. They do not feel equally cold despite equal temperature, because conduction rates differ. And the metal, being colder than your hand, takes heat from it rather than giving heat back, so it cannot feel warmer.

22. (D) Conduction is the passing of energy from particle to neighbouring particle. In copper the particles are tightly packed and constantly in contact, so energy is relayed quickly. In air the particles are widely separated and seldom touch, so the relay is feeble and slow. Air certainly contains particles — they are just spread out. Temperature does not set conduction ability; copper is not 'colder.' And air particles actually move fast; it is their large spacing, not slowness, that makes conduction poor.

23. (A) The body must carry heat from the burner into the food, which calls for a good conductor — metal. The handle must keep heat from reaching the hand, which calls for a poor conductor (insulator) — wood or plastic. Reversing them (wooden body, metal handle) would leave the food barely warmed while the handle conducts heat and burns the hand. An all-metal pan would make the handle hot, not cool. And an all-plastic pan would not heat the food well, since plastic is a poor conductor — defeating the cooking goal.

24. (D) Heated water expands, so the same mass takes more space, making it less dense (lighter) — it rises. Cooler, denser water sinks to replace it, and the cycle repeats, forming a convection current that warms the whole kettle. Hot water is lighter, not heavier, so it does not sink. Water conducts heat poorly, so the heat is not simply conducted straight up through still layers — the bulk movement of water does most of the carrying. And the water does not flash to steam at ordinary heating; it circulates as a liquid first.

25. (D) Convection currents rise wherever the water is heated, because that is where the water expands, lightens, and floats upward; the cooler water elsewhere sinks. Heating a corner therefore anchors the rising limb at that corner, not the centre. The current does not always rise centrally — it follows the heat source. Conduction through still water is far too slow to trace this loop; the dye moves because the water bulk-moves. And heated water becomes lighter, so it rises at the corner, not sinks.

26. (D) Warm air rises. A floor-level heater sends warm air up and around the whole room as a convection loop. A ceiling-level heater would warm air that is already at the top, where it tends to stay, leaving the lower (living) part of the room cold — so low placement heats a room better. Warm air does not fall down to fill the room; that reverses convection. Air conducts heat poorly, so heating does not spread by conduction regardless of height. And warm air rises, so the floor would not become the hottest part.

27. (C) Convection works only in fluids (liquids and gases) because the warmed parts must rise and circulate, carrying heat bodily. In a solid the particles are locked in their positions and cannot flow, so no convection current can form — heat spreads through iron by conduction instead. Paint colour affects radiation, not whether a solid can convect. No amount of heat lets a rigid solid's particles circulate. And solids certainly can be heated in the middle; they just transfer that heat by conduction.

28. (A) Convection is driven by warm (lighter) fluid rising and cold (denser) fluid sinking. Here the warm water is already on top and the cold below, which is already the stable layering, so there is no tendency to overturn and circulate. Warm water is lighter, not denser, so it belongs on top — the arrangement is stable, not blocked. Convection can certainly occur in tall cylinders when heated from below; shape is not the issue. And the cold layer does not 'block' cooling — there is simply no driving force for a current.

29. (B) The dark bare ground absorbs more sunlight and becomes hotter than the shaded grass; it warms the air just above it more strongly, and that hotter air expands, becomes lighter, and rises as a vigorous convection current, carrying the smoke up. Over the cooler shaded patch the air rises only weakly. Smoke's lift here comes from the rising warmed air, not from smoke being inherently lighter over one surface. Conduction does not bodily push air upward. And radiation travels in straight rays and does not lift particles by buoyancy — the buoyant rising air does that.

30. (A) The hot air rises away from you, so convection cannot be warming your face. The warmth that crosses the gap — and even passes through a sheet of glass — is radiation: it travels in straight lines, needs no medium, and transparent glass lets much of it through. Convection is ruled out because the hot air goes up, not around the glass. Air is a poor conductor and the gap is large, so conduction is negligible — and a chain of touching air does not deliver this warmth. And the fire does not warm you by shoving air through glass; the radiation does.

31. (B) Every warm object emits radiation, and the hotter it is, the more it emits each second. So the 400 °C plate radiates far more than the 100 °C plate. A cooler object does not shed heat faster — it emits less. Identical size and colour do not equalise emission when the temperatures differ so much; temperature is the dominant factor here. And objects radiate their own heat regardless of sunlight, so they radiate even in a dark room.

32. (C) In a vacuum there are no particles to touch (so no conduction) and no fluid to flow and circulate (so no convection). Radiation, however, needs no medium and travels through empty space, so the probe sheds heat by radiating it away. There is no surrounding gas to carry convection. There is no matter to conduct through. And it is wrong that no heat can leave — radiation works precisely where the other two fail, which is why bodies in space still cool.

33. (A) A dull, dark surface is a good emitter of radiation, so kettle M radiates its heat away faster and its water ends cooler. A shiny, polished surface is a poor emitter, so kettle N holds its heat longer — it cools slower, not faster. Equal starting water does not make their cooling equal, because the surface type changes how fast each radiates. And objects radiate their own heat regardless of sunlight, so they lose heat by radiation indoors too.

34. (A) A dull dark surface is good at both emitting and absorbing radiation; a shiny surface is poor at both. So the black teapot loses (emits) heat faster, keeping tea hot for less time, while in sunlight a black object gains (absorbs) heat faster and warms quicker. One property — dark surfaces are strong emitters and absorbers — covers both facts. Shiny surfaces are poor, not good, at both, so they cannot explain it the reverse way. Dark surfaces both absorb and emit well, not one without the other. And colour affects emission too, not absorption alone, so the teapot result is fully explained by surface type.

35. (C) A vacuum has no particles in contact (so conduction fails) and no fluid to flow (so convection fails). Radiation, needing no medium, crosses freely — so exactly one claim, number (3), is correct. Conduction does NOT cross a vacuum, so 'two' is wrong. Not all three work — only radiation does. And 'none' wrongly denies radiation, which genuinely does cross empty space (that is how the Sun warms the Earth).

36. (A) A good handle must NOT pass heat to the hand, so it should be a poor conductor (insulator) — dry wood fits. Silver, aluminium, and copper are all metals and good conductors, so any of them would carry the pan's heat to the grip and burn the hand. Being the best conductor (silver) makes the handle hottest, not coolest. Aluminium, though light, is still a metal and conducts heat well — 'light metals barely conduct' is false. And comfort or durability does not override the fact that a copper handle would get dangerously hot.

37. (A) Conduction needs touching particles and convection needs a flowing fluid, so removing the matter between the walls (a vacuum) chokes off both of those paths. Radiation, however, crosses a vacuum, so it must be fought separately — by making the surfaces shiny, since shiny surfaces are poor emitters of radiation. Reversing the two (vacuum stops radiation, shiny stops conduction) is backwards. The vacuum alone cannot stop radiation, so it does not block all three. And the shiny walls do not block conduction or convection, so they do not handle all three either.

38. (C) Insulation depends on how poorly the wall conducts. Foam is full of trapped air pockets, the poorest conductor, so it keeps the drink hot longest. A thin metal cup conducts the drink's heat outward fastest, so it cools the drink soonest. Ceramic sits between them. Metal does not 'seal heat in' — it conducts heat out quickly, the opposite. Same drink and room do not equalise them, since the wall materials conduct very differently. And trapped air in foam is a BETTER insulator than a solid ceramic wall, so foam beats ceramic.

39. (B) Air's widely spaced particles conduct heat very poorly, so when air is trapped and held still (as in wool or a sweater) it barely lets heat through — an excellent insulator. But when air moves (wind), it carries heat away bodily by convection, so it no longer insulates and instead cools you. So air is not always a perfect insulator — only when still. Moving air does not conduct better; it removes heat by convection, a different mechanism. And air neither destroys nor creates heat; it only transmits or carries it.

40. (D) A thin shiny sheet has almost no trapped air, so it cannot insulate the way wool does. Instead, its shiny surface is a poor emitter and a good reflector of radiation, so it reflects the body's own radiated heat back toward the person, slowing radiative heat loss. The blanket does not generate warmth — no material makes heat from being touched. A thin sheet does not trap a thick air layer, so that is not its mechanism. And 'conducting the cold away' is backwards: there is no flow of cold, and conducting heat outward would cool the person.

41. (C) A dark surface is a strong absorber AND a strong emitter of radiation. By day it absorbs more sunlight, so the black shirt feels hotter; at night, facing away from the fire, that same strong-emitting black surface radiates the wearer's body heat away faster. One property explains both. A surface does not switch from absorbing to reflecting by time of day — its absorbing/emitting nature is fixed. White does not absorb more by day; it reflects more. And the night effect is from emission, not cloth thickness, since the shirts are identical except for colour.

42. (D) The job of the absorber plate is to take in as much solar radiation as possible and pass that heat to the water. Dull dark surfaces are the best absorbers of radiation, so black absorbs the most sunlight and heats the water most effectively. Black absorbs rather than reflects, so 'reflects the most' is wrong. White reflects more and absorbs less, so it would be worse, not better — and the choice is about absorption, not cost. And the colour of the plate matters greatly; the glass merely lets sunlight in and traps warmth, it does not replace the absorbing surface.

43. (A) A white surface reflects most of the Sun's radiation, so the white house's walls absorb less heat and the rooms stay cooler. A dark surface absorbs much more, so the brown house's walls take in more heat and the rooms get hotter. 'Dark colours keep buildings cool by absorbing heat' is self-contradictory — absorbing heat warms the building. Equal wall thickness does not equalise them, because the surface colour changes how much radiation is absorbed. And white reflects, not absorbs, so the white house is cooler, not hotter.

44. (B) In direct sunlight a dark coat absorbs more of the Sun's radiation, so the black coat warms the wearer more by day. The catch is that a dark surface is also a better emitter, so at night (no Sun) it would shed the body's own radiated heat faster — a genuine trade-off. White reflects sunlight, so it absorbs less and feels cooler, not warmer, in the sun. Black does not 'make heat'; it absorbs the Sun's radiation, so it is not warmer at every time. Equal size and shape do not equalise cooling, because the surface type sets the emission rate. And no sealed box keeps heat forever — it always radiates heat away to cooler surroundings.

46. (D) By day the land heats faster than the sea. The air over the warm land rises, and cooler air flows in from over the sea to replace it — that inflow from sea to land is the sea breeze. It is not a land breeze, and the sea does not heat faster than land by day. The Sun does not physically drag air sideways; uneven heating drives the breeze. 47. (D) At night the land loses heat and cools faster than the sea, so the sea ends up warmer. The land is COOLER than the sea at night, not warmer, so 'land stays warmer' is wrong. And the reversal is caused by the land-sea temperature swap, not by an automatic rule.

48. (A) The breeze is driven by the temperature difference between land and sea. By day the land is warmer (sea breeze); by night the land is cooler (land breeze). Near dawn and dusk the land and sea are passing through nearly equal temperatures, so the difference shrinks to almost nothing and the driving force fades — winds go quiet and shift. There is no fixed two-hour-stop rule; it depends on the temperatures matching. The Sun is low near the horizon at dawn and dusk, not overhead. And the temperature difference is smallest, not greatest, at those changeover times — that is exactly why the wind is weak then.

49. (A) Both breezes are the same convection mechanism: air rises over the warmer surface and cooler air flows in from the cooler surface. The only thing that changes between day and night is which surface is warmer — the land by day, the sea by night — so the wind simply reverses direction. The same process runs both ways, confirming the teacher. The land breeze is not conduction; air conducts heat poorly, and the breeze is bulk air movement. The Moon does not drive the land breeze; uneven heating and cooling do. And neither breeze is radiation — both are convection of air.

50. (B) Sea and land breezes are convection driven by a temperature difference between land and sea: air rises over the warmer surface and cooler air flows in. If both are at exactly the same temperature, no surface is warmer, no air preferentially rises, and so neither breeze can form. The claim that the sea is 'always cooler' is false — by night the sea is the warmer one. A coast does not always have a breeze; without a temperature difference there is none. And nights produce a land breeze only because the land cools below the sea — with no difference, even that does not occur.

51. (B) Warm air rises over the warmer surface, leaving a gap near the ground that is filled by air flowing in from the cooler surface. So the surface breeze always moves FROM the cooler region TOWARD the warmer one: sea-to-land by day (land warmer) and land-to-sea by night (sea warmer). It does not blow from warm to cool at ground level — that air has risen, not flowed sideways. It is not always sea-to-land; the direction reverses at night. And while warm air does rise vertically, the breeze we feel is the horizontal inflow replacing it, not a purely upward wind.

52. (B) The warmth of clothing comes mainly from trapped still air, which conducts heat poorly. Each gap between layers traps another pocket of still air, so three layers create more insulating air pockets than one thick layer of the same cloth, slowing heat loss more. Weight does not set warmth — trapped air does. Clothes do not generate body heat, so three shirts do not 'produce' more heat. 54. (A) Wool is full of trapped still air and is a poor conductor, so wrapping it around the pot greatly slows the stew's heat from escaping — the best insulation here. 57. (D) A thermos drastically reduces all three modes — the vacuum starves conduction and convection of the matter they need, and the shiny walls cut radiation — but it cannot remove every path. Heat still leaks slowly through the cap, the seams where the walls join, and by some radiation, so over hours the cocoa drifts to room temperature. The thermos does not generate heat of its own to 'run out.' A vacuum slows heat loss, it does not speed it up. And there is no flow of 'cold' into the cocoa — cooling is the slow escape of the cocoa's own heat to the cooler room.

58. (C) Step 1: the Sun's heat crosses the vacuum of space and is absorbed by the dark land — that crossing of empty space and absorption is radiation. Steps 2–3: the heated air over the land rises and cooler sea air flows in to replace it — bulk movement of a fluid, which is convection. The Sun cannot conduct heat through space (no matter to conduct through), so step 1 is not conduction. Warm air rising and flowing is not radiation, so steps 2–3 are not radiation. And the moving air is convection, not conduction, since air conducts heat poorly and here it is physically flowing — so 'all conduction' is wrong.

59. (D) A laboratory thermometer has no kink, so its thread moves freely. The warm hand heats the bulb's liquid, which expands and pushes the thread up to about 33 °C; once the hand is removed the liquid cools toward room temperature, contracts, and the thread falls back toward 25 °C. This demonstrates the expand-on-heating, contract-on-cooling behaviour the thermometer relies on. Only a clinical thermometer's kink holds the highest reading — this one does not. The thread cannot keep rising once the hand is gone, since no more heat is being added. And with no hand on it the thermometer reads the room's 25 °C, not 0 °C.

60. (B) Both objects are at 2 °C, so a thermometer reads 2 °C on each — they are equally cold by measurement. But the metal is a good conductor and pulls heat from the warm foot rapidly, chilling the skin fast, so it FEELS far colder; the foam, a poor conductor, draws heat slowly and feels less cold. Foam does not feel colder, and it is not at a lower temperature. Feel and temperature do not always match — that mismatch is the whole point, since the metal feels colder despite equal temperature. And the thermometer would NOT read lower on the metal, because the metal is not genuinely colder — it only conducts heat away faster.
