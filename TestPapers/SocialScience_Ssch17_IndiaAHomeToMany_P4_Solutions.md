## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** The chapter dates the welcome back millennia: Arab merchants came in the 7th century and Jews even earlier, so 'only recently' is plainly wrong. The Tibetan and Baha'i arrivals are genuine modern examples, but they show the ethos CONTINUED into modern times, not that it BEGAN then. Scheduled-tribe recognition of the Siddis is a modern legal status laid over a community brought to India centuries ago; it dates the protection, not the arrival, and so does not establish a recent start.

**2. (C)** 'Atithi devo bhava' ('a guest is like God') honours the particular guest, whereas 'vasudhaiva kutumbakam' ('the whole world is one family') is the broader civilisational ideal embracing all humanity. The first distractor reverses the two scopes. Neither saying is a trade rule, so the second is false. And both are timeless values the chapter presents as guiding India across the ages, not split between ancient and modern eras.

**3. (D)** 'A home to many' captures India's characteristic spirit, or ethos, of acceptance that drew many communities over millennia. India is the receiver, not a conqueror dragging peoples home, so the first distractor inverts the relationship. Acceptance was not sold to paying traders only; refugees and even enslaved-origin communities were drawn in, refuting the second. And newcomers kept their own identities rather than melting into one Indian identity, so the third contradicts the chapter's core point.

**4. (A)** The chapter ties vasudhaiva kutumbakam to concrete enrichment: foods, words, crafts, music and faiths brought by newcomers broadened, not weakened, the culture, so welcoming outsiders strengthened it. The Siddis were not kept apart; they were drawn into society and constitutionally protected, so the first distractor misstates the evidence. Atithi devo bhava never demanded newcomers drop their customs, which contradicts the second. And India did not expel communities, so the pluralism pairing is false.

**5. (D)** The chapter projects the value forward: in a world where diverse peoples share space ever more closely, treating humanity as one family is a working principle for living together, which is exactly why modern examples like the Tibetans and Baha'is are cited. 'One family' means kinship, not one nation ruling others, so the first distractor twists it. The value addresses all of society, not traders alone, refuting the second. And the chapter calls it relevant NOW, not a dead relic, so the third reverses the point.

**6. (D)** Fleeing attack for one's beliefs is seeking refuge; chasing trade profit is seeking opportunity, so the order is refuge then opportunity. The first distractor reverses them. The second names persecution (the thing fled) and asylum (a synonym for refuge) rather than the two motive categories the chapter uses. And conquest is not one of the chapter's two paths, so the last is outside the framework.

**7. (B)** The Siddis break the model because they came by neither voluntary path: they were brought enslaved. The Parsis fit the model squarely as refuge-seekers and did not conquer, so the first is false. The Arab merchants came freely for trade, not under force, so the second misdescribes them. And the Tibetans came as refugees fleeing danger, not as rulers, so the third is wrong.

**8. (D)** The Polish children fled wartime danger and the Cochin Jews fled persecution, so both came for refuge; the Armenians traded and the Arab merchants who founded the Mappila community traded, so both came for opportunity. 'All four for trade' ignores the two refuge cases. The third option misfiles the Armenians as refugees and the Jews as traders. And none of these four were enslaved, so the last is false.

**9. (C)** The chapter's core distinction is choice: a refugee is pushed out by danger or persecution and has little real choice, while the immigrant comes freely seeking opportunity. Keeping traditions was open to all newcomers, so the first distractor invents a false limit. Refugees did join Indian society, the Tibetans, Parsis and Jews among them, so the second is false. And both refugees and immigrants appear across ancient and modern times, so the era split in the third is invented.

**10. (A)** To emigrate is to leave a country and to immigrate is to enter one, so leaving Persia is emigrating and entering India is immigrating. The first distractor swaps the two directions. A single move is naturally both an emigration from the old land and an immigration into the new, so the 'cannot also be' claim is false. And a refugee fleeing persecution is precisely a kind of migrant, so 'not migration' is wrong.

**11. (C)** The Tibetans fled danger and were sheltered, so 'refuge from danger' is the right match. The Siddis did not come for trade; they were brought enslaved, so the first distractor is false. The Parsis came for refuge from persecution, not trade, so the second misfiles them. And the Arab merchants came freely for trade, not as persecuted refugees, so the third reverses their motive.

**12. (D)** The Siddis fit neither voluntary path because they were brought enslaved, revealing that involuntary arrival is a third reality the simple two-path model leaves out. The Parsis chose refuge and settled rather than ruled, so the first is false. The Armenians came to trade, not to conquer, so the second is wrong. And because the Siddis genuinely break the model, 'none of the three' is false.

**13. (C)** The synagogue is the Jewish house of worship, the fire temple is the Zoroastrian (Parsi) one, and the church belongs to Christian communities such as the Armenians and Syriac Christians, so all three differ. The first distractor swaps synagogue and fire temple and wrongly assigns the church to the enslaved-origin Siddis. The second hands these buildings to Arab Muslims, Tibetan Buddhists and Parsis, none of whom owns them. And the third scrambles all three assignments.

**14. (B)** The Bene Israel and Cochin Jews are two of India's Jewish communities, both preserving their faith and building synagogues. African-origin enslaved arrivals describe the Siddis, not the Jews, so the first is false. The Kerala Muslim community formed by Arab settlers is the Mappila, refuting the second. And Zoroastrian fire-temple builders in Gujarat are the Parsis, so the third names the wrong faith entirely.

**15. (D)** Surviving synagogues alongside full participation in Indian life is the model of identity-preserving integration. It refutes the idea that acceptance demanded giving up one's religion, so the first is false. It refutes total separation, since the Jews were part of Indian society, so the second is wrong. And it has nothing to do with ruling the older inhabitants; the theme is coexistence, not domination, so the third is off-point.

**16. (D)** The Syriac Christians are among the world's oldest Christian communities, proving Christianity's roots in India are ancient. They did not first arrive in the 20th century, refuting the first. They were not introduced by the African-origin Siddis, who are a separate community, so the second is false. And while the Armenians were also Christians, Christianity in India is not limited to them; the Syriac Christians are a distinct, older Kerala community, so the third is wrong.

**17. (A)** Both are old communities that kept their faith while integrating, the chapter's model of identity-preserving integration. Neither was enslaved; that path belongs to the Siddis, so the first is false. Neither came as a conquering army, so the second is wrong. And the entire point is that they KEPT their religions, so the claim that they abandoned them in the third is the opposite of the truth.

**18. (A)** The Syriac Christians group with communities such as the Jews and Parsis who settled and preserved their faith, an instance of identity-preserving integration. They did not conquer Kerala, refuting the first. They did not abandon their religion; preserving it is the point, so the second is false. And they were not enslaved; that was the Siddis, so the third misclassifies their arrival.

**19. (A)** Zarathushtra was the human prophet who founded Zoroastrianism; the one God worshipped is Ahura Mazda. The first distractor swaps prophet and God. Zoroastrianism worships one God, not many gods of nature, so the second is false. And Zarathushtra was the faith's ancient founder, not an Arab merchant who began it in India, so the third confuses two unrelated figures and places the founding in the wrong land.

**20. (D)** Zoroastrianism is one of the oldest religions, worships one God (Ahura Mazda) and emphasises choosing good over evil. It is neither new nor polytheistic, so the first is false. It is not a branch of Judaism, and the synagogue is the Jewish house of worship, not the Zoroastrian fire temple, refuting the second. And it is a religion, not a healing system; Sowa Rigpa is the Tibetan 'Art of Healing', so the third confuses two different things.

**21. (C)** The Parsis fled persecution (refuge) and settled in Gujarat while keeping their fire temples, combining refuge with identity-preserving integration. They did not come to trade or rule Gujarat, so the first is false. The enslaved-then-scheduled-tribe story is the Siddis', not the Parsis', refuting the second. And the Parsis are an ancient arrival, unlike the 20th-century Tibetan refugees, so the third misdates them.

**22. (A)** The Parsis settled mainly in Gujarat and are marked by their fire temples, while the Jews are associated with their synagogues. The first distractor swaps the houses of worship, giving the Jews fire temples and the Parsis synagogues. Tibet and monasteries belong to the Tibetan refugees, so the second is wrong. And Armenia and churches belong to the Armenians, so the third misassigns both markers.

**23. (D)** The Parsis fled persecution seeking refuge, which directly breaks the 'all came to trade' claim. They did not conquer or rule Gujarat, so the first is false. They were not enslaved; that path belongs to the Siddis, refuting the second. And far from refusing to settle, they put down lasting roots and kept their fire temples, so the third is wrong.

**24. (D)** Zoroastrianism is among the oldest religions and the Syriac Christians prove Christianity's ancient roots in Kerala, so the first option is correct on both counts. The first distractor reverses the age of Zoroastrianism and misdates Christianity. Islam reached the west coast peacefully through Arab merchants from the 7th century, before the later invasions, so the second is false. And the faiths span ancient to modern, not 20th century alone, so the last is wrong.

**25. (B)** Islam first reached India peacefully with Arab traders on the west coast from the 7th century, distinct from and earlier than the later military invasions, which is exactly the misconception being corrected. It did not first appear in the 20th century, refuting the first distractor. It was not brought by the Siddis, who have a separate African-origin history, so the second is false. And the early arrival was by peaceful trade, not sea conquest, so the third is wrong.

**26. (A)** The oldest mosque, raised where Arab traders settled, is concrete proof that Islam came early and peacefully by trade, not solely by the later invasions. It was not the fruit of conquest, refuting the first. It was built by Arab Muslim settlers, not by the African-origin Siddis, so the second is false. And the Parsis kept their Zoroastrian faith rather than adopting Islam, so the third is simply untrue.

**27. (C)** The chapter keeps these as two distinct events, with the peaceful 7th-century trading arrival coming centuries before the later invasions. They are not one event, so the first distractor is false. The peaceful arrival actually disproves 'spread only by conquest', refuting the second. And it shows Islam DID spread by trade, contradicting the third.

**28. (B)** The Mappila are the Kerala Muslim community that formed as Arab merchants settled and married locally, an opportunity-driven trade arrival that produced a lasting community. The Tibetan-refugee shelter, the enslaved African Siddis and the Polish-children rescue are three separate stories about entirely different communities, so each remaining option describes a different group's history.

**29. (A)** The Arab merchants who settled and the Mappila who grew from them show a community forming peacefully through trade and intermarriage, the opposite of 'by force'. So they disprove 'spread only by conquest', refuting the first. They also show trade CAN create lasting communities, contradicting the second. And the Arab merchants came freely for opportunity, not enslaved like the Siddis, so the third is false.

**30. (C)** The Mappila grew from free Arab traders who settled and intermarried, whereas the Siddis were brought to India enslaved, a fundamental contrast of choice. The first distractor reverses the two. Neither began as a conquering army, refuting the second. And neither is described as fleeing religious persecution; that motive fits the Parsis and Jews, so the third is wrong.

**31. (A)** The Siddis stand out for arriving enslaved, by no voluntary path, unlike the refuge-seekers and traders. Keeping one's original faith was true of many communities, such as the Jews and Parsis, so the first is not unique to them. An ancient faith also fits the Zoroastrians and others, refuting the second. And marrying into local families describes the Arab merchants and the Mappila, not the enslaved Siddis, so the third is misattributed.

**32. (B)** The Siddis are of African origin and were brought to India enslaved over a long span from about the 7th to the 19th centuries. Tibetan refugees of the 1950s, Armenian traders and Polish wartime children are three separate communities, each with a different origin and a different reason for being in India, so none of the remaining options describes the Siddis.

**33. (A)** A scheduled tribe is a community the Indian Constitution recognises for special support and protection, the Siddis' modern status. It is the opposite of a ban on settling, since the recognition treats them as citizens deserving help, refuting the first. They are an Indian community of African descent, not a foreign trading guild, so the second is false. And the status confers protective support, not ruling power, so the third is wrong.

**34. (A)** The Siddis began enslaved yet are now a scheduled tribe with constitutional protection, showing India eventually brought even an involuntarily-arrived community into society's protections. This contradicts the idea that only wealthy traders were welcome, so the first is false. It contradicts expelling the involuntarily-arrived, refuting the second. And it contradicts keeping such communities permanently outside; the opposite happened, so the third is wrong.

**35. (B)** Both the Armenians and the Arab merchants came for opportunity through trade and settled, the Armenians even building churches. Neither fled persecution as refugees; that fits the Parsis, Jews and Tibetans, so the first is false. Neither was enslaved like the Siddis, refuting the second. And neither was a 20th-century war refugee; that describes the Polish children and the Tibetans, so the third is wrong.

**36. (A)** The Armenians built churches while trading and settling, showing even opportunity-seekers kept their faith and identity while joining Indian society, the same pattern as the refugee communities. They did not trade away their religion, refuting the first. Building churches signals lasting roots, not their absence, so the second is false. And trading and settling is the opposite of conquering, so the third is wrong.

**37. (B)** The Baha'is, who also came seeking acceptance, show India's openness continuing into modern times and embracing diverse faiths. Acceptance did not end with the ancient communities, so the first is false. It was not limited to traders, since the Baha'is and Tibetans were not traders, refuting the second. And it spanned many religions, from Jews and Christians to Zoroastrians, Muslims and Baha'is, so the third is wrong.

**38. (A)** The Baha'is and the Tibetan refugees are the chapter's clearest modern examples of continuing acceptance and refuge. The Cochin Jews and Syriac Christians are ancient communities, so the first pair misses the 'modern' point. The Parsis (ancient refuge) and Arab merchants (early trade) are also old arrivals, refuting the second. And the Siddis' enslaved arrival spanned earlier centuries while the Armenians were earlier traders, so the third pair does not make the modern point.

**39. (C)** The 'Good Maharaja' sheltered Polish children fleeing wartime danger, a clear act of giving refuge. He did not build India's oldest mosque; that traces to the Arab merchants, so the first is false. He did not found Zoroastrianism; the prophet Zarathushtra did, long before, refuting the second. And recognising the Siddis as a scheduled tribe is a modern constitutional act, not the Maharaja's deed, so the third is wrong.

**40. (C)** The Polish children fled wartime danger and were sheltered, so they were refugees. They sought safety, not trade profit, so the opportunity label in the first is wrong. They were sheltered out of compassion, not brought enslaved like the Siddis, refuting the second. And they came from far away during the war, so they were certainly not original inhabitants, making the third false.

**41. (D)** Both the Polish children and the Tibetan refugees are instances of India sheltering people fleeing danger, the same value of refuge. The Arab merchants came for trade, an opportunity case, so the first link fails. The Siddis were enslaved, a wholly different arrival, refuting the second. And church-building belongs to the Armenians' trade-and-identity story, not to giving refuge, so the third is off-point.

**42. (C)** Sowa Rigpa joined India's AYUSH programme, showing a refugee community enriching Indian knowledge. It was added alongside, not in place of, India's older systems, so the first is false. Being included in AYUSH means it was shared, not kept secret, refuting the second. And it was preserved and nationally recognised rather than abandoned, so the third is wrong.

**43. (C)** Sowa Rigpa entering AYUSH proves refugees can enrich the host culture, refuting the idea that they add nothing or weaken it. The chapter affirms, rather than denies, that India sheltered refugees, so the first is not the misconception addressed. The point is not to settle whether Sowa Rigpa is a religion; it is a medical system, so the second is beside the point. And the Tibetans came for refuge, not trade, making the third a separate matter.

**44. (C)** The Tibetans were given refuge and then contributed Sowa Rigpa to Indian medicine, combining refuge with cultural enrichment. India did not admit only profitable traders; it sheltered refugees who brought no trade goods, refuting the first. Refugees did not have to surrender their knowledge; they shared it through AYUSH, so the second is false. And the example shows enrichment, not weakening, contradicting the third.

**45. (B)** Persecution is hostility and ill-treatment, often for religious or other reasons, exactly what many refugees fled. It is the opposite of a warm welcome, so the first is false. It is not a trade agreement, refuting the second. And shelter offered to the endangered is refuge or asylum, which is what persecuted people SEEK, not the persecution itself, so the third confuses the two ends of the refugee's story.

**46. (D)** A refugee flees persecution (ill-treatment) and seeks refuge (shelter), the correct cause-and-response. Reversing them, fleeing shelter to seek ill-treatment, makes no sense, so the first is false. They are opposites, not synonyms, refuting the second. And persecution is the danger fled, not the shelter given; calling persecution 'the shelter' in the third confuses the two ends of the journey.

**47. (B)** 'Cosmopolitan' means at home with many cultures, a 'citizen of the world', exactly the openness the chapter describes. India was not selective by wealth, sheltering refugees and enslaved-origin communities too, so the first is false. It did not erase newcomers' cultures; they kept their identities, refuting the second. And clinging to a single culture is the closed attitude the chapter argues against, so the third is the opposite of cosmopolitan.

**48. (C)** Tolerance is the attitude of accepting and respecting beliefs different from one's own, while pluralism is the social condition of many communities actually coexisting, an attitude versus a state of society. Both are positive, not about rejection or domination, so the first is false. Neither concerns medicine or worship, refuting the second. And leaving or entering a country is emigrate or immigrate, an unrelated pair, so the third is wrong.

**49. (C)** Ethos means the characteristic spirit or guiding values of a people, for India the spirit of acceptance the chapter describes. It is not a system of medicine, which would suggest Sowa Rigpa, so the first is false. It is not a place of worship such as a synagogue or fire temple, refuting the second. And it is not trade goods, so the third is wrong; ethos is about values, not healing, buildings or commerce.

**50. (D)** Cosmopolitan means at home with many cultures, tolerance means respecting different beliefs, and ethos means a people's guiding spirit, the only correctly matched set. The first and second distractors each shuffle these three definitions onto the wrong words. And the third assigns medicine, worship and trade, none of which matches any of the three social terms.

**51. (B)** Building synagogues, fire temples and churches while becoming part of Indian life is identity-preserving integration; keeping their identities did not mean staying apart. The buildings prove lasting settlement, so 'refused to settle' in the first is false. Keeping these houses of worship is the opposite of giving up their faiths, refuting the second. And it has nothing to do with ruling the older inhabitants; the theme is coexistence, not domination, so the third is wrong.

**52. (B)** The chapter's balanced claim is identity-preserving integration: newcomers joined Indian society yet kept their faiths, languages and customs, neither total assimilation nor total separation. Full assimilation, losing all origins, overshoots one way, so the first is false. Complete separation overshoots the other, refuting the second. And taking control of India misreads coexistence as conquest, so the third is wrong; the middle path is the chapter's actual position.

**53. (B)** Surviving synagogues, fire temples, churches and languages are the chapter's clearest proof that newcomers kept their identities while living within Indian society. They were not handed separate territories to rule, so the first is false. Adopting one common religion and language would be LOSING their identities, refuting the second. And they settled permanently rather than leaving when questioned, so the third is wrong.

**54. (A)** The chapter argues diversity enriched Indian culture, adding foods, words, crafts, music and faiths, directly refuting the 'weakened' claim. It did not strip away older culture; it added to it, so the first is false. It did not leave culture unchanged; the borrowings are visible changes, refuting the second. And it did not make communities identical; distinct communities coexisted while contributing, so the third is wrong.

**55. (D)** Everyday borrowed foods, words, crafts and music show that mixing communities enriches a culture rather than impoverishing it. These contributions became part of Indian life rather than destroying it, so the first is false. They are visible changes, not an unchanged culture, refuting the second. And the borrowings show that language, cuisine, crafts and music crossed into India, far more than trade goods, so the third is wrong.

**56. (C)** The chapter's overall verdict is that diversity is a source of strength and richness, in keeping with vasudhaiva kutumbakam. It is not a weakness that left India vulnerable; the evidence shows enrichment, so the first is false. It is not a problem to be solved by removing minorities; pluralism preserves them, refuting the second. And it is certainly not unimportant; diversity is the chapter's central, valued theme, so the third is wrong.

**57. (B)** Being attacked for one's faith is persecution, and the shelter she was given is refuge, so she fled persecution and received refuge. Reversing them, fleeing shelter and receiving ill-treatment, makes no sense, so the first is false. Tolerance and pluralism are positive values of the host society, not things one flees or receives as shelter, refuting the second. And an ethos is a guiding spirit while a synagogue is a Jewish house of worship; neither labels what she fled or the shelter she got, so the third is wrong.

**58. (B)** The Parsis fled persecution, and the Polish children and Tibetans fled danger, so all three came seeking refuge. None came for trade, so the opportunity label in the first is false. None was brought enslaved; that path fits only the Siddis, refuting the second. And the Parsis are an ancient arrival while only the Polish children and Tibetans are modern, so 'all in the 20th century' in the third is wrong.

**59. (A)** The Jews fled persecution and the Tibetans fled danger (refuge), the Armenians traded (opportunity), and the Siddis were brought enslaved, the only fully correct sort. 'All four for trade' ignores the refugees and the enslaved Siddis, so the first is false. The second misfiles the Armenians as refugees and the Siddis and Tibetans as traders. And labelling the Jews and Armenians as enslaved is plainly wrong, since that path belongs only to the Siddis, so the third is false.

**60. (B)** All three claims are wrong, so the count is none. Not everyone came to trade; the Parsis, Jews and Tibetans fled persecution or danger, and the Siddis were brought enslaved. Refugees did not lose their identities; they kept synagogues, fire temples, churches and languages. And diversity did not weaken the culture; it enriched it with foods, words, crafts, music and faiths. Each 'only this one is correct' option still endorses a refuted misconception, so all three distractors are false.
