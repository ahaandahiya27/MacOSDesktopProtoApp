Worked Solutions

1. (A) The Harappans were skilled in copper and bronze but did not work iron; iron tools became common only in the later, Ganga-plains phase. So an iron plough is an anachronism for the First Urbanisation. Planned drainage, a script and standardised weights are all genuine, well-attested features of Harappan cities, so none of those is the odd one out.

2. (C) After the Harappan urban order collapsed, urban life was largely absent for about a thousand years and people returned to village life, losing large-scale trade, writing and administration. It was not a continuous city life, nor a brief hundred-year gap, nor a flourishing of iron cities — those cities of the second wave appeared only at the END of this long non-urban stretch.

3. (B) The second wave grew up in the Ganga plains, about a thousand years after the Harappan collapse, with its own new elements — iron tools, punch-marked coins, fresh capitals. Calling it a 'revival' would wrongly imply the same people resumed the same Indus cities; in fact it was a separate, later development in a new region, so 'Second Urbanisation' fits better.

4. (C) An 'urban order' is the whole working system — trade, writing, administration — not just the physical structures. The strongest evidence is that all of these faded and people reverted to villages. The claim is not supported by saying life simply continued, or that bigger cities or iron appeared at once; those would actually contradict a loss of urban order.

5. (C) When independent lines of evidence — physical remains and ancient books — point the same way, the reconstruction is much more trustworthy. Neither source replaces the other: texts cannot substitute for digging, and excavated objects do not record spoken words. And the texts of this period were not written by the much earlier Harappans, whose script remains undeciphered.

6. (B) The Harappan civilisation came first; long after its collapse, settled clans formed janapadas; these merged into the larger mahaajanapadas; and the mahaajanapadas in turn gave way to the first empires. Janapadas must precede the mahaajanapadas (the larger states grew out of them), and empires come after the mahaajanapadas, not before — which rules out the other orders.

7. (C) 'Where the people set foot' points to a place a community settled and identified with — a clan's home territory. The image of a foot does NOT mean a moving army (the whole point is that they stopped moving and settled), nor a coin, nor a sprawling empire, which is far larger than a single clan's land.

8. (A) A state needs settled people, a defined territory and an organising authority — and janapadas had all three: clans tied to land and led by a rajaa. They did have a leader, so it is wrong to say they had none; they were not Harappan creations; and coins (silver, not iron) belong to a later stage, not the origin of janapadas.

9. (B) The bigger states formed by amalgamation were the mahaajanapadas ('great janapadas'), and the best-known list names sixteen. 'Ganas' and 'sanghas' are not the general term for these states — they describe a particular non-monarchical TYPE among them — and 'janapada' is the smaller earlier unit, not the larger merged one.

10. (D) Rich alluvial soil, access to iron, and river-and-route connectivity together let surplus food feed cities and armies — exactly what states need. The plains were neither cold-and-dry nor riverless (rivers were a key advantage), and the Harappan heartland lay in the northwest, not the Ganga plains, so it was not Harappan capitals that seeded these states.

11. (C) A moat is a deep, wide, water-filled ditch placed before the walls so enemies cannot easily approach — a defensive feature, paired with the ramparts. It was not built as a reservoir for festivals, nor a garden boundary, nor a marketplace; those would not explain why it sits OUTSIDE the fortification ring.

12. (D) A narrow gate funnels traffic, so a small guard force can watch and regulate movement — useful for security and for collecting tolls or taxes. It is not about drainage, appearance, or letting large animals pass abreast (a wide gate would do that); the controlling-access reason is the one tied to how a fortified state manages its capital.

13. (D) If a site is occupied continuously for 2,500 years, the safest conclusion is that its location had enduring advantages — fertile surroundings, river access, trade position. Continuity does NOT mean these cities are frozen and unchanged, nor that the much earlier Harappans founded them, nor that they lacked walls (in fact they were fortified mahaajanapada capitals).

14. (C) The existence of advisory assemblies shows that a good rajaa governed WITH counsel rather than purely by his own will. It does not mean the king was powerless (he still ruled), nor that he was annually elected (kingship was hereditary in monarchies), nor that kings were absent — the assemblies advised the king, they did not abolish him.

15. (C) 'Hereditary' means power is inherited by birth, so the crown usually went to the king's son or kin. That is exactly what distinguishes a monarchy from a gana/sangha, where the ASSEMBLY chose the ruler. It was not decided by an annual vote, nor handed to the richest trader, nor automatically to a foreign conqueror.

16. (D) In a gana/sangha, major decisions — including who would be rajaa — lay with the assembly, settled by debate and a vote if necessary. That is the very feature that makes it a republic rather than a monarchy, where succession was by birth (eldest son). Priestly or foreign-imposed selection is not how these early republics chose their leaders.

17. (D) What makes a state a 'republic' is that authority rests with an assembly that decides and chooses the ruler, rather than a king inheriting the throne. Size, army strength and the use of coins were shared with many monarchies and do not define a republic; the decision-making power of the assembly is the defining trait of Vajji and Malla.

18. (A) The accurate picture is a majority of monarchies plus at least two republics (Vajji and Malla). So the blanket claim is wrong because of those exceptions — but it is also wrong to swing to the opposite extreme and say none had kings, since most DID. The mahaajanapadas were not defined by being southern, so that qualifier is irrelevant.

19. (B) These republics gave decision-making to assemblies of clan and family heads — broad for their time, but not the one-person-one-vote inclusion of a modern democracy. They certainly HAD assemblies (that is their hallmark), their rulers were NOT chosen purely by birth (that would make them monarchies), and decision-making was not restricted to women — so 'early' rather than 'modern' is the right qualification.

20. (D) The Harappans worked copper and bronze; iron became widespread only later and powered the Ganga-plains cities. So the claim that the Harappans used iron is reversed and wrong. Neither wave relied on gold tools, and coins (when they came) were silver punch-marked pieces, not iron — making the copper/bronze-versus-iron contrast the correct one.

21. (D) Iron's importance is practical and agricultural: tougher axes and ploughshares opened forested, heavy land, increasing harvests that could support non-farming city dwellers and soldiers — the foundation of states. Iron was a working metal, not an object of worship; it did not abolish trade (which in fact grew); and books were not printed in this period.

22. (A) Better iron weapons made armies more effective, which helped powerful states defeat and absorb rivals, producing larger kingdoms — a step toward empires. It did not end warfare (it intensified it), did not end farming (which actually expanded with iron ploughs), and writing's appearance is a separate matter, not a result of iron weapons.

23. (B) They are called 'punch-marked' because marks were punched onto the metal; they were silver, not shells or poured gold. The name has nothing to do with boxing prizes. Cowrie shells were a different, earlier medium of exchange, and casting is not the method these particular coins were made by.

24. (C) Barter's awkwardness is the 'double coincidence of wants' — both sides must want what the other offers — which coins neatly avoid by acting as a common measure of value. Barter was not limited to night-time, did not depend on banks, and did not require iron tools; coins simply made exchange easier than swapping goods directly.

25. (A) Coins turning up far from their origin is the natural signature of long-distance trade carrying them along the routes; regions used neighbours' coins and some even reached abroad. It is not evidence of forgery, nor of a single shared coin (each region usually made its own), nor of coins being confined to one city — that would contradict the very find being explained.

26. (B) A jaati was a community defined by occupation, with skills and customs handed down within families. It is not a defensive feature (that is a moat), not a travel route (that is a patha), and not a coin. The varna-jaati framework was about social and occupational organisation, not fortification, transport or money.

27. (A) Kshatriyas were the varna associated with protection and warfare. Brahmins were linked to knowledge and rituals (not battle), Vaishyas with trade and agriculture (not rituals), and Shudras with service occupations (not ruling as warriors). Only the Kshatriya-warrior pairing matches the roles described.

28. (C) The chapter stresses that in this early phase the system was more flexible, with movement between occupations possible. It did exist (so denying it is wrong), it belongs to the Ganga-plains age and not the much earlier Harappans, and it was not instantly rigid — that very rigidity came later, which is exactly the misconception being corrected.

29. (D) 'Uttara' means north and 'dakshina' means south, so the Uttaraapatha was the northern route and the Dakshinaapatha headed south — the names themselves give the directions away. They were not reversed, were not confined to a coastal east-west line, and spanned many regions rather than lying inside one mahaajanapada.

30. (D) To go from the northern plains into the peninsula, the southern route crossed the Vindhyas, the dividing range between north and south India. The Himalayas lie far to the north (the wrong direction), the Aravallis run through the northwest, and the Western Ghats edge the western coast — none of these is the range a route from Kaushambi southward would cross.

31. (C) The routes were arteries for people and ideas as well as merchandise — pilgrims, armies, beliefs and coins all travelled along them, spreading culture. They were not limited to royal letters, not only to soldiers, and not merely to iron ore; it was precisely this mix of people, goods and ideas that helped unify the regions.

32. (D) The southern trio were the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas. Magadha, Kosala and Avanti were powerful NORTHERN mahaajanapadas; Vajji, Malla and Anga also lay in the north/east; and Kuru, Panchaala and Matsya were northern janapadas/mahaajanapadas too — only the Chola-Chera-Pandya set is southern.

33. (C) The south's prized resources — gems, gold, spices — pulled it into the trade networks of the wider land. A cold climate is neither accurate nor a draw for trade, the absence of rivers would hinder not help, and the south's coastal position (closeness to the sea) actually aided maritime trade rather than cutting it off.

34. (B) By this date trade, coins and ideas had linked the regions into a single connected land, with influence spilling beyond India's borders. It was not depopulated, not physically fragmented into islands, and certainly not restored to Harappan rule — the Harappan civilisation had ended a thousand years earlier.

35. (B) As the land integrated, the mahaajanapadas gave way to the early empires — the next, larger stage of state formation. They were not newly founded then (that was centuries earlier), did not all turn into republics, and did not migrate to the Indus valley; their era was ending, not beginning.

36. (A) The driving sequence runs from iron, to cleared land, to surplus food, to cities and states — each step enabling the next. Coins came AFTER barter and helped trade grow rather than stop it; cities growing did not regrow forests or erase iron; and empires came LAST, after janapadas and settled clans, not before them.

37. (B) Magadha's strength flowed from its fertile Ganga location: rich harvests, river routes for movement and trade, and access to resources like iron — the very ingredients the chapter ties to powerful states. It was not riverless (rivers were an asset, not a defensive gap), was a monarchy rather than a republic, and lay in the north, not the southern Pandya country.

38. (D) The clearest support is that cities founded in this second wave, like Varanasi, are still alive and busy after roughly 2,500 years — an unbroken thread. The Harappan (First-Urbanisation) cities are NOT still inhabited, iron remains in use, and barter is no longer the main form of trade — so none of those supports the claim.

39. (A) The essential contrast is WHO decides: a single inheriting king in the monarchy versus a decision-making assembly that also picks the ruler in the republic. Both relied on farming, both maintained armies, and both belonged to the iron age — so the farming, army and metal claims fail to capture the real difference, which is the locus of political power.

40. (A) Since iron tools enabled the land-clearing and farming that produced the surplus behind the cities, removing iron would have starved that surplus and weakened the urban wave. Without iron, cities would grow SLOWER, not faster; the Harappan collapse had already happened long before and is unrelated; and there is no reason coins would appear sooner — the whole money economy rested on the trade that iron-fed growth made possible.

41. (A) The Second Urbanisation itself disproves the claim: after roughly a thousand years without cities, urban life reappeared in a new region. The refutation is NOT that the Harappan cities survived (they were indeed lost), nor that there was no gap (the gap is well attested), nor a sweeping claim of cities since the Stone Age — the point is precisely that cities can be lost AND can later re-emerge.

42. (C) The prefix 'maha-' (great) marks the difference in scale: mahaajanapadas were the bigger states into which the earlier, smaller janapadas merged. Neither word names a coin or a route, the janapada came BEFORE (not after) the mahaajanapada, and being a republic or monarchy is not what separates the two terms — size and stage of development are.

43. (B) Walls, tax collection and armies require an authority able to gather and direct resources — the hallmarks of organised states. They are not the marks of rootless wanderers or of stateless religious camps, and they were new developments of the Ganga age, not copies of the earlier Harappan cities, whose order had long since collapsed.

44. (D) Taxes funded the costly business of running a state — soldiers, walls, officials. They did not replace farming (taxes were often levied ON farm produce), did not aim to choke trade (states benefited from trade), and certainly did not abolish rulers — collecting revenue is one of the very things a ruler did.

45. (B) An iron plough lets a farmer break tougher soil and grow more, generating the surplus that supports town-dwellers — the engine of urban growth. Harvests rise rather than fall, more surplus means MORE trade not less, and a larger, settled, taxed population needs rulers and administration more than ever — so the other chains run backwards.

46. (B) The republics spread decision-making across the heads of leading families — broader than one king's will, yet not the universal participation of a modern democracy. They did not give every adult an equal vote, did not concentrate everything in one rich individual, and were not run by foreigners — they sat between absolute monarchy and full democracy.

47. (A) Coins exist to make exchange easier, so their widespread use is direct evidence that commerce had expanded beyond what barter could handle. They were not primarily ornaments, not made merely as grave goods, and their rise marks a move AWAY from barter, not toward it — so only the trade-growth reading fits.

48. (B) Shishupalgarh, in the east with its square fortified plan, was the capital associated with Kalinga. Gandhaara lay in the far northwest, Avanti in central-western India, and Magadha (in Bihar) had its own capitals such as Rajagriha — none of those matches an eastern, square-planned site.

49. (A) Walls, water-ditches and pinch-point gates are a coherent defensive-and-control package, exactly what a wealthy state with neighbouring rivals would build. Beauty, water storage and free movement are each contradicted by at least one feature — narrow gates restrict movement, and the whole set is about security, not decoration or rainwater.

50. (C) The sixteen were large states existing around the same era and spread across a broad region, from the northwest to the east. They were not a sequence of empires one after another, not a knot of villages in one valley, and not aliases for one kingdom — they were many distinct, sizeable, contemporaneous states.

51. (B) Coins travelling far from home is the natural result of long-distance trade moving them along the routes, and one explanation covers both scattered finds. It is not that each coin was minted where found, not that all regions used a single shared coin (each usually struck its own), and not that coins were confined to their region — that would make the finds impossible.

52. (D) The developments are interlinked: agricultural surplus underpinned cities and states, the resulting trade called for coins, and each fed back into the others — a single web of cause and effect. They were roughly contemporary (not spread across separate millennia), surplus farming preceded and enabled coins (not the reverse), and all unfolded in the same land.

53. (D) In a monarchy the rajaa held power yet was supposed to take counsel from the sabhaa and samiti and from ministers — shared advice, not shared rule. The assemblies did not govern in his place (that would be a republic), they were not powerless ornaments, and they did not annually elect the king, whose office was hereditary.

54. (C) Older Vedic words for assemblies alongside later republican terms imply a continuous, developing tradition of collective decision-making. It does not mean the Vedas postdate the mahaajanapadas, nor that assemblies sprang up from nowhere (the Vedic terms show earlier roots), and gana/sangha denote republics, the opposite of a hereditary kingship.

55. (A) Fertile soil and iron give surplus, but the chapter ties powerful states to connectivity too — trade carried goods, coins and ideas that strengthened them. Cut off from routes, such a region would lose that advantage and likely stay weaker. Isolation is not portrayed as protective, trade did matter, and nothing guarantees empire from soil and iron alone.

56. (A) Coins appeared in the LATER, Ganga-plains wave; the Harappans had no coinage at all, so dating a coin to the Harappan period is the error. The Harappans did not use gold coins either, coins certainly were used in ancient India (just later), and punch-marked coins were silver, not iron — so only the urbanisation-dating correction is right.

57. (C) The Second Urbanisation took root where its enabling conditions converged — the fertile, iron-rich, route-linked Ganga plains — rather than reviving the distant, long-collapsed Indus cities. The Ganga plains were intensely farmed (not farmless), and iron was central there (not absent), so the rebuild-the-Indus and no-iron explanations both fail; the convergence of new conditions in a new region is the key.

58. (D) 'Necessary but not sufficient' means iron was required yet not enough on its own — it had to act together with rich soil, trade links and ruling authority to yield cities and states. Saying iron alone instantly built cities is the opposite (claiming sufficiency); saying iron was absent denies its necessity; and saying cities preceded iron contradicts the chapter's sequence in which iron came first and enabled the growth.

59. (B) The sixteen is the best-known traditional list, but ancient texts grouped states in their own ways, so it is a useful convention rather than a precise modern headcount — hence a little caution. The number was not certainly a hundred, the mahaajanapadas were real states, and the list comes from the later textual tradition, not from the much earlier Harappans.

60. (A) Larger states formed as more powerful janapadas, backed by iron arms and surplus wealth, defeated or absorbed weaker ones into bigger mahaajanapadas. The Harappans had long since vanished and played no part; the janapadas did not dissolve from loss of interest in land (clans were strongly tied to their territory); and the direction of change was small-to-large, not empires splitting states into janapadas.
