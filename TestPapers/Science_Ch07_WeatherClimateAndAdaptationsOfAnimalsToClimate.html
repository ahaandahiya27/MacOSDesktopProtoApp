Worked Solutions

1. (C) "Tomorrow … 32 °C with light rain in the evening" describes the day-to-day state of the atmosphere — that is weather. Climate would be the long-term average; altitude and humidity-only are red herrings.

2. (B) Weather is the short-term, day-to-day state of the atmosphere; climate is its average taken over many years. Option (A) reverses the two; (C) wrongly equates them; (D) is false — both are measured.

3. (A) By convention, climate is the average weather taken over a long period, usually at least 25 years (often quoted as ~30). A week, month or single year is far too short to smooth out year-to-year weather variation.

4. (D) A single day's snowfall is one weather event; climate is the long-term average, so one cold day cannot establish that the climate has changed. (A) and (B) wrongly treat one day as climate; (C) confuses weather's daily change with climate.

5. (B) A pattern that repeats year after year (heavy June–September rain) is part of the long-term average — Mumbai's climate. One day's rain would be weather; altitude and wind speed are single factors, not the overall pattern.

6. (A) The scientific study of weather is meteorology (its practitioners are meteorologists). Geology is rocks/Earth, astronomy is space, biology is living things.

7. (D) Weather can change within hours. Climate (A) changes only over decades; altitude (B) and latitude (C) of a place are essentially fixed.

8. (A) Comparing Chennai (warm, humid all year) with Delhi (hot summers, cold winters) over the long run is a comparison of climate. The other options describe single-moment weather details, not the long-term pattern.

9. (B) Crop choices for a whole season rest on the region's typical long-term conditions — its climate — not on the weather of the single day of sowing, nor soil colour or noon wind speed.

10. (B) Altitude and distance from the sea are genuine climate-shaping factors (higher = cooler; coastal = milder). House colour, car count, weekday and one afternoon's cloud cover do not determine a region's climate.

11. (C) Temperature falls with height (about 6.5 °C per km), so a high-altitude town like Shimla is cooler than the nearby plains. Humidity, wind speed and one day's rainfall do not explain a steady year-round coolness.

12. (C) The sea heats and cools slowly, so it moderates the air temperature of nearby land, reducing the gap between hot and cold extremes. It does not raise altitude, block all sunlight, or remove all humidity (in fact coasts are more humid).

13. (D) As altitude increases, temperature generally decreases. So the answer is "decreases"; it does not rise, stay constant, or behave like (C).

14. (A) The Indian monsoon winds pick up huge amounts of moisture from the warm Indian Ocean and bring rain over land. The Thar Desert and dry plateau are dry sources; Himalayan snow does not feed the monsoon rains.

15. (C) Because the sea moderates temperature and adds moisture, the coastal town P has smaller day-to-night temperature swings and higher humidity than inland Q. (A) reverses this; coasts still get rain (B is false); being on the coast does not mean higher altitude (D).

16. (B) The Sun is the ultimate source of the energy that heats air, land and sea, drives winds and the water cycle — i.e. almost all weather. The Moon, wind and ocean currents are not the original energy source (wind and currents are themselves Sun-driven).

17. (D) Weather is hard to forecast far ahead because the atmosphere is a constantly changing system with many interacting factors, so small differences grow quickly. (A), (B) and (C) are simply false statements.

18. (A) Through a normal day the air is usually coolest just before sunrise, after the ground has lost heat all night, and warmest in the early afternoon. Noon and "exactly midnight, always" are traps; early afternoon is the hottest, not the coolest.

19. (C) Day-to-day weather forecasts are reliable only for roughly the next few days; they cannot be trusted for ten or a hundred years (that is the realm of climate projections, which work on averages, not daily detail).

20. (D) Even though the Sun is highest at noon, the ground keeps absorbing more heat than it loses for a while afterwards, so the air keeps warming and peaks in the early afternoon (thermal lag). The Sun's distance, the Moon, and "humidity falling to zero" are false explanations.

21. (D) A maximum–minimum thermometer records both the highest and the lowest temperatures reached during a day. A rain gauge measures rain, an anemometer measures wind speed, a barometer measures air pressure.

22. (B) Rainfall is measured with a rain gauge, which collects rain in a graduated container and reports it in millimetres. The other instruments measure temperature, mass, or length — not rainfall.

23. (D) Total rainfall = 25 + 15 = 40 mm. (10 mm subtracts instead of adds; 20 mm averages; 375 = 25 × 15 multiplies — all traps.)

24. (B) The day's "maximum temperature" is simply the highest temperature reached during that day — not an average, not the midnight value, and certainly not the minimum.

25. (C) Humidity (relative humidity) is the amount of moisture in the air as a percentage of the maximum the air can hold, so it is reported as a percentage. Millimetres are for rain, °C for temperature, km/h for wind.

26. (A) The gap between the day's highest and lowest temperatures is the temperature range (max − min). Humidity, rainfall and wind speed are different quantities.

27. (C) Temperature range = maximum − minimum = 34 − 22 = 12 °C. (56 adds the two; 22 and 28 just copy single readings — traps.)

28. (D) A rain gauge measures only rainfall (depth of collected water, in mm). It does NOT measure air temperature — that needs a thermometer. The other three options all describe what a rain gauge does measure.

29. (D) Compute each day's range (max − min): Mon 30−22 = 8, Tue 31−23 = 8, Wed 33−24 = 9, Thu 36−22 = 14, Fri 34−25 = 9. Thursday's range (14) is the largest. (Picking the highest max alone, or assuming all equal, are traps.)

30. (B) The highest maximum temperature in the row 30, 31, 33, 36, 34 is 36 °C, recorded on Thursday. (Don't confuse "highest maximum" with "largest range" from the previous question — both happen to fall on Thursday here, but for different reasons.)

31. (D) Total weekly rainfall = 0 + 12 + 0 + 30 + 8 = 50 mm. (30 takes only the wettest day; 42 forgets the 8; 10 is unrelated — traps.)

32. (D) The heaviest single day is the largest value, 30 mm on Thursday. Tuesday (12) and Friday (8) are smaller; Monday is 0.

33. (B) Reading the table, humidity is 90% at 06:00, 55% at noon, 70% at 18:00. The lowest value, 55%, is at noon — air is often driest in the warm middle of the day. It did not stay constant.

34. (B) "How hot it got" is the highest temperature reached, i.e. the maximum temperature, 28 °C. Humidity is moisture, rainfall is rain depth, and the minimum (18 °C) tells how cool it got, not how hot.

35. (A) Average = (30 + 32 + 34 + 36) ÷ 4 = 132 ÷ 4 = 33 °C. (36 is just the highest, not the average; 30 the lowest; 132 forgets to divide — traps.)

36. (C) Hot all year + very heavy rainfall + dense forest is the hot-and-wet tropical (rainforest) climate. A desert is hot-and-dry, polar is very cold, and cold-and-dry does not fit "hot" and "heavy rain".

37. (B) Polar regions are marked by extreme cold and ice cover for most of the year. Heavy rain/rainforest, hot sand dunes and "mild all year" describe other climates.

38. (B) Very high daytime temperature with almost no rainfall is the hot-and-dry (desert) climate. Polar is cold, tropical rainforest is wet, and cold-and-humid does not fit "very hot, no rain".

39. (A) Tropical rainforests, with warmth and abundant water all year, support the greatest variety (biodiversity) of life. Polar ice, hot sandy deserts and high snowfields are comparatively species-poor.

40. (C) A tropical rainforest climate combines high temperature with heavy rainfall. The cold/snow options and the "high temperature but almost no rain" (that is desert) options are wrong.

41. (C) Blubber is a thick fat layer that insulates the polar bear against extreme cold and stores energy for lean times. It is not mainly for floating, running speed, or attracting a mate.

42. (B) White fur lets the polar bear blend with the snow and ice so it can approach prey unseen (camouflage). It does not keep it cool, make it easy to see, or reflect away its own body heat.

43. (A) Small ears and a short tail mean less exposed surface area, so the bear loses less body heat in the cold. (D) is the opposite (and wrong); hearing in storms and "swimming straight" are not the reason.

44. (D) Huddling together puts the birds' warm bodies close, cutting the exposed surface area of each and reducing heat loss, so the group stays warmer. It is not for catching fish, flying, or cooling down (it is already freezing).

45. (C) Wide, large paws spread the bear's weight so it does not sink into soft snow and help it grip slippery ice. They are not for burrowing, tree-climbing, or flying.

46. (C) A polar animal is best served by thick fur, small ears, white colour and a fat layer — all reduce heat loss and aid camouflage. The other combinations (large ears, dark/bright colour, thin skin, green stripes) would lose heat or fail to camouflage in snow.

47. (A) Long strong limbs and a grasping tail let rainforest monkeys climb and swing among the trees where they live and feed. Warmth, fat storage and snow camouflage are cold-climate needs, irrelevant in a hot wet forest.

48. (A) Bright green colouring lets a tree frog or insect blend in with the green leaves (camouflage from predators). It is not for keeping warm, glowing in the dark, or floating.

49. (B) The toucan's large light beak lets it reach fruit on thin branches that could not hold its weight, and handle the fruit. It is not for polar winters, sand-burrowing, or underwater fishing.

50. (C) The lion-tailed macaque is a monkey of the tropical rainforests of the Western Ghats in India. It does not live in polar caps, deserts, or snowfields.

51. (D) In dense forest, vision is blocked by foliage, so howler monkeys, parrots and others rely on loud calls and sounds to communicate over distance. "Body heat", "winter fur" and "fat layers" are not communication methods.

52. (C) Migration is the seasonal long-distance travel of animals to escape harsh climate or to find food and breeding grounds. Growing winter fur, a daily colour change, or staying put in one nest are not migration.

53. (A) Siberian cranes appear in India in winter because they migrate from far colder Siberia to escape its harsh winter, returning when it warms. They are not Indian residents, do not change species, and arrive in India's winter (not summer).

54. (A) Migration is triggered chiefly by changes in temperature, day length, and food availability — the natural cues of an approaching harsh season. Leaf colour on one tree, grain prices and onlookers are not the triggers.

55. (B) Rather than endure a harsh season where it is, the migrating animal moves to a region with more favourable conditions (food, warmth, breeding sites). It does not stop eating, grow overnight, or turn invisible.

56. (A) Thick fur, a small surface area for the body's volume, and white colouring all reduce heat loss and give snow camouflage — classic polar-region adaptations. They would be useless or harmful in a desert, rainforest or warm lake.

57. (D) A monkey's grasping hands and long tail are adaptations for climbing trees in a warm, wet forest, so it is ill-suited to bare, freezing polar ice (no trees, deadly cold, no fur/fat for it). It is not adapted to cold, ocean-flight, or hot-sand burrowing.

58. (A) A rounder, compact body has a smaller surface-area-to-volume ratio, so it loses heat more slowly — an advantage in the cold. (B) is the reversed (false) version; the body shape does affect heat, and it is unrelated to height.

59. (C) Strong limbs and a grasping tail for climbing wet trees is a hot-wet tropical-forest adaptation. Blubber, white snow-fur, and small heat-saving ears are all cold-climate adaptations, the opposite of what the question asks.

60. (D) Very large, thin ears have a big surface area, which would lose heat fast — the worst feature for surviving polar cold. Thick fur, wide weight-spreading paws and a fat layer all help in the cold, so the large ears are LEAST helpful.
