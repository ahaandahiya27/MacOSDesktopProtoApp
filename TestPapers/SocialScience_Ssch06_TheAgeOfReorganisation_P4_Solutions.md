## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** The 'Dark Centuries' label is undermined by showing the age was creative and connected: flourishing art schools, trade and Sanskrit works prove vitality, not darkness. A single reunifying emperor is false (the age was defined by many competing kingdoms) and would in any case not address cultural 'darkness'. The claim that every kingdom was foreign-ruled and suppressive is false, since most powers (Shungas, Satavahanas, Tamil kingdoms) were Indian and culture flourished. Leaving behind no coins or inscriptions is the opposite of the truth, since those material remains are our chief sources for the period.

**2. (D)** The chapter presents an internal breakup as the cause, occurring before foreigners entered the weakened northwest; weakness precedes and enables the entry. The reverse claim that outside attack caused the collapse inverts the sequence. There was no joint Maurya-foreigner rule, and there is no account of the Mauryas inviting foreigners to defend the frontier; the frontier simply weakened after the empire fragmented.

**3. (C)** The argument rests purely on chronology: a cause must precede its effect, so a later invasion cannot be the cause of an earlier collapse. The claim that internal politics is 'always' more important is an overgeneralisation not made by the chapter and not what makes this particular argument valid. The invaders were a different people, not the Mauryas renamed. And the northwest had been Mauryan territory before the breakup, so that distractor is factually false.

**4. (D)** Pushyamitra was the Mauryan commander-in-chief who killed the last emperor from within — an internal coup — whereas the Indo-Greeks were outsiders who entered the weakened northwest. They were not two foreign invasions: Pushyamitra was an insider, not a foreigner. Neither involved inheritance or election; Pushyamitra seized the throne by force. And neither was a Tamil southern power moving north; the Indo-Greeks came from the northwest and the Shungas were of the heartland.

**5. (D)** The defining feature of the age is plurality: many rival kingdoms in different regions replacing the single Mauryan empire. It was not a larger single empire, nor a single foreign empire over all India, and it was certainly not stateless, since the chapter names many active kingdoms such as the Shungas, Satavahanas, Tamil kingdoms and Kushanas.

**6. (A)** When a marriage immediately converts enemies into allies, it is functioning as diplomacy through matrimony — the chapter's point about matrimonial alliances. Calling it purely private ignores the political effect just described. It is a marriage, not a sacrifice, and it builds alliance rather than extracting tribute.

**7. (D)** The ashvamedha let a king proclaim paramountcy over every land his freed horse crossed without challenge, so it parallels a public claim of supreme overlordship. It did not choose a successor, was not a marriage treaty, and did not begin a calendar — starting a dated era is the role of a samvat such as the Shaka era.

**8. (D)** A queen performing the kingship-claiming horse-sacrifice shows both that royal women could hold genuine authority and that the dynasty still kept Vedic ritual (since the rite is Vedic). Both inferences are valid. Denying the second is wrong because performing a Vedic sacrifice is direct evidence the ritual survived; denying the first is wrong because a sovereignty-claiming rite implies real power; and the record plainly concerns a ritual act, not trade.

**9. (C)** A mother-derived royal name together with a land-donating queen mother jointly point to the high standing of royal women. The name is not that of a god, and a single fact cannot establish that 'naming after gods' was a custom. 'Gautami' is a personal or lineage name, not a city. And being called 'son of Gautami' does not make the king a monk; he ruled as king.

**10. (A)** Mother-names for kings, queens endowing gifts, and a queen performing the sovereignty-claiming sacrifice all point to women holding real authority, directly refuting 'only kings held power'. Ship-coins, gold hoards and milestones are about trade routes, not women's power. The stupa, pillar and anicut concern religion, blending and irrigation. Sangam poems, the calendar and Kanishka's coins concern literature, timekeeping and royal religion — none speaks to royal women's authority.

**11. (C)** The ashvamedha embeds a political claim in its own procedure: the king claims every land the unchallenged horse crosses, making it inherently political. It was performed by several rulers, not once in all history. It was not the only rite women performed, and the point is not that women alone performed it. And it was a Vedic sacrifice requiring priests, so the 'no priests' claim is false.

**12. (B)** The pairing shows religious patronage was not exclusive: a Vedic-reviving house could still support Buddhist art. It does not prove secret conversion; the stupa was genuinely Buddhist, not a relabelled temple; and Buddhism was clearly still alive, since its monuments were being adorned at the time.

**13. (D)** The Shungas held north and central India while the Satavahanas built their power in the Deccan. The first wrong option reverses these two regions. The Tamil far south belonged to the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas and Kalinga to Kharavela. The northwest and the Central Asian steppe were the world of foreign rulers such as the Indo-Greeks and Kushanas, not the Shungas or Satavahanas.

**14. (A)** A stupa is a dome-shaped Buddhist monument, such as Bharhut, often surrounded by carved railings. A river-diverting dam is an anicut, a Tamil poetry anthology is a Sangam work, and a counted era is a samvat — none of these is a stupa, and a victory pillar (like the Heliodorus pillar) is yet another distinct thing.

**15. (C)** The Shungas are remembered for reviving Vedic ritual and patronising the Bharhut Stupa's carvings. The Grand Anicut was a Chola irrigation work on the Kaveri. The Silk Route was the Kushanas' source of wealth. Greek-portrait coins belong to the Indo-Greeks, not the Shungas.

**16. (A)** The Bharhut railing is Buddhist religious art sponsored in the north-central heartland, while Indo-Greek coins are the main source for those rulers' names and faces. Neither is Mathura red-sandstone sculpture. Only the Indo-Greeks descended from Alexander's governors; the Shungas were an indigenous dynasty, so 'both foreign' is false. And neither object is a sea-trade token for Roman pepper, which is connected with the Satavahanas and Tamil kingdoms.

**17. (D)** Ship coins from a port point to flourishing sea trade through the Satavahanas' western Deccan ports. The image is not evidence of sea-worship, nor of losing land territory, nor of a navy against the Kushanas — the Kushanas were a northern LAND power reached by no Satavahana fleet.

**18. (B)** The Satavahanas prospered through Deccan sea ports, while the Kushanas controlled the overland Silk Route between China and the Mediterranean. Saying both used the land Silk Route ignores the Satavahanas' ship-coin sea trade; saying both used the sea ignores the Kushanas' inland route; and the last option simply reverses the correct pairing.

**19. (D)** Roman gold in Indian ports shows trade, not conquest; political rule would require signs like Roman governors or troops, which are absent. The coins were not forgeries — they are genuine Roman gold paid for Indian goods. Distance did not prevent contact, since the very coins prove goods moved between the two. And Indian kings minted at home, not in Roman mints.

**20. (A)** A land-grant inscription documents a concrete act in fixed wording, making it more reliable than a flattering praise-poem that might exaggerate. Poems of the age (such as the Sangam works) were preserved, so 'never written down' is false. Inscriptions were carved by craftsmen at a ruler's order, not written in the queen's own hand. And praise-poetry was not banned; Sangam verse openly praises generous chiefs.

**21. (A)** Kharavela ruled Kalinga and was a committed Jain, hence 'monk-king' even though he kept his throne. He did not abdicate to wander, was not a Kushana official in Gandhara (Kalinga is in the east, not the northwest), and founded no new religion; he followed the existing Jain faith.

**22. (C)** Hathigumpha is an inscription — writing cut into rock — and the chief source for Kharavela, illustrating reliance on inscriptions. The age did not leave continuous year-by-year court chronicles. Roman ledgers do not supply his record, and the Sangam poems are a southern Tamil tradition, not about Kalinga in the east.

**23. (C)** Inscriptions typically record deeds, grants and events in words (as at Hathigumpha), while coins preserve names, titles and portraits (as with the Indo-Greeks and Kanishka). Both are genuine ancient objects, not forgeries. Coins are not written chronicles, nor are inscriptions picture-only. And both clearly help identify rulers, so calling them useless is false.

**24. (B)** Kharavela ruled Kalinga on the eastern coast, while the Satavahanas held the Deccan plateau. The Deccan was Satavahana, not Kharavela's, and the Tamil far south belonged to the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas. The northwest and Central Asian steppe were the world of foreign rulers, and north-central India was Shunga country, not Kharavela's or the Satavahanas'.

**25. (A)** The three crowned kingdoms of the Tamil south were the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas. The Shungas, Satavahanas and Chedis lay in the north and Deccan and the east; the Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Kushanas were northwestern foreign rulers; and the Mauryas, Nandas and Guptas were northern imperial dynasties, not Tamil kingdoms.

**26. (D)** Sangam poetry sings chiefly of love, valour and generosity. It is not a manual of Vedic sacrifice, not a chronicle of the northern Kushana emperors, and not a record of Silk-Route tolls.

**27. (B)** Praise of chiefs for generosity shows that liberal giving was a valued royal virtue. The poems would make no sense if giving were illegal; they presuppose chiefs and unequal wealth rather than equality; and Sangam verse celebrates love, valour and generosity, not Vedic sacrifice.

**28. (D)** The Pandyas were a Tamil far-southern kingdom. The Cholas were also Tamil-southern, not Deccan (the Deccan was Satavahana). Kalinga was Kharavela's, not the Cheras'. And the northwest belonged to foreign rulers, not the Pandyas.

**29. (A)** Familiarity with Tamil goods could arise simply from commerce, so it evidences trade, not rule. Roman writers described many lands Rome never conquered, so the first distractor is false. The notes do not show Tamil kings ruling in Italy, and they do not show Romans founding the southern kingdoms — these are indigenous Tamil powers.

**30. (C)** The Satavahanas used Deccan ports and the Tamils used southern ports — both relied on the sea. Neither used overland routes to Rome; the land Silk Route was the Kushanas' connection to the Mediterranean, not these two southern powers'. The first two options wrongly assign a land route to one of them.

**31. (D)** A ship-coin (sea), Roman gold from sea commerce, and a land Silk-Route milestone together show India connected outward by both sea and land. They do not show conquest by Rome or China, contradict the idea of purely internal trade, and disprove reliance on a single route, since two different kinds of route are represented.

**32. (C)** The Grand Anicut is a dam built to distribute the Kaveri's water over the delta. It is not a Buddhist stupa, not a fort against Roman ships, and not a Greek victory pillar.

**33. (A)** By irrigating the delta into fertile rice land, the anicut underpinned Chola wealth — an irrigation work, not a military one, which is why a fort would not serve the same end. It was not a fortress, did not bar ships (it is an inland dam), and was not a boundary marker with the Pandyas.

**34. (D)** An anicut is a low check-dam that lifts river water into canals; the glossary has confused it with a samvat (a counted era). A dome monument is a stupa, a marriage treaty is a matrimonial alliance, and carved writing is an inscription — none of these is an anicut.

**35. (C)** The Indo-Greeks descended from the Greek satraps and settlers left behind after Alexander's campaigns. They were not Shungas (an indigenous heartland dynasty), not Chedis of Kalinga, and not the later Kushanas, who were a separate Central Asian people.

**36. (B)** A Greek envoy dedicating a pillar to an Indian deity is a clear sign of cultural blending and even adoption of Indian belief. It contradicts the ideas that the Indo-Greeks forced Greek gods on Indians, shunned Indian religion, or destroyed Indian shrines — the pillar is the opposite of all three.

**37. (D)** Because few Indo-Greek inscriptions survive, their coins are the chief source for these rulers' names and faces. The coins were genuine trade currency, not unused tokens; they were not Rome-minted; and they certainly did not carry Tamil Sangam poetry, a southern tradition unrelated to the northwestern Indo-Greeks.

**38. (C)** The Indo-Greeks moved in because the Mauryan collapse had left the northwest frontier weak — the breakup is the underlying cause. No Chola invitation brought them, the Kushanas did not build the Silk Route to admit them, and the Shaka calendar is unrelated to their entry.

**39. (B)** A coin combining a Greek-style portrait with an Indian deity in Indian script shows the issuer blended his own conventions with Indian ones. It refutes the idea that he rejected Indian language and gods; it does not imply a single subcontinental empire; and a coin itself shows engagement with trade and money, not indifference.

**40. (B)** The chapter's central theme is that the foreign rulers were mostly absorbed, with Indian culture staying dominant. They did not wipe out Indian culture, did not remain wholly separate (Heliodorus and Gandhara art show mixing), and never ruled the whole subcontinent as one empire.

**41. (C)** The foreign rulers are treated as contributors precisely because Indian culture remained dominant and absorbed what they brought, so both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion. The reason is not false (absorption is the chapter's theme), and the assertion is not false (they were largely integrated, not erasers), so the partial and wholly-false options fail.

**42. (B)** Indian themes at the core of foreign rulers' coins and art show India absorbed and transformed the newcomers. It did not expel them by force (their objects survive and were patronised), was not fully Hellenised (Indian themes remained central), and clearly did not halt art — the art is the very evidence.

**43. (C)** The Shakas are also called Indo-Scythians. The Indo-Greeks were a separate people of Alexandrian descent; 'Indo-Romans' is not a ruling group; and 'Indo-Cholas' is not a real term, the Cholas being an indigenous Tamil dynasty.

**44. (A)** A still-used Shaka-based calendar shows the period left enduring legacies, not just ruins. It contradicts the claims that nothing survived, that Romans created it, and that the age stood apart from Indian traditions — a calendar woven into Indian life proves the opposite.

**45. (B)** A samvat is a counted calendar era beginning from a fixed point, as with the Shaka era. A river dam is an anicut, a dome shrine is a stupa, and a royal marriage is a matrimonial alliance — none of these is a samvat.

**46. (A)** Kanishka is the famous Kushana ruler. Kharavela ruled Kalinga, Pushyamitra founded the Shungas, and Gautamiputra was a Satavahana — none of them Kushana.

**47. (B)** The Kushanas grew rich by controlling the overland Silk Route between China and the Mediterranean. The Grand Anicut was a Chola irrigation work, the Bharhut railings were Shunga-era Buddhist art, and the Sangam academies were a Tamil literary tradition — none was a Kushana source of wealth.

**48. (A)** The Silk Route was a web of land routes across Central Asia linking China and the Mediterranean. It was not a Deccan-to-Rome sea lane (that was Satavahana sea trade), not a Chola delta road, and not a poets' circuit.

**49. (C)** Coins showing his titles and a variety of gods reveal Kanishka's styling and the many faiths he patronised. They do not show rejection of religion (the many gods prove the opposite), do not place him in the Tamil south (he was a northern Kushana), and do not suggest he avoided trade — coinage itself implies commerce.

**50. (C)** Land trade via the Silk Route plus sea trade from Deccan and southern ports together show India linked outward by both land and sea. This refutes the ideas that trade was only village-level, that India was isolated, or that it relied on one route alone — two distinct kinds of route operated at once.

**51. (D)** Greek-style robes and wavy hair mark the Greco-Buddhist Gandhara school, while fuller figures in spotted red sandstone mark the indigenous Mathura school. Reversing them is the classic trap. 'Sangam' is Tamil poetry, not sculpture, and 'Bharhut' names a stupa site, not the red-sandstone school, which is Mathura.

**52. (A)** The textbook has swapped the two: Gandhara is the Greco-Buddhist style and Mathura the indigenous red-sandstone style. Neither is Tamil poetry; Mathura is not purely Greek (it is indigenous); and both are sculpture, not poetry.

**53. (B)** Confluence means traditions blending; Gandhara plainly fuses Greek form with Indian Buddhist content, so it shows confluence more clearly than the largely indigenous Mathura style. Gandhara was not influence-free (it is plainly Hellenistic in form), the styles are not reversed (Gandhara is the blended one, Mathura the indigenous one), and Gandhara is sculpture, not Tamil poetry.

**54. (B)** Cultural confluence means traditions merge into shared forms while the local Indian character stays dominant, as in Gandhara art. It is not the destruction or replacement of one culture, not strict separation, and not the spread of one unchanging culture — confluence requires mixing and a surviving local core.

**55. (D)** Coins, cave inscriptions and sculptures are genuine material remains from the period. Election ballots, newspapers, emails, photographs, passports, constitutions, voter rolls and tax records are all modern items that did not exist then and cannot be physical sources for it.

**56. (B)** Coins and inscriptions are physical sources that carry much of what we know, because continuous written chronicles are scarce for the age. People could read and write (the inscriptions prove it), buildings like stupas do survive, and the histories were not Roman translations — the point is the reliance on material evidence.

**57. (B)** The Mauryan breakup came first; the Satavahanas rose in the post-Mauryan Deccan; Kanishka's Kushana power came later still. The other orderings wrongly place Kanishka or the Satavahanas before the Mauryan breakup, which contradicts the chapter's chronology.

**58. (A)** The thesis needs BOTH division and creativity: many rival kingdoms coexisting with flourishing art, trade and Sanskrit works supplies both. A peaceful single empire removes the 'division' the student is arguing for; art and trade did not cease; and the foreign rulers did not ban Indian culture, since they were absorbed by it.

**59. (B)** The Shungas were an indigenous heartland dynasty while the Indo-Greeks were foreigners in the northwest, so this pair correctly contrasts indigenous with foreign. The Indo-Greeks and Kushanas were both foreign, not indigenous. The Cholas and Pandyas were both indigenous Tamil kingdoms. And the Satavahanas and Shungas were both indigenous Indian powers, so neither was foreign.

**60. (A)** The Kushanas grew rich from the overland LAND Silk Route, not from Deccan sea ports — that sea trade belonged to the Satavahanas, so this statement misdescribes its subject. The other three are accurate: the Shungas did revive Vedic ritual and adorn Bharhut, the Satavahanas' ship-coins do evidence sea trade, and the Cholas did build the Grand Anicut to water the Kaveri delta.
