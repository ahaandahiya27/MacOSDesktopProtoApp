## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** Financial infrastructure is the network that moves and manages money, so both the bank (deposits and loans) and the stock exchange (buying and selling shares) belong there; a canal carries water/goods and is physical, and a college is a social-welfare service. Grouping the bank with the canal confuses moving money with moving water. 'Costing money to build' is true of every project and does not make them all financial. And a stock exchange handles money flows, so it is financial, not social like a college or hospital.

**2. (A)** Finance complements physical infrastructure: money can be raised, lent and moved fast, so the ports and grids get used to their full potential. Finance does not replace physical infrastructure, so demolishing the ports is wrong. The two clearly interact, so 'never affect each other' contradicts 'complement'. And inclusive banking (e.g. Jan Dhan) aims to reach everyone, not only the wealthy.

**3. (A)** A power grid carries electricity (physical infrastructure); a payment system moves rupees between accounts (financial infrastructure), so they are different types. 'Both deliver something useful' is too loose - usefulness does not make two things the same type. The labels in the third option are swapped: the grid is physical and the payment system is financial. And the chapter explicitly treats banks, markets and payment systems as infrastructure, so 'only roads count' is false.

**4. (B)** To complement is to work alongside and complete: roads/grids move goods and energy, banks/markets move the money that builds and runs them, so each strengthens the other. 'Makes roads unnecessary' is replacement, not complement. They do different jobs, so 'exact same job' is wrong. And 'entirely unrelated' contradicts the very idea of complementing.

**5. (D)** Financial infrastructure moves and manages money: banks, the stock market and digital payments all qualify. A highway moves vehicles and goods, not money, so it is physical infrastructure and is the one to remove. The other three each handle money flows, which is exactly what makes them financial infrastructure.

**6. (B)** A fixed deposit locks money for a set term at higher interest; a savings account suits gentle saving with easy withdrawals; a current account suits a business making many daily transactions. The second option swaps the FD and current roles - a current account is not for locking money up, and an FD cannot be drawn on dozens of times a day. One account cannot serve all three needs well. And an FD cannot give easy daily withdrawals, so the last option is wrong too.

**7. (D)** Banks keep some deposits as reserves and lend most out, yet manage inflows and reserves so withdrawals can be met - the depositor's right to withdraw stays safe. Only the RBI prints currency, so a commercial bank does not create notes. The bank did not keep every rupee untouched - it lent 80,000 - so the locker myth is wrong. And it need not recall the loan that day; routine reserves and fresh deposits cover ordinary withdrawals.

**8. (D)** A savings deposit earns interest and, since the bank lends most deposits out, the money works in the economy; hidden cash simply sits idle. A bank deposit is safer than cash at home, not less safe. A savings deposit does not become shares unless he buys them. And a commercial bank lends existing rupees - it does not print money.

**9. (D)** A deposit is money you place with the bank (you may earn interest); a loan is money the bank gives you that you repay later with interest you pay - opposite directions of money flow. The first wrong option reverses those directions. The second swaps who pays whom: a depositor earns interest, a borrower pays it. And both arrangements involve interest, so 'handed over free' is false.

**10. (B)** Your balance and right to withdraw are kept safe, but the actual rupees are not idle - the bank lends most of them out, keeping only reserves; so 'untouched' is the misleading word. 'Safe' is fair, since deposits are safer than hidden cash. Banks do record deposits in accounts, so 'account' is not the problem. And the last option simply restates the locker myth the chapter corrects.

**11. (A)** Compounding earns interest on interest. Year 1: 10,000 grows to 11,000. Year 2: 11,000 grows to 12,100. Year 3: 12,100 grows to 13,310. Adding a flat 1,000 each year (13,000) is simple interest, not compounding. Adding interest only once gives 11,000, which ignores years two and three. And 10,000 unchanged ignores all growth.

**12. (B)** Simple interest adds 10% of 2,000 = 200 each year, so A earns 200 + 200 = 400. Compounding: year 1 gives 200 (balance 2,200); year 2 gives 10% of 2,200 = 220 (balance 2,420), so B's interest is 420. The extra 20 is interest-on-interest. Equal rate and time do not give equal totals once compounding applies. Simple interest is the smaller total, not the larger, so 'A earns 420' is reversed. And compounding helps even over two years - it does not require a very long period to beat simple interest.

**13. (D)** Doubling from 1 gives 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128 on squares 1 to 8, so square 8 holds 128 - the chessboard story showing how repeated doubling (the idea behind compounding) explodes upward. Adding 2 each time (16) is simple growth, not doubling. One grain per square (8) ignores doubling entirely. And 64 is the count on square 7, one doubling short.

**14. (D)** Letting interest stay in the account makes it join the balance and earn further interest - compounding, which is strongest over long periods like 18 years; the neighbour breaks the chain by withdrawing his interest, so his money grows only in simple steps. Untouched money in a savings account does not lose value or shrink. And both time and rate drive compounding, so the two totals differ greatly - the rate alone does not determine the outcome.

**15. (C)** A compound balance grows on its previous total, so each year's interest is larger than the year before, bending the line upward - that is interest-on-interest. The rate stays the same; the bend comes from the method, not a rising rate. No new deposits are needed; the same principal grows. And compounding builds on the principal plus accumulated interest - it does not ignore the principal.

**16. (A)** A loan is repaid with interest: the 1,00,000 borrowed is the principal, and the extra 7,000 is the interest - the price of using the bank's money for a year. The first wrong option swaps the two labels. A dividend is a shareholder's profit share, not a borrower's payment, and the baker took a loan, not a deposit. And the RBI does not set an individual loan's figures - the bank charges interest; the benchmark rate is a different idea.

**17. (B)** Banks lend mostly from depositors' money, so a loan not repaid threatens both savers' funds and the bank's survival - hence the careful checks. The bank does not print loan money; it lends real deposits. A loan is credit given in trust of repayment with interest, not a gift. And the RBI does not cover defaulted private loans - that risk is the bank's to manage.

**18. (B)** Credit is money lent in the trust that it will be repaid - exactly a loan given today against a promise to repay tomorrow with interest. Handing the bank money to keep safe is a deposit, the reverse direction. Printing money is the RBI's role and is not lending on trust. And selling an ownership unit is a share, a stock-market transaction, not credit.

**19. (C)** Compounding works on loans as on savings. Year 1: 1,000 grows by 20% (200) to 1,200. Year 2: 1,200 grows by 20% (240) to 1,440 - the second year's interest is larger because it is figured on 1,200. Adding a flat 200 twice (1,400) is simple interest. Charging once gives 1,200, dropping the second year. And a loan with interest grows beyond the principal, so 1,000 is wrong.

**20. (A)** The bank lends most of your deposit at a higher rate than it pays you, and shares part of that earning back as your interest. Idle money earns nothing, so the government idea is wrong - and banks, not the government, pay deposit interest. Banks do not print money. And unrepaid loans are losses, not a source of interest for depositors.

**21. (D)** Interest paid is 5% of 4,00,000 = 20,000. Interest earned is 11% of the 3,00,000 lent = 33,000. Net = 33,000 - 20,000 = 13,000, the gap from charging more than it pays. The loss option applies the wrong rates to the wrong sums. The rates are not equal (11% vs 5%), so it does not break even. And 16% on all 4,00,000 invents a rate and a base that do not exist.

**22. (C)** Deposited savings fund loans for homes, businesses and farms, so the money circulates and supports growth; buried cash cannot do this. Banks do not burn deposits. The interest comparison is reversed - deposits earn interest while buried cash earns nothing. And deposits chiefly fund domestic lending, not a wholesale export of money abroad.

**23. (B)** Banks lend most deposits out, so your savings funding a farmer's seeds is the deposit at work, connecting savers and borrowers. A sealed, untouched locker is the very myth the chapter rejects. Turning deposits into stored gold would still leave them idle. And posting the cash back would simply remove it from the banking system.

**24. (A)** An account lets a person save safely and reach formal credit, reducing reliance on costly informal lenders - the heart of inclusive banking. It does not ban borrowing; it widens fair access to it. It does not force risky share investment. And it does not remove the need to save - it makes saving safer.

**25. (D)** Inclusive banking means the benefits of banking - safe savings, easy payments and access to credit - reach everyone, not just the well-off. It widens access rather than limiting it. Opening an account is not the same as receiving free monthly cash. And it brings people into the formal banking system, not away from it toward barter.

**26. (C)** The RBI was established in 1935 and Jan Dhan was launched in 2014. The first wrong option swaps those two dates. The years 1950 and 1853 are distractor dates from general knowledge that belong to neither event.

**27. (A)** The RBI is the 'banker to banks' - commercial banks keep accounts there, while ordinary people bank at commercial banks - so a personal RBI account is not available at any age. That rules out both 'yes' options. And the RBI very much deals with money - it prints currency and regulates banks - so 'no role in money' is plainly wrong.

**28. (A)** Printing the nation's currency is the RBI's exclusive role as central bank. Accepting deposits, giving home loans and allowing withdrawals are everyday jobs of commercial banks, where ordinary people bank - the RBI does not offer these services to the public.

**29. (B)** The RBI does not open accounts for individuals - that is for commercial banks - so the schoolchildren's savings accounts are outside its role. Printing currency, setting the benchmark rate and supervising banks are all genuine central-bank functions described in the chapter.

**30. (D)** The phrase means the RBI does for commercial banks what they do for individuals: it holds their accounts and lends to them. It is not a rival chasing ordinary depositors. It is the central bank, not a subsidiary of any commercial bank. And it serves the country's own banking system, not only foreign banks.

**31. (C)** Supervision means regulating and overseeing banks so the system stays stable and trustworthy. It does not mean running each branch day to day. The RBI is not a rival competing for customers. And issuing shares is a stock-market matter, not central-bank supervision.

**32. (B)** The RBI is the central bank - banker to banks and regulator - not a place for personal accounts, so savings go to a commercial bank; it is not merely a bigger version of one. It is genuinely a bank (the central bank), so 'not a bank at all' is wrong. And since it holds no personal accounts, the no-interest claim is beside the point - the issue is that individuals cannot bank there.

**33. (C)** The benchmark rate is what the RBI charges banks; raise it and banks' costs rise, so they lend dearer and borrowing tends to cool. A rise makes borrowing costlier, not cheaper, so 'lend more cheaply' reverses the logic. The benchmark rate influences lending broadly, not the price of every product. And it does not freeze share prices - shares still rise and fall with companies and the market.

**34. (C)** Inflation is a general rise in prices over time, so money buys less - exactly the falling buying power described. Compounding is interest growing on interest, a savings idea, not a price rise. A dividend is a shareholder's profit share. And a benchmark-rate cut is an RBI lending decision, not the price rise itself.

**35. (A)** A cut in the benchmark rate lowers banks' borrowing costs, so they tend to lend more cheaply and loan interest can fall, favouring borrowing now. A cut lowers, not raises, borrowing costs. The RBI does not set the price of the house. And borrowers pay interest - they do not receive dividends, which go to shareholders.

**36. (D)** If prices race ahead, the same money buys less, quietly shrinking the worth of savings and wages, so the RBI keeps inflation in check to protect money's value. Rising prices do not make everyone richer. The value of money matters greatly to daily life. And the chapter frames inflation as a threat to be controlled - any interest change does not outweigh the erosion of buying power, so it is not framed as a help to savers.

**37. (B)** Whether by cheque or electronic transfer, the same rupees shift between accounts - the methods differ in speed and convenience, not in what moves. No new money is created by a payment. The shopkeeper receives money, not shares. And modern transfers move account balances electronically, not by mailing notes.

**38. (D)** Electronic transfers move money between accounts almost instantly, while a cheque must be presented and cleared, taking longer - so transfers suit urgent payments. Neither method creates new money. A cheque remains a legal payment instrument, so a transfer is not the only legal way to pay. And a cheque orders payment of money, not the movement of shares.

**39. (A)** A cheque is a paper instrument ordering your bank to pay a named person a stated sum from your account. An ownership unit with a profit claim is a share. An RBI promise to print money describes neither a cheque nor reality. And a record of added interest is a statement entry, not a cheque.

**40. (B)** UPI shifts existing rupees from her account to the seller's account in seconds - no cash needed. A phone does not print currency. The app moves money; it does not turn an account into shares. And a UPI payment is electronic and immediate, not settled by a posted cheque.

**41. (C)** UPI is praised as India's own innovation - a fast, smartphone-based payment system admired and taken up beyond India. It was built in India, not copied from abroad. It is digital and modern, not a paper system older than cheques. And the central bank is the RBI, not UPI.

**42. (C)** UPI moves the same rupees between accounts almost instantly - it is a payment method, not new money, and it has not replaced the rupee. Barter is goods-for-goods with no money, the opposite of a money transfer. And a share is company ownership, unrelated to paying a seller through an app.

**43. (C)** UPI shifts existing rupees from customers' accounts to the shopkeeper's account; no new money is made and nothing physical is stored in the phone. It does not create rupees - a payment system moves money, it does not mint it. It does not produce shares, which are company ownership. And the phone holds no physical notes - the balance sits in the bank account.

**44. (A)** Owning shares makes you a part-owner, and 5 of one lakh shares is a tiny fraction, so you own a very small part. You are not the sole owner with so few shares. Buying shares makes you an owner, not a salaried employee. And a shareholder owns part of the company, unlike a lender who is owed fixed interest.

**45. (B)** A dividend is the share of profit paid to each shareholder, so that is what you receive. Interest is paid on deposits or loans, not as a profit share to owners. You bought ownership; you did not lend the company money. And a dividend is not a refund of your purchase price - you still keep your shares.

**46. (C)** Shareholders earn through dividends (a profit share) and through a rising share price they can sell at a gain. They do not get fixed monthly interest or guaranteed refunds - those describe a deposit. They are owners, not salaried employees getting free shares. And they are not depositors repaid principal plus interest; a share's value can also fall.

**47. (B)** Banks and stock markets both move and manage money, so both are financial infrastructure. They do not move goods (transport), provide welfare services (social), or carry power (energy) - those are different infrastructure types.

**48. (A)** A savings or fixed deposit gives Asha safety and a certain return; shares suit Bina's appetite for risk and possible higher gains. Swapping them ignores that shares carry risk and deposits are safe. Shares are not always safe, so 'shares for both' is wrong. And a fixed deposit does not lose value, so the last option misstates how deposits work.

**49. (D)** A deposit earns interest the bank states in advance, so its return is known; a share's worth swings with company fortunes and the market, so it is uncertain. The first wrong option reverses which one floats. Deposits pay interest and shares may pay dividends - not the same fixed amount for both. And the RBI does not guarantee share gains; shares carry real risk.

**50. (D)** Share prices move up and down, so shares carry real risk - they are not a guaranteed grower like a fixed-interest deposit. They do not always grow or never fall. A savings deposit is safe with fixed interest, so it is the safer of the two, not the riskier. And shares and savings are not identical, so the claim cannot stand.

**51. (C)** No real bank asks for your OTP, PIN or password, so anyone who does is a fraudster - refuse and report on helpline 1930. Reading out the OTP, adding your PIN, or sending a payment would each hand control to the scammer; the claim that it is 'for your safety' is the very trick used in such fraud.

**52. (B)** OTPs, PINs and passwords are the secret keys to your account; a real bank never requires them from you, so any request signals fraud - one principle covering all three rules. Banks do not regularly need them, so daily changing is not the point. Fraud is not limited to foreigners. And these secrets protect bank accounts and payments, not just share dealings.

**53. (B)** Keeping your OTP, PIN and password private and reporting fraud on 1930 is the safe approach. Writing the PIN on the card hands it to any thief. Sharing the OTP with 'bank' callers is exactly the scam, since a genuine bank never asks. And posting a password anywhere, even to family, exposes it to misuse.

**54. (C)** Medieval moneylenders worked at benches - 'banca' in Italian - giving us 'bank'; a failed one had his 'banca rotta', a 'broken bench', the source of 'bankrupt'. The root is 'bench', not 'gold vault'. Neither word comes from 'cheque' or 'currency'. And the 'to trust' root ('credere') gives 'credit', a different word.

**55. (A)** From settling a debt (an 'end' to it), 'finance' grew to mean managing money: saving, lending and investing. Road-building is physical infrastructure, not finance. Printing notes is only the RBI's narrow task, not all of finance. And barter is exchange without money, the opposite of money management.

**56. (C)** Finance complements physical infrastructure: banks and markets raise and move the money that lets factories and projects be built and used, which the roads-only town lacks. Roads are useful regardless of who owns them, so 'useless unless owned by a bank' is false. Finance complements, not replaces, roads, so the town still needs them. And ordinary factories are funded through commercial banks and markets, not built by the RBI, which is the central bank.

**57. (C)** Simple interest adds 10% of 5,000 = 500 each year, so 500 + 500 = 1,000. Compounding: year 1 gives 500 (balance 5,500); year 2 gives 10% of 5,500 = 550 (balance 6,050), so interest is 1,050. The difference is 1,050 - 1,000 = 50, the interest-on-interest. Equal rates do not give equal totals once compounding applies, so '0' is wrong. The '100' option reverses which method is larger. And compounding at 10% does not double the interest each year, so '500' is far too large.

**58. (A)** A loan is credit - money lent in trust that it will be repaid later with interest - unlike a gift, which is not repaid. So 'need never be repaid, like a gift' is wrong. A borrower pays interest; the loan does not earn the borrower interest. And a loan is money the bank gives the borrower, not money the borrower gives the bank - that would be a deposit.

**59. (A)** A higher benchmark rate raises banks' costs, so they charge more for loans, cooling borrowing and spending - the trade-off the RBI weighs against fighting inflation. Loans do not become free. The rate and borrowing are tightly linked, not unrelated. And the RBI does not shut the stock market to control prices.

**60. (D)** A deposit is safe with a known interest return; a share is an investment that can rise or fall; a loan is money the person must repay with interest; UPI simply moves existing rupees. Not all three grow at one fixed rate - the share has no fixed rate and the loan is a cost, not a gain. UPI moves money, it does not create it. And a loan costs the borrower interest; it does not earn them interest the way a deposit does.
