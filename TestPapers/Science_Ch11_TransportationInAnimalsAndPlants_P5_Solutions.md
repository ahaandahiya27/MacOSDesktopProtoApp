## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** Three functions are tested: oxygen carriage (normal), clotting (normal), defence (failing). Only the defence role is impaired, and that role belongs to white blood cells, so they are the deficient component. Red cells carry oxygen, which the chart says is normal, so anaemia is not indicated. Platelets and clotting are explicitly normal here, so a clotting fault is ruled out. Plasma is the liquid carrier; it does not engulf bacteria — that is the white cells' job — so blaming plasma misnames the defender.

**2. (D)** The packed lower layer is the cells; the yellow upper layer is plasma. Cell fractions are R 60 percent, P 45 percent, Q 40 percent, giving the order R, P, Q. The distractor that ranks by yellow column mistakes plasma for cells. There is no rule that a balanced split carries the most cells. Haemoglobin sits inside the red cells in the packed layer, not dissolved in the yellow plasma, so a tall yellow column signals more plasma, not more cells.

**3. (D)** A nucleus-free red cell has more space for haemoglobin, raising oxygen carriage; that is the trade-off in the correct option. Losing the nucleus means the red cell cannot divide, so the claim it divides faster is backwards. White cells fight germs, not clotting (that is platelets), so the second wrong option misassigns the job. Haemoglobin belongs to red cells; a white cell losing its nucleus would not start carrying oxygen, so the last option is unfounded.

**4. (D)** Low iron means too little haemoglobin, reducing oxygen carriage and causing the breathlessness of Patient 1; iron is the dietary link. Slow clotting from small cuts points to too few platelets in Patient 2. Swapping these reverses cause and effect. White cells handle germ defence, not oxygen carriage or clotting, so blaming them for both symptoms is wrong. Plasma volume and red-cell count are not what control oxygen carriage and clot speed here, so the last option misattributes both.

**5. (A)** Iron is a component of haemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying pigment in red cells, so iron raises oxygen carriage — the correct functional link. Clotting is a platelet job and does not depend on iron, so the platelet claim is wrong. Plasma is mostly water with dissolved substances, not built from iron. White cells are not made of iron, and germ defence does not hinge on it, so the last option is false.

**6. (D)** White cells are the body's defenders, so their number rises to meet an infection and falls when it is over; red cells, with a different job, stay steady — exactly the observed pattern. Red cells do not turn into white cells; the cell types are distinct. Platelets clot blood and do not convert into white cells. Plasma is the liquid medium and does not solidify into cells, so the last option is biologically impossible.

**7. (C)** Plasma carries dissolved substances such as digested food and the waste urea around the body — the correct list. Oxygen is carried chiefly by haemoglobin in red cells, not bound to plasma, so the first wrong option overstates plasma's gas role. A clot forms at a wound and is not shuttled around whole. Plasma carries dissolved materials, not whole undigested food chunks or bone fragments, so the last option is wrong.

**8. (A)** The goal is oxygen delivery, and red cells with their haemoglobin carry oxygen, so they are the directly responsible component. Platelets handle clotting, which is not the stated aim. White cells defend against germs; that is unrelated to oxygen transport. Plasma is the liquid carrier of dissolved substances, but oxygen is carried by haemoglobin in red cells, so plasma proteins are not the answer.

**9. (B)** Blood leaving the lungs has just picked up oxygen, and oxygen-rich haemoglobin is bright red, so that sample is brighter — the correct reading. Platelet count does not set blood colour, so the entering-sample claim fails. Blood is not blue inside the body; all of it is red, brighter when oxygen-rich, so the 'only lung blood is red' idea is a misconception. Oxygen loading at the lungs does change the colour, so the equal-brightness option is wrong.

**10. (B)** Red cells are by far the most numerous, are nucleus-free and carry oxygen; white cells are rarer, keep a nucleus and fight germs — the correct pairing. The first wrong option swaps the jobs and names. Platelets do not carry oxygen, so the second wrong option fails. The last option keeps the right names but swaps their jobs, assigning germ-fighting to red cells and oxygen carriage to white cells.

**11. (C)** Nucleus-free discs are red cells that carry oxygen; the rare nucleated cells are white cells that fight germs. Swapping these two jobs (first wrong option) is the classic flip. Clotting belongs to platelets, and plasma is a liquid not made of cells, so the second wrong option misassigns both. Urea is carried dissolved in plasma, not on red cells, so the last option is wrong.

**12. (A)** An artery carries blood pumped directly from the heart at high, pulsing pressure, so it spurts with each beat — the correct synthesis. Blood returning slowly to the heart describes a vein and would not spurt. Valves are a vein feature and do not create arterial spurts. Arteries actually have thick walls, not thin ones, so the wall-thickness claim is backwards.

**13. (A)** Thick muscular walls with pulsing flow and no valves mark arteries, so V1 and V4 are both arteries — V4 is the artery to the lungs, which still pulses even though it carries oxygen-poor blood. Thin walls with valves and low flow mark a vein (V2). A wall one cell thick with the slowest flow is a capillary (V3). The wrong options misname the thin-thick-and-valve pattern or wrongly demote the pulmonary artery to a vein because of its blood content rather than its structure and direction.

**14. (B)** Blood in veins is at low pressure, and valves prevent backflow so blood keeps moving toward the heart — both corrections needed. The first wrong option repeats the false high-pressure premise. Valves do not produce the heart sounds (those are the heart's own valves closing). Vein valves are passive flaps, not active pumps, so the pumping claim is wrong.

**15. (B)** Blood leaves the heart through an artery, passes through capillaries where it exchanges oxygen with the muscle, and returns through a vein, giving artery then capillaries then vein. Starting with a vein reverses the trip. Capillaries are the middle exchange step, never first. Veins follow the capillary exchange, so artery-vein-capillaries puts the exchange step last, which is wrong.

**16. (B)** The rule is a useful generalisation, but the vessel to the lungs (an artery) carries oxygen-poor blood and the vessel back from the lungs (a vein) carries oxygen-rich blood — the named exception. Claiming no exception ignores the lung circuit. Saying arteries always carry oxygen-poor blood reverses the usual case. The rule does not depend on age, so the child-reversal claim is invented.

**17. (C)** Arteries take blood straight from the pumping heart, so pressure is highest there; capillaries slow the flow so substances have time to exchange with cells. Pressure is not highest in veins (low-pressure return) nor in capillaries. Vein valves exist because of low pressure, not because flow is slowest there. Highest pressure and slowest flow do not coincide in the arteries, so the last option is wrong.

**18. (B)** All blood is red — darker when oxygen-poor — and the bluish look is an optical effect of light through skin and vessel walls, the correct account. Blood does not start blue and redden in air. Oxygen-poor blood is dark red, not blue. Plasma is pale yellow, not blue, so it cannot tint the vessels blue.

**19. (A)** Capillary exchange depends on the wall being a single cell thick; thickening it would hinder exchange — the correct consequence. A thicker wall does not push substances out faster; it blocks diffusion. A muscular wall does not turn a vessel into a pump. Thick walls do not trigger clotting, so the clotting claim is unfounded.

**20. (A)** Carrying blood toward the heart, having valves, and oozing at low pressure all describe a vein; its valves prevent backflow of low-pressure blood — the correct combined statement. Arteries carry blood away from the heart and lack such valves. Capillaries have no valves and are exchange vessels. The last option correctly names a vein but wrongly says its valves cope with high pressure; vein blood is at low pressure.

**21. (C)** Separating oxygen-rich from oxygen-poor blood means the body receives fully oxygenated blood, maximising oxygen delivery — the real advantage. Loudness of the heartbeat is set by valve closure, not by separation. Separation does not speed colour change, which depends on oxygen loading at the lungs. The heart is muscular and must contract to pump, so the no-muscle claim is false.

**22. (D)** Thick muscular lower chambers pump blood out forcefully through the arteries, while the thinner upper chambers receive blood from the veins — structure matches function. The first wrong option reverses receive and pump. The heart does not store urine; that is the bladder. The pulse is an artery expanding, not a valve in a chamber, so the last option misreads it.

**23. (C)** The lub-dub is the heart's valves closing during pumping; the wrist throb (pulse) is an artery expanding as a surge of blood enters it — the correct pairing, and both occur once per beat. Capillaries are silent exchange vessels, and the pulse is not a vein-valve snap. The lub-dub is not breathing, and the pulse is not clotting. Heart valves are in the heart, not at the wrist, so the last option mislocates them.

**24. (D)** The pulse is the rhythmic expansion of an artery as each surge of pumped blood passes through, the correct identification. It is not a vein-valve snap, which is a common misconception. Blood does not clot and re-dissolve each beat. The lungs move air for breathing, not a throb down the arm, so the air option is wrong.

**25. (A)** Rate per minute is beats divided by seconds times 60: 33 divided by 22 is 1.5 beats per second, times 60 gives 90 beats per minute, which is a little above a typical resting rate. The 66 figure would come from a wrong division. Reporting 33 forgets to scale the 22 seconds up to a full minute. The 110 figure does not match the arithmetic.

**26. (A)** In 30 seconds the resting heart (70 per minute) beats 35 times and the post-sprint heart (140 per minute) beats 70 times — twice as many — matching the muscles' raised demand for oxygen and food and faster waste clearance. Halving the count reverses the comparison. A higher rate does raise real flow, so the no-change claim is wrong. A 30-second window holds half a minute's beats, not a full minute's, so 140 in 30 seconds is double-counting.

**27. (C)** A resting rate of 126 is well above the typical 72 to 80 band, so it is much higher than usual. Values above 100 are not the normal resting figure. A larger number means a faster, not slower, heart, so 'lower' is backwards. Human hearts can exceed 100 beats per minute (for example during exercise), so 126 is not impossible.

**28. (D)** If one beat takes 0.75 seconds, the number of beats in 60 seconds is 60 divided by 0.75, which equals 80 beats per minute. Reading 0.75 itself as the rate ignores the conversion. The 45 figure has no correct basis. Dividing 100 by 0.75 uses the wrong total (a minute is 60 seconds, not 100), giving the wrong 133.

**29. (D)** A: 20 divided by 15 times 60 equals 80 per minute. B: 26 divided by 20 times 60 equals 78 per minute. So A is slightly faster despite B having a larger raw count over a longer time. The second option mistakes the larger raw count for a higher rate. The counts do not both give 78. Longer counting does not automatically raise the rate, so the last option's reasoning and numbers are wrong.

**30. (B)** The double loop sends blood to the lungs to reload oxygen before each body trip, so tissues get oxygen-rich blood — the central benefit. The body does not receive oxygen-poor blood directly; that is the point of the lung loop. The heart cannot skip the lung loop, which constantly reloads oxygen. Mixing the two streams would lower delivered oxygen, the opposite of the benefit.

**31. (A)** Water rises in the xylem by the transpiration pull created at the leaves, so the plant lifts water without any pump — the matching and its limit. Plants have no heart in the roots or anywhere. The phloem carries food, not water, and does not pump water up. Stomata are leaf pores for water vapour and gas exchange, not soil-water suckers, so the last option is wrong.

**32. (B)** Excretion is the removal of harmful wastes; taking in and digesting food is the opposite — gaining nutrients — so it is not excretion. Removing urea as urine, losing water and salts in sweat, and passing nitrogen waste through the kidneys are all genuine forms of excretion.

**33. (C)** The kidney's microscopic filtering units are nephrons, and they sit inside the kidneys, not the bladder (which only stores urine). Stomata are leaf pores, not kidney units. Platelets are blood cells that help clotting, not kidney filters, so both name-substitutions are wrong.

**34. (B)** Urine forms in the kidney, runs down the ureter to the bladder for storage, then leaves through the urethra: kidney, ureter, bladder, urethra. The other orders scramble these — for instance placing the urethra before the bladder, or starting at the bladder, both of which contradict where urine is made and stored.

**35. (C)** Urea is made in the body cells, carried dissolved in the blood, filtered by the kidneys, and excreted in urine — the correct trace. Xylem and stomata are plant tissues, not part of human excretion. Urea is not made in the bladder nor breathed out by the lungs. Urea is not produced inside urine, and the heart does not store it, so the last trace is wrong.

**36. (B)** Urea is a harmful nitrogen waste that must be removed, not a stored food, so the claim is wrong. It is not broken down for energy. The kidneys filter urea out rather than stockpiling it as a reserve. Blood is coloured red by haemoglobin, not urea, so the last option is doubly wrong.

**37. (D)** Sweat and urine both remove water and some wastes, so the two routes balance: more sweating allows less urine and the reverse — they share the task. Heavy sweating does not shut the kidneys off. Sweat does remove some water and salts, so it is a form of excretion, not merely cooling. Neither organ absorbs oxygen through the skin, so the last option is irrelevant.

**38. (A)** Dialysis replaces the failed kidneys by filtering wastes from the blood. A transfusion replaces lost blood, not kidney filtering. Transpiration is water loss from plant leaves, not a medical machine. Circulation is the body's own blood movement and is unrelated to replacing kidney function, so the last option is wrong.

**39. (C)** Plants release some gaseous waste through stomata and store other wastes in leaves, bark, gums and resins — the correct picture. Plants have no kidneys or ureters. Wastes are not all routed up the xylem and out the roots; the xylem carries water and minerals upward. Plants do produce wastes, so the no-waste claim is false.

**40. (D)** Gums and salt-laden leaves that drop are both ways plants store or shed waste. Food transport is the phloem's job and is not what gum represents. Water moving up the trunk is the xylem's role, unrelated to gum or salt deposits. Oxygen release is a gas-exchange process, not the meaning of gum or accumulated leaf salts.

**41. (A)** Plants make less harmful waste and can store some, so their excretion is slower and simpler than animals' — the accurate comparison. Plants have no kidneys, so they cannot be faster for that reason. Plants have no heart or waste vessels. Plants do make waste, so excretion is not impossible.

**42. (D)** Hot, dry, windy air speeds transpiration, so the leaves lose water faster than the roots can replace it even from moist soil, causing wilting. The soil water has not turned poisonous. Xylem does not turn into phloem. The plant has not permanently stopped making food; wilting is a temporary water-balance problem, so the other options are wrong.

**43. (D)** Xylem carries water and minerals upward from roots to leaves — the correct tissue and direction. Phloem carries food, not water and minerals up. Xylem does not carry food downward; that is the phloem's role. Stomata are leaf pores for vapour and gas, not water-conducting tubes, so the last option is wrong.

**44. (B)** Root hairs vastly increase the root's surface area in contact with soil water, boosting absorption — the correct feature. Root hairs are not green and do not photosynthesise. They have no muscles and do not pump. They absorb water and minerals; they do not carry food down into the soil, so the last option is wrong.

**45. (C)** Water lost from the leaves creates the transpiration pull, which draws water up the stem; it originates at the evaporating leaves — the correct answer. Plants have no heartbeat. A phloem push from the roots is not how water rises; the phloem carries food, and the pull comes from the top. Clotting is a blood process, not a plant water-lifting force.

**46. (B)** Hot, dry, moving air speeds transpiration, strengthening the pull, so shoot 2 takes up water faster. Heat does not seal the stomata; transpiration rises, not stops. Conditions strongly affect transpiration, so the two shoots differ. Transpiration does not reverse the flow and push water back down, so the last option is wrong.

**47. (A)** Xylem carries water up; blocking it cuts off water to the leaves, which suffer first. Food travels in the phloem (still intact), not the xylem, so 'food can no longer reach the leaves' misnames the cargo. Food reaching the roots is also a phloem job, unaffected here. Minerals move up in the xylem, not out through the phloem, so the last option confuses both tissue and direction.

**48. (C)** The phloem can carry food in more than one direction at once, supplying both a root tip and a fruit simultaneously. Xylem carries water and minerals upward, not food in all directions. Stomata are leaf pores, not food-transport tubes. Root hairs absorb water and minerals from soil; they do not distribute food, so the last option is wrong.

**49. (A)** The phloem carries food down from the leaves; cutting it stops food reaching the trunk and roots below, which starve. Water still rises through the intact xylem, so the leaves above do not lose their water supply. The xylem was not cut, so the whole tree does not fail at once for that reason. The starvation is below the ring, not limited to the removed strip, so the last option is wrong.

**50. (B)** Xylem carries water and minerals up, phloem carries food in multiple directions, and the ureter carries urine — all correct. The first wrong row swaps xylem and phloem cargo and makes the ureter carry blood. The second makes the phloem carry water and the ureter carry food. The third assigns urine to the xylem and water to the ureter, all wrong.

**51. (B)** An Amoeba is tiny, so diffusion across its surface supplies and removes materials fast enough — no transport system needed. It does need oxygen, food and water like any cell. It has no blood; it relies on diffusion. It does move materials internally; it simply does so by diffusion, so the no-movement claim is wrong.

**52. (B)** In a large body, inner cells are too distant from the surface for diffusion alone to supply them, so a transport system is needed. The body does have a surface; the problem is distance, not the absence of a surface. Large organisms certainly need oxygen, food and water. Being surrounded by water is not why transport is needed, so the last option is irrelevant.

**53. (A)** The kidney removes wastes dissolved in water as urine, and transpiration is the loss of water from leaves, so water is the shared substance. Haemoglobin and platelets are blood components found only in animals, not in plant transpiration. Bone is unrelated to either process, so it is wrong.

**54. (D)** The closest analogy is that the heart moves blood as the transpiration pull moves water up the xylem, while noting plants lack a pump — the correct, carefully qualified match. The xylem moves water up, not blood back like veins. Phloem carries food, not waste like kidneys. Root hairs absorb water and minerals; they do not pump like ventricles, so the last analogy is wrong.

**55. (A)** Animals use a heart and vessels; plants use xylem for water up and phloem for food — the fully correct summary. Animals have no xylem, and plants have no heart, so the first wrong row mixes systems. Veins carry blood toward the heart (not away), and phloem carries food, not water, so the second row has two errors. Plants have no heart, so the last row is wrong.

**56. (C)** A fine mesh of thin tubing is the capillaries (exchange vessels); floppy valved return pipes are the veins (returning blood with valves). The thick stiff pipes — not the thin mesh — are the arteries, so the first wrong option misassigns them. Valved return pipes are veins, not arteries, so the second is reversed. Heart chambers are the pump, not the thin mesh, so the last option is wrong.

**57. (C)** Cool, humid, still air slows transpiration, so moving the plant into shade reduces water loss without harm. A fan of warm dry air would speed transpiration and worsen loss. Blocking the root hairs starves the plant of water and harms it. Sealing the xylem cuts off the water supply entirely and would kill the plant, so it is not a safe way to slow loss.

**58. (D)** Arteries have thick, muscular, elastic walls to take the high pressure of blood pumped from the heart — a feature veins lack. Backflow-preventing valves are a vein feature, not an artery one. A single-cell-thick wall describes capillaries. Carrying blood toward the heart is the veins' job, not the arteries', so the other options all belong to different vessels.

**59. (D)** Cells continuously consume oxygen and food and make waste, so blood must keep circulating to resupply and remove waste — the sound reason. Blood does not turn blue when still; it is always red. The point of circulation is supply and waste removal, not keeping the heart sounds going. Blood does not turn into urine; urine is made by the kidneys from filtered waste, so the last option is wrong.

**60. (C)** One heartbeat produces both the lub-dub (valves closing) and one arterial surge felt as a pulse, so both counts equal the heart rate — not a coincidence. The pulse is an artery expanding, not made by veins. The agreement is not an illusion of sound speed. The stethoscope is counting heartbeats, not breaths, so the breath-count option is wrong.
