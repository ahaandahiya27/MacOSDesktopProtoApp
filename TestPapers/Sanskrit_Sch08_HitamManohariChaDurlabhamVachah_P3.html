Worked Solutions

1. (D) The verse praises speech that is both हितम् (beneficial) AND मनोहारि (pleasing) together — दुर्लभम् precisely because both must hold. A true helpful correction said in encouraging words is both useful and agreeable. Pleasant flattery of poor work is मनोहारि but not हितम् (it does no good). A humiliating rebuke may be हितम् in content but is not मनोहारि. Silence is neither. Only the first satisfies both qualities.

2. (B) वचः (speech) is a neuter noun; an adjective in Sanskrit agrees with its noun in gender, number and case. हितम्, मनोहारि and दुर्लभम् are all neuter singular adjectives describing वचः. They are not nouns in apposition, not verbs (वचः takes no verbal agreement), and not indeclinable adverbs — they inflect to match वचः.

3. (C) च means 'and', so 'or' is one error. दुर्लभम् means 'rare / hard to find', so 'easy to find' is a second, opposite error. वचः ('speech') is rendered correctly as 'speech', so that is not a third error. Exactly two independent mistakes are present.

4. (A) Either single quality alone is comparatively easy to produce; the verse's whole point is that combining both — being useful AND pleasing at the same moment — is दुर्लभम् (rare/hard). So 'both together' is the hardest, while either quality singly is easier. 'Both is easiest' inverts the verse; 'all equal' ignores why the combination is praised; and 'both together' is hard, not impossible.

5. (D) The opposite of मनोहारि (pleasing) is harsh/unpleasant. Keeping हितम् (useful) but making it harsh yields useful-but-harsh speech. Because the ideal requires BOTH qualities, useful-but-harsh fails the मनोहारि half and is not the दुर्लभम् ideal. It is not the ideal, is still useful (not useless), and lacks the 'pleasing' quality, so it is not 'both'.

6. (A) दुर्लभम् signals 'rare but attainable', which is an invitation to strive, not to surrender. The verse therefore motivates sustained effort. 'Impossible, so don't try' confuses दुर्लभम् with अशक्यम्; 'common, no effort' contradicts दुर्लभम्; and dropping usefulness ignores the हितम् half demanded by च.

7. (C) The enclitic च follows the last of the items it conjoins, linking it back to the earlier item(s): हितम् ... मनोहारि च = 'useful and pleasing'. It is not meaningless, it joins the adjectives (not whole sentences here), and it gathers the preceding item, not only what follows.

8. (C) Swapping च ('and') for वा ('or') lowers a strict 'both qualities at once' bar to an easy 'either one will do' bar — a major loosening. Replacing दुर्लभम्, वचः or मनोहारि with exact synonyms keeps the meaning unchanged, so those do not alter the standard.

9. (A) च is the conjunction 'and', वा is the disjunction 'or', and the किम्-stem (का feminine, किम् neuter) is interrogative 'what/which'. The other mappings scramble these distinct functions; mistaking च for 'or' is exactly the verse-weakening error the chapter warns against.

10. (C) Vowel-sandhi (e.g. इ + अ) needs a vowel ending meeting a vowel beginning. च begins with the consonant च्, so although मनोहारि ends in the vowel -इ, no vowel-vowel junction arises and the words stay separate. च is a particle (not a verb), मनोहारि does end in a vowel, and च does not universally block sandhi.

11. (D) Speech that is merely useful OR merely pleasing is common, so reading (ii) clashes with दुर्लभम्. Demanding both at once (reading i) is what makes such speech rare, so only (i) is consistent with दुर्लभम्. Hence च must be 'and'.

12. (B) दुर्लभम् qualifies the specific speech defined by हितम् + मनोहारि च, i.e. the both-useful-and-pleasing kind, not speech as such. Ordinary or merely-pleasing or loud speech is common. The debater drops the two adjectives and overgeneralises.

13. (B) दुर्- means 'difficult / bad / hard-to', so दुर्लभम् = 'hard to obtain = rare'. It expresses difficulty, not absolute negation; the word for 'impossible' is अशक्यम्. It is the opposite of 'easily obtained', and the prefix is meaningful.

14. (C) दुर्लभम् marks something rare yet reachable through effort; अशक्यम् (a- 'not' + शक्य 'doable') marks the genuinely impossible. They are not synonyms, the meanings are not reversed, and neither means 'easy' or 'pleasing'. The chapter deliberately uses दुर्लभम्, encouraging striving.

15. (B) 'Never possible' is अशक्यम्. The poet instead chose दुर्लभम् (rare/hard), which leaves the ideal attainable. मनोहारि means pleasing, and सुलभम् ('easy to obtain') is the very opposite of what would be meant. The word choice itself proves the ideal is not declared impossible.

16. (B) सु- conveys ease/goodness, दुर्- conveys difficulty/badness. Attached to the same -लभम् they yield opposites: सुलभम् 'easily obtained' vs दुर्लभम् 'hard to obtain (rare)'. The prefixes are not synonymous, not empty, and not reversed in meaning.

17. (B) Since दुर्लभम् means rare yet attainable, urging continued practice fits perfectly. It does not mean impossible (that is अशक्यम्), nor common (that would make practice needless), and the adjective can describe any rare-but-reachable thing, not only speech.

18. (C) Labelling the ideal दुर्लभम् (rare but attainable) frames it as a goal worth working toward, encouraging persistence. Resignation belongs to अशक्यम्; complacency belongs to सुलभम्/common; indifference contradicts a chapter all about cultivating fine speech.

19. (A) The prefix अ- (a-) negates: हितम् 'beneficial' → अहितम् 'not beneficial / harmful'. It does not intensify, does not pluralise, and is not decorative. This same negating अ- appears in अशक्यम् ('not doable = impossible').

20. (D) मनः + हारि literally means 'that which carries off / captivates the heart' — pleasing/charming. It is not 'mind-harming' (that would be unpleasant), not 'mind-giving', and not 'heart-hiding/subtle' (that confuses it with सूक्ष्म). So मनोहारि = pleasing.

21. (D) हितम् (beneficial) serves the listener's welfare; मनोहारि (heart-captivating) appeals to their feelings. The reversal swaps the two qualities. Neither concerns wealth, and neither is about boastfulness or curiosity.

22. (B) हितम् here predicates a quality of वचः and agrees with it (neuter singular nominative). It is not a verb (अस्ति is the verb), not a genitive (तस्य is the genitive), and not an adverb. Adjective–noun agreement in gender/number/case is the relationship.

23. (C) -शस् forms distributive adverbs meaning 'by units of': क्षणशः 'moment by moment', कणशः 'particle by particle'. It is the opposite of 'all at once', and it is adverbial, not a dative ('for') or ablative ('from') case ending.

24. (D) Adverbs in -शस्/-शः are indeclinable: they keep one fixed form and never inflect to agree with a noun. They are not adjectives (no gender agreement), not declined nouns, and not verbs. This is why each simply attaches to its noun-action unchanged.

25. (B) The standard pairing is विद्या (knowledge) gathered क्षणशः (moment by moment) and अर्थः (wealth) gathered कणशः (particle by particle) — each noun gets its own adverb. Swapping them or using one adverb for both breaks the verse's careful one-adverb-per-noun structure.

26. (C) बिन्दु ('drop') + -शस् → बिन्दुशः 'drop by drop' is exactly the distributive-adverb pattern of क्षणशः and कणशः. It is not an agreeing adjective like हितम्, not a verb like अस्ति, and not a case-form of a pronoun like तस्य.

27. (A) Moment-by-moment and particle-by-particle both stress steady, incremental gathering — small consistent efforts adding up. They explicitly reject sudden windfalls, mere luck, and (in the chapter's wider theme) empty boasting in place of real accumulation.

28. (C) क्षण ('moment') is a unit of time, fitting study, which accumulates over moments; कण ('particle/grain') is a small material unit, fitting wealth, which accrues coin by coin. The pairing is meaningful, not arbitrary, not reversed, and not merely for sound.

29. (B) Daily attentive study accumulates knowledge over units of time, i.e. क्षणशः ('moment by moment'). कणशः ('particle by particle') is reserved for wealth; the gain was not sudden; and boasting gathers nothing. So क्षणशः is right.

30. (C) Wealth in the verse is gathered कणशः ('particle by particle') — coin by coin matches this material, unit-by-unit saving. क्षणशः refers to time-moments and is paired with knowledge, not money. The verse's lesson on patient accumulation does apply, so 'neither' is wrong.

31. (D) Both adverbs stress gradual, patient gathering, so the lesson is that knowledge and wealth alike require steady effort over time. The verse honours, not dismisses, both; boasting gathers nothing; and knowledge and wealth remain distinct things, not interchangeable.

32. (B) The -वत्/-वान् suffix is possessive ('having'), so क्रियावान् = 'action-possessing = a doer'. It does not mean 'lacking' (that sense uses a- or हीन), nor 'opposing', nor 'a little'. The doer is praised over the mere talker.

33. (A) क्रिया is the action-noun of कृ 'to do'; क्रियावान् ('possessing action') therefore praises the actual doer of deeds. It is the opposite of a mere talker (विकत्थन), of one who avoids activity, and of one who only plans. Doing, not saying, is the point.

34. (A) The contrast is action versus mere talk: क्रियावान् acts, विकत्थन brags. It is not about wealth, not about curiosity vs character, and the doer is not 'always silent' (he simply lets deeds, not boasts, do the talking — and the boaster is certainly not 'useful').

35. (B) A acts and delivers — a क्रियावान् (doer). B brags about undone work — a विकत्थन (boaster). The chapter praises the doer, so A is commended and B criticised. The roles are not reversed, and merely 'turning in' or 'speaking' does not collapse the doer–boaster distinction, which turns on deeds versus brag.

36. (A) Praising the doer reflects the general value of कर्म (deeds) above कथन (mere talk). It is not the reverse, not a wealth-over-knowledge claim, and not a counsel of total silence — the chapter still prizes हितम् मनोहारि speech, only not empty boasting.

37. (D) 'Let your work speak' captures action over boasting — the क्रियावान् ideal. Loud promises without delivery describe the very विकत्थन being criticised; valuing a boast over a deed inverts the lesson; and counselling total inaction contradicts a chapter that praises doing.

38. (B) The boaster's flaw is privileging talk over action — failing the कर्म-over-कथन standard. His fault is not slow wealth-gathering (कणशः is in fact the right way), not excess curiosity (a virtue), and certainly not 'too much beneficial speech' (an empty boast is the opposite of हितम् वचः).

39. (C) सु ends in उ and उक्ति begins with उ; like (savarna) vowels उ + उ coalesce into the long ऊ, yielding सू + उक्तिः = सूक्तिः. It is not a diphथong सो-, not a semivowel स्व-, and the vowels do not stay separate — sandhi merges them. So सूक्तिः = 'a good/well-spoken saying'.

40. (C) सूक्तिः = सु + उक्ति, literally a 'good saying'; सूक्ष्म means 'subtle/fine/minute' and contains no उक्ति element. They merely share the सू- sound. So सूक्ष्म is not about saying, and the words are not synonyms; the presence or absence of उक्ति is the reliable test.

41. (A) Both सूक्तिः (सु+उक्ति, 'good saying') and सुभाषितम् (सु+भाषित, 'well-spoken') name a wise saying, so they are synonyms. Boasting (विकत्थनम्), a treasure-store (भाण्डागारः) and curiosity (कुतूहलम्) are unrelated meanings.

42. (B) सुभाषितम् = सु ('well/good') + भाषितम् (past participle of भाष् 'to speak') = 'well-spoken (saying)', mirroring सु + उक्ति in सूक्तिः. The 'six faults' reading confuses सु with षट्; 'hidden light' and 'proper noun' are invented misparsings.

43. (C) सूक्तिः is a good/well-spoken saying — a memorable nugget of wisdom. It is not a boast (विकत्थन), not an inventory of treasure (भाण्डागार), and not a meaningless subtle riddle (confusing it with सूक्ष्म). So a wise saying is expected.

44. (A) कुतूहलम्, its gloss कौतुकम्, and जिज्ञासा all centre on the desire to know — curiosity, the learner's virtue. None concerns boasting, rest, or greed for wealth. This eagerness drives the patient gathering of knowledge.

45. (D) जिज्ञासा (wish to know) and कुतूहलम् (curiosity) fuel the moment-by-moment (क्षणशः) gathering of knowledge that the verse describes. Curiosity drives learning, not the particle-by-particle gathering of wealth, not boasting, and certainly not the avoidance of effort.

46. (D) कुतूहलम् is the eager wish to know — shown by the curious child who questions and seeks answers. Boasting of knowing all (asking nothing), refusing to study, and hoarding coins all lack curiosity; the first even contradicts it. So the questioning, book-seeking child fits.

47. (A) Calling knowledge or सूक्तिs a भाण्डागारः frames them as a precious store worth accumulating and treasuring — fitting the क्षणशः/कणशः theme of patient gathering. It is not a store of boasts, not useless words to discard, and not an unreachable goal.

48. (B) A भाण्डागारः (storehouse) grows by additions over time, mirroring knowledge built up क्षणशः (moment by moment) — patient accumulation. It is not filled instantly, the metaphor is explicitly applied to knowledge (not only wealth), and a treasure-trove is full of value, unlike an empty boast.

49. (A) A library grown book by book over years is a treasure-trove (भाण्डागारः) of knowledge accumulated moment by moment (क्षणशः) — exactly the chapter's metaphor. It is not a store of boasts, not a mark of impossibility, and not meaningless subtle riddles.

50. (D) शीलम् (character/conduct) is held as the deepest value: it outranks outward advantages such as eloquence or riches. The chapter does not exalt wealth or speech above character, nor treat character as relevant only to kings — it is everyone's core virtue.

51. (C) Despite fine speech, useful counsel and wealth, dishonesty means he lacks शीलम् (good character) — the chapter's deepest value, which outranks all outward gifts. He clearly has speech, may have curiosity, and has wealth; what is missing is character.

52. (C) The repeated शीलम्, joined to honest speech (हितम् मनोहारि) and real deeds (क्रियावान्), points to a unified ideal: a person whose character shows in conduct, words and action. It is not about mere outward impressiveness, wealth alone, or eloquence alone.

53. (D) दुर्लभम् (rare but reachable) plus क्षणशः/कणशः (gathered bit by bit) together teach that worthwhile things take patient, sustained effort yet are attainable. They are not easy/instant, not impossible (that is अशक्यम्), and not won by boasting.

54. (A) Correct: दुर्लभम् 'rare', च 'and', क्रियावान् 'doer', सूक्तिः 'good saying'. The traps: दुर्लभम् is not 'impossible' (अशक्यम्) or 'common'; च is not 'or' (वा); क्रियावान् is not 'boaster' (विकत्थन); सूक्तिः is not 'subtle' (सूक्ष्म). Only the first set keeps every meaning right.

55. (A) Knowledge (विद्या) is gathered moment by moment (क्षणशः) and wealth (अर्थः) particle by particle (कणशः), one distinct adverb per noun. Swapping them, or using a single adverb for both, breaks the verse's structure.

56. (D) The chapter's explicit contrast is doer (क्रियावान्) versus boaster (विकत्थन). हितम् and मनोहारि are paired qualities, not opposites; सूक्तिः and सुभाषितम् are synonyms; क्षणशः and कणशः are parallel adverbs (different units, not opposites).

57. (D) च means 'and' (so 'or' is wrong); दुर्लभम् means 'rare/hard, not impossible' (so 'never found' is wrong); क्रियावान् means 'doer', the opposite of a talker/boaster (so 'great talker' is wrong). Every one of the three claims is mistaken.

58. (A) च demands BOTH qualities and दुर्लभम् means 'rare but reachable', so the correct restatement is 'both useful and pleasing speech is rare yet attainable'. The others drop the 'both' (using 'or'/'common'), confuse rare with impossible, or invent a harm claim.

59. (B) The chapter weaves together: हितम् मनोहारि speech, क्षणशः/कणशः patient gathering, क्रियावान् over विकत्थन (act, don't boast), and शीलम् (character) as the guide. The other summaries invert these — loud/quick/boastful, idle/luck-trusting, or pleasing-only — and miss the chapter's ethic.

60. (D) सूक्तिः (good saying), कुतूहलम् (curiosity), भाण्डागारः (treasured knowledge) and शीलम् (character) together commend wise speech, curiosity, valued learning and good character. They do not endorse boasting/laziness/hoarding, idleness/luck, or harshness/greed.
