(A) The residue left after boiling a clear filtrate is made of substances that were dissolved and survive evaporation — dissolved inorganic salts like nitrates and phosphates, which the chapter classes as inorganic impurities. Dissolving in water does not convert a salt into an organic compound; classification depends on the substance, not on whether it dissolves. And not everything in sewage is organic — the very point of the sorting is that dissolved inorganics form a separate class.

2. (B) Pathogens are living microbes that enter chiefly with human and medical waste, so a clinic and households dominate that class. Dissolved inorganic salts (nitrates, phosphates, metal salts) come largely from industrial effluent like a dye-mill and from fertiliser-laden field runoff, so those dominate the dissolved-inorganic class. Fields contribute nutrients and runoff, not the heaviest pathogen load. A dye-mill releases chemicals and dissolved salts, not the main microbe load, and a clinic certainly does not add only clean rainwater. The sources are not interchangeable; the whole reason sewage is mixed is that different sources add different dominant contaminant classes.

Grit removal never precedes screening, because screens protect the works from the bulky debris first. Aeration of dissolved organics happens after, not before, the scum-and-sludge separation in the settling tank. And settling/skimming come after grit removal, not before it.

The settling tank, not the aeration tank, removes fine suspended mud, and dissolved organic matter is consumed by aeration, not settled out or digested in the sludge digester.

Plastic-and-rag debris is not manure — it is bulky inorganic waste. Grit is inert mineral sand, not a soil nutrient. And not everything that came out of the sewage is fit for fields; the bar-screen debris and grit are wastes, while the digested sludge is the useful product.

55. (D) The aeration bacteria feed on dissolved organic matter; they do not reliably wipe out the pathogens, so a dedicated disinfection step is added to kill the surviving germs before release. Disinfection is for germ-killing, not for adding nutrients. It is not for settling grit, which is removed early in the grit chamber. And it is not unnecessary — the aerobic bacteria do not eliminate all germs, which is the whole reason disinfection exists. The claim misjudges what the aeration bacteria actually do.

56. (B) Dishwater first enters the underground sewer, then at the plant the physical units (bar screen and grit chamber) take out bulky debris and grit, then aeration's aerobic bacteria clean the dissolved organics, and finally the treated water can recharge groundwater. Aeration cannot precede the physical removal of debris and grit, which protect the works. The drop reaches the sewer before any treatment, so the sewer step comes before the physical units, not after. And groundwater recharge is the last stage, after the water has been cleaned, not before aeration.

57. (A) Putting chemicals into the sewer is not safe: they can poison the bacteria the plant needs, and chemicals not handled by treatment can still pass on to the river, so the river is not protected and the process is harmed too. Sewers do not fully neutralise everything before a river — that is wishful. The bar screen sieves bulky solids and cannot remove dissolved chemicals. And sewers do connect to treatment plants, so that reasoning is false. The defence ignores the damage to the bacteria and the carry-through to the river.

58. (D) Among these on-site options, the vermi-processing toilet routinely produces useful manure as earthworms convert the waste; the chemical toilet's role is to break down waste and cut odour and germs where no sewer exists; and a septic tank stores and partly treats waste, needing periodic pumping of its sludge. The chemical toilet does not make drinking water. A septic tank's normal output is not piped cooking gas. And the three do not all make equal biogas — that is not the defining product of any of them in this comparison. Only the vermi toilet's manure fits 'useful product of normal working.'

59. (A) The settling tank is physical — suspended solids settle out as sludge by gravity. The aeration tank is biological-aerobic — air-fed bacteria consume dissolved organics. The digester is biological-anaerobic — air-free bacteria decompose sludge to biogas. The other options scramble these: the settling tank does not grow bacteria or make biogas, the aeration tank does not settle sludge or remove grit, and the digester does not screen debris or settle sludge. Only the first statement pairs each stage with its true nature and material.

60. (A) The chapter's point is that prevention — keeping sewage away from drinking water — stops disease before it starts and is better than relying only on curing the sick; it does not declare medical treatment pointless. It does not claim doctors can cure no waterborne disease. Clean drains have nothing to do with hospitals burning biogas instead of treating patients. And it does not say sanitation completely replaces medical care; the lesson is that prevention is preferable, not that cure is worthless. Over-reading 'prevention is better' into 'cure is pointless' is the error.
