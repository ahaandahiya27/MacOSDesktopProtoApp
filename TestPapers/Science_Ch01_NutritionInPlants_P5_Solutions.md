## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** Halving the light halves photosynthesis: 24 → 12 units fixed. Respiration is unchanged at 5 units released. The air sees the net effect: 12 taken in minus 5 put back = 7 removed, a net fall of 7. The idea that respiration stops in light is the day-respiration misconception — it never switches off, so 19 is wrong. Counting only the 12 photosynthetic units ignores the 5 the leaf simultaneously returns to the same air. Halving the light does not silence photosynthesis altogether, so treating respiration as the only active process and predicting a rise of 5 is wrong.

**2. (C)** At the compensation point photosynthetic uptake equals respiratory release. Raising the light speeds photosynthesis while respiration stays roughly steady, so uptake now outpaces release and the air loses carbon dioxide — a net fall. The compensation point is not a fixed reading; it is just the balance for one particular light level, so 'still zero' is wrong. Brighter light does not drive respiration up to match; respiration is not controlled by light intensity that way. High light makes the leaf photosynthesise more, not shut down to 'protect chlorophyll' — that is invented behaviour.

**3. (A)** Plant dry mass is mostly glucose, starch and related substances whose carbon comes from carbon dioxide fixed in the leaves; roots take up water and dissolved minerals only, never whole solid soil that 'hardens' into wood — so the 'solid soil through the roots' line is the error, and that mass belongs with the carbon-dioxide share. Saying minerals dominate is the food-from-soil misconception the chapter overturns. Water does supply the hydrogen built into glucose, so denying it any mass is wrong, and oxygen is a product released, not a building block absorbed. Minerals are needed for health but form only a small fraction of dry weight, so calling them most of it is wrong.

**4. (D)** A green fern makes its own food, so it is an autotroph and the producer that begins a food chain — but it still draws carbon dioxide, water and light from its surroundings, so that label holds too. Being a food-maker, it is not a heterotroph, so that one is false: three of four are correct. The claim that an autotroph needs nothing is the independence misconception — raw materials are still required. Respiring at night does not make a plant a heterotroph; heterotrophy means taking ready-made food, which it does not. Producer status depends on making food from sunlight, not on body size, so the 'largest organism' rule is invented.

**5. (B)** In photosynthesis water and carbon dioxide are the raw materials; oxygen is released as a product and traces to the water the plant used. The carbon dioxide's carbon goes into glucose, so attributing the escaping oxygen to that gas confuses raw material with the released product. Glucose is the food made and stored, not a source the leaf breaks apart to bubble oxygen away during food-making. Soil minerals are taken up for health but are not the source of the oxygen gas a leaf releases.

**6. (A)** The written equation is photosynthesis run backwards: it lists the food and oxygen as raw materials and the gases as products. Photosynthesis takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out glucose and oxygen, so swapping the two sides fixes both faults at once. Sunlight and chlorophyll are the correct conditions above the arrow, so changing them is wrong. Starch is a later storage form, not the immediate raw material, and nitrogen is not part of this equation. Leaving the left side unchanged keeps glucose and oxygen as raw materials, which is exactly the error.

**7. (A)** Carbon dioxide is a raw material; supplying more of it lets a well-lit leaf build more glucose and store more starch, deepening the iodine colour. But the energy that drives photosynthesis is light — the gas is consumed, not burned for energy. The extra colour reflects more starch actually made, not iodine reacting differently to dissolved gas. Carbon dioxide cannot stand in for sunlight as the energy source; without light no starch forms however much gas is present.

**8. (C)** Starch forms only where every requirement is met. The white centre lacks chlorophyll, so it never makes starch, covered or not; the foil-covered green is starved of light. The one region with chlorophyll AND light is the uncovered green ring, and only it turns blue-black. Chlorophyll alone cannot make starch in shade — light is also needed — so the whole-ring answer is wrong. The uncovered white tissue still lacks chlorophyll and cannot photosynthesise, so 'both uncovered regions' is wrong. Pale, chlorophyll-free tissue makes no starch, so the white-centre answer is backwards.

**9. (A)** Photosynthesis needs chlorophyll. A red leaf only looks non-green because a red pigment masks chlorophyll that is still present, so it makes starch and tests positive; a white patch contains no chlorophyll, so it makes none and stays brown. Calling red pigment 'a kind of chlorophyll' is false — colour is not the same as the pigment. White tissue does not make and hide starch; with no chlorophyll there is no photosynthesis to make any. A red leaf makes its own food rather than drawing it ready-made from the trunk, so that explanation misstates how leaves feed.

**10. (D)** On one variegated leaf the green and white parts share the same light, air and water; only chlorophyll differs, so comparing their starch tests isolates chlorophyll. Foil over half a green leaf changes LIGHT between the halves, not chlorophyll, so it isolates light instead. Dark-versus-light whole plants again test light, not the pigment. Sealing a plant and measuring gas demonstrates gas exchange but does not single out chlorophyll as the variable. Only the variegated-leaf comparison cleanly isolates chlorophyll.

**11. (B)** Both discs had starch; the difference is access. Boiling in water softens and breaks the cells, and alcohol removes the waxy green chlorophyll, so iodine can penetrate and reveal the starch in the boiled disc. The raw disc's intact waxy surface blocks iodine, so its real starch goes undetected. The boiling does not create starch — the question states both discs already had it. Alcohol removes chlorophyll; it does not add food. Iodine detects starch by turning blue-black rather than destroying it, so the 'iodine destroys starch' idea is invented chemistry.

**12. (B)** Destarching empties the leaves of pre-existing starch, so any blue-black at the end must be new starch made during the experiment. Skip it and stored starch could give the carbon-dioxide-free leaf a false positive, wrecking the comparison — so the step is essential, not busywork. Potassium hydroxide removes carbon dioxide but does not by itself guarantee the result is interpretable without a clean starch baseline. Destarching empties starch; it does not 're-green' a leaf. Stomata handle carbon dioxide regardless of stored starch, so destarching does not speed gas entry.

**13. (A)** Only the lamp distance changed, and the rate falls in step with the weakening light, showing light intensity controls photosynthesis; chlorophyll is not consumed by dimming and works again when the lamp returns. So the loss-of-chlorophyll story is wrong. The bubbles are oxygen, a product released in light — carbon dioxide is taken in, not bubbled out. The plant sits in water throughout, so it cannot 'run short of water'; the limiting factor is the light reaching it.

**14. (C)** Stomata are the pores for gas exchange and water-vapour loss. Closing them cuts carbon-dioxide intake (i), which starves photosynthesis (ii), and reduces transpiration (iv). But light does not enter through the stomata — it passes through the leaf surface regardless — so (iii) is false. Treating closed pores as a light seal is the mistake in the 'all four' and the '(iii) and (iv)' options. Stomata control gas exchange as well as water loss, so limiting their job to transpiration alone is too narrow.

**15. (A)** Carbon dioxide enters mainly through the lower-surface stomata; greasing the lower surface clogs them, starving leaf 2 of carbon dioxide so it makes the least starch. Grease on the upper surface blocks few stomata and does not stop light, which passes through the surface anyway, so leaf 1 is little affected. The untouched leaf 3 keeps all its stomata and makes the most starch, not the least. The boiling steps clean the leaf before testing, so grease does not simply 'stop iodine reacting' for all three; the real difference is how much starch each leaf made.

**16. (B)** The droplets are water vapour lost by transpiration through the stomata, then condensed in the bag. A living leaf's stomata transpire; a dead leaf's no longer do, so fewer droplets form — neither leaf manufactures water. Water is a raw material the plant uses, not something it makes from sugar. The sealed, identical bags rule out leaked rain as the difference. Xylem carries water inside the plant but does not squirt liquid out of the surface; loss is as vapour through stomata.

**17. (D)** Water (with the dye) rises from the roots through the xylem, so the dye appears high in the leaves; food made in the lit leaf (the labelled sugar) travels away through the phloem, here down to the roots. The role-swap option reverses both tissues' jobs. A plant has two distinct transport tissues, so 'xylem for both' is wrong. Stomata are gas-exchange pores and chlorophyll is a pigment, not transport channels, so that option misnames the structures.

**18. (C)** Phloem carries food made in the leaves downward and outward; the ring breaks that path, so food coming down from the leaves cannot pass and accumulates on the leaf side — just ABOVE the ring. Below the ring is cut off from the descending food, so no pile-up there, making the gardener's prediction wrong. The leaves still get water through the intact xylem and keep photosynthesising, so they do not stop making food. The xylem carries water upward, not the leaf's sugars downward, so it cannot deliver sugar below the cut.

**19. (A)** A broad, flat, thin leaf spreads a large area to the sun, and chlorophyll-rich cells near the lit upper surface catch that light — the chapter presents this as an adaptation for capturing sunlight to make food. Water loss does occur through stomata, but the shape's purpose in this chapter is food-making, not cooling. A thin leaf is not a water store — fleshy organs store water. The shape makes a leaf catch wind, not resist it, so 'heavy to resist wind' is wrong.

**20. (C)** Carbon dioxide falls only where photosynthesis outpaces respiration. The green moss and green alga both have chlorophyll, so in bright light they pull more carbon dioxide out than they release — two jars. Cuscuta lacks chlorophyll and, with no host, only respires, so it raises carbon dioxide; counting it as a photosynthesiser is wrong. A bracket fungus is a heterotroph that only respires, so it also raises carbon dioxide — 'all four' is wrong. The moss has chlorophyll and does photosynthesise; its size does not stop it lowering carbon dioxide, so 'only the alga' undercounts.

**21. (C)** Cuscuta thrives only on a LIVING host and dies on dead wood — the mark of a parasite. The fungus thrives on DEAD wood and harms nothing alive — the mark of a saprotroph. Calling Cuscuta a saprotroph contradicts its death on the dead post; it cannot feed on dead matter. Lumping both as parasites ignores that the fungus needs no living host. Cuscuta harms its host rather than helping it, so it is no symbiont, and the fungus has no chlorophyll, so it is no autotroph.

**22. (D)** Parasitism harms one partner; in both the lichen and the nodule each side benefits — the alga and bacteria gain shelter and supplies, the fungus and plant gain food or usable nitrogen — so both are symbiosis. The nodule is a genuine mutual partnership, not the plant parasitising the bacteria. Fungi and bacteria are living organisms, so denying that either is a partnership is false. Neither food-maker is 'robbed': each gives food and receives something it needs in return.

**23. (A)** The lichen's alga has chlorophyll and makes the partnership's food, while the fungus supplies water, minerals and shelter — so the alga fits 'makes its own food in an alliance'. Rhizobium does not photosynthesise; it fixes nitrogen and takes food from the legume, so it is not the food-maker. In a lichen it is the alga, not the fungus, that makes food. Cuscuta lacks chlorophyll and makes no food at all; it steals everything from its host.

**24. (D)** The pea and the pitcher plant are green and photosynthesise; the pea gets usable nitrogen from Rhizobium in its nodules, the pitcher plant gets nitrogen by digesting trapped insects. Cuscuta lacks chlorophyll, makes no food, and draws nitrogen with all its nutrition from a living host. Ordinary plants cannot fix air nitrogen through their leaves, so the 'all fix it through leaves' claim is false. The pea and pitcher plant clearly make their own food, so 'none makes its own food' is wrong. Cuscuta is the one that cannot photosynthesise, so calling it the sole food-maker inverts the facts.

**25. (B)** The green leaves photosynthesise, supplying energy-rich food, so the plant survives without insects — it does not starve, because insects were never its main food, only its nitrogen source. Losing that nitrogen source in poor soil leaves it short of nitrogen, hence the pale, stunted growth. 'Grows normally' ignores that nitrogen, though not a same-day photosynthesis raw material, is still needed for healthy green growth. The plant does not become a parasite on neighbours; it remains a green food-maker simply lacking nitrogen.

**26. (D)** Carbon dioxide comes from the AIR through the stomata, not from soil bacteria, so the bean keeps photosynthesising and does not die for lack of it — the day-prediction is wrong. What it loses is usable soil nitrogen, needed for healthy green growth, so it grows pale and stunted. Killing nitrogen-fixing bacteria removes a nitrogen SOURCE, it does not free extra nitrogen, so faster growth is wrong. Nitrogen is needed for growth and colour but is not a same-day photosynthesis raw material, so 'cannot photosynthesise at all' overstates its role.

**27. (B)** It is one element, nitrogen, but most plants cannot use the gaseous form; nitrogen-fixing bacteria (or fertiliser) must convert it into a usable soil form before roots can absorb it — so the second student is right. The 'different element' claim is false; the element is the same, just in an unusable gaseous state. Tomatoes, like all plants, need nitrogen for healthy green growth, so denying the need is wrong. Since the 'different element' half is false, 'both are right' cannot hold.

**28. (C)** Continuous wheat drains soil nitrogen. Growing a legume, whose Rhizobium nodules fix atmospheric nitrogen, and ploughing it back restores nitrogen naturally — the chapter's crop-rotation remedy. Denser wheat drains nitrogen faster, not restores it. Water does not carry nitrogen gas into the soil in a usable form; fixing requires bacteria, not flooding. Burning stubble does not replace nitrogen — much is lost to the air as the stubble burns — so ash is not a reliable nitrogen return.

**29. (A)** Green plants capture sunlight's energy as food in photosynthesis; that captured energy passes up the chain as one organism eats another, so the origin is sunlight, not minerals. Minerals support the rice's health but carry no energy into the chain. Oxygen is used to RELEASE energy from food during respiration, but it is not itself the energy source. Prey animals merely transfer energy that originally came from the plant; eating does not create new energy.

**30. (B)** Light is the algae's energy source; persistent shade starves them, so the producers decline first. Their consumers, the tadpoles, then run short of food and decline, and the fish above them follow — the impact travels up from the base. The fish do not increase; losing the chain's base harms the whole chain. Less light means less algae, not softer, more nutritious algae for the tadpoles. Algae are autotrophs needing light, not silt particles, so 'food from the silt' is false.

**31. (D)** In the light the terrarium balanced because photosynthesis and respiration recycled each other's gases. In the dark photosynthesis stops but respiration continues, taking in oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide with nothing to replace or remove them, so oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises. A plant does not store and 'let out' oxygen at night — respiration uses oxygen. Respiration never pauses, so 'nothing changes' is wrong. Photosynthesis needs light, so no 'night-time food-making' absorbs carbon dioxide in the dark.

**32. (D)** In light the plant's photosynthesis adds oxygen and prolongs the flame. In the dark there is no photosynthesis, so no oxygen is added, while the plant respires and uses oxygen — competing with the flame, which goes out sooner than candle-alone. A plant releases oxygen in the light, not the dark, so 'most oxygen when dark' is wrong. The plant does affect the dark jar by respiring, so 'no effect' is wrong. A flame needs oxygen, not carbon dioxide, to keep burning, so supplying carbon dioxide would not help it.

**33. (D)** A zero-crossing is the instant photosynthetic oxygen output equals respiratory oxygen use. Dimming the light drops photosynthesis below respiration, so oxygen used now exceeds oxygen made and the meter shows a net intake. The crossing is only a balance for that light level, not a fixed point, so dimming does disturb it. Respiration is not slowed by dimming light, so it does not 'release spare oxygen'. Respiration never stops with the light, so both processes do not halt together.

**34. (B)** If the chlorophyll were destroyed, the plant could not make fresh starch — yet a 6-hour-lit disc does turn blue-black, proving the chlorophyll still works. The dark step only emptied stored starch (destarching), giving a clean baseline. A negative starch test means no starch, not lost chlorophyll, so the conclusion is unsound. Iodine works on any leaf that actually contains starch; the dark disc tested negative because it had none, not because iodine fails. The dark removes starch rather than adding it, so the dark disc rightly tested negative.

**35. (A)** Boiling in water softens the leaf and breaks open its cells so the later alcohol and iodine steps can reach the tissue — this is the correct claim. A positive iodine test shows starch, which can occur in chlorophyll-free tissue such as a potato tuber, so it does not prove chlorophyll. Removing chlorophyll is the ALCOHOL step's job, not the water boil — a classic mix-up. Skipping destarching lets leftover starch give false positives, so it certainly does affect whether the result can be trusted.

**36. (A)** On one uniformly green leaf with the same air and water throughout, foil over half changes only the LIGHT between the lit and shaded halves, so comparing them isolates light. A variegated leaf differs in chlorophyll between its parts, so it isolates chlorophyll, not light. The carbon-dioxide-free versus normal-air pair isolates carbon dioxide. The nitrogen-soil comparison tests a growth nutrient, not the same-day light requirement. Only the half-foil set-up cleanly isolates light.

**37. (B)** A potato tuber has no chlorophyll yet tests deep blue-black because it stores starch, so a positive test does NOT prove the tissue is green — the first half fails. A raw leaf disc full of starch can test brown if its waxy, unsoftened surface keeps iodine out, so a brown result does NOT prove no food was made — the second half fails too. Hence both halves are wrong, and neither counter-example obeys the rule.

**38. (C)** Photosynthesis requires light as its energy source. With everything present but light, no food is made, so the destarched leaf stays starch-free and tests brown — warmth cannot stand in for light, so the classmate's prediction fails. Chlorophyll captures light energy but cannot make starch in the dark on its own. Darkness does not change iodine chemistry; iodine turns blue-black only where starch is present, and here there is none.

**39. (B)** Iodine turns blue-black with starch. The potato and bread, both plant storage foods, are starch-rich and react; egg white is a protein and oil is a fat, neither rich in starch, so they barely change. A weak reaction means little starch, not the richest source, so that option is backwards. Iodine detects starch in cooked, dead tissue too — the boiled potato and baked bread react well — so 'only living plant cells' is false. The colour indicates starch, not chlorophyll, which is the green pigment and unrelated to this reaction.

**40. (A)** The mango tree builds food from carbon dioxide and water using light — an autotroph. The cow cannot make food and eats ready-made plant material — a heterotroph. The mushroom has no chlorophyll and feeds on dead, decaying matter — a heterotroph of the saprotroph kind. The tree clearly does build food from inorganic materials, so 'all three heterotrophs' is wrong. The mushroom makes no food, so it is not an autotroph alongside the tree. A cow digests grass; it does not photosynthesise, so it is not the autotroph.

**41. (D)** A saprotroph feeds on DEAD and decaying matter, not on a living host — that description actually fits a PARASITE, so it is the false sentence. An autotroph does build food from inorganic raw materials using light — true. A parasite does take food from a living host and harm it — true. An insectivorous plant does photosynthesise and trap insects mainly for nitrogen — true. Only the saprotroph sentence is wrong.

**42. (A)** The log-dweller thrives on dead, decaying wood and harms nothing alive — a saprotroph. The vine dies on lifeless metal and flourishes only on a living host — the mark of a parasite, not a saprotroph, since a saprotroph could feed on dead matter. So 'two saprotrophs' is wrong: only one fits. Dead wood cannot be 'harmed', so the log-dweller is no parasite. Both lack chlorophyll and make no food, so neither is an autotroph that 'lost its colour'.

**43. (B)** In bright light photosynthesis outpaces respiration: the plant releases more oxygen than it uses and consumes more carbon dioxide than it makes, so oxygen ends slightly higher and carbon dioxide slightly lower. Respiration does not outpace photosynthesis in bright light, so the reversed answer is wrong. Plants do not convert oxygen into nitrogen — nitrogen is inert here and unchanged. Both gases cannot rise: each process's product is the other's raw material, so the net effect is oxygen up, carbon dioxide down, not both up.

**44. (C)** Producers begin food chains because they capture sunlight and create new food, adding fresh energy. A saprotroph makes no new food; it only decomposes matter that already held energy, so it is not a starting producer. Saprotrophs do feature in food webs as decomposers, so 'cannot take part at all' is too strong. A mushroom has no chlorophyll and makes no food, so 'makes far more food' is false. Green plants make their own food and do not eat mushrooms to begin a chain.

**45. (B)** Even a pure carnivore depends on plants indirectly: its prey (and its prey's prey) ultimately ate plants, so the energy in every meal traces to plant food, and the oxygen the snake breathes is released by plant photosynthesis — the dependence runs through the whole chain. A snake does not need to eat plants directly for this to hold. The claim that only herbivores depend on plants ignores this indirect chain. Snakes get no minerals 'straight from the soil'; the key dependence is plant-made food and plant-released oxygen.

**46. (C)** Rhizobium fixes nitrogen and takes food from the legume; it does not photosynthesise, so it is the one non-autotroph. The mango tree, green alga and photosynthetic microbes all make their own food. So exactly one is not an autotroph. Insect-trapping does not strip the pitcher plant of autotrophy, and lacking ordinary leaves does not stop the cactus stem from photosynthesising — so two and three overcount. Rhizobium does not photosynthesise, so 'all six' is wrong.

**47. (C)** As light fades, photosynthesis (which adds oxygen) nearly stops, but respiration (which uses oxygen) continues, so oxygen consumption now exceeds production and the level falls. **48. (B)** An autotroph makes its own food with chlorophyll and light. Releasing oxygen bubbles when lit is a hallmark OF photosynthesis, again supporting rather than disproving the claim. Only the no-chlorophyll, host-fed observation disproves it.

**49. (B)** Starch forms only where chlorophyll AND light coincide. The only region with both chlorophyll and light is the uncovered green border, so only it turns blue-black. **50. (D)** Both interventions cut off carbon dioxide. Potassium hydroxide absorbs it from Leaf X's air; grease clogs the lower-surface stomata through which carbon dioxide enters Leaf Y. **51. (D)** Xylem carries water (and dissolved minerals) UP from the roots; phloem carries the FOOD made in the leaves outward and downward. The student swapped both the substances and the directions, so NEITHER claim is correct. Xylem carries water, not food, so the 'xylem carries food up' half is wrong. Phloem carries food, not water, so the 'phloem carries water down' half is wrong. The two tubes have distinct jobs and do not simply share the work, so 'both correct' is false.

**52. (C)** The shaded leaf transpired little but still tested positive for starch, so it clearly made food despite low water loss — disproving the student. The two are distinct processes: transpiration is water vapour leaving the stomata, photosynthesis is food being built, and one can be low while the other proceeds. Since the shaded leaf made food with low transpiration, transpiration is not required for photosynthesis and they need not rise and fall together. They are not one process under two names — the observation of low loss with successful food-making separates them.

**53. (C)** A seed carries a food store that fuels early growth even without light, so the shoot does NOT come from dark photosynthesis. Photosynthesis needs light, so in the dark the seedling cannot make more food once the seed's store is used up, and it dies — not from lack of air. So both halves of the child's reasoning are wrong. The death is from exhausted stored food, not thirst. Chlorophyll cannot make starch in the dark, so 'dark photosynthesis' is impossible. A damp drawer supplies moisture, not ready-made food, so it did not feed the seedling.

**54. They do not take ready-made food from the soil; that is the food-from-soil misconception the chapter overturns. Ordinary green plants do not trap insects for energy; they photosynthesise using light. **55. Taking food ready-made from the soil is the central misconception the chapter overturns. Animals cannot photosynthesise, so plants and animals do not make food the same way. **57. **58. Light is the energy source but cannot substitute for the missing carbon-source gas. Water supplies hydrogen, not the carbon, so it cannot replace carbon dioxide. **59. (B)** In light, the plant's photosynthesis recycled the carbon dioxide and supplied oxygen for both organisms. In the dark photosynthesis stops, yet the plant and the snail both respire — using oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide — so oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises. Neither organism 'stores and releases' oxygen at night; respiration consumes oxygen. Respiration never pauses, so 'nothing changes' is wrong. Without light there is no photosynthesis to absorb carbon dioxide, so it rises rather than falls.

**60. (D)** Light is the energy source for photosynthesis; as it fades the plant makes less and less food despite ample carbon dioxide, water and warmth, so it weakens and dies — light is the limiting factor. Carbon dioxide is a raw material the plant uses, not a poison, so the student's diagnosis is wrong. Keeping carbon dioxide topped up does not consume chlorophyll; the two are unrelated. A green plant does not turn into a saprotroph feeding on chamber walls — that is not a real mode by which a green plant lives.
