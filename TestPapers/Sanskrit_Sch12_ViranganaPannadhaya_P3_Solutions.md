## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** Panna was indeed a heroic woman (वीराङ्गना), a wet-nurse (धाया), and lived in the षोडशे शतके (16th century) — those three hold. The single false claim is that she was the prince's birth-mother: she was his foster-mother / nurse, not his mother. So exactly three of the four are correct. Counting all four ignores the birth-mother error; counting only one or two undercounts the true statements.

**2. (C)** Udai Singh II belongs to the Maharana lineage of Mewar, and the events are placed in the षोडशे शतके, i.e. the 1500s. The Maurya (3rd c. BCE), Gupta (4th c. CE) and a 17th-century Mughal framing all misplace the century given in the text as the sixteenth.

**3. (D)** Though she served as the prince's धाया, the chapter's whole point is that her act of supreme sacrifice raises her to a वीराङ्गना (heroine) honoured in history. 'Servant' captures the job but not the heroism. She was not the queen, not an army commander, and she certainly did work in the palace.

**4. (A)** A historical account is grounded in real people, places and time — here Udai Singh, Mewar and the 16th century. Talking animals point to a fable, a step-by-step process to a science lesson, and an undated imaginary land to fiction — none of which fit this chapter.

**5. (B)** Panna saved the prince Udai Singh, who lived and later ruled because Panna's son was killed in his place. Panna's own son died and so could not become king; the assassin was the killer; the question asks who became king through her act, which is the rescued prince.

**6. (B)** The wicked rival first forms the evil plot (R); learning of it, Panna substitutes her own son (Q); then the assassin strikes the boy in the bed (P); and finally history honours Panna in golden letters (S). Putting the swap before the plot (Q first), or the strike before the swap (P before Q), or the honour first (S first) all break the cause-and-effect chain.

**7. (D)** The सप्तमी (locative) typically means 'in/at/during', so षोडशे शतके = 'in the sixteenth century', fixing when the story happens. 'To' would be the dative (चतुर्थी), 'by/with' the instrumental (तृतीया), and 'of' the genitive (षष्ठी) — none of which matches a locative ending here.

**8. (A)** धाया = धात्री = a wet-nurse / foster-mother (one who nourishes the child). The 'patience/courage' sense belongs to धैर्य (a different word), a bedroom is शयनागारम्, and a deceptive scheme is कुतन्त्रम् / छल.

**9. (B)** The confusion appears when धाया (wet-nurse) is used where धैर्य (courage/patience) is intended — 'great धाया on the battlefield' should be धैर्य. The other three sentences each use the right word for the right sense, so they reveal no confusion.

**10. (A)** धाया and धैर्य share consonants but differ in a vowel, and they mean entirely different things — so vowel changes are meaning-bearing. Treating vowels as interchangeable, assuming identical consonants force identical meaning, or calling vowels merely decorative all contradict this distinction.

**11. (C)** Panna was the prince's धाया, his foster-mother / wet-nurse, while his birth-mother (प्रसूता) was someone else. She was not his birth-mother, not both, and not a tutor; this distinction is why placing her own son in the bed was such a personal sacrifice.

**12. (D)** धाया is a common noun for the role 'wet-nurse'; 'Panna was the धाया of Udai Singh' uses it as a role-title. Treating धाया as a personal name, applying it to the prince, or reading it as a place all misuse the word.

**13. (D)** धैर्य names a virtue (courage/patience); applying it to a cowardly, deceitful plot misuses a praise-word. धैर्य does not mean wet-nurse (that is धाया), nor a bedroom (शयनागारम्), nor deception (छल) — so only the 'virtue misapplied' reading is right.

**14. (B)** दुष्ट ('wicked') + बुद्धि ('intellect') = an intellect turned to evil, i.e. cunning but corrupt. It does NOT mean 'no intelligence' (that would be मूर्ख / a stupidity word), nor virtuous wisdom, nor bravery and loyalty.

**15. (C)** Designing a कुतन्त्रम् shows planning ability, so the flaw is moral (दुष्ट), not intellectual (मूर्ख). He was not merely foolish, not good-hearted, and certainly not plan-less — the plot itself was deliberate.

**16. (A)** The instrumental case (तृतीया, ending -ेन here) expresses the means: छलेन = 'by/through deception', the method of attack. It is not a destination (dative), a possessor (genitive) or a place (locative).

**17. (D)** Preferring deception to an open, honest challenge marks cowardice — he used a hidden assassin rather than fight fairly. The honourable, generous and loyal readings all contradict a plot to murder a sleeping child by trickery.

**18. (B)** कु- ('bad') + तन्त्रम् ('plan/device') = a wicked scheme, apt for a murder plot. कु- does not mean 'great', 'new' or 'royal'; the prefix's job here is precisely to brand the plan as evil.

**19. (B)** The infinitive मारयितुम् ('to kill') gives the intention behind coming, not an accomplished fact — and in the story the prince was NOT killed (the substituted boy was). It is not past tense (that is अमारयत्), does not mean 'protected', and is not a name.

**20. (C)** The plot aimed at the prince; Panna's substitution meant her own son lay in the bed and was killed instead. Panna survived (she was not the target), the rival was the schemer (not a victim), and a death certainly occurred — so only the prince-targeted, son-died reading is correct.

**21. (C)** The trick succeeded because the assassin assumed whoever lay in the prince's bed was the prince — he could not distinguish the two boys. He did not know the truth, the prince had not fled (he was hidden/safe within the plan), and Panna would never have revealed the prince.

**22. (B)** The purpose-word मारयितुम् ('to kill') fixes the deadly intent, ruling out a mere scare. धाया (nurse), शयनागारम् (bedroom) and षोडशम् (sixteenth) carry no information about the rival's intent at all.

**23. (B)** Because the killer expected the sleeping prince in his own bedroom, substituting the son in that very bed guaranteed the mistaken-identity killing. A bedroom is not a barracks, not outside the palace, and not a throne-room — those readings misunderstand both the word and the tactic.

**24. (D)** The chapter's core fact: the prince was saved (lived), the substituted son was killed (died), and Panna survived. Any version that kills the prince, spares the son, or kills Panna mis-states the central tragedy and triumph of the story.

**25. (D)** The famous, painful fact is that Panna gave up her son, not her own life — she lived on. She did not 'sacrifice the assassin' (a villain, not a sacrifice), and she certainly did not save her son by giving up the prince — that reverses the whole story.

**26. (C)** Most heroes risk their own lives; Panna's rarer, harder choice was to let her own son die so another woman's child (the prince) might live. Risking only oneself, giving up money, or losing a day's work all fall far short of that uniqueness.

**27. (B)** त्याग = giving up / renunciation, and सर्वस्व = one's everything, so सर्वस्वत्याग = surrendering all one has. It is the opposite of gaining; it is far more than a token gift; and it is the opposite of keeping wealth safe.

**28. (C)** सर्वस्वम् means one's complete own; for a mother the dearest 'everything' is her child, and Panna gave her son's life. It was not gold, not merely her job, and certainly not a 'golden letter' (which belongs to the स्वर्णिमाक्षरैः idiom, not to what she surrendered).

**29. (D)** सर्वस्वत्याग means giving up ALL one has; donating one coin while keeping everything is the very opposite. Giving an only child, surrendering everything, and Panna's act each are total sacrifices that do fit the term.

**30. (D)** त्याग is renunciation; सर्वस्वत्याग is renouncing one's all, the exact opposite of receiving a reward. It does not mean reward, battle, or palace — Panna's act was a loss freely given, not a gain.

**31. (B)** Both are heroic, but the distinctive feature of Panna's सर्वस्वत्याग is that what she gave was her own child's life, not just her own. The weapon, payment, and army-versus-man contrasts are irrelevant to why her sacrifice is singled out.

**32. (C)** Her fidelity to the trust placed in a धाया — guarding the prince — drove her to pay the unbearable price of her son's life, which is loyalty of the highest order. She sought no crown, was not coerced, and her grief shows the loss was anything but welcome.

**33. (A)** The accurate summary: prince saved, son substituted and killed, Panna alive. Swapping the victims (prince killed instead), killing both boys to save herself, or redirecting the assassin onto the rival all distort the events the chapter records.

**34. (A)** स्वर्णिमाक्षरैः ('in golden letters') idiomatically marks lasting, glorious remembrance. It is not about secrecy, forgetting, or bureaucratic records — the gold stands for honour that endures.

**35. (C)** Instrumental plural -ैः gives 'by/with [many] golden letters', so the name is written WITH golden letters. It is not a dative ('to'), genitive ('of'), or a singular locative ('in one letter') — the plural instrumental is the key.

**36. (C)** The locative plural -सु means 'among/in', placing Panna within the ranks of वीराङ्गनाः (heroic women). 'By' would be instrumental, 'of' genitive, and 'to' dative — the locative simply situates her in that honoured set.

**37. (A)** The idiom fits because her sacrifice merits undying honour — 'golden letters' is figurative for glory, not literal gold work. She did not write with gold ink, trade in gold, or work as a goldsmith; reading it literally misses the metaphor.

**38. (D)** वीर ('brave/hero') + अङ्गना ('woman') = a brave woman, the exact honour for Panna. It is not a place, not a weapon, and not a king (she did not rule); the feminine अङ्गना is what makes वीराङ्गना the right word over a generic वीरः.

**39. (C)** वीराङ्गना praises her as a heroine, while कुतन्त्रकारिणी ('maker of an evil plot') would brand her wicked — the two cannot both be true of her. 'Woman + nurse', 'brave + loyal', and 'heroic + honoured' are all compatible pairs, so they create no contradiction.

**40. (C)** The infinitive (purpose, 'to kill') is मारयितुम्, marked by -तुम्. अमारयत् is past ('he killed'), मारयिष्यति is future ('he will kill'), and मारयतु is imperative ('let him kill') — only the -तुम् form is the infinitive.

**41. (D)** मारयिष्यति is future ('he will kill'). The infinitive 'to kill' is मारयितुम्, the past 'he killed' is अमारयत्, and the imperative 'let him kill' is मारयतु — each a distinct form.

**42. (D)** To express purpose ('came to kill'), Sanskrit uses the infinitive मारयितुम् with the verb of coming. The past अमारयत्, future मारयिष्यति, and imperative मारयतु cannot serve as a purpose-infinitive after आगच्छत्.

**43. (A)** रक्षितुम् carries -तुम् and means 'to protect' — an infinitive like मारयितुम्. अरक्षत् is past, रक्षति present, and रक्षतु imperative; none has the infinitive -तुम् ending.

**44. (C)** The infinitive states the intended action, not its outcome — you would need a finite past verb (like अमारयत्) to assert it happened. मारयितुम् is not plural, not past (so it does not assert success), and not a noun naming a person.

**45. (B)** अमारयत् is genuine past tense ('he killed'). मारयितुम् is the infinitive (not past), मारयतु is imperative (not future), and मारयिष्यति is future (not an infinitive) — so only the अमारयत् pairing is correct.

**46. (A)** An infinitive like मारयितुम् typically completes a verb of wishing/coming/intending — here ऐच्छत् ('wished'), giving 'wished to kill'. It does not depend on the subject (दुष्टबुद्धिः) or the object (बालकम्), and an infinitive of purpose does not stand isolated from a governing verb.

**47. (D)** मारयतु is an imperative ('let him kill'), not an infinitive; 'to kill' is मारयितुम् (-तुम्). The student is wrong that मारयतु means 'to kill', and 'to kill' is neither अमारयत् (past) nor मारयिष्यति (future).

**48. (A)** 'In the sixteenth century' needs the ordinal in the locative: षोडशे शतके. 'षोडश शतानि' counts sixteen centuries (cardinal + plural), 'षोडश शतके' mismatches a cardinal with a locative noun, and षष्ठे शतके means the SIXTH century — not the sixteenth.

**49. (C)** षष्ठे = 'sixth', a different century from the षोडशे ('sixteenth') the chapter gives. षष्ठे does not mean sixteenth; षोडश is the CARDINAL 'sixteen' (not the ordinal needed); and षष्टिः means 'sixty' — only षोडशे fits 'sixteenth'.

**50. (B)** षष्ठे शतके is the 6th century and षोडशे शतके the 16th; the difference is 16 − 6 = 10 centuries earlier. It is not one century (that would be 15th), not sixty (a confusion with षष्टिः 'sixty'), and certainly not 'later'.

**51. (D)** The correct matches: षोडश = sixteen (cardinal), षोडशम् = sixteenth (ordinal), षष्ठम् = sixth (ordinal), षष्टिः = sixty. The other rows scramble these, e.g. confusing the cardinal/ordinal of sixteen or mixing 'sixth' with 'sixty'.

**52. (A)** गृह means 'house', so राजगृह = royal house/palace (a place); पुत्र means 'son', so राजपुत्र = king's son = prince (a person). Reversing them, or calling both places or both persons, ignores the meanings of गृह and पुत्र.

**53. (D)** राजपुत्रस्य शयनागारम् = 'the prince's bedroom', and राजगृहम् = the palace that contains it: a bedroom (place) belonging to the prince (person) within the palace (place). The other readings nonsensically put a place inside a person, or introduce a prison (कारागारम्) not mentioned here.

**54. (A)** शयनागारम् = sleeping-room; the -आगारम् ('room/abode') element also forms कारागारम् = prison (कारा = confinement). राजपुत्रम् is a person (prince), षोडशम् a number, and धाया a nurse — none is a kind of room.

**55. (B)** राजगृहम् = the palace, the destination, so the line is 'he went to the palace'; 'the prince' is राजपुत्रम्. राजगृहम् is not a verb, does not mean 'prince', and अगच्छत् ('went') is the verb — confirming the destination is the palace.

**56. (C)** मारयितुम् ('to kill') and सर्वस्वम् ('one's everything') are both right. The others each contain two errors: धाया is a nurse not a prince and कुतन्त्रम् is an evil plot not a good one; राजगृह is a palace not a bedroom and षोडशम् is the ordinal 'sixteenth' not the cardinal; छलेन is 'by deception' not 'by truth' and दुष्ट is 'wicked' not 'foolish'.

**57. (B)** छलेन (instrumental, 'by deception') and षोडशे शतके (locative, 'in the 16th century') are both correctly labelled. The others fail: मारयितुम् is infinitive (not past) and अमारयत् is past (not infinitive); स्वर्णिमाक्षरैः is instrumental PLURAL (not nominative singular) and षोडश is a cardinal (not ordinal); वीराङ्गनासु is locative plural 'among heroines' (not instrumental) and मारयतु is imperative (not future).

**58. (B)** The accurate summary keeps the 16th century, Panna as धाया, her son (not herself) dying, the prince surviving, and मारयितुम् as the assassin's purpose ('to kill'). The wrong options variously mis-date it to the 6th century, kill Panna, make her a queen who kills the assassin, replace son with wealth, or mis-call मारयितुम् a past-tense 'he killed'.

**59. (A)** The 'golden letters' tribute belongs to Panna because her सर्वस्वत्याग is what deserves eternal honour, while the assassin's treachery is condemned, not celebrated. The assassin is no hero, the gold is a metaphor (not a payment), and the tribute is Panna's, never the assassin's reward.

**60. (A)** The fully consistent statement: a दुष्टबुद्धि (wicked) rival's assassin came मारयितुम् (purpose: to kill) the prince; Panna's सर्वस्वत्याग (her son's life) saved the future king, and she is honoured in golden letters. The distractors err by calling the rival merely मूर्ख and killing the prince/Panna; by mis-glossing मारयितुम् as past 'he killed' and swapping who sacrificed; or by inventing an honest challenge and reducing the sacrifice to mere wealth saving her own son.
