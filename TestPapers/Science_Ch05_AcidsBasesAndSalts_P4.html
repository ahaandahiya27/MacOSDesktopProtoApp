Worked Solutions

1. (A) Blue litmus turns red only in an acid. Of the four, only lemon juice (citric acid) is an acid, so it is the single liquid that turns blue litmus red. Lime water and soap solution are both bases, which leave blue litmus unchanged (they turn red litmus blue instead). Sugar water is neutral and leaves blue litmus unchanged. Since exactly one strip turned red, that liquid is the acid, i.e. lemon juice.

2. (C) Turning blue litmus red is an acid property, so removing every such substance removes all the acids. Curd (lactic acid) and vinegar (acetic acid) are acids, so they are removed. Milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) and lime water are bases, which do not turn blue litmus red, so both remain. Keeping only milk of magnesia would wrongly discard the equally basic lime water; keeping vinegar would keep an acid.

3. (A) The first solution is a base (red litmus to blue) and the second is an acid (blue litmus to red). Mixed in exactly neutralising amounts they cancel, so the mixture is neutral and changes neither litmus paper. Adding one extra drop of the second sample (the acid) tips the mixture slightly acidic, so it now turns blue litmus red. It would not turn red litmus blue (that needs excess base), and neutrality is not permanent once more acid is added.

4. (A) Leaving both litmus papers unchanged and phenolphthalein colourless are exactly the results of any neutral liquid. Distilled water is neutral, but so are common salt water and sugar water, which would give identical results. So the tests establish neutrality, not identity; the liquid need not be distilled water. Distilled water does not turn phenolphthalein pink (only a base does) and does not turn blue litmus red (only an acid does).

5. (C) Turmeric turns red only in a base and stays yellow in acids and in neutral liquids. Soap solution is basic, so the paper turns red. Lemon juice is acidic, so the paper stays yellow. Plain water is neutral, so the paper stays yellow. The order is therefore red, yellow, yellow. Reversing red to the acid (lemon) misremembers turmeric's direction; turmeric does not turn red in acids or in water.

6. (C) China rose shows dark pink (magenta) in acids and green in bases. Vinegar is acidic (acetic acid), so it turns china rose magenta. Soap solution is basic, so it turns china rose green. Swapping the two reverses china rose's direction. China rose is not a single fixed colour, and it is not colourless like phenolphthalein in acid; it goes magenta there.

7. (C) Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a base. Lime water and milk of magnesia are bases, so both turn it pink, giving a count of 2. Tamarind water is acidic (tartaric acid) and sugar solution is neutral, so both leave phenolphthalein colourless. Counting 1 forgets that both lime water and milk of magnesia are bases; counting 3 wrongly includes an acid or neutral.

8. (A) Methyl orange is yellow in bases and red in acids. Lime water is a base, so methyl orange is yellow in it; dilute hydrochloric acid is an acid, so methyl orange is red in it. The order is yellow then red. Reversing them confuses the directions; methyl orange shows neither pink nor colourless (those are phenolphthalein behaviours), and the acid would not also be yellow.

9. (D) Sour taste is the signature of an acid no matter how weak or strong it is; a sharper sour taste simply means a more strongly acidic liquid, not a base. Intensity of a taste does not switch the chemical family. A base would taste bitter, not sour, and a neutral liquid has no sharp taste, so neither of those follows from extreme sourness.

10. (D) Neutralisation gives a salt plus water, and the salt depends on which acid and base reacted. Hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide gives sodium chloride; sulphuric acid with sodium hydroxide gives sodium sulphate. So the two solids are different. They are not both common salt, since the acids differ. None of the base remains after exact neutralisation, and matter is not destroyed, so a solid salt does form in each.

11. (D) Phenolphthalein is pink only in a base and colourless otherwise. When the pink vanished, the base had been neutralised and the solution was about neutral. Adding more lemon juice (acid) makes the solution acidic, which is also colourless for phenolphthalein, so it stays colourless. Adding more liquid does not bring back pink, excess acid never makes phenolphthalein pink, and green is not a phenolphthalein colour.

12. (B) Turmeric turns red only in a base. P turns red litmus blue, so P is a base and turns turmeric red. Q changes neither litmus, so Q is neutral and leaves turmeric yellow. R turns blue litmus red, so R is an acid and leaves turmeric yellow. Hence only P turns turmeric red. R, being acidic, does not; Q, being neutral, does not.

13. (C) Every acid-base neutralisation gives a salt and water, and the salt is built from the base's metal part combined with the acid's acidic part. Here that salt is not sodium chloride, because neither sodium nor chlorine is involved; sodium chloride only forms from hydrochloric acid and a sodium base. Neutralisation does not regenerate the starting acid, and hydrogen is released when acids meet metals, not in plain acid-base neutralisation, where no base is left over either.

14. (C) Baking soda is a base; vinegar is an acid, so they undergo a neutralisation that releases carbon dioxide gas, seen as fizzing. Pure water is neutral, so there is no acid for the baking soda to react with and little gas is produced. Vinegar is the acid (not a base) and water is neutral (not an acid). The bubbles are a gas produced by a real reaction, not trapped air, which is why they appear only with the acidic vinegar.

15. (C) Each sting is relieved by neutralising it with the opposite family. The ant sting is acidic, so a mild base, baking soda paste, neutralises it. The wasp sting is basic, so a mild acid, vinegar, neutralises it. Reversing the two would add the same family and worsen each. Baking soda would help the acid sting but worsen the basic wasp sting, and lemon juice is an acid, useless on an acidic ant sting.

16. (B) Phenolphthalein is a synthetic, lab-made indicator, so it is the one discarded when keeping only natural ones. Litmus comes from lichens, turmeric from the turmeric plant, and china rose from hibiscus flowers, so all three are natural and are kept. Discarding any of the three natural indicators would be wrong.

17. (A) Slaked lime is a base. Added to acidic bed A it neutralises the excess acid and improves the soil. Added to already-basic bed B it adds more base, making it even more basic and worsening it. Lime is not neutral, so it does not balance any soil; it is not acidic, so it does not fix the basic bed; and it is not an inert salt, since it clearly changes acidic soil.

18. (D) Turmeric turns red only in a base, so when the acid cup finally turns turmeric red, enough basic soap solution has been added to neutralise all the acid and then push past neutral into basic. It is therefore basic, not still acidic. Turmeric red does not mark the neutral point (neutral leaves turmeric yellow), and adding a base to an acid neutralises it rather than creating a stronger acid.

19. (D) Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a base, and a base also turns red litmus blue. A liquid cannot be basic for phenolphthalein yet neutral for litmus at the same time, so the two results contradict each other and the report is inconsistent. A neutral substance leaves phenolphthalein colourless, an acid leaves phenolphthalein colourless too (pink means base, not acid), and salt water is neutral, so none of those explain a genuine pink-with-no-litmus-change result.

20. (C) Solution 1 is pink with phenolphthalein, which happens only in a base. Solution 2 is colourless with phenolphthalein and turns blue litmus red, the mark of an acid. Solution 3 is colourless with phenolphthalein and changes neither litmus, so it is neutral. Hence base, acid, neutral. The other orders mislabel the phenolphthalein-pink solution or treat the blue-litmus-to-red solution as something other than an acid.

21. (D) Acidity is too much hydrochloric acid in the stomach. An antacid is a mild base, and a base neutralises an acid to give salt and water, removing the excess acid and easing the burning. A base does not make a stomach more acidic; that would worsen the problem. The relief is chemical neutralisation, not a sugar coating, and an antacid does not strengthen the acid.

22. (A) Blue litmus turning red marks an acid. In an acid red litmus shows no change (it is already red and only a base turns it blue), phenolphthalein stays colourless (it is pink only in a base), and turmeric stays yellow (it turns red only in a base). So all three remain unchanged. The other options apply base behaviours (red litmus blue, phenolphthalein pink, turmeric red) to what is actually an acid.

23. (D) Dissolved carbon dioxide makes carbonic acid, so the drink is mildly acidic. An acid turns blue litmus red, and china rose shows dark pink (magenta) in acids, so both of those happen. The drink is not basic, so red litmus would not turn blue and china rose would not turn green. It is not neutral, so litmus does change. Pairing acid litmus with a green (basic) china rose mixes the directions.

24. (B) Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides dissolve to form acids, so acid rain is acidic. Marble is calcium carbonate, which behaves as a base, so the acid reacts with and gradually corrodes it. The rain is not basic and the marble is not acidic, so the reversed chain is wrong. The rain is acidic, not neutral, and does more than wash dust; the marble does not react 'with itself'.

25. (B) Acid-base neutralisation releases heat, so the beaker warms up as the reaction proceeds. Evaporation, if anything, takes in heat and cools, so it is not the source of the warmth. Neutralisation produces a salt and water, not a stronger acid, so no acid-strengthening warms the beaker. The heat is from the reaction, not from external sunlight.

26. (B) China rose is magenta in acids and green in bases, so cup A is an acid and cup B is a base. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acids and pink in bases, so A (acid) stays colourless and B (base) turns pink. Reversing this would treat magenta as basic, which is wrong. Both pink would need both to be bases; both colourless would need neither to be basic, but B is.

27. (C) While pink, the lime water was basic. The pink vanishing marks the point where the lemon juice (acid) has neutralised the base, leaving the mixture about neutral. Continuing to add lemon juice makes the mixture acidic, so blue litmus now turns red. The mixture is not locked at neutral once more acid is added; lime water has been used up so it is no longer basic; and litmus has no green result.

28. (B) Chalk is calcium carbonate, which reacts with an acid to release carbon dioxide gas, seen as fizzing. The acid beaker therefore fizzes. Soap solution is basic, and a carbonate does not fizz with a base in this way, so the soap beaker does not fizz. Fizzing in both, or none at all, ignores that the reaction needs an acid. Chalk is not unreactive towards acids.

29. (D) Blue litmus turning red shows the waste is acidic, so it must be neutralised by a base; slaked lime is the standard cheap base used. Adding more acid makes it more acidic, not safer, and two acids do not cancel. Common salt is neutral and does not neutralise an acid. Sugar merely dissolves and has no neutralising action.

30. (A) Methyl orange is red in acids, so the beaker first held an acid. Adding the base sodium hydroxide neutralises the acid, and once enough base is added the mixture becomes basic, where methyl orange is yellow. So yellow signals the mixture has reached the basic range. The original was an acid (red), not a base; the indicator has not run out; and yellow marks basic conditions, not the exact neutral point.

31. (A) Bases turn red litmus blue and feel soapy. Lime water and washing soda solution are bases, giving a count of 2. Curd (lactic acid) and tamarind water (tartaric acid) are acids, and milk is roughly neutral, so none of those three is a base. A count of 1 misses one base, while 3 or 4 wrongly counts an acid or the neutral milk as basic.

32. (C) Soapy feel, turmeric turning red and phenolphthalein turning pink are all base properties, and they agree, so the liquid is a base. Soapy feel is a base trait, not an acid one. A neutral substance would change none of these indicators, so three positive base results rule out neutral. Tasting is unnecessary and unsafe; the indicator results already give a confident answer.

33. (D) Vinegar (acetic acid) is the acid. Sodium hydroxide and milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) are both bases. Common salt water is neutral. So acids = vinegar, bases = sodium hydroxide and milk of magnesia, neutral = common salt water. Salt water is not an acid, sodium hydroxide is not the lone base, and milk of magnesia is not neutral, so the other groupings misplace at least one substance.

34. (D) A bee sting is acidic and is relieved by neutralising it with a mild base such as baking soda paste. Honey is sweet but not a base, so it does not neutralise the acid. Sweetness has nothing to do with neutralising power, so sweet substances in general do not neutralise acids. The bee sting is acidic, not basic, so it does not need an acid, and honey is not an acid remedy anyway.

35. (D) Phenolphthalein is pink in bases and colourless in both acids and neutral solutions. So a colourless result only rules out a base; it cannot by itself tell an acid from a neutral substance. A second test, such as blue litmus, is needed: if it turns red the solution is an acid, otherwise neutral. Colourless does not 'always' mean acid, base or neutral, since it covers two of the three families at once.

36. (B) A neutral substance changes neither litmus paper, and water does exactly that, distinguishing it from acids (which turn blue litmus red) and bases (which turn red litmus blue) in one test. Turning blue litmus red would make water an acid; turning red litmus blue would make it a base; turning phenolphthalein pink would also mark a base. Only 'no change in either litmus' fits a neutral liquid.

37. (B) The original solution turned red litmus blue, so it was a base. Adding the acid neutralises it, and when neither litmus changes the mixture has reached neutral, where the acid and base have cancelled. It is not still basic, since red litmus no longer stays blue would otherwise show. It is not yet acidic, because that needs excess acid (blue litmus would then turn red). Neutralisation does not create a stronger base.

38. (A) Blue litmus turning red is an acid sign, phenolphthalein staying colourless is consistent with an acid (it is pink only in a base), and china rose magenta is the acid colour. All three agree on acidity, so the contents are an acid. China rose changing to magenta is itself the acid result, not a base sign. Colourless phenolphthalein alone could be neutral, but the litmus change rules that out, so the results are consistent, not contradictory.

39. (B) Red litmus turning blue shows ammonia solution is a base. A base turns turmeric red and turns china rose green. So turmeric is red and china rose is green. Turmeric staying yellow is the acid/neutral result, not a base one. China rose magenta is the acid colour, the opposite of what a base gives. Only 'turmeric red and china rose green' matches a base on both counts.

40. (B) Acid rain leaves acidic residue, and a base neutralises an acid, so a dilute basic wash can neutralise that residue and slow further attack on the calcium-carbonate stone. The stone being a base does not mean a base wash 'attacks' it; the wash targets the acid residue, not the stone. Adding more acid would worsen corrosion, and limestone is a base (calcium carbonate), not acidic, so it is certainly affected by acid rain.

41. (C) Leaving blue litmus blue (no change) plus turning phenolphthalein pink are both base behaviours; only a base does both. Soap solution is the base, so it fits. Orange juice is acidic (citric acid): it turns blue litmus red and leaves phenolphthalein colourless. Sugar water is neutral: blue litmus stays blue but phenolphthalein stays colourless, so it fails the phenolphthalein test. Hence soap solution is the only match.

42. (B) When bubbling stops and neither litmus changes, the acidic lemon juice and basic baking soda have just neutralised, leaving a neutral mixture. Adding two more drops of lemon juice puts acid in excess, making the mixture slightly acidic. It is not still exactly neutral once extra acid is added; baking soda has been used up so it is not basic; and lemon juice (an acid) certainly does affect a neutral mixture by making it acidic.

43. (D) Neutralisation always gives a salt and water, but the salt depends on the specific acid and base. Hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide gives sodium chloride (common salt), but, say, sulphuric acid with sodium hydroxide gives sodium sulphate instead. So the general claim is wrong; different pairs give different salts. Neutralisation can make common salt (from the right pair), and it is the salt that varies, not the water.

44. (A) Phenolphthalein is pink only in a base and colourless in both acids and neutral solutions. So a colourless reading could mean either an acid or a neutral substance, and a second indicator such as litmus is needed to decide. Phenolphthalein does not turn pink in acids (only bases), it does not turn red at all, and it does change colour for bases, so the other explanations misstate its behaviour.

45. (A) Phenolphthalein is the synthetic, lab-made indicator; turmeric, litmus and china rose are all natural. So turmeric (natural) with phenolphthalein (synthetic) is one of each. China rose is natural, not synthetic, so litmus-and-china-rose is two naturals. Calling phenolphthalein natural or turmeric synthetic reverses their true kinds, and litmus-and-turmeric are both natural.

46. (A) Neutralisation of an acid by a base releases heat, so the beaker warms up as the reaction proceeds; nothing is boiling away. Acid boiling away would, if anything, need heat put in and would not be caused by the reaction. A base does not freeze the mixture, and the salt being formed stays dissolved rather than boiling off. The warmth is simply the reaction's released heat.

47. (C) The pond is acidic and must be neutralised by a base; slaked lime is a cheap, widely used base for treating acidic water. Vinegar (acetic acid) and lemon juice (citric acid) are acids, so they would worsen the acidity rather than fix it. Adding more acidic runoff obviously makes the problem worse. Only slaked lime fits both 'a base' and 'cheap and common'.

48. (A) Methyl orange is red only in an acid. X turns blue litmus red, so X is an acid and turns methyl orange red. Y turns red litmus blue, so Y is a base, and methyl orange is yellow (not red) in it. Z changes neither litmus, so Z is neutral, and methyl orange stays yellow there too. Hence only X turns methyl orange red; Y is a base where it is yellow.

49. (C) Sour taste, blue litmus turning red, colourless phenolphthalein and china rose magenta are all acid behaviours, and they agree, so the liquid is an acid. China rose changing to magenta is precisely the acid colour, not a base sign. Colourless phenolphthalein alone could be neutral, but the sour taste and blue-litmus-to-red rule that out. The five results are consistent, not in conflict.

50. (B) China rose green is the base colour, so the solution is a base. A base turns red litmus blue, turns phenolphthalein pink and feels soapy, so that combination follows. Red litmus staying red with a sour taste describes an acid, the opposite. Blue litmus turning red is an acid behaviour, which a base does not show. Phenolphthalein staying colourless contradicts a base, which turns it pink.

51. (D) An antacid is a base, and it neutralises stomach acid to give salt and water, exactly matching acid plus base gives salt plus water. Dissolving sugar is mere mixing, not an acid-base reaction. Ice melting is a physical change of state, not a chemical neutralisation. An apple browning is a reaction with air, not between an acid and a base. Only the antacid example is a genuine neutralisation.

52. (C) Exact neutralisation uses up all the acid and base, leaving salt dissolved in the water formed. Boiling off the water leaves that solid salt behind. No acid remains, so it cannot be 'leftover acid'; no base remains either, since the amounts were exact. Matter is not destroyed, so a solid does form. The correct answer is the salt.

53. (B) Soda water contains dissolved carbon dioxide, which forms carbonic acid, so it is an acid, not a base; that is the wrong pairing. Vinegar (acetic acid) is correctly an acid, lime water is correctly a base, and milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) is correctly a base. So the single error is calling soda water a base when it is actually acidic.

54. (A) China rose shows dark pink (magenta) in acids, so a magenta china rose result agrees with blue litmus turning red and confirms the liquid is an acid. Phenolphthalein turning pink would indicate a base, contradicting the acid finding. Turmeric turning red also indicates a base, again contradicting it. Red litmus turning blue is a base behaviour too. Only china rose magenta is a second, independent acid sign.

55. (A) Changing neither litmus paper proves a liquid is neutral, but water is only one of many neutral liquids; salt water and sugar water give the same result. So the test cannot single out pure water. Water does not turn blue litmus red (it is neutral). Acids and bases do change litmus, so they are not the ones leaving it unchanged. Litmus works fine on colourless liquids, so that objection is false.

56. (D) In acid + base → salt + water, the water is genuinely a product of the reaction, formed when the acid and base combine, along with the salt. It is not merely the solvent water that was already present; the equation states water as a product. The leftover lemon juice would be excess acid, not water. And water is always one of the two products, so it is wrong to say only salt forms.

57. (C) Blue litmus turning red is the test for an acid. Vinegar (acetic acid) is an acid, so it fits. Soap solution and milk of magnesia are bases, which turn red litmus blue, not blue litmus red. Distilled water is neutral and changes neither litmus paper. So vinegar is the only acidic liquid among the four that turns blue litmus red.

58. (D) Red litmus turning blue is the test for a base. Ammonia solution and lime water turn red litmus blue, so two are bases. Vinegar is an acid and leaves red litmus red; distilled water is neutral and also leaves red litmus red. So exactly 2 of the four are bases. A count of 1 misses one base, while 3 or 4 wrongly counts the acid or neutral liquid as basic.

59. (B) China rose is magenta in acids and green in bases. Cup P (magenta) is therefore the acid, and cup Q (green) is the base; in a neutralisation cup P plays the acid. Saying green marks an acid reverses china rose's direction. Magenta marks an acid, not a base. China rose certainly distinguishes acids from bases, since it shows two different colours, so it is not useless here.

60. (B) At a clean neutral endpoint, neither litmus paper changes: blue stays blue and red stays red, showing no excess acid or base remains. If blue litmus turned red, an excess of acid would be present, meaning the endpoint was overshot with too much acid. If red litmus turned blue, an excess of base would remain, an overshoot the other way. Turmeric turns red only in a base, so turmeric red would actually signal an excess of base, not a perfect neutral point.
