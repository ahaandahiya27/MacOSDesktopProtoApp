Worked Solutions

1. (D) Because the nitrates pass straight through filter paper and remain in the clear liquid, they must be dissolved, not suspended. They are mineral salts, so they are inorganic. The option calling them suspended organic matter is wrong since suspended matter would be trapped by the paper. They are chemicals, not living things, so the microbe option fails. Scum is floating oil and grease, not a dissolved salt, so that option is also wrong.

2. (C) Oil and grease are light and float, forming the layer skimmed off the top called scum, so the top layer of Jar X is scum. The heavier solids sink and are removed from the bottom as sludge, so Jar Y gives sludge. Reversing the two contradicts the fact that oil floats while solids sink. Grit is removed earlier in a separate chamber, and biogas is a gas made later, so that pairing is wrong. Manure is dried, digested sludge, not the floating oily skim, so that option is also incorrect.

3. (A) Slow flow reduces turbulence so dense particles like sand and grit have time to settle by gravity. Dissolved nitrates stay dissolved no matter how slow the flow, so they cannot settle here. Aerobic bacteria are cultured later in the aeration tank where air is pumped, not in the grit chamber. Skimming of oily scum happens in the settling tank, not the grit chamber, so the purpose stated for slowing flow here is specifically grit settling.

4. (B) Bar screens catch large floating debris; without them, that debris travels on and chokes or jams the downstream equipment, which is the first and most direct problem. Dissolved nitrate levels are unaffected by screening because screens never remove dissolved substances. The aeration bacteria are not killed simply by debris passing through, so they do not die immediately. Letting debris through would make water dirtier, never instantly drinkable, so that option is the opposite of what happens.

5. (A) Air is bubbled into the aeration tank precisely so oxygen-loving (aerobic) bacteria can grow and eat organic waste, and the sludge digester is sealed off from air so anaerobic bacteria break down sludge. So the student's statement matches the chapter exactly. Claiming both need air ignores the sealed, air-free digester. Swapping the two reverses reality, since the aeration tank is the one being supplied air. Saying neither uses bacteria is false because both stages are biological.

6. (D) Biogas is the flammable gas formed when anaerobic bacteria break down sludge inside the closed digester. The aeration tank, being supplied air, hosts aerobic activity and does not make biogas. Manure is the dried, digested sludge, not the oily scum skimmed from the top, so that pairing is wrong. Bar screens only physically catch large solids and produce no gas, so they cannot make biogas.

7. (B) Treatment greatly reduces solids and organic load, but the chapter stresses the released water is cleaner, not guaranteed safe to drink. Claiming every impurity and germ is removed overstates what the process achieves. Equating it with bottled drinking water is the same overstatement. Saying it is dirtier than raw sewage is the opposite of the truth, since treatment clearly improves the water.

8. (B) Sewage first meets bar screens that catch plastics (ii), then the grit chamber where sand settles (iii), then the settling tank where oily scum is skimmed off the top (i), and finally aeration where aerobic bacteria consume leftover organic matter (iv). Starting with scum-skimming puts settling before screening, which is impossible. Starting with aeration reverses the whole plant. Putting grit removal before screening is wrong because large debris must be screened out first to protect the grit chamber.

9. (C) Removing gritty, abrasive sand early protects pumps and keeps it out of the organic sludge that is later digested, which is the real engineering reason. Sand is inert, not the most poisonous part of sewage. Biogas comes from bacterial digestion of organic sludge, not from sand. The settling tank is specifically designed to collect settled solids as sludge, so claiming it cannot hold solids is false.

10. (D) The chapter warns that cooking oil should not be poured down drains because it clogs pipes and hampers the treatment process. Calling oil the favourite food that speeds cleaning contradicts that warning. Oil cannot make water sweet or safe to drink. Manure is produced only by drying digested sludge at the plant, not spontaneously from oil in a sewer, so that option is fanciful.

11. (C) Solids that sink form sludge at the bottom, and the scraper moves this sludge for removal. Skimming the floating scum is the surface device's task, not the bottom scraper's. Bubbling air is done in the aeration tank, not by a scraper. Catching large plastics is the bar screen's job at the plant's entrance, so the bottom scraper deals only with settled sludge.

12. (A) Aerobic bacteria depend on the pumped air for oxygen; cut it off and they cannot work well, so organic matter is left poorly treated. Saying they thrive without air contradicts their need for oxygen. Biogas comes from sealed anaerobic digestion of sludge, not from an aeration tank that has merely lost its air. Dissolved organic matter cannot simply be filtered out by gravity, which is why bacteria are used to consume it in the first place.

13. (A) Wastewater leaves homes through sewers, is carried to the treatment plant for cleaning, and the cleaned water is then discharged into a river or water body. Putting the treatment plant before the sewer reverses the collection step. Sending water to the river before treatment skips cleaning entirely, which the system exists to prevent. Cleaned water is not piped back to home taps, and a biogas plant is not the first stop, so that option is wrong.

14. (C) Sewage carries harmful microbes such as the bacteria that cause cholera and typhoid; these are living and cause disease directly. Phosphate salts are dissolved chemicals, not living organisms. Sand is non-living mineral matter. Cooking oil is non-living organic liquid, so among the choices only the micro-organisms are alive and pathogenic.

15. (A) A septic tank is exactly the low-cost, on-site arrangement for a home without sewer connections. A full treatment plant is large, expensive infrastructure, not a single-home solution. Bar screens and grit chambers are individual stages inside a large plant and do nothing useful on their own under one toilet, so neither serves as an on-site sanitation system.

16. (A) After aeration the bacteria settle as activated sludge, and much of it is sent back into the aeration tank so the bacterial population keeps cleaning incoming water. Pouring it straight into a river untreated would defeat the purpose of treatment. Activated sludge sinks and is not skimmed as floating scum. Bar screens act on incoming large debris at the entrance, not on settled bacterial sludge, so that option is wrong.

17. (B) Treatment depends on living bacteria, and toxic chemicals can poison and kill them, crippling the cleaning and letting harm pass into rivers. Chemicals do not make the purely mechanical bar screens work faster. They cannot instantly produce safe manure, which only comes from properly digested and dried sludge. They certainly do not boost the drinking-water supply, so the real danger is the death of the helpful microbes.

18. (A) Bar screens are coarse physical filters that stop big floating debris but cannot touch substances that are dissolved or microscopic. They cannot remove dissolved nitrates, which slip through easily. They are not disinfectants and kill no microbes. They certainly do not make sewage completely impurity-free, since fine and dissolved matter remain for later stages.

19. (B) Cholera and typhoid spread when sewage contaminates drinking water; safe toilets and sewers keep waste separated from clean water, breaking that path. Adding nutrients to drinking water is not what sanitation does and would not protect health. Sanitation lowers, not raises, microbes reaching taps. Brighter biogas flames have nothing to do with stopping waterborne disease, so the protective link is keeping sewage out of drinking water.

20. (B) Large objects go first at the bar screens (i), then heavy grit settles in its chamber (iii), then sludge and scum are separated in the settling tank (iv), and finally aerobic bacteria handle leftover organic matter (ii). Starting with bacterial treatment reverses the whole plant. Putting grit before screening is wrong because debris must be screened first. Starting with sludge and scum places settling before screening and grit removal, which cannot happen.

21. (C) Digested sludge is dried into manure for fields, and the biogas given off is burned as fuel. Calling the leftover solid scum is wrong because scum is the floating oil skimmed far earlier, and biogas is not a drinking-water disinfectant. Swapping the two so the solid becomes fuel and the gas becomes manure is backwards. Grit is removed at the start and is not a digester product, so that option fails.

22. (C) Organic contaminants come from living matter; food scraps, faeces and cooking oil all qualify. Dissolved iron salts are inorganic minerals, not organic matter, so they are the odd one out. The other three are classic examples of the organic load in domestic sewage, which is why only the iron salts answer the 'not organic' question.

23. (A) The grit chamber takes out only dense sand and pebbles; lighter suspended solids, oily scum and dissolved impurities go on to the settling and aeration stages. Claiming all solids are gone ignores those later-removed materials. Saying only pure water remains is the same overstatement. The grit chamber removes solids rather than adding any, so the 'adds new solids' option is also false.

24. (D) Medicines and chemicals can poison the living bacteria essential to treatment, so they should not be flushed. They do not energise the bacteria; they damage them. They cannot convert sewage into drinking water. Bar screens are mechanical and gain nothing from such chemicals, so harming the helpful bacteria is the genuine reason to keep these items out of the drain.

25. (D) The digester works without oxygen using anaerobic bacteria, while the aeration tank is bubbled with air for aerobic bacteria, so they are opposites in their air supply. Saying the digester gets air and the aeration tank is sealed reverses the facts. Claiming both are sealed ignores the aeration tank's air pump. Claiming both are aerated ignores the sealed digester, so only the stated contrast is right.

26. (D) Human waste left in the open carries disease germs that rain can wash into wells, ponds and rivers people use, spreading illness. Open waste does not become safe biogas, which needs sealed anaerobic digestion. It contaminates rather than increases clean water. There are no helpful aerobic bacteria living in taps that this could feed, so the real hazard is contamination of water sources.

27. (B) After grit removal, the settling tank lets finer suspended solids sink and collect as sludge. Bar screens are coarse and only catch large objects, not fine suspended particles. The digester acts on sludge already collected, it is not a sieve for incoming suspended solids. The aeration tank's air is meant to feed aerobic bacteria, not to settle solids, so the settling tank is the correct stage.

28. (C) Air is pumped into the aeration tank so aerobic bacteria work there, while the digester is sealed so anaerobic bacteria work without air. Reversing the two contradicts the air supply that defines each. Saying both use air ignores the sealed digester. Saying both are air-free ignores the air pump on the aeration tank, so the matched pairing is the only correct one.

29. (D) In a vermi-processing toilet, earthworms break down human excreta into compost-like manure, avoiding pollution. Flushing waste straight into a river is the opposite of environment-friendly. The toilet does not burn waste, and biogas is not made this way. There is no air pump growing aerobic bacteria in such a toilet, so the earthworm-to-manure description is the right one.

30. (A) Earlier steps remove solids and much organic matter but not all germs, so a disinfectant like chlorine is used to kill remaining harmful microbes before discharge. Chlorine is not added to enrich water with nitrates. Settling of sand was already done in the grit chamber, not by chlorination. Biogas comes from anaerobic digestion of sludge, not from chlorinating clarified water, so disinfection of microbes is the purpose.

31. (C) Used water arrives from homes, from industries and institutions, and from farms, so all three feed the sewer, showing sewage has mixed sources. Limiting it to household toilets ignores industrial and agricultural inputs. Sewage is contaminated used water, not clean rooftop rainwater. The plant cleans sewage, it does not create it, so the multi-source description is correct.

32. (B) The settling tank is recognised by quiet conditions where sludge sinks and scum floats, with no aeration. Vigorous air bubbling to grow bacteria is the signature of the aeration tank, not the settling tank. Catching plastic bags on metal bars describes bar screens. Collecting flammable gas from a sealed dome describes the digester, so the sludge-plus-scum-with-no-air picture identifies the settling tank.

33. (B) Nitrates and phosphates act as nutrients that can over-fertilise a water body and upset its natural balance, which is why sewage must be treated first. They do not make lake water safe to drink. Being dissolved, they do not settle out as sand. They do not spontaneously become biogas in open lake water, which would need sealed anaerobic digestion, so the nutrient-overload effect is the correct consequence.

34. (C) Scum is the floating layer of oils and grease removed by skimming the surface. Heavy material scraped from the bottom is sludge, not scum. The flammable gas from the digester is biogas. Dried sludge spread on fields is manure, so among the choices only the floating, skimmed oily layer correctly defines scum.

35. (D) Untreated sewage pollutes rivers and spreads diseases like cholera and typhoid, so it is cleaned first to protect water and health. Dumping does not usefully deepen a river. Sewage treatment is not run to supply construction sand. It is not about heating river water, so disease prevention and pollution control are the real reasons.

36. (D) The bottom scraper's job is to move settled sludge for removal, so if it jams, sludge piles up at the bottom. Skimming of surface scum is a separate surface device's task, unaffected by the bottom scraper. The aeration tank's air supply is a different system entirely. Bar screens at the entrance are unrelated mechanical bars, so the direct effect is sludge accumulating at the settling tank's floor.

37. (C) Plain rinse water carries no toxic chemicals or pipe-coating grease, so it is the safest to drain. Paint thinner is a harsh chemical that can kill helpful bacteria. Cooking oil coats and clogs pipes. Expired medicines contain chemicals that damage the treatment microbes, so among the four only the plain water is essentially harmless to the system.

38. (B) Sludge goes to the sealed digester for anaerobic breakdown, while the clarified water is sent to the aeration tank to be aerated for aerobic bacteria, two separate streams in two conditions. Treating both together in one air-free tank ignores the aeration step. Swapping so the water is digested without air and the sludge aerated reverses the design. Bacteria act in the digester and aeration tank, not on the mechanical bar screens, so only the stated split is correct.

39. (C) A chemical toilet adds chemicals that decompose waste and control smell and germs, a handy on-site option where there is no sewer connection. It does not produce drinking water. It does not run an air pump to grow aerobic bacteria. It is not designed to collect biogas, so the chemical breakdown of waste for sanitation is its real purpose.

40. (D) Treatment removes solids and consumes organic matter, so the outlet water is much cleaner than the raw inlet sewage. It is not dirtier; that would mean treatment failed. The two are not identical, since treatment clearly changes the water. Yet 'cleaner' does not mean 'guaranteed drinkable', so claiming it is safe to drink overstates the result; the correct point is that it is far less laden than the inlet.

41. (A) Even a good plant is undermined if households flush oils, chemicals and solids that clog pipes or poison the treatment bacteria, so home habits matter. More oil does not help the plant; it harms it. Home habits do not rearrange the plant's fixed sequence of steps. Households are not asked to disinfect water themselves, so the reason is that careless dumping burdens and damages the system.

42. (D) Bar screens are metal bars that physically block large debris, with no biology involved. The aeration tank uses aerobic bacteria, which is biological. The digester uses anaerobic bacteria, also biological. The settling of activated sludge involves bacteria too, so among the choices only the bar screen works by a purely mechanical action.

43. (B) Biogas is a gas produced by anaerobic digestion of sludge, whereas scum is the floating oil and grease skimmed off the settling tank, so they are completely different. They are not both a floating oily layer, since biogas is a gas. They are not both gases, since scum is a floatable matter. Biogas is not bottom sludge and scum is not a gas, so the only correct judgement is that they are distinct.

44. (C) Sealing the digester keeps out air so anaerobic bacteria can decompose the sludge and yield biogas. Exposing it to air would favour aerobic, not anaerobic, action and produce no biogas. The digester is about bacterial breakdown, not sun-drying. Plastics are already removed at the bar screens long before, so screening is not the digester's role; the sealed, air-free condition is the key.

45. (A) A surge of sand and grit is exactly what the grit chamber is built to settle out, protecting later stages. The digester handles organic sludge, not incoming sand. Chlorination kills microbes and does nothing for grit. The aeration tank grows bacteria and is not meant for heavy sand, so the grit chamber is the stage that matters here.

46. (C) Grit is dense inorganic sand taken out early in its own chamber, while sludge is the largely organic solid that settles in the later settling tank and can be digested. They are not the same material in one tank. Grit is not a floating oily layer (that is scum) and sludge is not a gas. Grit is mineral, not organic, and sludge is certainly not drinking water, so only the first description holds.

47. (A) Keeping drains and toilets clean prevents blockages and overflows that would expose people to disease-carrying waste, protecting health. It reduces, not increases, microbes reaching homes. It does not involve pouring chemicals into sinks. It has nothing to do with kitchen biogas flames, so preventing exposure to waste is the genuine benefit.

48. (D) The roles are swapped: the sealed digester is air-free and uses anaerobic bacteria, while the aerated tank supplies air for aerobic bacteria. So the statement is wrong on both counts, not correct. It is not the case that only the digester part is wrong while the aeration part is right, since both are reversed. The two tanks do not share the same aerobic bacteria, because their air conditions differ.

49. (D) Treated water is clean enough to be returned to the ground to recharge aquifers, making good use of it even though it is not certified drinking water. Calling it pure drinking water needing no care overstates its quality. It is cleaner, not dirtier, than raw sewage. Recharging groundwater does not generate biogas, so the sensible reuse of cleaner water is the right reason.

50. (C) Flammable gas from a sealed dome is biogas produced by the anaerobic digester. The aeration tank supplies air to bacteria and does not collect flammable gas. Bar screens merely block solids and release no useful gas. The settling tank separates sludge and scum without collecting flammable gas, so the sealed-dome biogas points to the digester.

51. (A) Big debris is screened first, grit settles next, then the settling tank separates sludge and scum, and finally aerobic bacteria consume remaining organic matter. Starting with bacterial digestion reverses the plant. Putting grit before debris is wrong because debris is screened first to protect the grit chamber. Starting with scum places settling before screening and grit removal, which is impossible.

52. (B) Being natural does not stop solid scraps and tea leaves from accumulating and clogging pipes, which blocks sewage flow. They do not dissolve to improve water; they pile up. They cannot become drinking water inside a pipe. There are no aerobic bacteria living in home taps to consume them, so the real trouble is pipe blockage.

53. (B) The chapter stresses treated water is cleaner but not certified drinking water, so a downstream village should not drink it untested. The plant does not make water dirtier on purpose. Treated water has far less sewage load than the inlet, not more. Rivers do not automatically purify everything that enters, so the caution rests on 'cleaner, not guaranteed safe'.

54. (C) The valuable outputs are biogas, burned as fuel, and the dried digested sludge used as manure. The plastics caught by screens and the grit are wastes to be discarded, not useful products. Scum is not a cooking fuel and grit is not a fertiliser. Raw untreated sludge dumped in a river is pollution, not a benefit, so only the biogas-and-manure pair is genuinely useful.

55. (A) Removing gritty sand early keeps it from wearing out aeration equipment and burying the helpful bacteria. Sand is inert and is not food for aerobic bacteria. The aeration tank is built for biological treatment, not for removing grit, so it would not handle grit better. Grit settles by gravity in its chamber and does not need aerating, so protecting the later aeration stage is the reason.

56. (D) Activated sludge is the settled clump of aerobic bacteria formed in the aeration tank after they consume organic waste, and much is recycled back. It is not the screened plastic debris, which is mechanical waste. It is not the digester's biogas, which is a gas. It is not the inert grit from the grit chamber, so the bacterial settled mass is the right description.

57. (B) Typhoid and cholera spread through water contaminated by sewage, so poor sanitation that lets sewage mix with drinking water is the likely cause. Producing biogas does not cause these diseases. Recharging groundwater with treated water is a safe reuse, not a disease source. Bar screens catching plastics is normal operation and unrelated to outbreaks, so sewage in drinking water is the root cause.

58. (A) Large solids are handled physically by screens and settling, but dissolved organic matter must be broken down biologically by aerobic bacteria in the aeration tank. Bar screens cannot remove dissolved impurities, only large solids. The grit chamber settles sand, not dissolved impurities, and the digester acts on collected sludge. Dissolved organic matter is not skimmed as scum, so only the mechanical-versus-biological split is correct.

59. (D) A septic tank lets solids settle and partly decompose, but the sludge builds up and must be pumped out from time to time for safe disposal or treatment. The settled waste does not become drinking water. A simple home septic tank does not automatically pipe fuel to the kitchen. Solids genuinely accumulate, so the claim that the tank never needs attention is false; periodic emptying is required.

60. (B) Good sanitation keeps disease-causing sewage out of drinking water, preventing illnesses like cholera and typhoid in the first place, which is more effective than treating people after they fall sick. It is not that doctors cannot treat such diseases; prevention simply avoids the suffering. Clean drains are not run to make biogas for hospitals. Blocked drains spread contamination and make water less safe, not more, so prevention through sanitation is the point.
