## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** Vegetative propagation copies one parent's material without any fusion of gametes, so every offspring is a clone; without the reshuffling that comes from combining two parents' contributions, the cutting route cannot itself introduce new traits, regardless of how seedless the fruit is.

**2. (B)** Seeds form from two parents' gametes, so their offspring vary; that variation means some individuals chance to resist a new threat. The cuttings are identical copies, so a weakness in the parent is shared by every plant, allowing uniform devastation.

**3. (B)** A potato's eyes are buds, and shoots grow from those buds; gouging them out leaves the stored food intact but removes the growth points, so that piece cannot send up new shoots even though it has plenty of food.

**4. (C)** Bryophyllum's leaf margins carry buds that each grow into a new plant — a vegetative, asexual route. The fern's brown patches are spore cases; its young plants come from spores. So one is bud-based vegetative propagation and the other is spore-based, which are genuinely different mechanisms.

**5. (A)** Budding is the pinching-off of a single outgrowth from a parent, which is exactly what the yeast shows. Fragmentation is the breaking of the body into pieces that each regrow, which is what Spirogyra shows. So only the alga's case is fragmentation.

**6. (C)** The mushroom, moss and fern are united by reproducing through spores. The potato instead grows new plants from the buds (eyes) of its underground stem, a vegetative route, so it is the one that does not share the grouping feature.

**7. (B)** A spore is shielded in a tough case and stays dormant through unfavourable conditions, then germinates when it turns moist. It needs no partner and does not rush to germinate; that ability to wait out a drought is its key advantage over constant division.

**8. (C)** Money plants root readily from stem cuttings, giving a quick, reliable clonal supply. Breeding trials need variation, which comes from seeds produced by fertilisation. Matching each goal to the right route means cuttings for the money plant and seeds for the tomatoes.

**9. (C)** Grafting is a vegetative method: the joined shoot grows on as a copy of the chosen variety, so its fruit shows that variety's traits. There is no fusion of gametes, so the fruit is not a new blend; the rootstock mainly supplies roots.

**10. (A)** Asexual reproduction is defined by the absence of gamete fusion, not by the presence of buds. Bulbs and underground stems are vegetative (asexual) routes, and spores are asexual units too, so all three examples are asexual.

**11. (D)** Both cuttings come from one parent with no gamete fusion, so both are genetically identical clones. The rooting medium can affect how well each grows but does not change the inherited material, so the two roses are not genetically different.

**12. (D)** Pollen is produced in the anther, held up by the filament; the sticky receptive tip is the stigma, and the ovules sit inside the ovary. Matching each clue to those parts gives anther, filament, stigma, ovary in that order.

**13. (C)** Each flower carries only one set of reproductive parts — one only stamens, the other only a pistil — so each is unisexual (a male and a female flower). They are not bisexual, because a bisexual flower must carry both sets within the same bloom.

**14. (B)** The receptive surface is the stigma; below it lies the style, and at the base sits the ovary that holds the ovules destined to become seeds. So the descent runs stigma to style to ovary.

**15. (A)** Seeds develop from ovules, and the ovules are inside the ovary; removing the ovary removes the ovules. Pollen is still made in the anthers and the stigma can still receive pollen, but with no ovules there can be no seeds.

**16. (B)** Seeds come from ovules; with no ovules present, fertilisation cannot lead to any seeds. Pollen release, stigma reception and insect visits all depend on other parts that are intact, so only seed formation is blocked.

**17. (C)** During pollination pollen is deposited on the stigma, the receptive tip of the pistil. The filament merely supports the anther; it is not the landing surface, and the anther makes pollen rather than receiving it.

**18. (A)** The tassel carries male flowers that shed pollen and the cob carries female flowers that become grain. Since the male and female parts are in separate flowers, maize bears unisexual flowers on one plant, not a single bisexual flower.

**19. (D)** Pollen is manufactured in the anther, so that is the source to dust for a pure sample. The stigma is merely the receiving surface; it does not produce pollen, so collecting from it would not yield the grains she needs.

**20. (A)** If the anthers shed pollen before the stigma is ready, the flower's own pollen has dispersed by the time its stigma can receive any. So self-pollination is hindered and pollen arriving from another flower of the same kind is what can reach the receptive stigma.

**21. (D)** Carrying both anthers and a stigma in one bloom only makes self-pollination possible. Whether it actually happens depends on factors like the timing of pollen release versus stigma readiness, so self-pollination is enabled but not guaranteed.

**22. (A)** Moving pollen to the stigma of a different plant of the same kind is cross-pollination. The later seed formation depends on fertilisation, a separate fusion step, so the transfer itself is just cross-pollination.

**23. (A)** With the bag sealing out all outside pollen, the only source left is the flower's own anthers. Fruit set therefore means its own pollen reached its own stigma — self-pollination — followed by fertilisation.

**24. (C)** Removing the anthers strips the flower of its own pollen, so any pollen reaching its stigma must come from another plant; that is cross-pollination. Seeds cannot form without pollination, so the seed set confirms pollen arrived from elsewhere.

**25. (A)** Colour, scent and sticky pollen attract and cling to insects, so X is insect-pollinated. Light, dry, abundant pollen and the lack of showy features suit wind carriage, so Y is wind-pollinated.

**26. (D)** Wind delivers pollen with no aim, so most grains miss any stigma; producing an enormous surplus raises the odds that a few will land where they should. Insects, by contrast, ferry pollen more directly, so far less is required.

**27. (D)** Nectar, scent and landing-pad petals are adaptations to attract insects. Releasing pollen into flowing water so it is carried to other flowers is an adaptation for water pollination. So the two flowers are pollinated by insects and water respectively.

**28. (D)** An insect's job is pollination — moving pollen to a stigma. Seeds form only after fertilisation, when the male gamete fuses with the egg in the ovule, an event that happens after the pollen lands, not during the bee's visit.

**29. (A)** Showy colour and scent are investments that pay off only when pollinators must be attracted. Wind responds to neither, so wind-pollinated grasses gain nothing from bright petals or fragrance and remain plain.

**30. (C)** Self-pollination lets the plant set seed without relying on an outside agent or another plant, so reproduction is secure even when pollinators are absent. It does not maximise variation — that is the strength of cross-pollination — and it still requires a zygote.

**31. (B)** The distinction is about destination: self-pollination delivers pollen within the same flower or plant, whereas cross-pollination delivers it to a different plant of the same kind. Both can yield seeds, and neither necessarily requires an insect.

**32. (A)** The pollen grain grows a pollen tube that pushes down through the style and into the ovary, carrying the male gamete to the ovule where fusion occurs. There is no path along the filament, through petals, or via the roots.

**33. (B)** Pollen landing on the stigma is pollination; the fusion of the male gamete with the egg is fertilisation. Fertilisation cannot occur until pollen has arrived and a tube has grown, so pollination must come first.

**34. (B)** The dusted stigma shows pollination succeeded, so the breakdown must lie later. With no fruit, fertilisation did not take place: the male gamete never fused with the egg, so no fruit could develop from the ovary.

**35. (D)** Fusion of the male gamete and the egg produces a single cell, the zygote, which later develops into the embryo. The seed coat and fruit form afterward from other parts, and the pollen grain existed before fusion.

**36. (D)** The ovule matures into the seed, the surrounding ovary swells into the fruit, and the zygote within grows into the embryo. Confusing any pair — for example swapping seed and fruit — is the classic error this checks.

**37. (C)** The pod is the fruit of the pea, and fruits develop from the ovary. As the ovary ripens into the pod, the now-redundant petals, stamens and stigma wither and fall, leaving the swelling ovary behind.

**38. (B)** Each seed develops from one fertilised ovule, so eight seeds imply the ovary held at least eight ovules. Anthers and stigmas do not become seeds, and a single ovule yields a single seed rather than splitting into many.

**39. (B)** Pollen first reaches the stigma, then fertilisation forms the zygote, then the ovary ripens into a seed-bearing fruit, and finally a seed germinates into a new plant. So the order is pollination, zygote, fruit, new plant.

**40. (C)** An ovule matures into a seed, and the zygote it contained develops inside it into the embryo. The fruit forms from the whole ovary, not from a single ovule, and ovules never turn into stigmas or pollen.

**41. (A)** Pollination only delivers pollen; a fruit forms only after fertilisation occurs. A flower that is pollinated but in which the gametes never fuse sets no fruit, directly disproving the claim that pollination alone guarantees fruit.

**42. (C)** Fine hairs catch the wind and keep a light fruit aloft, so the dandelion is wind-dispersed. A buoyant, air-filled husk lets the coconut float and ride currents, so it is water-dispersed. Each structure is matched to a different agent.

**43. (B)** Hooks that catch on fur or clothing and sweet fruits that are eaten and the seeds later dropped are both ways animals carry seeds away. Both scenarios are therefore animal dispersal, by two different mechanisms.

**44. (C)** The sudden splitting of the dried pod throws the seeds out by itself; the lack of hooks, hairs or a buoyant husk rules out animals, wind and water as the means. This self-powered scattering is dispersal by explosion.

**45. (B)** Colour and sweetness lure animals to eat the fruit; the hard seeds survive and are deposited elsewhere, carrying the plant to new ground. Juiciness adds weight rather than aiding flight, and does not make fruits float or burst.

**46. (D)** Seeds landing in a dense cluster at the parent's base must share the same light, water, minerals and space, so they compete heavily. Dispersal would relieve this crowding; the other options invent effects that do not occur.

**47. (C)** Carrying seeds away spreads the species to fresh ground that may suit it, increasing its chances of establishing new populations. Dispersal does not add weight, hasten pollination, or replace the need for fertilisation.

**48. (A)** Papery wings catch the wind, a buoyant husk floats on water, and hooks cling to animal fur — three different structures for three different agents. So the three seeds are not adapted to one common agent.

**49. (A)** Wind dispersal favours lightness and surfaces that catch air, such as hairs, wings or a tiny body. A heavy seed with a thick stony coat resists being lifted or carried, so that feature is the least useful for wind.

**50. (C)** Lacking usable seeds, this banana cannot rely on seed reproduction; new plants arise vegetatively from its underground stem. That clonal route needs no seeds, explaining how the variety keeps spreading despite being seedless.

**51. (D)** A seed formed inside a fruit after fertilisation is the product of fusing gametes — the hallmark of sexual reproduction. Underground stems, leaf-margin buds and cuttings are all vegetative routes that involve no gamete fusion.

**52. (B)** A seed is the outcome of fertilisation, the fusion of male and female gametes. A spore is produced asexually with no such fusion. That presence or absence of gamete fusion is the deepest distinction between the two.

**53. (A)** Leaf-margin buds, underground-stem growth and yeast budding are all asexual, with no gamete fusion. The mango seed forms after fertilisation, so it alone is sexual reproduction and is the odd one out.

**54. (C)** Hydra reproduces by budding — an outgrowth swells and separates. Among the options, yeast does the same, budding off new cells. Spirogyra fragments, the fern uses spores, and the potato's eyes are buds on a stem, not a budding-off of the whole organism.

**55. (D)** An insect's role is limited to transferring pollen to a stigma, which is pollination. Fertilisation — the fusion of the male gamete with the egg — occurs afterward inside the ovule and is not something the insect performs.

**56. (B)** Stem cuttings are a vegetative, clonal route, so every new plant copies the single parent exactly and grows fast. Seeds and cross-pollination introduce variation, and the variety does not reproduce by spores, so those routes fail the identical-copy goal.

**57. (B)** A fern reproduces by spores and never bears a flower at all, directly disproving the claim. The mango, rose and papaya all do flower, so they cannot serve as counter-examples to a statement about flowers being essential.

**58. (A)** Seed plants come from the fusion of two parents' gametes, which mixes traits and can throw up new combinations. Cuttings are clones with no such mixing, so Bed B stays identical to the parent while Bed A can vary.

**59. (D)** Encouraging roots on a branch while it is still joined to the parent, then severing it, is layering — a vegetative method producing a clone. It is not fragmentation, which is a body breaking into self-sustaining pieces, nor anything to do with pollen or spores.

**60. (D)** Vegetative propagation grows new plants from a stem, root or leaf of one parent without gamete fusion, so the sugarcane stem, sweet-potato root and Bryophyllum leaf all qualify. A seed, however, is the product of fertilisation, making it the non-vegetative route in the list.
