Worked Solutions

1. (A) Harsha came to the throne around 606 CE, in the early seventh century, while the Arab capture of Sindh under Muhammad bin Qasim is dated to 712 CE — so Harsha is earlier by about a century. Reversing the order ignores that 606 is smaller than 712. The two are not the same year, and 1206 is the start of the Delhi Sultanate, far too late for Harsha.

2. (D) A traveller sees only part of a society, may misunderstand what he sees, and may favour places (like the Buddhist Nalanda) that match his own interests, so his account is one source to be cross-checked against inscriptions, coins and local records. Accepting every word uncritically forgets these limits; rejecting it wholesale wastes genuinely useful testimony; and making it the only source ignores the richer Indian evidence.

3. (D) 'Tri-' means three: the Palas of Bengal, the Gurjara-Pratiharas of the north-west and the Rashtrakutas of the Deccan all fought for Kanauj. Naming only two forgets the Deccan power that repeatedly intervened. The Tamil trio (Cholas, Pandyas, Cheras) fought in the far south, not for Kanauj, and the Guptas had already faded before this contest began.

4. (B) Kanauj had become a symbol of paramount authority in the north, so holding it announced a ruler's supremacy — a prestige worth a long campaign. It was not uniquely rich in gold, the Rashtrakutas already held a powerful Deccan kingdom, and Kanauj sits inland in the Ganga plain, not on the coast.

5. (D) A contest that no side could finish means the rivals were roughly matched — the hallmark of several strong regional kingdoms in balance. A single empire or total peace would have ended the fighting at once, and the Arabs never ruled the north; they were confined to Sindh.

6. (C) Standing on the north-western frontier, the Pratiharas were the shield that absorbed and checked Arab pressure, so Arab power got no further than Sindh. They did not invite conquest; their resistance had the opposite effect. They were rivals, not surrenderers, in the Kanauj contest, and Nalanda was a Pala foundation in Bengal, far to the east.

7. (A) The Rashtrakutas of the Deccan are credited with the monolithic Kailasa temple at Ellora; the Pallavas of Kanchi carved the rock-cut rathas at Mahabalipuram; and the Palas of Bengal patronised the Buddhist university of Nalanda. The other pairings swap these famous links, for instance crediting the southern Pallavas with the Deccan's Ellora or the Bengal Palas with a Tamil-coast monument.

8. (A) Monolithic means 'one stone': sculptors cut away the hillside to release a complete temple from a single rock, working top-down with no margin for error — a feat of planning and skill. Brick assembly, wood and cast iron all describe the opposite of carving from one rock, and none matches what Ellora actually is.

9. (D) The Pallavas (Kanchi) and Chalukyas (Vatapi) were neighbouring rivals whose wars are a feature of southern politics in this age, even as both sponsored fine temple architecture. They were competitors, not partners, ruled in the south rather than Bengal, and had nothing to do with the Arab conquest of distant Sindh.

10. (C) A ratha is literally a chariot, and at Mahabalipuram the Pallava sculptors cut shrines from rock in the shape of chariots. A village assembly is a sabha, a reservoir is a tank (eri), and a tribute-paying subordinate is a samanta — none of these is a temple form.

11. (C) The Pala kings supported Buddhism and the famous universities of Nalanda and Vikramashila, drawing scholars from across Asia. The Mahabalipuram rathas were Pallava work, Ellora's Kailasa was Rashtrakuta, and Sindh was the Arab foothold — none was a Pala project.

12. (D) A scholar stationed at the gate to test applicants is a screening device, which implies the institution guarded its quality and selectivity. Open admission with no test is the opposite of what a gate-examiner implies; a gate-scholar marks a place of learning, not an army camp; and a great university taught many subjects, not a single topic.

13. (D) Both monuments are rock-cut — the temple emerges from the stone itself rather than being assembled — so they showcase a shared mastery of carving rock. Paper printing, paper money and steel casting are unrelated technologies and describe none of these stone monuments.

14. (A) The Tamil south saw the old Chola, Pandya and Chera lines revive as strong kingdoms in this period. The Palas, Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas were the northern Tripartite trio; the Mauryas, Shungas and Satavahanas belong to much earlier centuries; and the Hunas, Shakas and Kushanas were foreign-origin powers of still earlier times.

15. (D) The Uttaramerur inscriptions lay out qualifications and a selection procedure for the village sabha, giving rare evidence of local self-government under the Cholas. They are not about temple carving, mathematics, or Sindh — those belong to Ellora, Brahmagupta and the Arabs respectively.

16. (D) A samanta was a lesser ruler who accepted the overlord, supplied tribute and soldiers, and governed his own territory — so the king ruled in layers, not directly. There was no salaried daily-reporting bureaucracy reaching every region, no nationwide election, and certainly no Arab governors outside the Sindh foothold.

17. (B) If villages ran many of their own affairs through a sabha and samantas governed the regions, then authority was layered, passing through intermediate hands rather than from the king to each person. A king touching every villager directly contradicts the layered system, there was no national parliament, and the Arabs held only Sindh.

18. (A) A land grant transferred land or its revenues to a recipient — a brahmana, temple or official — and over time produced a stratum of landholders, which is why these grants matter to social history. It did not strip villagers of everything; a devotional poem and a rock-cut shrine are entirely different things.

19. (B) Organised guilds and thriving towns are marks of a working, prosperous economy, so they refute the 'dark age' picture. A ruined economy would lack guilds and towns; barter-only trade would not need guilds; and the Arabs, confined to Sindh, did not control the subcontinent's commerce.

20. (B) An eri was an artificial water reservoir that captured rain for farming and household needs — evidence of careful water management. It was not a chariot, a monastery, or a subordinate ruler; those are unrelated things drawn from elsewhere in the chapter.

21. (B) Grants organised landholding, guilds organised trade, and tanks organised water — together they describe an economy that was managed and productive. Ruin, the absence of trade, or the absence of landholders each contradict at least one of these three institutions.

22. (A) Monuments, universities, mathematical breakthroughs and a new devotional movement are exactly the kind of creative achievements a 'dark age' supposedly lacks, so they overturn the label. The other options simply restate the discredited 'dark age' claim rather than answering it.

23. (C) Brahmagupta gave the world systematic rules for using zero in arithmetic — a landmark contribution that further disproves the 'dark age' idea. The Rajatarangini was Kalhana's work, the Sindh conquest was an Arab military event, and the Alvars were Tamil devotional poets — none of these is Brahmagupta.

24. (C) A major advance in mathematics during these very centuries is direct proof of intellectual vitality, so it specifically rebuts the 'dark age' charge. The other three statements are themselves correct facts, not claims that the rules of zero are used to refute.

25. (D) The earliest Bhakti saints — the Alvars and Nayanars — composed in Tamil in the south, so that is where the movement is said to begin. It did not arise in Arab Sindh, on the Central Asian steppe, or around Kanauj in the north.

26. (A) Bhakti centred on a direct, emotional bond between worshipper and god and welcomed people regardless of status, which set it apart from the priest-led sacrifices of earlier practice. It was not sacrifice-based, not restricted to royalty, and not an Arab import.

27. (B) The Alvars sang of Vishnu and the Nayanars of Shiva, both in Tamil — a standard pairing. Swapping their deities, making them both Buddhist, or turning them into pilgrims and generals all misidentify these Tamil devotional poets.

28. (D) In 712 the Arabs took Sindh and then made little further headway, so their power remained a limited frontier presence. They did not overrun all of India, they certainly reached India (Sindh is in the subcontinent), and their rule there did not stretch into modern times.

29. (D) Indian powers along and beyond the frontier — above all the Pratiharas and rulers like Lalitaditya of Kashmir — fought back and bottled the Arabs up in Sindh. It was active resistance, not a voluntary halt; the Chinese did not intervene; and India plainly had not been wholly conquered, since most of it remained in Indian hands.

30. (D) Each statement fails a key fact of the chapter: the age was creative, not dark; the Arabs took only Sindh, not most of India; and the Tripartite Struggle involved three powers, not two. Since every one is mistaken, the count of wrong statements is three.

31. (B) These centuries lacked any all-India empire but were full of powerful regional states — Palas, Pratiharas, Rashtrakutas, Cholas and others — competing with one another. There was no single empire, no absence of states, and no Arab rule outside Sindh.

32. (D) Epigraphy reads inscriptions cut into stone or metal, which preserve grants, genealogies and village rules from this period. Coins are the field of numismatics, stars belong to astronomy, and travel diaries are a separate kind of literary source.

33. (A) A prashasti is a panegyric composed to glorify the king, so it tends to magnify successes and pass over defeats — bias the historian must allow for. It was not written by enemies, is the opposite of neutral, and concerns the ruler's achievements rather than ordinary village affairs.

34. (A) Kalhana's Rajatarangini is a rare attempt at ordered, historical narrative — a chronicle of Kashmir's kings — which is why it stands out. It is not a mathematics text, a devotional poem, or an Arab account of Sindh.

35. (B) Examples like the Palas' rise and the regional variation of jatis show that social position was not frozen, so the 'completely rigid' label is wrong. The chapter does not deny that jatis existed, claim a single uniform group, or say caste was abolished — only that it could shift.

36. (C) The Kayasthas illustrate occupational groups becoming recognised jatis, showing that the social order was still forming and changing. This is the opposite of an unchangeable jati, and has nothing to do with foreign rule or rock-cut temples.

37. (C) Multiplying jatis and changing status are signs of a system in flux, so it was complex and evolving rather than fixed. It was neither simple-and-static, nor abolished, nor uniform everywhere — the regional variety is part of the point.

38. (D) Evidence of influential queens shows women could wield real authority in this period. They were neither wholly absent nor universally barred, but nor did any single woman rule the entire subcontinent — that overstates the case.

39. (C) Because no one emperor dominated the north, an opening existed for ambitious regional powers to compete for supremacy at Kanauj — so the power vacuum is exactly why the three-cornered struggle happened, making the reason a genuine explanation. Both statements are accurate, and they stand in a cause-and-effect relation.

40. (A) Capturing Sindh in 712 is a fact (the reason is true), but it did not lead to ruling most of India — Arab power stayed confined to that foothold, so the assertion is false. The capture of one province cannot support a claim of subcontinent-wide rule.

41. (B) The Pratiharas guarded the north-west, the Rashtrakutas held the Deccan in the centre-south, and the Palas lay in Bengal to the east — a genuine west-to-east sequence. The other lists scramble these well-known territories or substitute the Tamil dynasties, which all belong to the far south, not a west-to-east line.

42. (B) Royal inscriptions glorify their own patron, so conflicting claims signal bias on both sides, and the historian must cross-check them against other sources. Length does not prove truth, both records still hold useful clues rather than being worthless, and mere age does not guarantee accuracy.

43. (C) The Pallavas ruled from Kanchi in the Tamil country and the Chalukyas from Vatapi (Badami) in the Deccan. Swapping their capitals, or placing them in Bengal, Kashmir, Sindh or Kanauj, all misassign these two southern powers.

44. (C) A Buddhist visitor naturally took close interest in Buddhist institutions, so his enthusiastic notes on places like Nalanda reflect his own faith — a useful caution for the historian. He did not ignore religion, had no reason to praise Sindh's Arabs, and wrote in Chinese, not Tamil.

45. (C) The Uttaramerur inscriptions set out the actual qualifications and selection rules for the village sabha, making them the direct evidence for Chola local government. Xuanzang described the north a century or more earlier, Brahmagupta wrote on mathematics, and the Rajatarangini concerns Kashmir's dynasties — none addresses Chola village elections.

46. (A) Rival kingdoms each sponsored temples, universities and poets to outshine their neighbours, so fragmentation went hand in hand with a creative flowering. It did not destroy culture; there was no single emperor; and temple-building plainly continued across the many states.

47. (C) That regional powers had to fight over Kanauj after Harsha shows no successor inherited his paramountcy — the north fragmented. There was no unchallenged heir, the Arabs did not seize the north, and the kingdoms competed rather than peacefully merging.

48. (D) Both are rock-cut, but Kailasa is a vast, complete temple released from one rock by cutting downward — a larger-scale feat than the smaller Mahabalipuram shrines. It is not brick, not a university (that describes Nalanda/Vikramashila), and it is a great temple, not a tiny chariot.

49. (A) Seventh century comes before eighth, which comes before twelfth, so the order runs Brahmagupta (7th), Sindh (8th), Rajatarangini (12th). The other orderings put the twelfth-century chronicle or the eighth-century conquest ahead of the seventh-century mathematician, breaking the timeline.

50. (A) Because samantas held real local power and could shift loyalties, the overlord's strength rested on keeping them faithful — authority was mediated, not direct. The king did not run every village himself, samantas did owe tribute and troops, and there was no national vote in this system.

51. (B) Inscriptions and grants fix in stone or metal the practical details of land, revenue and local self-rule, filling gaps that king-centred narratives miss. They are not foreign-authored, not focused on royal feelings, and exaggeration is more a feature of prashastis than of routine grants.

52. (C) The chapter gathers temples, universities, mathematics, Bhakti and vigorous kingdoms to argue the era was creative and dynamic. This refutes the 'dark age' verdict, the idea of one static empire, and the notion of complete foreign rule — none of which fits the evidence.

53. (A) A Bengal dynasty backing a university and a southern dynasty codifying village self-rule both show regional powers actively building institutions of learning and governance. This contradicts the ideas that only one northern empire patronised culture, that kingdoms destroyed such institutions, or that culture depended on Sindh's Arabs.

54. (B) The Bhakti saints sang in Tamil and other local tongues and welcomed all, in contrast to the more exclusive, priest-led Vedic sacrifices. It is not true that Bhakti used an unintelligible language, that it was limited to kings, or that the two traditions were the same.

55. (B) Ratha-style rock-cut shrines on the Tamil coast point to the Pallavas of Kanchi and their work at Mahabalipuram. The Rashtrakutas built Ellora in the Deccan, the Palas patronised universities in eastern Bengal, and the Pratiharas held the distant north-west — none fits a Tamil-coast ratha.

56. (B) Relying on one observer risks accepting his limits and biases, so the responsible method is to corroborate him with independent evidence like inscriptions and coins. Xuanzang did visit India, prosperity can be studied through evidence, and coins are one useful source among several, not the only one.

57. (C) The accurate picture is a foothold in Sindh from 712 that Indian powers prevented from growing. The Arabs did not conquer all India, they did hold Sindh (so they were not wholly excluded), and they never ruled Bengal or the Tamil south.

58. (C) Scholars arriving from across Asia show that India's universities had international standing and that travel and exchange flourished, contradicting any picture of isolation. It was not cut off, it plainly had great institutions of study, and the Arabs held only Sindh, not the whole land.

59. (B) A sabha is the self-governing village assembly and a samanta is a tribute-paying subordinate ruler — two pillars of the layered politics of the age. Swapping the two, or equating them with a temple, reservoir, poem or pilgrim, misdefines both terms.

60. (A) Each item — mathematics, learning, art and devotion — is a creative achievement, and together they make the case that the era flourished rather than declined. They do not show an end of achievement, they came from many different kingdoms rather than one empire, and they were Indian, not Arab, accomplishments.
