## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** 'Reorganisation' fits because political units were constantly broken up and reformed into competing kingdoms, yet this happened alongside vigorous cultural and commercial life — not decline. Saying nothing of value was produced ignores the art schools, coins and Sanskrit works the period left. There was no single empire merely shuffling provinces; the defining feature is plurality of states. And the age was full of warfare, so 'no warfare, peaceful unity' is false.

**2. (D)** The Mauryan collapse is presented as an internal breakup; foreigners entered the northwest only after that frontier had already weakened. The Kushanas arrived later and never seized the whole heartland in one blow. The Indo-Greeks did not co-rule with the Mauryas — they came after the breakup. And the Satavahanas, a Deccan power, did not topple the Mauryan capital.

**3. (B)** Because the weakening comes before the invasions in time, the weakening is the cause and the entry of foreigners the effect — a cause must precede its effect. The reverse claim, that invasions caused the collapse, inverts the sequence. The Mauryas and invaders were distinct peoples, not the same. And the northwest had indeed been Mauryan territory before the breakup.

**4. (A)** The Shunga dynasty began when the Mauryan commander-in-chief assassinated the last emperor and seized power — an internal coup, not a foreign conquest. There was no election of kings. He did not inherit by marriage, and he led no foreign army; the takeover came from inside the Mauryan establishment.

**5. (B)** The age combined many rival kingdoms (political competition) with cross-cultural mixing in art, coinage and religion (cultural blending). It was not war-free; competition often meant fighting. It was not a dark age, given Gandhara/Mathura art and Sanskrit works. And many of its kingdoms — the Satavahanas, the Tamil kingdoms, the Shungas — were Indian, not foreign.

**6. (A)** When a marriage immediately converts enemies into allies, it functions as diplomacy conducted through matrimony — the chapter's point about matrimonial alliances. Treating it as merely private ignores the political effect just described. It is a marriage, not a sacrifice to the gods, and it builds alliance rather than extracting tribute.

**7. (A)** The ashvamedha let a king proclaim paramountcy: lands the freed horse crossed without challenge were claimed as his. It did not choose a successor, was not a wedding rite, and did not begin a calendar era — that role belongs to a samvat such as the Shaka era.

**8. (A)** A queen performing the kingship-claiming sacrifice shows women could hold real authority — overturning the idea that only men ruled. It does not show the rite had lost its meaning; she used it precisely because it asserted sovereignty. And her performing a Vedic sacrifice shows the Satavahanas kept, not abandoned, such rituals.

**9. (D)** The Shungas are remembered for the Vedic revival and patronage of the Bharhut Stupa's carvings. The Grand Anicut was a Chola work on the Kaveri. The Silk Route was the Kushanas' base of wealth. Greek-portrait coins belong to the Indo-Greeks, not the Shungas.

**10. (B)** The pairing shows religious patronage was not exclusive: a Vedic-reviving house could still support Buddhist art. It does not prove secret conversion, the stupa was genuinely Buddhist (not a relabelled temple), and Buddhism was clearly still alive, since its monuments were being adorned.

**11. (B)** A stupa is a dome-shaped Buddhist monument, like Bharhut, often ringed by carved railings. A victory pillar (e.g. the Heliodorus pillar) is a different thing. A river-diverting dam is an anicut, and a Tamil poetry anthology is a Sangam collection — none is a stupa.

**12. (B)** The Shungas held north and central India; the Satavahanas built their power in the Deccan. The Tamil far south belonged to the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas; Kalinga was Kharavela's. The last option reverses the correct regions of the two dynasties.

**13. (D)** Ship coins point to flourishing sea trade, which fits the Satavahanas' use of Deccan ports. The image is not proof of sea-worship, nor of losing land, nor of a navy against the Kushanas — the Kushanas were a northern land power reached by no Satavahana fleet.

**14. (D)** A mother-derived royal name plus a land-donating queen mother together show the high standing of royal women. The name is not that of a god, 'Gautami' is a personal/lineage name rather than a city, and being called 'son of Gautami' does not make the king a monk.

**15. (C)** Such queens are known from inscriptions recording their gifts. They did not campaign against Rome, did not write the Tamil Sangam poetry (a southern tradition), and did not design Gandhara art (a northwestern Greco-Buddhist style).

**16. (D)** The Satavahanas reached Rome through sea trade via their Deccan ports — hence the ship coins. They had no land border with Rome, did not control the overland Silk Route (that was the Kushanas), and were an independent kingdom, not a Roman province.

**17. (A)** Kharavela ruled Kalinga and was a committed Jain — hence 'monk-king' though he kept his throne. He did not abdicate to wander, was not a Kushana official in Gandhara, and founded no new religion; he followed the existing Jain faith.

**18. (D)** Hathigumpha is an inscription — writing cut into rock — and is the chief source for Kharavela, illustrating reliance on inscriptions. Sangam poems are a southern tradition, not about Kalinga; Roman ledgers do not supply his record; and a single inscription, not a coin portrait, is what informs us here.

**19. (C)** The three crowned kingdoms of the Tamil south were the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas. The Shungas, Satavahanas and Chedis lay to the north and in the Deccan; the Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Kushanas were northwestern foreign rulers; and the Mauryas, Nandas and Guptas were northern imperial dynasties.

**20. (D)** The age is named after the Sangam — collections of early Tamil poetry that are our main window onto it. It is not named for battles, not for a royal-electing assembly, and not for a Buddhist council in the Deccan.

**21. (C)** Sangam poetry sings chiefly of love, valour and the giving of gifts. It is not a manual of Vedic sacrifice, not a chronicle of the northern Kushana emperors, and not a record of Silk-Route tolls.

**22. (C)** Roman gold on the Tamil coast reflects sea trade in which Rome bought spices and goods, paying in coin. There is no record of a Roman conquest or taxation of the south, the Tamils minted at home rather than in Rome, and their kings were not crowned in Rome.

**23. (D)** The Pandyas were a Tamil far-southern kingdom. The Cholas were also Tamil-southern, not Deccan (the Deccan was Satavahana). Kalinga was Kharavela's, not the Cheras'. And the northwest belonged to foreign rulers, not the Pandyas.

**24. (C)** The Grand Anicut is a dam built to distribute the Kaveri's water over the delta. It is not a Buddhist stupa, not a fort against Roman ships, and not a Greek victory pillar.

**25. (B)** An anicut is a low check-dam that lifts river water into irrigation canals. A counted era is a samvat, a dome monument is a stupa, and a marriage treaty is a matrimonial alliance — none is an anicut.

**26. (A)** By irrigating the delta the anicut created fertile rice land that underpinned Chola wealth. It was not a fortress, did not block ships (it is an inland dam), and was not a boundary marker with the Pandyas.

**27. (C)** The southern kingdoms prospered through maritime trade, including with Rome — confirmed by Roman coin finds. They did not control the land-based Silk Route (a Kushana asset), took no tribute from the Kushanas, and held no Central Asian mines.

**28. (C)** Roman familiarity with Tamil-coast goods reflects busy sea trade between south India and the Mediterranean. It does not imply conquest, colonisation by Romans, or Tamil rule in Italy — trade contact, not political control, is what such knowledge shows.

**29. (D)** The Satavahanas used Deccan ports and the Tamils used southern ports — both relied on the sea. Neither used overland routes to Rome; the Silk Route (land) was the Kushanas' connection to the Mediterranean, not these two powers'.

**30. (A)** The Indo-Greeks descended from the Greek satraps and settlers who remained after Alexander's campaigns. They were not Shungas (an Indian dynasty), not Chedis of Kalinga, and not the later Kushanas, who were a separate Central Asian people.

**31. (C)** A Greek envoy dedicating a pillar to an Indian god is a clear sign of cultural blending and even adoption of Indian beliefs. It contradicts the ideas that the Indo-Greeks forced Greek gods on Indians, shunned Indian religion, or destroyed Indian shrines.

**32. (D)** With few inscriptions surviving, Indo-Greek coins are the chief source for these kings' names and faces. They were genuine trade currency, not Rome-minted, and certainly did not record Tamil Sangam poetry.

**33. (B)** The Indo-Greeks moved in because the Mauryan collapse had left the northwest frontier weak — the breakup is the cause. No Chola invitation brought them, the Kushanas did not build the Silk Route to admit them, and the Shaka calendar is unrelated to their entry.

**34. (B)** The foreign rulers were mostly absorbed, with Indian culture staying dominant — the chapter's central theme. They did not wipe out Indian culture, did not remain wholly separate (Heliodorus and Gandhara art show mixing), and never ruled the whole subcontinent as one empire.

**35. (A)** The Shakas are also called Indo-Scythians. The Indo-Greeks were a separate people of Alexandrian descent; 'Indo-Romans' is not a ruling group; and 'Indo-Cholas' is not a real term — the Cholas were an indigenous Tamil dynasty.

**36. (C)** A still-used Shaka-based calendar shows the period left enduring legacies, not just ruins. It therefore contradicts the claims that nothing survived, that Romans created it, or that the age stood apart from Indian traditions — a calendar woven into Indian life proves the opposite.

**37. (C)** A samvat is a counted calendar era beginning from a fixed point, as with the Shaka era. A river dam is an anicut, a dome shrine is a stupa, and a royal marriage is a matrimonial alliance — none of these is a samvat.

**38. (C)** Kanishka is the famous Kushana ruler. Kharavela ruled Kalinga, Pushyamitra founded the Shungas, and Gautamiputra was a Satavahana — none of them Kushana.

**39. (A)** The Kushanas grew rich by controlling the overland Silk Route between China and the Mediterranean. The Grand Anicut was a Chola irrigation work, the Bharhut railings were Shunga-era Buddhist art, and the Sangam academies were a Tamil literary tradition — none was a Kushana source of wealth.

**40. (B)** The Silk Route was a web of land routes across Central Asia linking China and the Mediterranean. It was not a Deccan-to-Rome sea lane (that was Satavahana sea trade), not a Chola delta road, and not a poets' circuit.

**41. (A)** Coins showing his titles and a variety of gods reveal Kanishka's styling and the many faiths he patronised. They do not show rejection of religion (the many gods prove the opposite), do not place him in the Tamil south, and do not suggest he avoided trade — coinage itself implies commerce.

**42. (C)** Land trade via the Silk Route plus sea trade from Deccan and southern ports together show India linked outward by both land and sea. This refutes the ideas that trade was only village-level, that India was isolated, or that it relied on one route alone.

**43. (B)** Greek-style robes and wavy hair mark the Greco-Buddhist Gandhara school; fuller figures in spotted red sandstone mark the indigenous Mathura school. Reversing them is the classic trap. 'Sangam' is Tamil poetry, not sculpture, and 'Bharhut' names a stupa site, not the red-sandstone school — which is Mathura.

**44. (B)** Gandhara art fuses Greek (Hellenistic) form with Buddhist subjects. The red-sandstone indigenous style is Mathura, Tamil poetry is the Sangam tradition, and dam engineering describes the anicut — none of these is Gandhara.

**45. (A)** Mathura art is the indigenous red-sandstone school with fuller forms. Hellenistic robes and wavy hair belong to Gandhara, love-and-heroism poems are Sangam works, and ship-stamped coins are Satavahana — none describes Mathura.

**46. (D)** The textbook has swapped the two: Gandhara is the Greco-Buddhist style and Mathura the indigenous red-sandstone style. Neither is Tamil poetry, Mathura is not purely Greek (it is indigenous), and both are sculpture, not poetry.

**47. (D)** Cultural confluence means traditions merge into a shared style without the local character being erased — Gandhara blends Greek form with Indian Buddhist content. It is not the destruction of one culture, not influence-free art (it is plainly Hellenistic-influenced), and not a Vedic revival.

**48. (A)** The chapter links the period to the great Sanskrit epics, the Mahabharata and the Ramayana. The Sangam anthologies are Tamil, not Sanskrit; the Arthashastra belongs to the earlier Mauryan court; and Hathigumpha is an inscription, not a literary epic.

**49. (A)** Indian themes at the core of foreign rulers' coins and art show India absorbed and transformed the newcomers. It did not expel them by force (their objects survive and were patronised), was not fully Hellenised (Indian themes remained central), and clearly did not halt art — the art is the very evidence.

**50. (A)** Confluence is shown by blended Gandhara art, Kanishka's multi-deity coins and thriving Sanskrit literature. It is not shown by one imposed uniform style, by the absence of foreign elements (confluence requires them), or by religions vanishing — the period's religions remained vigorous.

**51. (C)** Coins and inscriptions are physical sources that carry much of what we know, since continuous written chronicles are scarce. People could read and write (inscriptions prove it), buildings like stupas do survive, and the histories were not Roman translations — the point is the reliance on material evidence.

**52. (C)** Coins, cave inscriptions and sculptures are genuine material remains from the period. Election ballots, newspapers, emails, photographs, passports, constitutions, voter rolls and tax software are all modern items that did not exist then and cannot be physical sources for it.

**53. (B)** The Mauryan breakup came first; the Satavahanas rose in the post-Mauryan Deccan; Kanishka's Kushana power came later still. The other orderings wrongly place Kanishka or the Satavahanas before the Mauryan breakup, which contradicts the chapter's chronology.

**54. (D)** The thesis is supported by many rival kingdoms existing alongside flourishing Gandhara/Mathura art, lively trade and the Sanskrit epics — division and creativity together. A peaceful single empire would remove the 'division' the student is arguing for; art and trade did not cease; and foreign rulers did not ban Indian culture — they were absorbed by it.

**55. (D)** A coin combining a Greek-style portrait with an Indian deity in Indian script shows the foreign rulers blended their conventions with Indian ones. It refutes the idea that they rejected Indian language and gods, does not imply a single subcontinental empire, and a coin itself shows engagement with trade, not indifference to it.

**56. (C)** The foreign rulers are indeed treated as contributors because Indian culture remained dominant and absorbed what they brought — so both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion. The reason is not false (absorption is the chapter's theme), and the assertion is not false (they were largely integrated, not erasers), so the partial and wholly-false options fail.

**57. (D)** Where the Mauryas formed one large empire, the following age splintered into many rival kingdoms — the heart of 'reorganisation'. It was not a still-larger empire, not one foreign empire over all India, and certainly not stateless — the chapter is full of named kingdoms.

**58. (A)** A sea-trade ship coin, Roman gold from sea commerce and a Silk-Route (land) marker together show India linked to distant lands by sea and land. They do not show conquest by Rome or China, contradict purely internal trade, and disprove reliance on a single route — two different routes are represented.

**59. (B)** The Shungas were an indigenous dynasty of north-central India; the Indo-Greeks were foreigners of Alexandrian descent who moved into the northwest after the Mauryan breakup. They were not both Greek-descended foreigners, not Tamil southern powers, and the labels are not reversed — the Shungas were native, the Indo-Greeks foreign.

**60. (B)** Poems celebrating chiefs for generosity show that liberal gift-giving was a valued royal virtue in the Tamil south. The poems would make no sense if giving were forbidden, they presuppose chiefs and unequal wealth rather than equality, and Sangam verse sings of love, valour and generosity — not Vedic sacrifice.
