Worked Solutions

1. (C) India's First Urbanisation was the Harappan (Indus / Sindhu-Sarasvati) civilisation, with its planned cities, drains and writing. The mahaajanapadas, Maurya Empire and Cholas all came much later, in or after the SECOND Urbanisation — so they are time-period traps.

2. (D) After the Harappan order broke down in the early 2nd millennium BCE, urban life was largely absent for about a thousand years before cities returned in the Ganga plains. "Five thousand years" confuses the age of the whole story with the length of the gap.

3. (C) "Urban order" means trade, writing, sanitation and administration held together — not just buildings. The clinching evidence is that people slipped back into simple village life, losing those systems. Building larger cities (A) or forming mahaajanapadas (D) happened only much later; iron (B) belongs to the later Second Urbanisation.

4. (D) Drainage, writing and citadels were all features of the Harappan cities that were lost. Iron, however, was NOT a Harappan feature at all — the Harappans used copper and bronze — and iron only became widespread later. So iron did not "disappear with" the Harappans; it had not yet arrived.

5. (C) The 1st-millennium-BCE wave of city-building in the Ganga plains is the Second Urbanisation (the Harappan being the First). "Third" and "Vedic Urbanisation" are invented labels; "Harappan revival" wrongly implies the old civilisation came back.

6. (A) Correct order: Harappan civilisation (ii) → janapadas form (iii) → these merge into the sixteen mahaajanapadas (i) → the first empires (iv). Option C wrongly puts the mahaajanapadas before the janapadas they grew out of.

7. (A) Because there are no written histories from that age, historians use two main sources: archaeology (digging up city remains) and ancient literature (books that name and describe the cities). Photographs, TV and the Constitution are all modern, so the other options are anachronisms.

8. (D) Confidence is highest when two independent kinds of evidence agree — the excavated city remains AND the ancient books. A single poem (A) or "looking old" (B) is weak; the Harappans (C) did not write about the Second Urbanisation.

9. (B) jana = "people", pada = "foot", so janapada means "where the people have set foot", i.e. where a clan has settled. The other options confuse it with an army, a coin or an empire.

10. (C) A janapada was a territory settled by a clan and led by a rajaa — settled, not wandering (A), and it did have a ruler, unlike the "no ruler" guild in B. A single gateway (D) is far too small.

11. (B) Janapadas count as early states because settled clans identified with their land, needed a rajaa to lead and protect them, and grew larger through trade — the marks of an organised state. They did have rulers (so A is wrong) and were not Harappan (C) or coin-based (D).

12. (D) Janapadas merged into mahaajanapadas by about the 8th–7th centuries BCE. The 2nd–1st centuries BCE (A) is the age of empires, the 20th–19th centuries BCE (C) is Harappan times, and "centuries CE" (B) is far too late.

13. (D) "maha-" means "great", so mahaajanapada = "great janapada" (a bigger state formed by merging). The other options ignore the word's parts entirely.

14. (A) The most frequently cited list names 16 mahaajanapadas. 10, 12 and 20 are near-miss number traps.

15. (A) Ashmaka lay in central India near the Godavari River. The Ganga and Sindhu belong to the north, and the Kaveri lies far to the south — geography traps.

16. (C) The Ganga plains were ideal because fertile soil produced food surpluses, iron sources were nearby, and trade routes linked the region — exactly the conditions big states need. The plains were warm and well-watered (not cold/dry, A; not riverless, B), and the Harappans had not built there (D).

17. (C) A moat is a deep, wide, water-filled ditch outside the walls, for defence. A watchtower (A), market (B) and palace (D) are all parts of a city but not a moat.

18. (C) Narrow gateways let a few guards control who and what entered or left (useful for defence and for taxing trade). Beauty (A), drainage (B) and elephant traffic (D) are not the chapter's reason.

19. (D) That cities like Rajagriha and Varanasi are still alive 2,500 years later suggests their sites had lasting natural and strategic advantages that keep attracting people. It does not mean nothing ever changed (A), that the Harappans founded them (B), or that they were unfortified (C).

20. (D) Magadha, Kosala and Avanti were monarchies. Vajji and Malla (A) were the republics; sabhaa/samiti (B) are assemblies, not states; Cholas and Cheras (C) were southern kingdoms.

21. (D) The advisory assemblies were the sabhaa and samiti. Uttaraapatha/Dakshinaapatha (A) are trade routes, varna/jaati (B) is the social system, and the Cholas/Pandyas (C) are southern kingdoms.

22. (A) The words sabhaa and samiti first appear in the Vedas — the oldest texts — showing how ancient the idea of assemblies is. Buddhist, Jain and Tamil texts came later.

23. (A) The assemblies were made up mostly of the elders of the clan, whose experience commanded respect. Foreign merchants, royal children and soldiers were not their main members.

24. (C) That a good rajaa was "not supposed to rule by himself" shows even monarchs were expected to take advice from assemblies and ministers. It does not mean he had no power (A), that he was elected yearly (B — that fits republics, not monarchies), or that there was no army (D).

25. (D) "Hereditary" means the position passed by birth, so the next rajaa was normally the son of the previous one. Being chosen by an assembly (A) describes the gana/sangha republics, not a monarchy.

26. (A) A monarch's duties included revenue (taxes), law and order, and an army with fortifications. He did NOT select his successor by public election — succession was hereditary — so that is the odd one out.

27. (A) A non-monarchy where the assembly took major decisions was a gana or sangha. A janapada (B) is just a settled territory, a varna (C) is a social category, and a moat (D) is a ditch.

28. (C) Vajji (Vrijji) and Malla were the ganas/sanghas. Magadha, Kosala, Avanti, Anga, Gandhaara and Kuru in the other options were monarchies.

29. (D) In a gana/sangha the rajaa was selected by the assembly through discussion and, if needed, a vote — not inherited (A), not chosen by Harappan priests (B, an anachronism), and not appointed by a foreign empire (C).

30. (C) What makes Vajji and Malla republics is that their assemblies took major decisions and even selected the ruler — power lay with the group, not one hereditary king. Army size (A), area (B) and coin use (D) are not the defining feature.

31. (D) The correct reply is that NOT every mahaajanapada was a monarchy: at least Vajji and Malla were ganas/sanghas where the assembly decided. "No exceptions" (A) and "only in the south" (C) repeat the error; "none had kings" (B) overcorrects, since many WERE monarchies.

32. (C) They were "early republics" but not modern democracies because decisions were taken jointly by the heads of leading families, not by every individual. They DID have assemblies (so A is wrong) and the rajaa did NOT inherit power (B describes a monarchy).

33. (A) The big new metal of the Second Urbanisation was iron (the Harappans had mastered copper and bronze). Gold, silver and tin are distractors — silver was used for coins, but the transformative tool/weapon metal was iron.

34. (D) The student is wrong because iron became widespread only later, by the late 2nd millennium BCE, as a technology of the SECOND Urbanisation — after the Harappans. Iron WAS used in ancient India (so A is false) and it is harder, not softer, than bronze (B), and not used only for coins (C).

35. (C) Iron mattered because iron axes and ploughs cleared forests and turned heavy soil, creating the food surpluses that fed city populations and armies. It was not worshipped to draw crowds (A), did not replace trade routes (B), and was not used to print books (D).

36. (B) Stronger iron weapons made armies more powerful, fuelling wars and the growth of bigger kingdoms through consolidation. Warfare did not disappear (A), farming did not end (C), and writing is unrelated here (D).

37. (A) India's first coins were silver punch-marked coins. Copper paisa, gold dinars and cowrie shells are later or different forms of money — date/type traps.

38. (D) They are "punch-marked" because symbols were punched (stamped) into the soft silver. The fanciful options (cloth-punching, fist shape, boxing) miss the literal meaning.

39. (A) Coins were invented mainly to make trade easier than barter — a trusted standard-weight piece of metal anyone would accept. Making people rich (B), paying only in gold (C) or decorating temples (D) were not the purpose.

40. (B) Barter is clumsy because you must find someone who has what you want AND wants what you have (a "double coincidence of wants"). Barter needs no bank (A), is not limited to night (C), and does not require iron tools (D).

41. (C) Usually a mahaajanapada minted its own coins, but coins from neighbouring regions were used and traded too, even reaching abroad. So claims that no foreign coins were used (A), that only Harappans minted (B), or that coins were banned outside the capital (D) are wrong.

42. (B) Society organised itself through a two-fold system of jaati (occupational community) and varna (four broad categories). Sabhaa/samiti (A) are assemblies, gana/sangha (C) are types of state, and the routes (D) are for trade.

43. (B) A jaati is an occupational community whose specific skills and customs pass from parents to children. A defensive ditch (A) is a moat, a trade route (C) and a coin (D) are unrelated.

44. (D) The Kshatriyas were expected to defend society and the land and to wage war if necessary. Brahmins handled knowledge and ritual, Vaishyas trade/farming, Shudras service roles.

45. (D) The Brahmins were associated with preserving and spreading knowledge and performing rituals. Kshatriyas (A) were the warriors — the classic mix-up with Q44.

46. (B) The chapter corrects the "always rigid" idea by noting that early on the system was more flexible, and people could change occupations when circumstances (like a drought) demanded; it grew rigid only later. So "never existed" (A), "only Harappans" (C) and "rigid from day one" (D) are wrong.

47. (A) The northern route was the Uttaraapatha ("uttara" = north). The Dakshinaapatha (B) was the southern route; "Rajagriha road" and "Vindhya path" (C, D) are invented names.

48. (A) The Dakshinaapatha started near Kaushaambi/Prayagraj and crossed the Vindhya Range to head south. The Himalayas and Aravallis lie to the north/northwest, and the Western Ghats run along the southwest coast — geography traps.

49. (B) Along the great routes moved not only goods but also pilgrims, armies, ideas and coins — which is how distant regions came to share a culture. The "only…" options (A, C, D) wrongly narrow this.

50. (B) Shishupalgarh, with its square plan and strong fortifications, was the capital of Kalinga in the east. Magadha, Gandhaara and Avanti were elsewhere.

51. (C) Around 400 BCE the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas emerged in the south. Magadha/Kosala/Avanti (A) were northern monarchies, Vajji/Malla/Anga (B) were eastern/republican, and Kuru/Panchaala/Matsya (D) were northern janapadas.

52. (B) The south was drawn into trade because of its valuable resources — gems, gold and spices — which the rest of India wanted. A cold climate (A), lack of rivers (C) or being far from the sea (D) would have discouraged trade, not encouraged it.

53. (A) By about 300–200 BCE the Subcontinent had become one vibrant, interconnected land, with goods and culture even reaching beyond India. It was certainly not empty (B), split into islands (C), or back under the Harappans (D).

54. (B) Around that same time the mahaajanapadas came to an end, making way for the great empires. They were not just being founded (A), did not all turn into republics (C), and did not relocate to the Indus (D).

55. (C) The valid causal chain is: iron spread → more farmland cleared → food surplus → cities and states grew.
