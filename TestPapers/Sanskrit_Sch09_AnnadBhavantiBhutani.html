Instructions: Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Questions test the Gita 3.14 cycle and its grammar. Watch the classic traps: भूतानि means 'beings / creatures' (root भू), NOT 'ghosts'; अन्नात् is the ABLATIVE ('from food', a source not a maker), and अन्नाद् in अन्नाद्भवन्ति is the same case after sandhi (त् -> द् before भ्); जानीयात् is the OPTATIVE ('one should know'), not past tense; ब्रह्म (neuter) is the all-pervading Absolute, not the creator-god ब्रह्मा or the priest ब्रह्मन्. Keep the cycle order straight: action -> yajna -> rain -> food -> beings. Sanskrit in Devanagari. Do not write on this paper — mark answers on your answer sheet.

Worked Solutions

1. (B) It means 'from food, beings come to be'. It does not say beings create food, food destroys beings, or beings eat ghosts.

2. (A) The verse is from the Bhagavad Gita (3.14). It is not the Ramayana, the Arthashastra, or the Isha Upanishad.

3. (C) भूतानि means 'beings / living creatures'. It does not mean ghosts, foods, or rains.

4. (A) भवन्ति means 'they come to be / arise' (from भू). It does not mean destroy, eat, or 'should know'.

5. (D) अन्नात् is ablative, 'from food'. 'To food' is dative, 'of food' genitive, and 'without food' would need a different construction.

6. (D) Food comes from rain, rain from yajna, and yajna from action (karma). The other orderings scramble the chain.

7. (B) Food comes from rain (पर्जन्य). Rain comes from yajna, yajna from action, and beings from food.

8. (D) Rain comes from yajna (sacrifice). Food comes from rain, not the reverse; beings and herbs are elsewhere in the cycle.

9. (C) Yajna comes from action (कर्म). Chemistry, beings and food are not its source in the verse.

10. (A) The chain ends in beings (भूतानि). Not ghosts, herbs, or chemistry.

11. (A) The cycle teaches interdependence - everything depends on the rest. Not independence, chaos, or destruction.

12. (D) Human action ultimately supports the food and life of all beings. Not nothing, only the rich, or ghosts.

13. (B) अन्नात् is the ablative of अन्न ('from food'). It is not nominative, accusative, or dative.

14. (B) The ablative marks food as the source from which beings arise - dependence, not a maker, enemy, or ruler.

15. (B) Ablatives express 'arise from', so the chain shows dependence, not manufacture, destruction, or chance.

16. (D) The -द् results from sandhi: अन्नात् + भवन्ति, where त् becomes voiced द् before the voiced भ्. It is not a plural ending, a root, or a tense.

17. (C) त् becomes the voiced द् because the following भ् is voiced. Plurality, tense and gender are irrelevant here.

18. (B) Despite the sandhi, अन्नाद् is still the ablative ('from food'). It is not nominative, accusative, or genitive.

19. (A) Classical Sanskrit भूतानि means beings / creatures. Not ghosts, foods, or sacrifices.

20. (D) Here भूतानि means beings / creatures, not the folk-Hindi 'ghost', 'spirits of the dead', or 'demons'.

21. (D) भूत and भूतानि come from भू ('to be'). ज्ञा is 'to know', पा 'to drink', and नी 'to lead'.

22. (A) Context decides: Vedanta/Gita gives 'being', folk-Hindi 'ghost'. Not the speaker's age, time, or number.

23. (C) In the Gita context it is about all living beings arising from food. Not ghosts, nor food from ghosts, nor destruction.

24. (B) ब्रह्म here is the all-pervading Cosmic Power (the impersonal Absolute). A priest is ब्रह्मन्, the creator-god ब्रह्मा, and a fox शृगालः.

25. (A) ब्रह्म = सर्वव्याप्तः परमेश्वरः = 'the all-pervading Supreme'. Not a local deity, a human teacher, or a herb.

26. (D) This ब्रह्म is neuter (the impersonal Absolute). Masculine ब्रह्मा is the creator-god and ब्रह्मन् a priest; it is not feminine.

27. (D) ब्रह्मा is the creator-god, distinct from the neuter Absolute ब्रह्म. A priest is ब्रह्मन्; a herb is ओषधि.

28. (A) ब्रह्मन् means a priest. The creator-god is ब्रह्मा, the Absolute neuter ब्रह्म, and a sacrifice यज्ञ.

29. (C) The cycle expresses the omnipresent ब्रह्म (Cosmic Power). Not a ghost, fox, or boaster.

30. (B) रसायनशास्त्रम् means 'chemistry'. Astronomy is खगोलशास्त्रम्, and grammar व्याकरणम्.

31. (C) शास्त्रम् means 'science / treatise'. A herb is ओषधि, a ghost भूत (in folk use), and a sacrifice यज्ञ.

32. (A) रसायन, once Ayurvedic tonics, is now reused for chemistry. Not astronomy, poetry, or cooking.

33. (B) It shows Sanskrit can adapt old vocabulary to new ideas. It is not unable to describe science, word-less, or unused.

34. (C) जानीयात् means '(one) should know' (optative). It is not past 'knew', passive 'is known', or the command 'know!'.

35. (B) जानीयात् is in the optative (विधिलिङ्). It is not future, past, or imperative.

36. (C) The optative expresses 'should / ought to'. It is not 'will', 'did', or a plain present fact.

37. (C) जानीयात् is the optative of ज्ञा ('to know'). भू is 'to be', पा 'to drink', and गम् 'to go'.

38. (B) जानीयात् = अवगच्छेत् = 'should understand / should know'. Not 'did understand', 'is understood', or a command.

39. (C) The optative gently states what one ought to do. Not what definitely happened, is happening now, or is forbidden.

40. (D) यज्ञ is a Vedic fire-ritual. Image-based worship is पूजा; a herb is ओषधि; a ghost is folk भूत.

41. (B) पूजा is devotional, often image-based worship; यज्ञ is the Vedic fire-ritual. पूजा is not a chemistry term or a verb.

42. (C) In the Gita, यज्ञ broadly means selfless action including ritual. Not only temple worship, chemistry, or food.

43. (C) They differ in ritual tradition - fire-offering (यज्ञ) vs devotional worship (पूजा). Not just spelling, tense, or number.

44. (D) ओषधीनाम् means 'of herbs / plants'. Not of ghosts, sacrifices, or chemistry.

45. (D) ओषधीनाम् is the genitive plural ('of herbs'). It is not ablative singular, accusative plural, or nominative singular.

46. (A) उत्पत्तिक्रमम् is 'the order of origin'. Not destruction, an experiment, or a ghost story.

47. (B) The order of origin is the chain action -> yajna -> rain -> food -> beings. Not the Sun-names, six faults, or va-qualities.

48. (D) ज्ञेयः is a gerundive, 'should be known / is to be known'. Not 'known' (ज्ञात), a knower, or knowledge.

49. (B) ज्ञेयः means 'to be known', while ज्ञात means 'already known'. It is not a knower or knowledge itself.

50. (D) Just as कार्य = 'to be done', ज्ञेय = 'to be known'. Not 'already known', 'a doer', or 'an action'.

51. (A) जिज्ञासा means 'curiosity / the wish to know'. Not a sacrifice, herb, or ghost.

52. (C) Both come from ज्ञा, connecting the wish to know (जिज्ञासा) and the duty to know (जानीयात्). Not eating/drinking, ritual, or tense.

53. (A) भूतानि = beings. भूतानि are not foods, अन्नात् is 'from food' (not 'to food'), and जानीयात् is 'should know' (not 'knew').

54. (A) जानीयात् is the optative and अन्नात् the ablative. The other pairings misname the mood, case, or ज्ञेयः.

55. (C) ब्रह्म (neuter) is the Absolute and ब्रह्मा the creator-god. ब्रह्मन् is a priest; they do not all mean the same.

56. (A) Food comes from rain and rain from yajna. The other pairings scramble the cycle.

57. (C) The theme is the interdependent cycle of creation, rooted in the all-pervading ब्रह्म. Not the fox fable, banking, or government.

58. (D) It shows ancient insight and modern science can describe the same world. Chemistry is not useless, the Gita is not only chemistry, and science need not contradict the verse.

59. (B) It teaches that all beings depend on food, which depends on rain, yajna and action in one cycle. The other readings are wrong.

60. (A) भूतानि means beings/creatures, and जानीयात् is the optative ('one should know'). The other options keep one or both errors.
