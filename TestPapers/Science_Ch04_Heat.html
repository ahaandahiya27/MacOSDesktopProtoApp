Worked Solutions

1. (B) Temperature tells how hot something is; heat is the *total* energy. The cup at 100 °C is hotter (higher temperature), but the bucket holds far more water, so it can hold more total heat even at a lower temperature. "Hotter always means more heat" (D) is the classic trap.

2. (D) Heat is a form of energy that flows from a hotter body to a colder one until both reach the same temperature. The "degree of hotness" (A) defines *temperature*, not heat; (B) and (C) confuse temperature with heat.

3. (C) Temperature is the degree of hotness and does not depend on how much water there is. Both mugs are at 90 °C, so both are at the same temperature — the larger one simply stores more heat.

4. (C) Heat always flows from the hotter object to the colder one. The rod is hotter, so heat flows from rod to water until both reach a common (equal) temperature; it does not flow back to make the rod hotter.

5. (B) The tea is hotter than the spoon, so heat flows from the hotter tea to the cooler spoon, warming the spoon. The spoon makes no heat of its own, and the tea loses (not gains) heat.

6. (A) A liquid expands (takes up more space) when heated and contracts when cooled. The thermometer turns this volume change into a moving thread; it does not rely on colour change, boiling, or weight change.

7. (A) When warmed, the liquid in the bulb expands and needs more room, so it is forced up the narrow tube, raising the thread. It is not pushed by air or made lighter than glass.

8. (C) A very narrow bore means even a tiny expansion of the liquid pushes the thread a large, easily seen distance, making fine temperatures readable. (Less liquid, no escape, cooler glass are not the main reason.)

9. (A) Halfway between the 0 and 10 marks is 5 °C. (Melting ice is actually 0 °C, but the question fixes the thread's *position* at the midpoint, which reads 5.)

10. (A) A clinical thermometer is built to read human body temperatures, so its scale runs only about 35 °C to 42 °C. The wide −10 to 110 range belongs to a laboratory thermometer.

11. (C) The kink (a sharp narrow bend) stops the liquid thread from slipping back when the thermometer is removed from the body, so the reading stays put until you read it.

12. (C) A laboratory thermometer is read *while still in contact* with the substance, so its thread must rise and fall freely as the temperature changes. A kink would trap the thread, so it is left out.

13. (D) A lab thermometer spans a huge range, so its divisions are coarse (usually 1 °C). It cannot read a fever to one-tenth of a degree the way a clinical thermometer can, so it is the wrong tool for option (D). It handles 4, 45 and 80 °C fine.

14. (C) Shaking jerks the trapped thread back down past the kink, below normal body temperature, so the next patient's reading starts from a low value and is correct. It is not for warming or cleaning.

15. (D) Each small mark is 0.1 °C. Two small marks past 37 means 37 + 2 × 0.1 = 37.2 °C. (37.02 wrongly treats each mark as 0.01.)

16. (B) The normal temperature of a healthy human body is about 37 °C (roughly 98.6 °F). 42 °C would be a dangerous fever; 25–30 °C is far too low.

17. (A) Ten equal divisions span 10 °C, so each division = 10 ÷ 10 = 1 °C. (0.1 °C would be right only if there were 100 divisions in that gap.)

18. (D) Each small division is 1 °C, so three divisions above 60 means 60 + 3 = 63 °C. (90 and 30 ignore the stated 1 °C division size.)

19. (B) Halfway between the 38 and 39 marks is 38 + 0.5 = 38.5 °C.

20. (A) 37 °C is about 98.6 °F, the normal body temperature, so a 37 °C reading means the child is at about normal — not feverish and not dangerously cold.

21. (C) Heat moving through a solid from the hot end to the cold end, while the solid itself stays put, is conduction. Convection needs the material to move; radiation needs no medium.

22. (D) Metal is a good conductor, so heat is conducted along the spoon from the flame end to the cool end, warming the handle. There is no air gap to radiate across along the metal, and the spoon makes no heat.

23. (B) Conduction is the main way heat travels through solids (their particles pass energy along while staying in place). Fluids transfer heat chiefly by convection; empty space, only by radiation.

24. (D) Metals are good conductors and non-metals (wood, plastic, rubber) are poor conductors, so heat travels fastest through the iron nail.

25. (D) Both are at room temperature, but metal conducts heat away from your warm hand quickly, cooling your skin fast, so it *feels* colder. Wood, a poor conductor, draws little heat, so it feels warmer. The metal is not actually colder.

26. (B) Wood is a poor conductor (insulator), so little heat reaches the handle, keeping it cool enough to hold. A good conductor (A) would make the handle hot — the opposite of what helps.

27. (B) Heat carried by the actual movement of a heated fluid (the warm fluid itself rising and circulating) is convection. Conduction passes heat without bulk movement; radiation needs no medium.

28. (D) Heated water at the bottom expands, becomes lighter (less dense), and rises; cooler, denser water sinks to replace it. This sets up a circulating convection current. Warm water does not stay at the bottom.

29. (A) Warm air rises and cool air sinks. Heating from near the floor lets warm air rise and spread; cooling from high on the wall lets cool air sink and circulate. Both use convection currents to mix the whole room.

30. (C) The crystals reveal the convection current: heated water rises in the middle (over the flame) and sinks along the cooler sides, tracing a looping path. It neither stays still nor only sinks.

31. (D) Between the Sun and Earth there is near-empty space with no air or matter to carry heat, so the heat must travel by radiation, which needs no material medium. Conduction and convection both need matter.

32. (B) Only radiation can cross a vacuum, because it needs no medium. Conduction needs particles in contact and convection needs a moving fluid, so neither works in a vacuum.

33. (D) The hot air rises straight up, so convection is not warming your face; instead, heat radiation travels in straight lines from the fire directly to your skin. That is why you feel warm facing the fire.

34. (C) Every warm object radiates heat, and the hotter it is the more heat it radiates each second. So a hotter object radiates more than a cooler one.

35. (D) The statement is true: radiation can cross empty space, while conduction (through particles) and convection (through a moving fluid) both need matter to travel through.

36. (D) Good conductors are metals: copper, aluminium and iron. The other lists mix in wood, plastic, air, wool, rubber, glass or cork, which are insulators (poor conductors).

37. (A) Trapped air is one of the best everyday insulators (poor conductors), which is why wool and sweaters keep us warm. Iron, aluminium and silver are all good conductors.

38. (C) The body should conduct heat (a metal pot to cook food), while the handle should *not* conduct heat (a wooden/plastic insulator) so it stays cool enough to hold. So: conduct; not conduct (insulate).

39. (C) The plastic or wooden handle works *because* it is a poor conductor, keeping the grip cool. The copper bottom, knife blade and aluminium foil all rely on being good conductors.

40. (A) A jacket makes no heat; it is a poor conductor (with trapped air) that slows the escape of your body's own heat, so you stay warm. It does not produce or reflect heat to warm you.

41. (A) Wool is a poor conductor, so the woollen wrap slows heat loss from cup Q, keeping it hotter than the bare metal cup P after ten minutes. Metal conducts heat away quickly, cooling P faster.

42. (B) Wool's fibres trap a lot of air, and trapped air is a poor conductor, so it slows the escape of body heat — that is why wool feels warmer than smooth cotton. Wool does not make heat or conduct faster.

43. (A) Two thin sweaters trap an extra layer of air between them, and trapped air is a poor conductor, so the layered arrangement slows heat loss better than a single thick layer of the same total thickness.

44. (A) Fluffing feathers traps more air among them, and trapped air is a poor conductor, so it slows the bird's heat loss and keeps it warmer. The bird is not making extra heat or improving conduction.

45. (D) Light (white) clothes reflect away most of the sunlight, so the body absorbs less heat and stays cooler — ideal for summer. Dark clothes would absorb more and feel hotter.

46. (C) Dark colours absorb more of the Sun's heat than light colours, so on a sunny winter day dark clothes feel warmer by soaking up sunlight. They do not reflect it or make heat themselves.

47. (B) Dark, dull surfaces absorb more radiation than shiny white ones, so the black-painted can heats up more in the Sun and ends up hotter than the white can.

48. (A) A sea breeze blows *from the sea toward the land*. (A land breeze would blow from land to sea — the opposite.)

49. (B) By day the land heats up faster than the sea. Air rises over the warm land, and cooler air flows in from over the sea to take its place — that incoming wind is the sea breeze (sea → land).

50. (B) At night the land cools faster than the sea, so now the sea is warmer. Air rises over the warmer sea and cooler air flows out from the land — a land breeze (land → sea).

51. (D) Wind blowing out to sea before sunrise is a land breeze: overnight the land has cooled below the sea's temperature, so air flows from the cooler land toward the warmer sea. It is not a sea breeze and does not need the Sun overhead.

52. (B) Both breezes are caused by warm air rising and cooler air flowing in to replace it, which is convection — here acting at the large coastal scale.

53. (B) The direction depends on which surface is warmer. By day the land is warmer than the sea (sea breeze, sea → land); by night the land is cooler than the sea (land breeze, land → sea). The reversed temperature difference reverses the wind.

54. (A) A clinical thermometer's scale stops at about 42 °C, far below 100 °C, so it simply cannot show boiling-water temperature (and the heat could burst it). The kink is unrelated to this limit.

55. (D) The water rises from cold to nearly boiling, so you need the wide range of a laboratory thermometer (about −10 °C to 110 °C). A clinical thermometer (35–42 °C) cannot cover that range, and not all thermometers read to 110 °C.

56. (C) Both are at the same temperature, but metal conducts heat away from your hand much faster than rubber, cooling your skin quickly, so the metal *feels* colder. The metal is not actually colder, and rubber makes no heat.

57. (C) Conduction and convection both need matter to travel through, so removing the matter (a vacuum between the walls) blocks those two paths and greatly slows heat loss. (Radiation is cut separately by the silvered, shiny walls.)

58. (B) Halfway between the −10 and 0 marks is −5 °C. (Reading it as +5 ignores the minus sign — a designed trap.)

59. (C) A white surface reflects most of the sunlight instead of absorbing it, so a whitewashed house absorbs less heat and stays cooler inside in hot, sunny regions.

60. (A) All three statements are correct: conduction needs matter (particles in contact), convection needs a moving fluid, and radiation needs no medium at all. So the count is three.

Solutions complete: 60 / 60.
