Worked Solutions

1. (C) The complete vertical top-to-bottom section through soil, showing all its horizons, is the soil profile. ("Texture" is about particle size; "percolation rate" is a drainage measure.)

2. (D) The A horizon is the topsoil — dark, rich in humus and minerals, and the layer where most roots grow. The O horizon is the surface litter, B is the lighter subsoil, C is broken parent rock.

3. (C) From the surface downward the horizons run O (organic litter), A (topsoil), B (subsoil), C (weathered rock), then bedrock — so O, A, B, C, bedrock.

4. (C) Soil forms by the slow weathering of rocks — water, wind, ice, and living things break rock into fine particles over a very long time. It is a gradual process, not a sudden one.

5. (D) Humus is the dark, crumbly material formed from decayed plant and animal remains; it makes the topsoil fertile. Clay and silt are mineral particles; bedrock is solid rock.

6. (D) Just below the topsoil (A horizon) lies the B horizon (subsoil) — lighter and harder, where clay and minerals washed down from above accumulate.

7. (B) The bottom of a soil profile is the unweathered solid rock, the bedrock or R horizon. Topsoil (A horizon), subsoil (B horizon) and the organic layer (O horizon) all lie above it.

8. (D) Soil forms extremely slowly — building even one centimetre of new topsoil can take hundreds of years. Soil is not made entirely of living things, it does contain rock-derived mineral particles, and it certainly does not form in a few days.

9. (D) The O horizon is built from plant litter turning into humus. Deserts have very few plants, so there is little litter — hence almost no O horizon. (It is not washed away by rain — deserts get little rain.)

10. (C) Sandy soil has the largest particles, clayey soil the smallest, and loamy soil is a mixture of several particle sizes. So the correct pairing is "sandy — largest particles."

11. (A) Big sand particles leave large gaps (pores) between them, so water drains straight through quickly. Sand does not dissolve, does not repel water, and it does have air spaces.

12. (B) Clayey soil has the tiniest, most tightly packed particles, so it drains slowest and holds the most water. Sandy drains fastest; loamy is in between.

13. (B) Loam is a balanced mix of sand, silt, clay and humus, so it holds enough water for roots yet still lets surplus water drain — ideal for most crops. It is not pure clay and does contain humus.

14. (A) Sandy soil has a high percolation rate, so far more water drains out of the bottom of the sandy pot than the clayey one in the same ten minutes. (The clayey pot holds the water and lets little through.)

15. (D) Black cotton soil (Deccan plateau) is sticky when wet, cracks when dry, and holds water — these are the signs of clayey soil.

16. (A) Sandy soil drains fast (high percolation rate) and holds little water (low water-holding capacity), so it loses its moisture quickly and dries out soon after rain.

17. (B) Soil is "alive" because a single gram can hold millions of micro-organisms (plus worms, insects, fungi). The other options deny that soil contains life.

18. (B) The four main ingredients of soil are mineral particles, air, water, and humus (organic matter). Plastic/glass/metal are pollutants, not natural soil components.

19. (A) When soil is water-logged, water fills the pore spaces and pushes the air out, so the roots get very little air (oxygen) — which is why they struggle.

20. (A) Weathering is the slow break-up of rock by water (flowing and freezing), wind, and growing roots over long times. A single bolt of lightning is a one-off event, not a normal soil-forming weathering agent.

21. (C) Heating moist soil drives off water as vapour, which condenses into droplets on the cooler glass — proof that the soil contained moisture (water).

22. (A) Dropping a lump of dry soil into water makes bubbles rise as water fills the pore spaces and pushes the trapped air out — a simple proof that soil contains air. (Heating shows water; weighing and rubbing show neither directly.)

23. (D) Percolation rate is the speed at which water seeps down through soil (volume of water ÷ time). It is not a weight, a humus measure, or an organism count.

24. (B) Percolation rate = volume ÷ time = 200 mL ÷ 20 min = 10 mL/min. (Multiplying instead of dividing gives the trap 4000.)

25. (A) Rate = 150 mL ÷ 5 min = 30 mL/min. (750 = 150 × 5, the multiply trap; 145 = 150 − 5, the subtract trap.)

26. (D) Same volume (300 mL): soil X takes 10 min, soil Y takes 30 min. X is faster, so X has the higher percolation rate — the mark of sandy soil. (Slower = clayey.)

27. (B) Rate = 240 mL ÷ 8 min = 30 mL/min. (248 = 240 + 8 is the add trap.)

28. (A) Time = volume ÷ rate = 200 mL ÷ 25 mL/min = 8 minutes. (Multiplying 200 × 25 gives the trap 5000.)

29. (A) Rate of P = 200 ÷ 8 = 25 mL/min; rate of Q = 360 ÷ 12 = 30 mL/min. Q (30) > P (25), so soil Q has the higher rate.

30. (C) For equal volumes, rate is inversely proportional to time. Sandy : clayey rate = (1/4) : (1/40) = 10 : 1. (Writing 4 : 40 = 1 : 10 reverses it — the classic trap.)

31. (A) Clayey soil, with its tiny tightly-packed particles, holds the most water — the highest water-holding capacity. Sand, gravel and coarse sand all drain freely.

32. (C) Sandy soil has large air-filled gaps between its big particles, so it is the best aerated. Clayey, compacted and water-logged soils all squeeze out the air.

33. (C) Paddy fields are kept flooded, so the soil must retain water — clayey soil, which holds water well, suits paddy best. Sandy soil and gravel would drain the water away.

34. (D) Cotton grows best in well-drained but humus-rich loamy or sandy-loam soil — enough water plus good aeration. Water-logged clay, bedrock and nutrient-free sand are all unsuitable.

35. (C) Mixing in sand opens up larger pore spaces, raising the percolation rate so the water-logged clay drains better. Adding more clay or water makes it worse; plastic sheeting blocks drainage.

36. (B) A soil that holds water well but is poorly aerated is clayey soil, and the crop matched to flooded, water-holding soil is paddy (rice). Cactus, groundnut and watermelon all prefer dry, sandy conditions.

37. (D) A cactus is adapted to dry conditions and thrives in well-drained sandy soil. Water-logged clay, flooded paddy fields and humus bogs would rot its roots.

38. (A) Percolation rate increases (slowest to fastest) as particle size grows: clayey (slowest) < loamy (medium) < sandy (fastest). So clayey, loamy, sandy.

39. (D) The wearing-away and removal of fertile topsoil by wind or water is soil erosion. Percolation is water seeping down; weathering forms soil; crop rotation maintains fertility.

40. (B) Cutting down forests on hill slopes removes the roots that bind the soil, so rain washes the topsoil away — the biggest erosion-accelerator listed. Planting trees, check-dams and contour ploughing all reduce erosion.

41. (D) Cutting flat, step-like platforms into a hillside is terracing; it slows water and stops soil washing down. Contour ploughing follows the slope's lines; crop rotation and overgrazing are unrelated.

42. (A) Ploughing along the slope's elevation (contour) lines so each furrow traps water is contour ploughing. Terracing cuts steps; deforestation and mulching are different practices.

43. (D) Plant roots bind the soil and the leafy cover/mulch breaks the force of falling raindrops, so the soil is held in place and washes away less. The cover does not turn soil to rock or block all rain.

44. (C) Rows of trees planted to slow the wind and shield a field from wind erosion are shelter belts. Check-dams trap runoff in gullies; terraces and furrows are ground-shaping methods.

45. (C) Crop rotation keeps soil fertile because growing legumes restores the nitrogen that cereal crops remove, so fertility is renewed without heavy fertiliser use.

46. (A) Legumes host Rhizobium bacteria in their root nodules; these bacteria fix nitrogen from the air into the soil as usable nitrates — the opposite of eating it up.

47. (B) Dumping plastic waste and harmful chemicals on the soil is soil pollution. Earthworms, percolating rain and humus from fallen leaves are all natural and beneficial.

48. (C) Once the tree roots that held the soil are gone, heavy rain on the bare hillside easily washes the loose topsoil away. Deforestation lowers fertility and water retention, not raises them.

49. (B) Bare, dry soil on a windy plain has no cover and loose fine particles, so it is most at risk from wind erosion. (Dry soil is not at risk of water-logging.)

50. (C) The topsoil is the humus-rich layer where crops get their nutrients, and because soil forms so slowly it takes centuries to rebuild — so losing it is a serious, near-permanent loss.

51. (B) A soil that holds enough water for vegetables yet still drains and is easy to till is loamy soil — the balanced mix. Pure sand drains too fast, pure clay water-logs, and bedrock is not soil.

52. (B) For equal volumes, the soil that takes the longest time has the lowest rate. Clayey takes 18 min (longest), so clayey has the lowest percolation rate. Sandy (2 min) is fastest.

53. (A) Rate = 300 mL ÷ 6 min = 50 mL/min. Time for 600 mL = 600 ÷ 50 = 12 minutes. (Doubling the volume doubles the time, from 6 to 12 min.)

54. (B) Loamy soil does NOT have the slowest percolation rate — clayey soil does; loam drains moderately. The other three statements (a mix of sand/silt/clay/humus, suited to most crops, holds water moderately) are all true, so the false one is option (B).

55. (D) Garden soil is rich in humus and holds moisture, providing food and water for organisms, so it supports far more life than dry, nutrient-poor beach sand. Weight and particle size are not the reason.

56. (A) Both drain 100 mL in 4 min, so both have the same overall rate — but soil B's column is twice as deep, so per unit depth the water moves more slowly through it. Hence soil B is the slower-percolating arrangement per unit depth.

57. (C) Clayey soil holds the extra water in its tiny pores, pushing out the air, so the roots are starved of oxygen and the plant suffers. (Clay drains slowly, not fast, and does not turn water into humus.)

58. (D) The damage comes from up-and-down ploughing on a bare, deforested slope. The best fix is to terrace the slope and plough along the contour, so water is trapped instead of channelling downhill. Ploughing up-and-down more or clearing more vegetation makes it worse.

59. (B) Water that percolates downward through the soil seeps into the ground and helps refill underground water stores (aquifers). It does not remove all groundwater, and it matters everywhere, not just in deserts.

60. (C) Rate = 480 mL ÷ 12 min = 40 mL/min. In 5 min the volume = 40 × 5 = 200 mL. (2400 = 480 × 5 is the multiply trap; 96 = 480 ÷ 5 ignores the rate.)

Solutions complete: 60 / 60.
