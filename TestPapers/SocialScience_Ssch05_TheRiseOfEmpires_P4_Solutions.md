## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** Y is an empire and X a kingdom; the essential difference the contrast pins down is the stacking of authority, with lesser kings kept on their thrones beneath one paramount ruler. The option about guaranteed wealth treats a frequent consequence as the defining trait, and an empire is not defined by being richer. A kingdom is not barred from keeping an army, so that option invents a rule. Greater area can accompany an empire but is not what structurally separates it from a kingdom; a very large kingdom would still not be an empire without subordinated kings.

**2. (B)** A throne-keeping king paying tribute is a tributary, while a salaried, realm-less appointee is a satrap, and both devices let an emperor reach lands he cannot personally rule, so the correct option names each rightly and states the shared aim. The option that swaps the labels reverses the two definitions and falsely says they end the need for an army. Calling both tributaries ignores that the governor holds no kingdom, and empires had no elections to 'choose' an emperor. The last option again swaps the definitions and absurdly makes subordinates larger than the overlord.

**3. (D)** Carrying orders, soldiers and news quickly across distance is the job of communication networks, not of the economy, so pairing a thriving economy with that task is the mismatched pair the question asks for. An administration of officials really does extend the centre's reach into far regions, a common currency and standard measures really do enable trade and uniform taxation, and a strong army really does defend frontiers and enforce authority, so those three pairings are all correct and therefore not the answer.

**4. (A)** Armies, officials and roads all cost money, and that money flows from a flourishing economy and its taxes, which is exactly why the chapter treats wealth as the foundation under the other supports. A strong economy does not make tribute pointless, since tribute remained a normal channel of imperial wealth. Wealth alone could not hold an empire without force and administration, so that option overstates the economy. Coins served the economy as money rather than as prized art, so the last option mistakes their purpose.

**5. (D)** When money and measures differ, merchants struggle to value and exchange goods across the two provinces and the state cannot tax them evenly, so commerce and revenue both suffer and economic unity frays, which is the chapter's logic. A fractured economy does not magically strengthen the army, does not let the emperor abolish officials, and has no power to boost food or iron output; those three options assert effects the chapter's reasoning does not support.

**6. (A)** Letting guilds govern themselves kept production and trade humming, and that prosperity fed the king's treasury through taxes, so disrupting it with constant policing would choke the very revenue he wanted, which matches the chapter. No law barred kings from trade, guilds and their members were in fact taxed, and guilds did not outrank the king; those three options rest on inventions the chapter contradicts.

**7. (C)** The two routes were long-distance overland arteries, and command of them meant taxing rich flows of goods while also moving armies and information, so the correct option ties together economy, communication and military reach. They were routes, not borders, and they were overland rather than ocean lanes, so two options misclassify them. Ashoka's edicts came generations later and were not the reason the routes mattered, so the last option reverses cause and date.

**8. (D)** Patronage of art and learning fosters a common culture that unites varied subjects, so neglecting it leaves an empire materially strong but culturally loose, a gap that force and bureaucracy cannot fill, which is exactly the chapter's point. Tribute depends on the army and administration the empire still has, soldiers depend on pay from a working economy, and forts depend on resources and labour, so none of those three would vanish merely because art is neglected.

**9. (B)** Iron tools such as ploughs let farmers work the heavy Ganga soil and grow more food, aiding the economy, while iron weapons equipped a strong army, so one resource served two different supports. Magadha was an inland power, not a navy of iron ships, and iron coins are not what the chapter credits, so the navy-and-coins option is wrong. The chapter does not attribute its rise to iron exports or to paying tribute in iron, and edict pillars came later under Ashoka, so the last two options invent uses the chapter does not give.

**10. (B)** Magadha's edge was a self-reinforcing bundle: rich farmland fed people, iron made tools and weapons, elephant forests added war-power, and the Ganga and Son moved goods and armies cheaply, so the correct option explains how the advantages worked together. Magadha was an inland power, so seaports and a navy are wrong; it did not rely on gold mines or the Silk Road; and it was not ringed by mountains and desert, nor could it forgo an army, so all three distractors describe a different setting.

**11. (C)** Uniting many kingdoms, minting coins and maintaining a huge army are imperial-scale acts, so this is what marks Mahapadma Nanda as pushing Magadha toward empire. The Arthashastra was Kautilya's work under the Mauryas, edicts on rock and pillar were Ashoka's, and the Nandas never fought Alexander, who turned back far short of Magadha, so each of those three belongs to someone else or never happened.

**12. (D)** Dhana Nanda's wealth came at his subjects' expense through oppression and crushing taxes, which is why loyalty collapsed and Chandragupta found support to overthrow him, so the correct option matches the chapter's cause-and-effect. He did not impoverish himself by giving the treasury away, he was not noted for refusing to mint coins, and he never surrendered his throne to a Greek; he was ousted by Chandragupta and Kautilya, so the three distractors invent the cause.

**13. (B)** The Nandas were the first to unify Magadha into a large power, and the Mauryas then took over and expanded much further, so the correct option captures their sequence and roles. The Mauryas, not the Nandas, built the great empire, only Ashoka of the Mauryas carved edicts, and the two dynasties were successive rather than simultaneous rivals, so each distractor reverses the order or the facts.

**14. (A)** Cheap river transport moved goods to market and armies to the field, serving the economy and the military together, which is the dual role the chapter highlights. The chapter does not credit river-worship as the source of Magadha's strength, the rivers were arteries rather than fixed borders, and the iron came from nearby hills rather than from the rivers, so the three distractors each misstate the rivers' role.

**15. (D)** Alexander's eastward march carried him over the Persian Empire before he touched Indian soil, and after beating Porus his exhausted troops mutinied against advancing, which forced his return, so the correct option pairs both facts. He never reached the distant Nandas and was not defeated by Chandragupta; the Maurya empire did not yet exist and the Nandas did not buy him off; and Rome lay far to the west and was never his conquest, so each distractor is doubly false.

**16. (C)** Alexander's territorial hold was indeed thin and brief precisely because he installed feeble satraps and opened contacts instead of consolidating direct rule, so both statements are true and the reason accounts for the assertion. The option saying the reason does not explain it misses that causal link; the option calling the reason false denies the documented weak governors; and the option calling the assertion false ignores that his Indian rule really was shallow.

**17. (D)** Alexander left feeble governors whose grip collapsed after his death, giving Chandragupta easy gains in the northwest, so that is the condition that helped him most. A strong united Greek army would have blocked rather than helped him, no Ashokan treaty existed because Ashoka came two generations later, and the Nanda soldiers were not his by birth since he had to overthrow the Nandas, so the three distractors are each impossible by date or fact.

**18. (C)** Alexander never firmly held Indian territory; he left weak satraps and opened new contacts, so his real mark was indirect, working through the openings he created rather than through lasting rule, which matches the chapter. He did not govern India for fifty years, he did not found the Maurya dynasty, which Chandragupta did, and the edicts were Ashoka's later work in Prakrit and Brahmi, not Greek inscriptions by Alexander, so the three distractors all overstate a direct role he never had.

**19. (C)** Porus ruled the Pauravas in the northwest and was defeated by Alexander near the Indus, so the correct option places him rightly. He was an Indian king, not a Macedonian governor, so the first distractor confuses him with a satrap; the Greek envoy who described the Mauryan court was Megasthenes, not Porus; and the Nanda dynasty was founded by Mahapadma Nanda, so the last two distractors swap him for entirely different figures.

**20. (B)** The Nandas rose first in the fifth century BCE, Alexander arrived about 326 BCE, Chandragupta founded the Maurya empire about 321 BCE, and Ashoka's Kalinga war came later in the third century BCE, giving the order Nanda, Alexander, Chandragupta, Kalinga. The option starting with Alexander wrongly puts him before the Nandas, the option placing Chandragupta before Alexander reverses 321 and 326 BCE, and the option starting with Chandragupta puts the Maurya founding before the Nandas, so all three break the established sequence.

**21. (A)** Chandragupta seized power by toppling the hated Nandas with the help of the strategist Kautilya, so the correct option names both the act and the mentor. Alexander did not crown him, the throne could not be inherited from Ashoka, who was Chandragupta's own grandson and ruled much later, and the chapter credits no Persian marriage, so each distractor either reverses the family order or invents foreign backing.

**22. (B)** Kautilya was also known as Chanakya and Vishnugupta, and his Arthashastra is a manual of statecraft and economics, so the correct option ties the right names to the right kind of book. Megasthenes was the Greek envoy who wrote the Indica, an outsider's account, not Kautilya's treatise; Panini was a grammarian and the Arthashastra is not a collection of edicts; and Porus was the king Alexander fought and the Arthashastra is not praise-poetry, so the three distractors mismatch both the name and the work.

**23. (C)** The seven limbs are the king, the ministers, the territory with its people, the fort, the treasury, the army and the allies, and Kautilya stresses that, like a body, the state falters if any one limb is weak, so the correct option names them and gives the body analogy. The other lists mix up social classes, geographical features or military assets, none matching the saptanga, and they attach false reasons such as a lucky number, geography alone, or military power alone.

**24. (C)** The treasury and army are two limbs because they do different jobs, one supplying resources and the other supplying force, and that distinct-function reason holds even though the treasury funded the army. They were in fact used together, the army was paid from the treasury rather than only in grain, and the treasury was the state's, not the guilds', so the three distractors each rest on a false premise the question itself contradicts.

**25. (C)** The Mauryas split the empire into provinces under governors and officials answerable to the centre, and spies supplemented this by reporting from afar and watching the officials themselves, so the correct option names both the structure and the spies' purpose. Villages were not left wholly independent, guilds governed crafts rather than provinces, and no emperor could tour every village yearly; the distractors also wrongly make spies a substitute for the army, the currency, or the governors rather than a supplement.

**26. (A)** Regular taxes gave the steady income that paid the army, the officials and public works, the lifeblood of a lasting state, which is why the chapter stresses taxation over windfalls. Taxation was a normal source of revenue rather than chiefly a punishment, it filled the state treasury rather than the guilds' coffers, and far from replacing trade it depended on a thriving economy to yield revenue, so the three distractors misstate its purpose.

**27. (A)** Bindusara was the middle Mauryan ruler, son of Chandragupta and father of Ashoka; Megasthenes was the Greek ambassador who described the court; and Kautilya was the mentor and author of the Arthashastra, so the correct option matches each rightly. The other three shuffle these identities, for instance making Bindusara the Greek envoy or the founder, Megasthenes the mentor, or Kautilya the last Nanda, all of which scramble the well-known roles.

**28. (B)** Megasthenes, a foreign envoy, wrote the Indica as an outside observer's record, whereas Kautilya, the insider mentor, wrote the Arthashastra as a manual of governance, so the correct option names each work and its nature. The first distractor swaps the two authorships, neither work is a religious scripture, and Megasthenes wrote no praise-poetry while the rock edicts were Ashoka's much later work, so the other options misassign the writings.

**29. (D)** The dhamma-mahamatras were Ashoka's officers charged with promoting and supervising his moral code among the people, which is why their title joins dhamma to the word for high officials. They were not frontier cavalry commanders, not tribute collectors, and not the stone-carvers of the edicts; those three duties belonged to other parts of the administration, so the distractors misplace their role.

**30. (B)** Administration carried the emperor's authority into regions he could never personally oversee, working alongside the army and treasury rather than displacing them, because force and funds were still indispensable, which corrects the teacher's claim. Officials did not exist to write praise-poetry, they helped collect taxes rather than abolish them, and they did not field private armies replacing the imperial one, so the three distractors invent roles the chapter does not give the administration.

**31. (A)** Ashoka's motive for the Kalinga war was conquest and expansion, and only the suffering it caused turned him toward Dhamma, so the student has cause and effect reversed, which the correct option fixes. The war certainly happened, so denying it is false; Kalinga's prior religion is not the point and is not stated as the reason; and there was no blanket religious ban on kings waging war, so the other corrections rest on inventions rather than the real reversal of order.

**32. (B)** Ashoka's own edicts confess his grief over the carnage at Kalinga, giving us his remorse directly in his words, which is the evidence the chapter cites. Megasthenes and Kautilya both belong to an earlier generation, before Ashoka's reign, so neither could record Kalinga, and a later commemorative temple is not the evidence the chapter relies on, so the three distractors point to sources that do not exist or predate the event.

**33. (D)** It was Ashoka's remorse over Kalinga that turned him from conquest to Dhamma, so without that remorse he would likely have kept warring and the moral edicts would never have been written, which is the chapter's logic. The empire would not have fallen instantly, Brahmi's much later decipherment is unrelated to Ashoka's feelings, and Chandragupta ruled generations earlier and could not be affected, so the three distractors assert effects the reasoning does not support.

**34. (C)** Dhamma was an ethical code calling for honesty, non-violence, tolerance and dutiful conduct, meant for all subjects whatever their faith, so the correct option describes it rightly. It was not a new religion replacing Buddhism, not a tax, and certainly not a doctrine of war; Ashoka used Dhamma to bind his peoples morally rather than to found a creed, raise revenue, or wage battles, so the three distractors mistake its very nature.

**35. (B)** Ashoka's empire stretched over almost all the subcontinent except the southern tip, while the northwest reaching toward Afghanistan lay firmly inside, so the correct option names one true exclusion and one true inclusion. The Ganga plains held the capital Pataliputra and were inside, the northwest was included rather than excluded, and the east toward modern Bangladesh was also inside, so each distractor either excludes a region that was in fact part of the empire or wrongly includes the south.

**36. (B)** Ashoka cut his messages into rocks and pillars set up throughout the empire, a lasting and public medium that reached scattered populations, which is exactly why he earns the title. Relying on soldiers reciting would reach few and fade, printing did not yet exist, and a single central temple would draw only those nearby, so the three distractors describe means that could not achieve wide, durable communication.

**37. (B)** A language is what people speak and a script is the set of signs used to write it, so Prakrit the spoken tongue and Brahmi the writing system are different kinds of thing, which is the classic correction. Swapping the two makes Brahmi a language and Prakrit a script, calling both Greek scripts is doubly wrong, and denying that either is a script repeats the confusion, so the three distractors all worsen the error.

**38. (C)** Using everyday Prakrit let Ashoka's words reach ordinary subjects, and choosing stone made the messages lasting and openly visible for generations, so the correct option gives both real reasons. Sanskrit already existed and writing surfaces other than paper were known, Prakrit was the popular rather than a secret tongue and cost was not the driver, and any language can be inscribed while Brahmi was not restricted to stone, so the three distractors rest on false claims.

**39. (C)** Brahmi is the parent script of many later Indian writing systems and Prakrit was the language of common speech, so the correct option states both lasting facts. Brahmi is not the Roman alphabet and Prakrit was popular rather than a refined court tongue; Brahmi has in fact been deciphered and Prakrit was not a priestly secret; and Brahmi is a script rather than a spoken court language with Prakrit being a language rather than a script, so the three distractors reverse or falsify these roles.

**40. (D)** India adopted the Lion Capital that once topped Ashoka's pillar at Sarnath as its emblem, and the dharmachakra at the centre of the flag, the wheel of dharma standing for righteous law and the Buddha's teaching, comes from the same Mauryan art, so the correct option links all three. The Sanchi stupa, the Dhauli elephant and the dancing terracotta figure were not chosen as the emblem, and the flag's wheel is not a war-chariot wheel, a map of trade routes, or a sun symbol, so the three distractors misname both the emblem and the wheel's meaning.

**41. (B)** In architecture a capital is the sculpted top of a column, here the four-lion piece, and Ashoka set his pillar at Sarnath because it was the site of the Buddha's first sermon, so the correct option gets both the term and the reason. The capital is not a capital city, a treasury, or an edict, and Sarnath was not Ashoka's coronation site, the Kalinga battlefield in Odisha, or where Chandragupta beat the Nandas in Magadha, so the three distractors confuse the term or misplace the site.

**42. (A)** From 232 BCE to about 185 BCE is roughly forty-seven years, so the empire fell within about half a century of Ashoka's death, and this swift decline illustrates the fragility the chapter stresses. Two centuries, a single year, and more than three hundred years all misjudge the gap, and they draw the wrong lesson, either of unusual stability, of instant collapse, or of long survival, none of which fits the arithmetic.

**43. (D)** The paradox is that an empire brings unity and wealth yet depends on force and tribute, so once the central power weakens far provinces seize the chance to break free and even mighty empires fall, which the correct option states together. Empires were in fact large and kept armies, so 'small and large' with 'no army' is false; they had subordinate kings and did collect tribute, contradicting the second option; and they spent heavily on armies, contradicting the third, so each distractor builds on a false premise.

**44. (A)** Rebellion is likeliest when central authority slackens under a weak ruler at the same time as the burden of tribute rises, giving both the opportunity and the grievance to revolt, which the correct option captures. A run of strong emperors keeps provinces in check, lighter tribute and peace reduce discontent, and better roads strengthen the centre's reach, so all three distractors describe conditions that would discourage rather than encourage breaking away.

**45. (C)** Because the edicts were set up across the territory Ashoka governed, mapping where they are found traces the reach of his empire and influence, which is what the spread reveals. The edicts are moral and administrative messages, not registers of Alexander's birth year, of market iron prices, or of guild counts, so the three distractors attribute to the inscriptions contents they never carried.

**46. (A)** Empires did not hold elections; the emperor's power came by conquest and succession, not by a vote, so 'holding regular elections to choose the emperor' is not one of the six supports and is the correct answer to this 'NOT' question. A strong army, communication networks, and support for art and learning are all genuine supports the chapter lists, so each of those three belongs and is therefore not the answer.

**47. (A)** Bindusara was the middle of the three great Mauryas, son of Chandragupta and father of Ashoka, so the correct option places him in the succession. He was not the mentor-author Kautilya, not the Greek envoy Megasthenes, and not the founder Chandragupta, so the three distractors hand him roles that belonged to other figures entirely.

**48. (A)** The chapter ties the Nandas' fall to lost popular loyalty under an oppressive king, so a just Dhana Nanda who kept that loyalty might have held on longer and resisted Chandragupta, which is the correct inference. A just Nanda has no bearing on Ashoka's later birth, would not have let Alexander conquer all of India, which never happened for separate reasons, and has nothing to do with how many copies of the Arthashastra Kautilya wrote, so the three distractors draw unrelated conclusions.

**49. (C)** A planned, prosperous, food-secure city reflects officials organising it and the state marshalling resources, so it illustrates strong administration and control over resources working together, which is the correct option. The absence of an army or currency would weaken rather than build such a city, tribute alone without an economy could not sustain it, and refusing art and trade describes neglect rather than the prosperity shown, so the three distractors name the opposite of what the city demonstrates.

**50. (A)** Maintaining a large standing army drains the treasury, so it depends on a flourishing economy and reliable taxes to fund it, and an army with no money soon collapses, which is the chapter's argument. There was no rule barring the army from provinces, soldiers were paid rather than unpaid volunteers, and although tributaries had their own forces the emperor still kept his own army, so the three distractors each contradict how the chapter describes the army.

**51. (D)** The chapter's overarching message is that maintaining an empire is even harder than building one, which is why even the mighty Mauryas declined after their strongest rulers, so the correct option states the central lesson. Empires do not last forever, a lone strong emperor is not enough without the other supports, and tribute alone cannot rule without an army and administration, so the three distractors each contradict the chapter's conclusion.

**52. (D)** Communication networks overcome distance, carrying commands, soldiers and information quickly so the centre can direct far regions it cannot watch in person, a job an army by itself cannot do, which the correct option states. Fast messages do not defend a frontier without an army, communication is not chiefly for collecting tribute, and it has nothing to do with abolishing the currency, so the three distractors misstate what the network does.

**53. (C)** Imperium points to supreme power, and the chapter uses the word's origin to underline that an empire rests on one ruler wielding such power over many lands and subordinate rulers, which the correct option states. The Mauryas and Nandas were not ruled by Roman emperors, empires were not all named in Latin by their founders, and an empire is defined by layered supreme authority rather than mere wealth, so the three distractors miss the point of the etymology.

**54. (C)** Different coins and measures in each region means the empire lacks a common currency and standards, so cross-regional trade and even taxation grow harder and the economy weakens, which the correct option identifies. The army, communication networks and patronage of art are not what is described as missing here, so the three distractors name supports the empire actually has and draw consequences that do not follow from the currency problem.

**55. (B)** Alexander overran the Persian Empire on his way east, but the Maurya empire had not yet been founded, coming into being only a few years after his departure under Chandragupta, so the correct option distinguishes the two. He did not conquer the Maurya empire, which did not exist, so the options pairing his conquest of it with or instead of Persia are false, and neither empire defeated and expelled him, so that option is false as well.

**56. (A)** Tributaries, satraps and officials are all devices for projecting the emperor's authority into regions he cannot personally rule, so the chapter ties them together as one connected solution to the problem of distance, which the correct option states. None of them was a method of choosing the next emperor, none existed to write praise-poetry, and they were instruments of collecting tribute and taxes rather than avoiding them, so the three distractors misread their shared purpose.

**57. (D)** The first fact shows guilds governing themselves in an orderly way, and the second shows kings allowing this, and the conclusion that fits both is that orderly, prosperous guilds boosted the trade that fed royal taxes, so leaving them alone served the king's interest. Soldiers did not run the guilds day to day, guilds organised craft and commerce rather than merely collecting tribute, and a body that fixes prices and elects a head is plainly not a rule-less family shop, so the three distractors contradict the very facts given.

**58. (B)** A tributary is a defeated king left on his throne in return for tribute, while a satrap is simply an appointee set to govern a province and owning no kingdom, so the correct option states the distinction precisely. The first distractor swaps the two definitions, neither role implies ruling more land than the overlord, and neither is fixed by ethnicity, so the three distractors reverse or invent the difference.

**59. (A)** The edicts are Ashoka's own public words and so best reveal his own thinking, while the Indica is a foreign envoy's account and so best provides an outsider's view, which the correct option matches rightly. The first distractor reverses the authorship, making the Indica Ashoka's and the edicts foreign; the second wrongly calls both Ashoka's own, denying any outside view; and the third wrongly calls both foreign reports, denying us Ashoka's direct voice, so each contradicts who wrote what.

**60. (D)** Chandragupta founded the empire about 321 BCE, his son Bindusara ruled next, then Ashoka fought the Kalinga war in the third century BCE, and the empire ended about 185 BCE, giving the order founder, Bindusara, Kalinga, end. Putting Bindusara before Chandragupta reverses father and son, putting Kalinga before Bindusara skips a generation, and putting the empire's end before the Kalinga war places the collapse before an event that happened during the empire, so all three distractors break the internal sequence.
