Worked Solutions

1. (C) Physical infrastructure covers transport, utilities, communication and energy. The fibre-optic network (communication), the water pipeline (utility) and the wind farm (energy) are all physical, giving three. The district court is a social-infrastructure facility, so it is not counted. Answers of two or one undercount the physical items, and 'all four' wrongly treats the court as physical rather than social.

2. (C) Infra means 'below' and structura means 'a building/structure', so the literal sense is the underlying base that lies beneath and supports the rest. 'Tallest structure above' inverts infra into 'above'. 'Built quickly and cheaply' confuses it with a speed term. 'Shared between far-apart places' borrows the tele/communicare roots of telecommunication, not infrastructure.

3. (C) The claim fails because much essential physical infrastructure is invisible — buried power grids, water pipelines and communication networks. They are unseen yet vital, directly contradicting 'cannot be seen, cannot be infrastructure'. The hospital, highway and port are all highly visible, so they cannot rebut a claim that is specifically about invisible systems.

4. (A) The chapter's two types are physical and social — a functional division (systems vs services for people), not a price one. 'Three official types' contradicts the two-fold split. 'All costs the same' is false. 'Cheap never counts' is invented. Only the function-based reasoning correctly explains why cost is the wrong axis.

5. (A) The chapter calls infrastructure the engine (driver) of development and divides it into physical and social arms — both ideas appear together only in the correct option. Limiting it to 'roads and buildings', or to 'only social services', or to 'only invisible systems', each repeats a misconception the chapter rejects, and 'enemy/final result' misstates its role.

6. (C) Sanitation supply systems are physical utilities; the chapter does list sanitation as a social-infrastructure aim too, but a treatment PLANT and pipes are the physical-utility hardware. A public park is a social-infrastructure facility. A mobile-tower network is physical communication. Only the chosen set assigns each correctly; the others either lump all as social, or swap the park and the network between physical and social.

7. (D) The chapter stresses that physical and social infrastructure are two arms working together; strong physical systems without healthy, educated people leaves development incomplete. 'Physical alone guarantees progress' and 'social plays no role' both deny the social arm. Demolishing working physical infrastructure would harm, not help, development.

8. (D) Infrastructure is the set of basic facilities a country needs; it includes BOTH social services like hospitals and physical systems like power grids, which is why both qualify. 'Both physical because both use electricity' misclassifies the hospital. 'Both social' misclassifies the grid. 'Only roads count' is the very misconception the chapter rejects.

9. (C) Power supply is physical energy/utility infrastructure, and because transport, communication and social services all need power, it underpins the rest. Calling it social confuses 'serves people' with the physical/social divide. It carries energy, not communication signals. Private household use does not strip a public supply system of being infrastructure.

10. (C) A court is social infrastructure (a service institution), so it is the odd one out. A gas pipeline is a physical utility, a telecom uplink is physical communication, and a hydroelectric dam is physical energy infrastructure — all three are physical.

11. (B) Good physical infrastructure (power, roads) plus social infrastructure (schools) gives X the foundation for economic and human development — the backbone idea. 'Y faster with less to maintain' and 'too much slows X' both invert the chapter's claim, and 'infrastructure does not affect development' contradicts its central thesis.

12. (B) The backbone metaphor highlights support and connection: economic activity rests on and is linked by transport, power, water and communication. The metaphor is not about expense, nor about rigidity/permanence, nor about serving only 'large organs'; reading it that way mistakes a structural-support image for cost, inflexibility, or selectivity.

13. (C) Transport infrastructure moves inputs and outputs quickly while energy infrastructure powers the machines, lowering cost and time — a multi-link cause-effect the chapter supports. The other options either claim infrastructure raises a factory's costs, reduce its benefit to cutting headcount, or deny any effect, all contrary to the backbone-of-the-economy idea.

14. (D) Physical systems handle the flow of goods, energy and information, while social services build human capability — the two complementary roles. The first distractor swaps the two roles. 'Both only move goods' erases the human-development role. 'City-only vs village-only' invents a geographic split the chapter does not make.

15. (D) Road-network size and railway passenger numbers are distinct facts; the chapter gives 'second-largest road network' and 'about 20 million rail passengers a day' separately, without claiming a matching rank. 'Always the same' is an invalid leap, 'no railways at all' contradicts the chapter, and 'smallest roads' denies the stated fact.

16. (B) An expressway is a high-speed inter-state national road built by the central government — that is its defining contrast with a village lane. A village-maintained local road is the opposite of an expressway. An elevated urban rail line describes a metro, and a coastal cargo route describes sea/port transport, neither of which is a road expressway.

17. (D) 'Quadrilateral' means four-sided; the network joins four major cities (Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata) into a four-sided circuit. It is not a village project, the name does not refer to lane count, and the four cities are Indian, not foreign capitals.

18. (A) NH44 is a major (notably long) national highway connecting far-apart regions. The other options describe entirely different infrastructure types — a metro (urban rail), a port (sea transport), and a solar park (energy) — none of which is a national highway.

19. (C) The Golden Quadrilateral is transport infrastructure; its benefit to a goods company is rapid movement between major cities. Supplying electricity is energy infrastructure, sewage treatment is a utility/social function, and a patent is a legal right over an invention — none of these is what a highway network provides.

20. (B) Roads, rail, metros, airports and ports all carry people or goods, so they are forms of physical transport infrastructure. They are not social services, not communication systems (which carry information), and not energy systems (which carry power).

21. (C) The chapter's key point is a change of purpose: built in 1853 for colonial extraction and control, the railways became India's lifeline only afterward. Saying they served Indians 'from the first day' keeps the colonial-benefit misconception; 'never a lifeline / still British' denies their present role; and 'built by Indians for free travel in 1853' is historically false.

22. (D) The chapter lists export of raw materials, sale of British goods, and rapid troop movement as the real colonial motives — so those three are NOT exceptions. The one that does NOT belong is 'free fast travel for villagers' benefit', which is precisely the myth the chapter corrects.

23. (A) Twenty million divided by twenty equals one million, so reading '20 million' as '20' understates the figure by roughly a factor of a million. Factors of a hundred, a thousand, or ten are all far too small to span the gap between 20 and 20,000,000.

24. (D) Railways built for colonial gain became a national lifeline, showing infrastructure can be repurposed for new and broader ends. 'Must be torn down', 'only one purpose', and 'useless within years' each contradict this lesson of adaptive, lasting value.

25. (B) Metros are intra-city rapid transit reducing road congestion, whereas the Indian Railways span the whole country between regions. The first distractor swaps their scopes. Both are rail, not sea transport. And both are physical transport, not energy or communication infrastructure.

26. (A) A metro's specific value is mass urban commuting that takes pressure off congested roads. Exporting raw materials describes colonial-era freight rail/ports, generating electricity is energy infrastructure, and moving containers between coasts is sea/port transport — none is the metro's role.

27. (A) Avis ('bird') points to flight, so aviation infrastructure is airports and aircraft moving through the air. Harbours and ships are sea transport, power stations are energy, and water pipelines are utilities — none matches the 'bird'/air meaning.

28. (B) The figure 159 refers to airports; sea ports are different facilities located on the coasts, and the chapter does not equate the two counts. 'Always equal' is an unwarranted leap, 'no airports' contradicts the figure, and 'all 159 are sea ports' misattributes the number.

29. (D) Airports (air) and sea ports (water) are both modes of moving people and goods, hence both physical transport infrastructure. They are not social services, not energy systems, and not communication networks.

30. (A) TEU (Twenty-foot Equivalent Unit) counts standard shipping containers, so 8,000 − 5,000 = 3,000 more containers at Port B, showing larger throughput. TEU is not a measure of train distance, of height, or of aircraft, so the other readings misuse the unit.

31. (A) A standard equivalent unit lets very different ports be compared on the same scale of container throughput. TEU has nothing to do with metro speed, crane height, or airport ownership, so those options misidentify what the unit standardises.

34. (A) J.C. Bose's work on radio-wave communication and the coherer is the chapter's evidence that India contributed to communication science, rebutting the 'entirely Western' claim. 36. (A) The coherer was an early detector that picked up and decoded wireless signals, which Bose improved. It did not generate railway power, pump water (a utility function), or carry passengers (a transport function), so those options confuse communication with energy, utilities, and transport.

37. (D) Choosing not to patent meant Bose did not lock up his ideas for personal exclusive gain, leaving them open for others' use. It does not imply the work was worthless, that he was legally barred, or that he kept everything secret — declining a patent is the opposite of hiding work away.

38. (D) A patent grants the holder the sole right to make, sell or use the invention for a set period, so patenting would have legally restricted others for those years. Patenting does not affect whether devices function, has nothing to do with ending colonial rule, and 'everyone could copy freely' is the opposite of what a patent does.

39. (B) Tele ('far') + communicare ('to share') means sharing information over distance, which a long-distance phone call exactly does. Driving on a highway is transport, loading containers is sea transport/logistics, and generating power is energy — none of these is sharing information at a distance.

40. (B) The essential split is what each moves: communication carries information, transport carries people and goods. The first distractor swaps these roles, 'both move only electricity' describes energy not these two, and 'communication abroad only / transport India only' invents a false geographic divide.

41. (A) Bose is credited with early radio-wave research and improving the coherer about 1895. He did not build highways (and 1853 is the railway date), did not design the Golden Quadrilateral, and did not invent the shipping container — those mix him up with unrelated transport facts.

42. (A) Hydro (dams), solar parks and wind farms all draw on renewable natural sources — water, sun and wind. None of them burns coal, all three are energy infrastructure (not social), and using a free natural source does not stop the built systems from being infrastructure.

45. (B) Switching to solar uses a clean, renewable source, reducing pollution — the definition of more sustainable infrastructure. Solar does not burn fuel (so it is not more polluting), it remains physical energy infrastructure (not social), and renewables are not useless for cities.

46. (D) A hospital is a social-infrastructure service, but it relies on energy infrastructure to operate — showing how the layers depend on each other. Consuming electricity does not make a hospital energy infrastructure, nor does a hospital's use make a power grid social, and hospitals certainly do need power.

47. (A) Schools, hospitals, courts and parks are all social infrastructure serving human needs — health, education, justice and recreation. None of them moves goods (transport), generates electricity (energy), or carries signals (communication), so the other options misclassify the whole group.

48. (C) Social infrastructure develops human capability — health and education — which feeds back into national growth. It does not speed cargo (that is transport), cannot replace physical infrastructure, and benefits society broadly, not just its builders.
