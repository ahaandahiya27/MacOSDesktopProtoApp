## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** A point equidistant from A and B lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB; equidistant from B and C means it lies on the perpendicular bisector of BC. The single point on both is their intersection, automatically equidistant from all three. Angle bisectors give the point equidistant from the SIDES (the incentre), not from the vertices. A midpoint of one segment is only equidistant from that segment's two ends. A whole perpendicular bisector handles only two towns, not all three, so a unique point is needed.

**2. (C)** Because RP = RQ, R lies on the perpendicular bisector of PQ, so RM ⊥ PQ and M is the midpoint, giving PM = 5 cm. Triangle RMP is right-angled at M with hypotenuse RP = 6, so RM² = 6² − 5² = 36 − 25 = 11, RM = √11. Subtracting the lengths (6 − 5) ignores the right angle. The radius 6 is the slant RP, not the vertical RM. Adding the squares would apply if RM were the hypotenuse, but here RP is.

**3. (C)** Triangle FNK has a right angle at N, so FK is its hypotenuse and FN is a leg; the hypotenuse is always the longest side, hence FN < FK. The perpendicular distance is the unique shortest distance from a point to a line, not equal to a slanted one. It is shortest, not longest, so FK < FN is reversed. The right-triangle inequality settles the comparison without needing NK's length.

**4. (D)** The centre is equidistant from A and B, so it lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB, whose foot is the midpoint, splitting the chord into 6 cm halves. The radius OA is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 6 (half-chord) and 8 (distance to chord): radius² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100, radius = 10. The 8 cm is only a leg, not the radius. Adding 6 + 8 ignores the right-angle relation. Subtracting the squares treats the half-chord as the hypotenuse, which it is not.

**5. (A)** W lies on the bisectors of two sides, so it is equidistant from all three vertices; that makes it equidistant from the third side's two endpoints, so it lies on that side's perpendicular bisector too. Hence all three are concurrent at the circumcentre for ANY triangle, not only equilateral ones. The 'three lines rarely meet' intuition fails here because of the shared equidistance property. The circumcentre is generally not on any side, so it is not that side's midpoint.

**6. (C)** The perpendicular bisector meets AB at the midpoint, so half of AB is 12 cm. Triangle with hypotenuse TA = 13 and one leg 12 gives the other leg = √(13² − 12²) = √(169 − 144) = √25 = 5 cm, the distance from T down to AB. The slant TA (13) is not the perpendicular distance. Subtracting lengths (13 − 12) ignores Pythagoras. Adding the squares treats TA as a leg, but it is the hypotenuse.

**7. (A)** Since AL = AR, A is the midpoint of LR. T, found by equal arcs from L and R, is equidistant from them, so it lies on the perpendicular bisector of LR — which passes through midpoint A and is perpendicular to LR (i.e. m). Thus AT is both the perpendicular bisector and perpendicular to m, so both claims hold. The radius only needs to exceed half of LR for the arcs to cross; it need not equal AL.

**8. (A)** Bisecting 60° gives 30°; bisecting that gives 15° (two halvings, so 60 ÷ 4). Adding 15° to the original 60° gives 75°. Three halvings would give 7.5°, but only two are described. The single half (30°) added gives 90°, but the question takes the half OF the half. A 20° piece would require trisecting 60°, which is impossible with compass and straightedge, so 80° is unreachable here.

**9. (B)** 50° = 150° ÷ 3, and 150° (= 60° + 90°) is constructible, but trisecting a general angle with these tools is impossible, so 50° is not constructible. By contrast 75° = 60° + 15° where 15° = half of 30° = half of half of 60°, all bisections. 105° = 60° + 45° with 45° = half of a constructed right angle. 7.5° = 60° halved three times. Each of those uses only bisection and addition of constructible angles, so they ARE constructible.

**10. (B)** 67.5° = 45° + 22.5°. A right angle (90°) is constructible; halving gives 45°, halving again gives 22.5°; adding 45° + 22.5° gives 67.5° — all by bisection and addition. Trisecting a general angle is impossible, so the 202.5° route fails. The 135° = 90° + 45° is itself constructible, and halving it does give 67.5°, BUT that distractor justifies 135° by trisecting 405°, an impossible step, so it is invalid as stated. Trisecting 22.5° to reach 7.5° is also impossible.

**11. (C)** Starting from 90°: first halving 45°, second 22.5°, third 11.25°. Three successive bisections of 90° give 90 ÷ 8 = 11.25°. Dividing 90 by 12 is not a bisection operation. Subtracting 7.5° is not part of any bisection chain. 5.625° would require a fourth halving, one more than the three stated.

**12. (A)** Three 30° angles total 3 × 30 = 90°. Bisecting that 90° gives 45°. Bisecting does not 'return' a single 30° piece — it halves the whole built-up angle. Subtracting 30° is not what bisecting does. No 45° is added; the operation is a single bisection, giving 45°, not 135°.

**13. (C)** Halving the GIVEN 96° angle is allowed regardless of whether 24° is constructible from scratch; 96 ÷ 2 = 48 and 48 ÷ 2 = 24, two valid bisections. The 'multiple of 15°' rule is about building from 60°/90°, not about halving a supplied angle, so it does not block this. Trisection of a general angle is impossible, so the 32° route fails. Halving once gives 48°, which is not 24° — closeness does not count.

**14. (B)** An angle is fixed by the ratio of chord to radius, not the chord alone. Keeping chord PQ the same while shrinking the radius means the chord now spans a larger fraction of a smaller circle, so it subtends a wider angle. Copying the chord faithfully only reproduces the angle when the SAME radius is used. A smaller radius does not directly mean a smaller angle; with a fixed chord it means a larger angle. The outcome is predictable from the chord-to-radius reasoning, no measuring needed.

**15. (A)** A long length is reproduced by stepping off smaller compass-carried pieces end to end along a straight line, each transferred exactly, so 7 = 4 + 3 works within a 5 cm span. The claim that it 'can never be copied' ignores stepping off. A single 7 cm arc is impossible when the span is only 5 cm. A marked ruler is measuring, not constructing; the compass plus straightedge suffice via stepping off.

**16. (A)** The triangle inequality gives 9 − 4 < x < 9 + 4, i.e. 5 < x < 13. The integers strictly between are 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 — exactly 7 values. Including the endpoints 5 and 13 (giving 9 values) is wrong, since those degenerate into straight lines. The difference 9 − 4 = 5 is just the lower bound, not a count. Counting from 5 up to 12 wrongly includes 5, which is excluded.

**17. (A)** The three angles of a triangle sum to 180°, so two of them must total under 180° to leave a positive third. Here 95° + 86° = 181° > 180°, so the arms from the base ends diverge and never meet; no triangle exists. A negative angle is impossible. A positive base cannot rescue an impossible angle pair. ASA does not require equal base angles, only that their sum stays below 180°.

**18. (D)** When the included angle is 90°, the two given sides are the legs and the opposite side is the hypotenuse: √(6² + 8²) = √(36 + 64) = √100 = 10. Adding the sides (14) or subtracting them (2) ignores the right-angle (Pythagorean) relationship. Subtracting the squares would apply only if one given side were the hypotenuse, but here both are legs.

**19. (C)** Two sides with the angle BETWEEN them is the SAS condition, which fixes a unique triangle. An angle OPPOSITE one of the sides is the ambiguous SSA case, which can give two non-congruent triangles. Three angles fix the shape but not the size, so infinitely many triangles fit. Sides 5, 6 and 11 fail the triangle inequality (5 + 6 = 11 is not greater than 11), so no triangle forms at all.

**20. (C)** The missing leg satisfies leg² + 8² = 17², so leg² = 289 − 64 = 225, leg = 15. Perimeter = 8 + 15 + 17 = 40 cm. Taking the leg as 17 − 8 = 9 ignores Pythagoras (and the arithmetic given is inconsistent). Taking it as 17 + 8 = 25 also ignores the right-angle relation. RHS requires the hypotenuse to be the longest side, not equal to a leg, so the triangle is perfectly constructible.

**21. (D)** With AB = AC, the base angles are equal; since they sum to 180 − 60 = 120°, each is 60°. All three angles are 60°, so the triangle is equilateral and BC = 6 cm. It is not right-angled (no 90° angle appears). It is not merely isosceles-with-longer-base; the third side equals the others. SAS happily allows two equal sides; that is the standard isosceles/equilateral case.

**22. (B)** With two sides hinged at the included angle, opening the angle wider pushes the far endpoints further apart, so the opposite side grows monotonically as the angle increases from 30° toward 150°. Fixing the two sides does NOT fix the third — it depends on the angle. A wider angle spreads, not folds, the endpoints. The growth is continuous up to 180°, with no peak at 90°.

**23. (B)** The side opposite the 90° angle is the hypotenuse, and the other two (3 and 4) are the legs: hypotenuse = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5. The sum 7 or difference 1 ignores the Pythagorean relation. A right angle opposite a specified side, with the other two sides given, fixes the hypotenuse exactly at 5, so it is not free to be any length over 4.

**24. (C)** Exterior angle = 180 − 168 = 12°. The exterior angles of a regular n-gon sum to 360°, so n = 360 ÷ 12 = 30. Dividing 168 by an arbitrary 14 has no geometric basis. Using 180 ÷ exterior instead of 360 ÷ exterior is the wrong total. Dividing 168 by 8 is also meaningless for finding sides.

**25. (A)** More sides means a larger interior angle, so 120 + 30 = 150°. Exterior = 180 − 150 = 30°, and n = 360 ÷ 30 = 12, a regular dodecagon. The octagon's interior angle is 135°, not 150°. A decagon's interior angle is 144°, not 150°. The figure 360 ÷ 150 = 2.4 counts tiles at a vertex, not sides, and rounding it to 9 is meaningless.

**26. (C)** Interior + exterior = 180°, and interior = 5 × exterior, so 6 × exterior = 180, exterior = 30°. Then n = 360 ÷ 30 = 12, a dodecagon. 'One-fifth' is a ratio of angles, not a count of sides, so neither 'hexagon' nor 'pentagon' follows. For a decagon exterior = 36° and interior = 144°, and 144 ÷ 5 = 28.8 ≠ 36, so the fifth relation fails there.

**27. (D)** If interior = 2 × exterior and they sum to 180°, then 3 × exterior = 180°, exterior = 60°, so n = 360 ÷ 60 = 6 (the hexagon: 120° interior, 60° exterior, and 120 = 2 × 60). For a square, interior 90° and exterior 90° are equal, not double. For a triangle, interior 60° and exterior 120°, so interior is HALF the exterior, not double. 'n = 12 because 12 is double 6' confuses side count with the angle ratio.

**28. (B)** If 360 ÷ interior = 2.5, then interior = 360 ÷ 2.5 = 144°. Exterior = 180 − 144 = 36°, so n = 360 ÷ 36 = 10, a decagon. For a dodecagon 360 ÷ 150 = 2.4, not 2.5. For an octagon 360 ÷ 135 ≈ 2.67. For a square 360 ÷ 90 = 4. Only the decagon gives exactly 2.5, confirming it cannot self-tile (2.5 is not whole).

**29. (A)** The self-tilers are the triangle, square and hexagon. Their EXTERIOR angles are 180 − 60 = 120°, 180 − 90 = 90°, and 180 − 120 = 60° respectively. The list 60°, 90°, 120° is the INTERIOR angles, not exterior. The pentagon (72° exterior) and octagon (45° exterior) do not self-tile. Including the pentagon (72° exterior) is wrong because the pentagon does not tile.

**30. (C)** As n grows past 6, the interior angle rises above the hexagon's 120° but stays below 180°. So 360 ÷ interior falls strictly between 360 ÷ 180 = 2 and 360 ÷ 120 = 3, a non-integer, so no whole number of corners can close a vertex. Interior angles never reach 180° for a real polygon, so 'exceeds 180°' is false. Side count alone does not block tiling — the angle-divisibility does. The exterior angle (360/n) stays positive, never negative.

**31. (C)** Self-tiling needs k × (interior angle) = 360° with interior = 180 − 360/n a valid regular-polygon angle. The only solutions are the triangle (k = 6), square (k = 4) and hexagon (k = 3), so k ∈ {3, 4, 6}. k = 5 would need a 72° interior angle, which no regular polygon has. k = 2 would need a 180° angle, impossible. Not every k ≥ 3 works — k = 5 is the clearest gap.

**32. (A)** Self-tiling requires 360 ÷ interior to be a whole number; 360 ÷ 150 = 2.4 is not, so it fails. Two corners give 2 × 150 = 300°, a 60° gap; three give 450°, a 90° overlap. Rounding the divisor up or down does not fix the gap or overlap. Being under 180° is necessary but far from sufficient — exact divisibility is the rule.

**33. (D)** Self-tiling needs 360 ÷ interior to be a positive integer. Here 360 ÷ 108 ≈ 3.33, 360 ÷ 135 ≈ 2.67, 360 ÷ 144 = 2.5 — none integers — so none self-tile. Being a multiple of 45° does not help the octagon (2.67 is still fractional). Exceeding 120° is irrelevant. Under-180° is necessary but not sufficient; all three fail the exact-division test.

**34. (B)** For 90° < A < 120°, dividing 360 gives 360 ÷ 120 = 3 < 360 ÷ A < 360 ÷ 90 = 4, so the quotient is strictly between 3 and 4 — never a whole number, so no self-tiling. The pentagon's 108° is in this band and does NOT tile (360 ÷ 108 ≈ 3.33), and it is not a multiple of 30° anyway. 'Three or four can fit' is exactly the rounding trap. A in this band is well under 180°, so that claim is false.

**35. (A)** A valid interior vertex must total exactly 360°. A total of 354° is 6° short, so an uncovered wedge (gap) remains and the vertex is invalid. A shortfall is a gap, not an overlap (an overlap comes from totals above 360°). 'Within rounding distance' is the classic trap; only exact 360° works. Totals under 360° are entirely possible — they simply signal gaps.

**36. (C)** Summing: 4 × 60 = 240° plus 2 × 90 = 180° gives 420°, which exceeds 360° by 60°, so the tiles pile up — an overlap. The number of tiles (six) does not guarantee closure; the angle sum does. Exceeding 360° is an overlap, not a gap. That the individual angles divide 360° is irrelevant; only their sum at the vertex matters.

**37. (D)** Add the corners: 90 + 90 + 60 + 60 = 300°, which is 60° short of 360°, so a gap remains and the vertex does NOT close. A sum of 300° is not 360°. A shortfall is a gap, not an overlap (an overlap would need a total over 360°). The count of four tiles is incidental; the sum 300° is what decides, and it fails.

**38. (C)** The leftover angle is 360 − 120 − 90 = 150°, and a 150° interior angle belongs to the regular dodecagon (12 sides). A hexagon (120°) would give 120 + 90 + 120 = 330°, a gap. A square (90°) gives 300°, a bigger gap. A triangle (60°) gives 270°, an even bigger gap. Only 150° closes the vertex exactly; no rounding is allowed.

**39. (D)** The leftover is 360 − 150 − 120 = 90°, which is exactly a square's interior angle, so a square closes the vertex (this is the 3.4.3.12-family arithmetic; the third tile is a square). A triangle (60°) would leave a 30° gap. A second hexagon (120°) would overshoot. A 90° regular polygon certainly exists — it is the square — so the 'no such polygon' claim is false.

**40. (B)** Leftover = 360 − 135 − 90 = 135°, which is the octagon's own interior angle, so a second octagon closes the vertex (the familiar square-and-octagon tiling has two octagons and one square at each vertex). A square (90°) would total only 315°. A hexagon (120°) would total 345°. A 135° regular polygon does exist — it is the octagon — so the 'no such polygon' claim is false.

**41. (D)** Leftover = 360 − 150 = 210°, and 210 ÷ 60 = 3.5, not a whole number, so triangles alone cannot fill it (you would need 3.5 tiles). Three triangles give 180°, totalling 330° (a gap). Four triangles give 240°, totalling 390° (an overlap). Neither three nor four closes it, so the configuration fails with triangles alone.

**42. (C)** Leftover = 360 − 120 = 240°, and each triangle is 60°, so 240 ÷ 60 = 4 triangles close it exactly. Dividing 240 by 120 wrongly uses the hexagon's angle for triangles. Three triangles give only 180°, leaving a 60° gap. Six triangles (360°) would leave no room for the hexagon, overshooting.

**43. (B)** Two hexagons use 2 × 120 = 240°, leaving 360 − 240 = 120°. Filling 120° with squares needs 120 ÷ 90 = 1.33 squares, not whole, so no exact closure. One square gives 90°, totalling 330° (a 30° gap). Two squares give 180°, totalling 420° (an overlap). A fractional 1.33 squares is impossible, so it cannot close.

**44. (C)** Summing the knowns: 60 + 60 + 90 = 210°, leaving 360 − 210 = 150°, which is the dodecagon's interior angle (the 3.3.4.12 vertex). A hexagon (120°) would total 330°, a gap. A second square would total 300°, a bigger gap. A 150° regular polygon does exist (the dodecagon), so the 'no such polygon' claim is false.

**45. (A)** Add the corners: 90 + 3 × 60 = 90 + 180 = 270°, which is 90° short of 360°, so a gap remains and these four tiles do NOT close the vertex (you would need, for example, one more square to reach 360°, giving the 3.3.3.4.4 arrangement). 90 + 180 = 270, not 360. A shortfall is a gap, not an overlap. The count of four tiles is incidental; the 270° sum decides, and it fails.

**46. (B)** The leftover is 360 − 108 = 252°. For k equal tiles of interior angle A to fill it, kA = 252 with A a real regular-polygon angle. Testing triangles (252 ÷ 60 = 4.2), squares (252 ÷ 90 = 2.8), hexagons (252 ÷ 120 = 2.1) all give non-integers, and 252° itself is impossible (over 180°). So no single repeated regular polygon closes a pentagon vertex — this is why the pentagon resists semi-regular tilings with one companion. The 126° idea misreads the leftover as 126 instead of 252. Rounding 4.2 to 4 or 2.8 to 3 leaves a gap or overlap.

**47. (B)** Rotating a copy of any triangle 180° about a side's midpoint forms a parallelogram, which tiles the plane by translation, so EVERY triangle tiles. At each vertex the three different angles (summing to 180°, so two full sets give 360°) arrange to close exactly. The angles-divide-360° rule is for a single REGULAR polygon meeting copies of itself corner-to-corner, not for general triangles. Both acute and obtuse triangles tile this way.

**48. (B)** The angles of any quadrilateral sum to 360°. By rotating copies, all four different angles can be brought to meet at each vertex, summing exactly to 360°, so EVERY quadrilateral (even non-convex) tiles the plane. The 'only regular polygons tile' rule applies to a single regular shape meeting itself, not to clever arrangements of an irregular tile. Individual angles need not divide 360°; it is their full set summing to 360° that matters. Parallelograms and squares are just special cases.

**49. (D)** Leftover after the triangle and square is 360 − 60 − 90 = 210°, split equally between two tiles, so each needs 105°. The regular-polygon interior angles go 60°, 90°, 108°, 120°, ... — none is 105°, so no such tile exists and the vertex cannot be completed this way. Two squares (90° each) give 60 + 90 + 90 + 90 = 330° (gap). Two hexagons give 60 + 90 + 120 + 120 = 390° (overlap). The needed 105° simply does not occur.

**50. (B)** Leftover = 360 − 60 − 150 = 150°, the dodecagon's interior angle, so a second dodecagon closes the vertex (the 3.12.12 tiling). A hexagon (120°) would total 330°, a gap. A square (90°) would total 300°, a bigger gap. A 150° regular polygon exists — the dodecagon — so the 'no such polygon' claim is false.

**51. (D)** Leftover = 360 − 90 − 120 = 150°, and 150 ÷ 60 = 2.5, not whole, so triangles cannot exactly fill it (2.5 tiles is impossible). Two triangles give 120°, totalling 330° (a 30° gap). Three triangles give 180°, totalling 390° (an overlap). Neither closes it, so this combination fails.

**52. (D)** Leftover = 360 − 120 − 150 = 90°. Splitting 90° between two equal regular tiles needs 45° each, but the smallest regular interior angle is 60° (the triangle), so no 45° regular polygon exists. 'Half an octagon' is not a regular tile. Two 30° tiles would give only 60°, not 90°. Two squares (90° each) would give 180°, far over the 90° leftover. So a single square (90°) is the only fit.

**53. (D)** Leftover = 360 − 135 = 225°, and 225 ÷ 60 = 3.75, not whole, so triangles alone cannot fill it. Three triangles give 180°, totalling 315° (a gap). Four triangles give 240°, totalling 375° (an overlap). A fractional 3.75 triangles is impossible. So an octagon plus triangles alone cannot close the vertex.

**54. (B)** The perpendicular bisector is perpendicular to GH; the 60° arm makes a 60° angle with GH, so the two are not parallel (their angles with GH differ). Two non-parallel lines in a plane always intersect when extended, so a long enough arm crosses the bisector. The arm is not 'horizontal' — it tilts at 60°. Lines from different points certainly can meet. The length of GH does not affect whether two non-parallel lines eventually cross.

**55. (D)** Five triangles cover 5 × 60 = 300°, leaving 360 − 300 = 60°. A 60° interior angle is exactly an equilateral triangle, so the only regular fit is another triangle (restoring the original six). A hexagon (120°) would overshoot the 60° gap. The leftover is 60°, not 300°. A 60° gap is filled perfectly by a triangle, so a regular polygon does work.

**56. (D)** The missing leg is √(25² − 7²) = √(625 − 49) = √576 = 24. The two legs (7 and 24) are perpendicular, so area = ½ × 7 × 24 = 84 cm². Using the hypotenuse (25) as a base with leg 7 ignores that the hypotenuse is not perpendicular to a leg. The leg 7 is fine — RHS only needs the hypotenuse to be the longest side, which 25 is.

**57. (A)** Two equal legs with a 90° included angle give an isosceles right triangle; the hypotenuse is √(5² + 5²) = √50 = 5√2. It is not equilateral, since the hypotenuse (5√2 ≈ 7.07) exceeds the legs. The hypotenuse is 5√2, not 10 (10 would need legs summing in a way Pythagoras forbids). A right angle can perfectly well sit between two equal legs, so it is not impossible.

**58. (D)** 105° is built from constructible parts (60° + 45°), and bisecting any already-constructed angle is a valid compass-straightedge step, yielding 52.5°. The 'multiple of 15°' idea is not a real constructibility rule — bisection halves whatever you have. Angles need not be 'even in degrees' to be bisected; the compass halves them geometrically. Trisection of a general angle is impossible, so that route is invalid anyway.

**59. (B)** Six triangles each contributing 60° total exactly 360°, so the vertex closes — but a semi-regular tiling needs TWO OR MORE different regular polygons. With only triangles it is the regular (monohedral) triangular tiling, not semi-regular. Six triangles close exactly with no overlap. Adding to 360° makes it a valid tiling, but sameness of tile keeps it regular, not semi-regular.

**60. (A)** Around any interior point there are 360° of turning, so the tile corners meeting there must total exactly 360° — under it leaves an uncovered wedge (gap), over it makes tiles overlap. 180° is only a straight angle along an edge, not a vertex full turn. 'At most 360°' permits gaps, which violate the tiling definition. Multiples of 90° like 270° (gap) or 450° (overlap) do not close a vertex; only 360° does.
