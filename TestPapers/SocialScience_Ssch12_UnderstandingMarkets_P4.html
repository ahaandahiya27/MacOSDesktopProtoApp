Instructions: Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. A market is any buyer-seller-price arrangement (online and weekly haats count, not just shop buildings); the cheapest price is not 'best' because a price below the seller's cost makes supply dry up, so the just-right clearing price is what matters; more demand against limited supply pushes price UP while plentiful supply pushes it DOWN; intermediaries (wholesalers, distributors, retailers, aggregators) ADD real value by bulk-buying, transporting, storing and breaking bulk into small amounts; and the certification marks must be matched to the right domain - FSSAI for food safety, ISI/BIS for manufactured goods, AGMARK for graded farm produce, BEE Star for appliance energy use - while MRP is the maximum a consumer may be charged, not a minimum. Worked Solutions

1. (C) A market needs buyers, sellers AND an agreed price; the app and the haat have all three, while the free gift has no price, so it is not a market. Not every transfer of a good is a market - the price element is essential, which rules out the free gift. A market need not be a physical gathering (the app counts) nor must it be online (the haat counts), so the two single-option answers are too narrow.

2. (D) What makes something a market is the meeting of buyers, sellers and an agreed price; the building and physical contact are incidental, so the skeptic is wrong to treat them as essential. The website plainly does show a real price the buyer pays, so denying that is false. The market is the exchange itself, not wherever the parcel is handled, so locating it at the depot is mistaken. And a remote weaver who sells goods for a price is a genuine seller.

3. (C) Buyers, a seller and goods are all clearly present; what never appears is a price both sides accept, so the missing ingredient is the agreed price. Eager buyers are present, so buyers are not missing. The fish are present, so goods are not missing. The seller is at the stall the whole afternoon, so the seller is not missing. Without a mutually acceptable price, no exchange completes and the market fails.

4. (C) The defining, shared feature is the buyer-seller-price exchange, which all four have despite their different settings, so grandeur is irrelevant. Prices commonly differ between a cart and a mall, so identical pricing is not the test. A haat-style cart need not open daily, so 'open every day' is false. The cart and website lack a roof and walls, so a permanent building is not a shared requirement.

5. (D) Needs are not automatically free; bottled or supplied water is bought and sold at a price, so it is a market good. The claim that water can never be sold ignores the everyday sale of treated water. Markets sell both needs and wants, not wants alone. And water is certainly a real, purchasable good. The presence of buyers, sellers and a price makes water trading a market like any other.

6. (A) A market requires buyers, sellers and a price; the friendly sticker swap has no price, so it is not a market. The haat has sellers, buyers and bargained prices, so it qualifies. The aggregator displays many sellers' priced offers, so it qualifies. The mall has many priced shops, so it qualifies. The odd one out is the free, price-less swap.

7. (C) Pricing below cost wiped out her means to restock, so supply vanished - showing the just-right price must let the seller cover costs and continue. The lowest possible number is therefore not 'best'; it can drive the seller out. The price is reached by both sides, not the buyer alone, so the seller's costs do matter. And an excessively high price would scare buyers off, so 'as high as possible' is also wrong.

8. (A) A workable price must rise above the seller's 90-rupee cost (or she loses money and stops) yet stay below the 130 she asked (or the buyer walks away); about 110 fits, leaving both better off. The buyer's first low offer is below cost, so it is not automatically fair. A figure of 60 is even further below cost and would end the sale. And the seller does not set the price alone - negotiation involves both sides, so 130 is not automatic.

9. (A) The market clears where demand equals supply - here 250 = 250 at 16 rupees. At 8 rupees buyers want far more than sellers will provide (a shortage), and at 24 rupees sellers offer far more than buyers will take (a surplus). The numbers do meet exactly at 16 rupees, so a working price plainly exists; neither the lowest nor the highest price balances both sides.

10. (B) Overpricing pushes buyers to the cheaper, fairly priced seller, so the overpriced seller sells little - the assertion is true and the reason explains exactly why. The reason is directly related, not unrelated. A higher price does not always raise sales; buyers walk away from overpriced goods, so that option is false. And price clearly affects how much buyers buy, so the 'no effect' option is also false.

11. (A) Low dry-season demand means a high price scares off the few buyers, so most umbrellas stay unsold and earnings drop; the price must reflect buyers' willingness. There is no law restricting umbrella sales to one season. 'Highest price means highest earnings' ignores that buyers refuse overpriced goods. And the government does not set seasonal umbrella prices - the just-right price emerges from demand and supply.

12. (C) A price the buyer loves may sit below the seller's costs; selling at a loss is unsustainable, so the seller stops - this is why the cheapest price is not 'best' for keeping goods available. Sellers do not refuse out of dislike for happy buyers. There is no general law banning low prices. And a low price does not automatically mean poor quality; the real issue is whether the seller can cover costs.

13. (D) An overpriced good drives buyers to the fairly priced seller, so Seller B sells little. A higher price does not guarantee more profit if buyers refuse to buy. Buyers do not split evenly when one price is clearly better - they favour Seller A. And buyers cannot force a below-cost price; the seller would simply stop, so Seller B is not made to sell at a loss forever.

14. (A) On the festival day, surging demand against a fixed supply pushes the price up; the next day, demand collapses while supply is unchanged, so the price falls - tracing both moves correctly. 'More buyers means cheaper' is backwards. Although supply never changed, demand did on both days, so the price does not stay fixed. And high demand never makes goods free; it makes them costlier, the opposite of zero.

15. (C) Both sides grew, but demand grew much more, so buyers compete for relatively scarce ice cream and the price rises. More sellers alone would push price down, but the much larger surge in buyers outweighs that. Because the two sides did not grow equally, the price does not stay the same. And a modest rise in supply does not send the price to zero when demand has jumped far more.

16. (C) A drought cuts supply against steady demand, so the price climbs; releasing stored stocks then increases supply and eases the price back - the correct two-step chain. A drought does not lower the price of a need; scarcity raises it. The price is not government-locked; it responds to the demand-supply balance. And adding supply lowers, not raises, the price, so a second rise is wrong.

17. (C) Rising demand combined with falling supply squeezes the good from both directions, maximising the upward pressure on price. Falling demand with rising supply does the opposite, pushing price down. Unchanged demand and supply give the price no reason to move. Rising supply against steady demand lowers price. Only the demand-up, supply-down case drives price up the most.

18. (B) The high price comes from heavy demand chasing a tiny supply; raising supply to match demand eases the scarcity and lowers the price toward balance. More advertising raises demand and pushes price up further. Making fewer phones deepens the shortage and raises price. Raising the price does not fix the underlying scarcity at its source - more supply does.

19. (B) Reduced supply meeting steady demand creates scarcity, which pushes the onion price up. Being a daily need does not make scarce onions cheaper; steady need keeps demand firm while supply falls. The prices of needs do move - people pay more for onions in a shortage. And a shortage raises, rather than removes, the price, so free distribution by sellers is wrong.

20. (A) A bumper harvest increases supply; with demand unchanged, sellers must compete to clear the extra stock, so the price falls - the correct cause-and-effect chain. More supply does not let sellers raise prices; that confuses supply with demand. Supply rose, not fell, so the second chain is wrong from the start. And supply did change (it rose), so 'stays the same' is incorrect.

21. (B) A flood of mangoes against steady demand is rising supply with unchanged demand, which pushes price down. The festival sweets case is rising demand against fixed supply, pushing price up. The phone case is falling supply against surging demand, a strong upward push. The scarce vaccine in an outbreak is low supply against high demand, again raising price. Only the mango glut lowers the price.

22. (B) A retail shop sells the smallest amounts (a packet or two), a wholesale market sells larger bulk lots, and a producer's factory turns out the largest volumes, so smallest-to-largest is retail, wholesale, producer. Putting wholesale before retail reverses two steps. Putting the producer first as the smallest is backwards. And the stages plainly sell different quantities - that difference is why the chain exists.

23. (B) A nearby retail shop is built for small, convenient purchases close to home, perfect for one packet at night. A wholesale market deals in bulk, not single packets, and is not designed for one late-night buyer. A haat opens only on certain days, not every hour, so it cannot serve a late-night craving. And an export house ships goods abroad, not single snacks to your door.

24. (A) Bulk selling spreads costs over many metres, so the wholesale per-metre price is lower; the retailer adds the cost and convenience of breaking bulk into small amounts, raising the per-metre price. The lower wholesale price is the same cloth in larger lots, not worse quality. The retailer is a separate link, not the producer. And the price gap arises from bulk versus small-quantity service, not a government order.

25. (D) Combining many sellers' offers in one place for buyers to compare is the job of an aggregator. The app does not make the goods, so it is not the producer. It does not buy and own the goods in bulk, so it is not a wholesaler - it merely lists others' offers. And it does not use up the goods, so it is not the consumer. Aggregation is the right role.

26. (A) A market that appears on fixed days and then disperses is a haat; a mall is permanent and enclosed, so the market described is not a mall - the correct pairing. A haat does not run all day every day, so the first distractor is wrong. The described market is a physical periodic gathering, not an always-available app. And a round-the-clock wholesale depot is the opposite of a periodic gathering.

27. (A) A bazaar of shops trading every day is a permanent market, so it is the odd one out among periodic and one-off arrangements. A weekly haat appears only on fixed days, so it is periodic. A once-a-year fair is a one-off seasonal market. And an alternate-morning cart visits only intermittently, so it too is not permanent. Only the daily bazaar is permanent.

28. (D) The app lets a small artisan reach a buyer in another state, showing markets connect distant parties and widen a small producer's reach. Markets are not limited to one village - this very example crosses states. A price is still paid through the app, so a price is not removed. And far from stopping small producers, such markets help them sell to many more buyers.

29. (A) Biscuits are first made by the producer, sold in bulk to a wholesaler, passed to a retailer, and finally bought by the consumer - producer, wholesaler, retailer, consumer. The other orders scramble the roles: the consumer cannot come first because nothing has been made yet, and the producer cannot come after the retailer or wholesaler because the goods must exist before they can be traded onward.

30. (D) Each step adds real services - moving, storing, breaking bulk - plus a fair margin, so the price builds from 240 to 380 along the chain. It is not pure cheating; the buyer gains a shirt conveniently nearby in single units. The rise is not one government tax. And the retailer is a separate link from the producer, each adding value, which is why the price grows step by step.

31. (D) The consumer is the final user, and the distributor carries goods from manufacturers and wholesalers out to scattered retailers - the correct pairing. The wholesaler resells in bulk rather than using the good, and the retailer sells to consumers rather than moving goods to factories. The producer makes the good and the consumer uses it, so neither moves goods to shops. And the retailer sells small amounts rather than using the good.

32. (D) The bakery makes the bread (producer) and sells single loaves to neighbours (retailer), filling both roles in one short chain. It does not buy bread in bulk or eat it, so it is not wholesaler-consumer. It does not ship bread abroad, so it is not distributor-exporter. And buying flour to bake does not make it 'only a consumer' - its defining roles toward buyers are producer and retailer.

33. (C) The retailer breaks the farmer's 800 kg into 3 kg lots a household can buy, so bulk-breaking is exactly the solution to the mismatch. The producer growing more does not fix the quantity gap. The exporter just moves potatoes abroad, not into small home-sized lots. And forcing the consumer to buy all 800 kg is the problem itself, not a fix - bulk-breaking by the retailer bridges large supply and small need.

34. (B) A chain of transporters, storers and distributors carries distant rice into the city in saleable amounts at a workable price - that is how distance is bridged affordably. The city family does not harvest the rice. Rice does not appear without transport and traders; moving it is real work. And the government does not grow all rice inside cities. The organised supply chain is the answer.

35. (C) The cloth reaches a distant shirt through a chain of wholesalers, distributors and retailers who transport it and split it into usable amounts. Buyers do not all travel to Surat - the chain saves them that. Goods do not move without traders; intermediaries do the work. And the government does not personally deliver each town's cloth. The distance-bridging supply chain is the correct explanation.

36. (C) The chain exists because each trader adds a real service - bulk buying, transport, storage and bulk-breaking - so the mango reaches the city buyer in usable form. There is no law fixing the number of owners. The traders are not useless; remove them and a city buyer could not easily get a single mango from a distant orchard. And it is impractical for the orchard owner to deliver to every city buyer, which is precisely why the chain of traders exists.

37. (B) Middlemen perform real services - moving, storing and breaking bulk so soap is available near you as a single bar - and the price reflects that value, not waste. Saying they 'do nothing' is the trap the reply refutes. Middlemen are separate links, not the producers. And they add to the factory cost because they add value, so the claim that they sell below cost is false.

38. (A) Cold-storing mangoes so they are available beyond the brief harvest is a clear value-adding service - the buyer gains access out of season. A trader who neither moves nor stores the crate adds little. A shop charging more for nothing extra adds no value. And hiding the price is unfair conduct, not value-adding. Genuine intermediation involves real services like storage that benefit buyers.

39. (D) Without intermediaries, a buyer wanting one bar would have to approach producers directly and probably buy in bulk - expensive and impractical for daily needs, which is exactly why the chain exists. Goods would not become free; someone still makes and moves them. Removing the convenient links makes buying harder, not easier. And producers cannot realistically hand-deliver single free bars to every home.

40. (B) When a farmer can handle selling directly, skipping some intermediaries trims the added margins, often lowering the price and raising the farmer's share. Short chains are not illegal - direct farm markets are common. Removing intermediaries does not make goods free; the farmer still charges a price. And chain length is unrelated to quality. Intermediaries add value but also cost, so sometimes a shorter chain benefits both ends.

41. (D) The distributor carries goods across distance to scattered retailers, and the retailer sells small amounts to final buyers - complementary links. Neither uses up nor makes the goods (those are the consumer and producer). The roles are not reversed - the distributor is not the one selling single items to homes. And printing MRP or setting taxes are not their defining jobs. Distance-moving versus small-quantity selling is the correct contrast.

42. (A) A retailer turns a bulk lot into single apples a buyer can grab nearby, adding storage, display and a margin, so the retail price tops the wholesale price. No law forces a doubling of prices. The apple does not change into another fruit. And wholesale prices are real prices the retailer actually pays. The mark-up reflects genuine value added in moving from bulk to a single convenient unit.

43. (B) Transporting, storing and breaking bulk are real services, so the higher city price reflects value the trader adds, not cheating. The trader is an intermediary, not the basket-maker, so it is not the producer's profit. There is no law forcing the village price in a distant city; the rise pays for moving and splitting the goods. The trader's role is genuine intermediation.

44. (C) A subsidy is government money used to lower a price or support producers, so a gas-cylinder subsidy makes cylinders cheaper for households. It does not raise the price - that is the opposite of its purpose. It is not a tax; a tax adds to the price while a subsidy reduces it. And it does not ban sales; it encourages affordable access. The intended effect is a lower, more affordable price.

45. (D) A street light is open to everyone and one user does not exhaust it for the next - the hallmarks of a public good, usually provided by the government. A chocolate bar eaten by one child is used up and private. A single shirt worn by one buyer is a private good. And a privately owned bicycle serves only its owner. The shared, non-using-up street light is the public good.

46. (B) Standards and marks protect buyers by certifying safety and quality an ordinary consumer cannot test alone - that is their purpose. They are not meant to maximise prices. They do not aim to halt private trade; markets continue more safely. And they are not designed to exclude poorer buyers. Consumer protection through verified quality is the government's goal.

47. (A) Curbing pollution from factories and markets protects society and the environment - a recognised government role. Helping factories pollute more would harm, not protect, society. Banning all manufacturing is not the aim; markets continue, just more responsibly. And the government does not run pollution for profit. Reducing pollution is the protective action.

48. (A) A bustling square and the women-run Ima Keithal show markets can be social and cultural centres, beyond mere trade. Markets mix communities rather than keeping them apart. They are not just tax-collection points. And many markets, like the Ima Keithal, are run by ordinary people, not the government. The melting-pot, meeting-point role is the point.

49. (B) MRP is the maximum a consumer may be charged, taxes included, so charging above it is not allowed - high demand does not override the printed ceiling. There is no law requiring an above-MRP charge in summer. And charging extra is the opposite of a subsidy, which lowers the price. The MRP caps what the shop may collect, regardless of season.

50. (B) MRP is the maximum a consumer may be charged, not a fixed or minimum price, so selling below it is permitted - the wholesaler is closer to correct. The retailer is wrong that MRP must be charged exactly; selling for less is allowed. It is not both a maximum and minimum. And MRP is a consumer-price ceiling printed by the maker, not a government-only price.

51. (B) Certification marks, clear label details and honest buyer reviews give real evidence of safety and quality, so they are the soundest things to rely on. A flashy advertisement is persuasion, not proof. The lowest price says nothing about quality and may even hint at a problem. And the seller's own promise is self-interested and unverified. Consumer awareness means checking independent signals.

52. (D) The FSSAI mark indicates the food meets recognised safety standards, making it the more trustworthy pick. A lower price does not make an uncertified snack 'better'; cheapness is not proof of safety. Certification marks do convey real safety information. And FSSAI concerns food safety, not energy efficiency - that is the BEE Star label's domain.

53. (C) Energy use is shown by the BEE Star label, food safety by the FSSAI mark, and graded farm produce by AGMARK - matching each to its proper domain. FSSAI is for food, not appliances; AGMARK is for farm produce, not food in that slot; and ISI is for manufactured goods, not energy. The only correct triple is BEE Star, FSSAI, AGMARK.

54. (D) FSSAI certifies food safety (the biscuit), ISI/BIS certifies manufactured goods meeting Indian standards (the iron), and AGMARK grades farm produce (the wheat) - the correct match. The other options shuffle these domains: AGMARK is not for biscuits, ISI/BIS is not for biscuits or wheat in those slots, and BEE Star concerns appliance energy use, not food. Only FSSAI, ISI/BIS, AGMARK fits.

55. (D) AGMARK certifies the grading quality of agricultural produce, NOT the energy efficiency of appliances, so that statement is false and is the odd one out. The FSSAI mark does signal food safety. The ISI/BIS mark does assure manufactured-goods quality. And the BEE Star label does indicate appliance energy efficiency. Only the AGMARK statement misassigns the mark's domain.

56. (C) An export is selling goods to a buyer in another country (tea sold abroad), and an import is buying goods from another country (crude oil bought from abroad), so the first statement is correct. The labels are not reversed. Selling and buying across borders are not both exports. And neither is a domestic sale, since a domestic sale happens within one country, not across borders.

57. (B) A haat serves a local area, a domestic market spans one country, and exports/imports cross between countries - so local to international runs haat, domestic, cross-country trade. Listing cross-country trade first reverses the order. Putting the national market before the local haat misorders the first two. And the three plainly cover different areas, so an ordering does exist.

58. (D) This fixes both errors: a rock-bottom price can drive the seller to stop selling, so cheapest is not always best; and a website with buyers, sellers and a price is a genuine market. Repeating that the cheapest is best and websites are not markets keeps both mistakes. Claiming only physical shops are markets and price is irrelevant is doubly wrong. And a market certainly needs buyers, sellers and a price - those are its defining ingredients.

59. (A) If buyers chase only the lowest price, prices can fall below sellers' costs; loss-making sellers eventually quit, so fewer goods remain - showing why the cheapest price is not 'best' for the market as a whole. Goods do not get endlessly cheaper without a downside; the downside is vanishing supply. Sellers will not trade at a loss forever; they stop. And the government does not compel below-cost selling.

60. (C) Markets arise because each person can produce only a few things yet needs and wants many; trading lets the wheat grower obtain cloth, tools and salt. There is no government order forcing a fixed shopping list, and people are free to make some things at home - they simply cannot make everything. Tax collection is one government activity but not the reason markets exist; the root cause is the human inability to be self-sufficient.
