Worked Solutions

1. (B) Identical rock weathering at different rates proves the rock chemistry cannot be the sole cause, so the fixed-rate idea is refuted by the data itself. Height by itself does not speed weathering — the desert plateau is high yet weathers slowly. Rock age is not the variable here; the two sites share the same rock. What differs is climate: heat and water together drive faster chemical and physical breakdown.

2. (A) The recorded pattern — depth rising slowly over thousands of years — directly contradicts the idea of one-season soil building, so any 'correct' verdict fails. Flood water moves sediment but does not manufacture mature, humus-bearing topsoil overnight. The data show the youngest sites had the least soil, not deep soil. Saying floods only remove soil overstates it; the real point is that genuine soil formation is a long weathering-plus-humus process.

3. (C) Both slopes share the same parent rock, so a 'softer bedrock' claim is ruled out by the setup. Gravity would not selectively favour one compass direction. Faster drainage would carry minerals away, not concentrate them, so that reasoning is backwards. The genuine driver is the warm, moist microclimate, which speeds weathering and supports vegetation whose litter becomes humus.

4. (B) Freezing water expands and does crack rock (frost wedging), so (i) is valid. Roots growing in cracks exert real prying force, so (ii) is valid. Wind-blown grit abrades surfaces, so (iii) is valid. The odd one is (iv): soil formation is gradual and cannot be finished by one night's frost.

5. (A) A dark thick surface layer signals accumulated humus, which comes from decayed litter, not from pale rock fragments. Subsoil does not physically lift the topsoil upward. Fast drainage carries dissolved minerals down, and minerals are not what make topsoil dark. The forest's steady litter supply plus protection from erosion is what built and preserved the humus-rich layer.

6. (A) A sealed dry case removes water, the key agent, so almost nothing happens. A permanent freezer keeps water solid and largely inert against the surface, limiting chemical attack. A dry cupboard still lacks moisture. The warm rainy forest supplies liquid water, root pressure and leaf-derived acids together — the most aggressive combination.

7. (B) Humus and organisms reside in soil, not in solid bedrock, so swapping them is wrong. Bedrock is not teeming with life, so 'equally full' is false. Soil is not pure organic matter — it is mostly weathered mineral particles plus humus, air and water. The defining contrast is that soil carries the living, organic component that solid bedrock lacks.

8. (C) The A horizon is the dark crumbly topsoil that loses material downward, not the layer that gains it. The C horizon is loose rock chips described separately below the pale band. Bedrock is the solid base, also described separately. The pale band stained with washed-down clay and iron is the B horizon, the zone of accumulation.

9. (C) Upward capillary rise would move material the wrong way and could not explain the A horizon losing while the B gains. Wind acts at the surface, not between buried horizons, and would not selectively enrich the B. Earthworm mixing is real but is not the mechanism that systematically leaches and re-deposits clay. Downward-percolating rain is the agent that strips the A and enriches the B.

10. (C) Bedrock lies deep below 15 cm and is not what a plough or a topsoil sale removes. The B horizon is subsoil below the topsoil, not the surface that was scraped. The C horizon is weathered parent rock far down, not the top or the most fertile layer. Selling the top 15 cm strips the A horizon — the humus-rich, nutrient-bearing topsoil — so yields fall.

11. (B) The dark humus-rich layer is the A horizon, not the C, so that correction is misplaced. The C horizon lies deep, below A and B, not above them. It is not made of organisms or leaves — those concentrate near the surface. The claim is broadly right: the C horizon is partly weathered parent rock with little humus.

12. (D) If the C horizon were at the surface it would show loose weathered rock chips, not a clay-stained accumulation band. A pale layer enriched with washed-down clay is not the humus-rich A horizon, and extra humus would darken it, not pale it. Bedrock does not weather overnight into stained clay. Erosion of the A horizon exposes the B horizon, which matches the described stained surface.

13. (B) Organic matter is concentrated near the surface and dwindles with depth, so any order putting bedrock or the C horizon first is reversed or scrambled. The B horizon gains washed-down minerals but less organic matter than the A. The correct gradient runs A (richest) down through B and C to bedrock (essentially none).

14. (D) The B horizon is neither the richest in humus nor a source of loose parent-rock chips, so both-from-B fails. Reversing the two — C for humus, A for rock — is backwards because humus is in the A and rock fragments are in the C. Solid bedrock yields neither humus nor loose fragments. The A horizon gives the humus sample and the C horizon gives the weathered rock fragments.

15. (B) Deserts are dry, so 'heavy rainfall washing it away' contradicts the setting. A profile can still form over weathered material whether or not bedrock is near the surface, and deserts do have rock beneath. Packing does not squeeze out humus. The real reason is biological: scant vegetation means almost no litter, so very little humus accumulates to form a dark A horizon.

16. (A) Air does not condense into water droplets on heating; only water vapour does, so the air explanation is wrong. Humus does not melt into liquid that climbs glass. Glass does not generate water. The droplets are condensed water vapour driven out of the moist soil, which is exactly what demonstrates the soil held moisture.

17. (D) Bubbles come from trapped air, not from water, so 'destroyed the water' is irrelevant. The clod releasing fewer bubbles is losing trapped air, not absorbing air from the water. Bubbles signal the presence of air, so 'only from soil with no air' is backwards. Compressing the clod collapses the pore spaces, leaving less air to bubble out.

18. (B) Sand and silt are mineral grains that do not burn off at these temperatures, so they are not the lost mass. Oxygen does not have negative weight. Soil does not turn into bedrock by heating. The mass falls and the colour pales because moisture evaporates and the dark humus is burned away.

19. (A) A higher percolation rate means water drains away quickly, which would leave less water held, not more, so that is contradictory. Clay has small particles, not large ones with wide gaps. Clay does contain some air spaces, so it cannot be entirely water. The greater water loss reflects clay's high water-holding capacity.

20. (D) Mineral grains do not evaporate in ordinary sunlight. Humus does not boil away from gentle sun-drying. Air does not vanish as soil dries; in fact it increases. Drying removes water, and the emptied pore space fills with air, so water is the component that changes most.

21. (C) Coolness and dampness describe moisture, not living organisms. A drainage time measures percolation, a physical property, not life. A brown stain shows colour or humus, not organisms. Finding millions of micro-organisms plus worms and insects in a gram of soil directly demonstrates that soil teems with life.

22. (A) Burrowing loosens soil rather than compacting it, so closed pores is the opposite of the effect. Earthworms add to humus through their casts, not strip it away. They do not transmute sand into clay. Their tunnels open the soil, boosting both air supply and water percolation to roots.

23. (B) Tiny tightly packed particles are clay, so P is clayey, ruling out 'sandy' first. Large loose grains are sand, so Q is sandy. A balanced humus-rich mix is loam, so R is loamy. Only the ordering clayey, sandy, loamy fits all three descriptions.

24. (C) Percolation rate and water-holding capacity are distinct, opposing properties, not one property, so 'the same' is false. Sandy soil drains fast but holds little, so it cannot hold the most water. Fast drainage means water leaves quickly, the reverse of high retention. Small tight pores raise retention but slow drainage, so the two cannot both be maximised.

25. (A) Clay drains slowest and holds the most water, so it cannot dry first. Sand drains fastest and holds the least, so it cannot stay soggiest. Different textures cannot all dry at once. The expected pattern is sandy driest soonest, clayey wettest longest, loamy intermediate.

26. (D) Loose sand has wide gaps full of air, so its aeration is good. Freshly ploughed loam has open pores and good air supply. Dry breezy sand is well aerated. Water-logged clay has fine pores jammed with water, leaving almost no air — the poorest aeration of the four.

27. (A) Compost does not transmute sand into clay, nor make it airless. It does not seal the soil against all percolation; sand still drains reasonably. It feeds organisms rather than sterilising the soil. Its real benefit is boosting water retention and nutrients in the otherwise fast-draining, poor sand while keeping decent drainage.

28. (A) Trampling does not change particle sizes into sand grains. It does not add humus, and added humus would speed not slow infiltration anyway. It does not enlarge pores — enlarged pores would speed drainage, contradicting the observation. Packing collapses the pores, so water percolates more slowly through the hardened soil.

29. (C) Large particles characterise sand, which here is the poorer habitat, so particle size is not the reason. Fast drainage would dry the soil and remove the moisture organisms need. Cold does not generally maximise soil life. The loamy patch wins because it supplies moisture, humus food and air pockets together.

30. (C) If they were directly related, fast drainage would mean high retention, but observation shows the opposite. They are not the same property measured twice. They are not both maximised in sand — sand holds the least. They move oppositely: high percolation goes with low retention, and vice versa.

31. (B) Mixing the soils would destroy the very comparison being made. Clay's percolation rate can be measured; it is just slow. Timing must begin when water is poured, not after draining ends. The flaw is the unequal water volume, which breaks the fair-test rule that only the soil should differ.

32. (D) Percolation rate is volume divided by time: 360 ÷ 8 = 45 mL/min. Multiplying 360 × 8 gives 2880, the wrong operation. Dividing time by volume (8 ÷ 360) gives the inverted 0.022. Subtracting 360 − 8 gives 352, which mixes unlike quantities. The correct rate is 45 mL/min.

33. (C) Soil A's rate is 240 ÷ 6 = 40 mL/min; soil B's is 240 ÷ 30 = 8 mL/min, so A : B = 40 : 8 = 5 : 1. Writing 1 : 5 inverts it — A is faster, not slower. Quoting 6 : 30 confuses times with rates and is even reversed. The volumes are equal but the rates clearly differ, so 1 : 1 is wrong.

34. (D) Compute rates: P = 420 ÷ 7 = 60 mL/min; Q = 480 ÷ 12 = 40 mL/min. The larger total volume does not mean the higher rate, so Q's 480 mL is a trap. A longer time alone signals slower, not faster, percolation. Being 'in the hundreds' says nothing about rate. P is faster by 60 − 40 = 20 mL/min.

35. (A) Time equals volume divided by rate: 270 ÷ 18 = 15 minutes. Multiplying 270 × 18 gives 4860, the wrong operation. Dividing rate by volume (18 ÷ 270) gives the inverted 0.067. Subtracting 270 − 18 gives 252, mixing unlike units. The answer is 15 minutes.

36. (A) The rate is 300 ÷ 12 = 25 mL/min, so in 20 minutes 25 × 20 = 500 mL drain. 180 mL would mean less water in more time, which is impossible at a steady rate. 308 mL comes from wrongly adding 300 + 8. 720 mL overshoots by misusing the figures. The correct volume is 500 mL.

37. (D) Equal volume in less time means a higher rate, so the fastest-draining sandy is highest and the slowest clayey is lowest; clayey-first reverses this. Equal volumes do not make equal rates when times differ. Loamy is intermediate, not first. The order from highest to lowest rate is sandy, loamy, clayey.

38. (B) Taking longer does not prove coarser grains — depth was the variable, not texture. Depth clearly does affect drain time, since the half-depth column drained faster. A faster time does not always prove coarser particles when other factors like depth differ. The soils are identical; the time gap comes from depth, not texture.

39. (C) S's rate is 540 ÷ 6 = 90 mL/min; T's is 540 ÷ 18 = 30 mL/min, so S is 90 ÷ 30 = 3 times faster. A factor of 2 misreads the times. Equal volumes do not make equal rates when times differ. 'One-third' inverts the comparison — S is faster, not slower. S's rate is three times T's.

40. (A) Rates are: soil 3 = 80 mL/min (fastest), soil 1 = 60 mL/min (middle), soil 2 = 20 mL/min (slowest). The fastest is the coarsest sandy, the slowest the finest clayey, the middle loamy. Calling soil 1 sandy ignores that soil 3 is faster. Calling soil 2 sandy reverses fastest and slowest. Equal volumes do not equal texture when times differ.

41. (D) Paddy needs water held around the roots, so it suits the slow plot A, not the fast-draining plot B. Groundnut needs drainage and air, the opposite of standing water, so it does not suit slow plot A. Percolation rate strongly affects crop choice. The sound match is paddy in slow A and groundnut in fast B.

42. (D) Y took four times as long for the same volume, so Y is slower, not faster — it cannot be the freer-draining loam. Equal volumes with very different times do not give equal rates. A high percolation rate marks the freely draining loam, not clay, so calling X clay reverses it. X at 40 mL/min is four times Y at 10 mL/min, matching loam over clay.

43. (C) The rate is 200 ÷ 5 = 40 mL/min; over the full 13 minutes that is 40 × 13 = 520 mL. 320 mL counts only the remaining 8 minutes, not the total. 200 mL ignores the extra time entirely. 260 mL uses the wrong arithmetic. The total after 13 minutes is 520 mL.

44. (B) Column 1's rate is 600 ÷ 10 = 60 mL/min; column 2's is 600 ÷ 15 = 40 mL/min, a difference of 20 mL/min. 5 mL/min comes from subtracting the times (15 − 10) rather than the rates. 0 mL/min wrongly assumes equal volume means equal rate. 100 mL/min adds the rates instead of subtracting. The excess is 20 mL/min.

45. (D) Humus and loose structure open pores, raising the rate, so 'humus blocks pores' is backwards. Equal volume with very different times does not give equal rates. The difference is from structure, not from the particles suddenly becoming sand. The managed field drains 480 ÷ 8 = 60 mL/min against the compacted field's 480 ÷ 24 = 20 mL/min.

46. (C) Clay has small particles, not large ones with wide channels — wide channels would speed flow anyway. Water is not chemically destroyed; it is merely slowed. Slow seeping means a low percolation rate, so claiming clay's rate is higher than sand's contradicts the observation. The fine, tightly packed pores of clay resist downward flow, causing the pooling.

47. (B) Cotton favours lighter, freer-draining sandy-loam, not water-logged clay, so cotton is the weaker fit here. Clayey-loam is in fact a productive soil, so 'supports nothing' is false. Clayey-loam holds water and drains slowly, the opposite of draining too fast. Wheat and gram match the moisture-holding clayey-loam.

48. (B) Flooding clay does not speed drainage or dry the roots — clay holds water tightly. Water does not transmute clay into sand. Gram is not a water plant; it does not want permanent flooding. Water-logging fills the pores, excludes air, and suffocates the roots, which is why permanent flooding harms gram.

49. (D) Clay's slow drainage suits paddy and other moisture-loving crops, so it is far from useless. The slowest soil is not best for every crop — groundnut, for instance, needs drainage. Percolation rate is in fact central to crop suitability. Clay's value lies in matching it to the right water-loving crop.

50. (C) Holding the most water is not always best; water-logging harms roots, so soil N is risky for vegetables. Slow drainage tends to fill pores with water and exclude air, the reverse of guaranteeing air. Soil M does hold some water, not none. Soil M's moderate retention plus surplus drainage gives the balanced, well-aerated bed needed.

51. (C) Paddy needs standing water, the opposite of fast-draining sandy-loam, so rice is a poor fit. Soil type does affect crop success, so 'any crop equally' is false. A fast-draining plot cannot stay flooded, so flooding-dependent crops fail. Cotton and lentils suit the lighter, well-drained sandy-loam.

52. (A) Soil weight is not what protects the slope; binding and rain interception do. Canopies slow and break rain but do not stop all of it from reaching the ground. Forests have rich topsoil — that is what they protect. Roots holding the soil and the canopy plus litter cushioning the rain are what reduce erosion.

53. (C) Terraces and contour ploughing do not work by evaporating water. They reduce, not increase, the slope's effective steepness, so 'steepening' is wrong. They protect soil rather than remove it. By slowing runoff they let water infiltrate rather than sweep topsoil downhill.

54. (D) Terraces address water erosion on slopes, not wind on a flat plain. Digging deeper does not shield the exposed surface from wind. Leaving the field bare maximises, not minimises, wind erosion. Shelter belts of trees slow the wind and protect the topsoil, the right measure for a windy plain.

55. (A) Nutrients for crops lie in the topsoil, not the bedrock, so bedrock-as-nutrient-store is wrong. Topsoil does not regrow in one season; it takes centuries. Topsoil is essential to farming, not harmless to lose. Its humus-rich, nutrient-bearing nature plus its very slow rebuilding make its loss so serious.

56. (D) Erosion is the physical removal of topsoil, not contamination by chemicals. Weathering breaks rock into soil and does not describe toxic dumping. Plastic and toxins do not form healthy humus or improve fertility. Adding harmful waste that poisons organisms and ruins crops is soil pollution.

57. (A) Mulch does not work by adding weight to the soil. It does not seal out all rain; water still infiltrates through it. It does not convert soil to bedrock. Mulch cushions the surface from rain and wind and conserves moisture, keeping the topsoil from being washed or blown away.

58. (B) A thin dark layer means scarce humus, the opposite of 'too much humus,' and the soil drains fast rather than water-logging. The subsoil is described as sandy and fast-draining, not thick clay holding water. High water-holding capacity would keep crops moist, not dry them out — a contradiction. The real causes are scant humus and rapid drainage pulling water past the roots.

59. (D) Sand does not dissolve in water. Clay does not push water upward against gravity; it merely slows downward flow. Sand has plenty of pore spaces — indeed wide ones — so 'no pore spaces' is false. The genuine reason is the difference in pore size: wide gaps in sand speed flow, narrow gaps in clay slow it.

60. (B) Carrying soil away is erosion, not weathering, so that pairing is swapped. The two are distinct, not identical: one breaks rock, the other transports the result. Weathering acts on rock in place and erosion can move loosened soil, so confining them to soil-only and bedrock-only is wrong. Weathering fragments rock where it lies; erosion then removes the loosened material.
