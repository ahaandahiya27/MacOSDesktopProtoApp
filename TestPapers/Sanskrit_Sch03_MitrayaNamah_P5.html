Worked Solutions

1. (D) नमः, स्वाहा and स्वस्ति are अव्यय — indeclinable particles that never change form. Their dative requirement is a fixed idiom of the language, not the consequence of any action. With a true verb such as 'दा' (to give), the dative marks the सम्प्रदान कारक — the recipient of an actual act of giving; the case is licensed by the verb's action. नमः carries no such action; it is simply the word 'salutation', and convention pins its addressee to the dative. The idea that they are nouns standing in the dative with the next word agreeing is wrong: agreement (in case/number/gender) holds between adjectives and nouns, not between an अव्यय and the word it governs — and नमः is invariable, so 'agreement' is impossible. Calling them imperative verbs of roots meaning bow/offer/bless invents a morphology they do not have; नमः is not a finite verb. The claim that the dative appears only because Sun-names take -आय confuses one example (मित्राय) with the rule: स्वस्ति प्रजाभ्यः and अग्नये स्वाहा govern the dative of words that are not Sun-names at all, proving the government is the particle's, not the noun's.

2. (B) The अ-stem masculine dative singular ending is -आय added directly to the stem: आदित्य + आय = आदित्याय. The इ- and उ-stems do NOT take -आय; their dative singular ends in -ए, and crucially the stem vowel first undergoes गुण-strengthening before the suffix joins: इ strengthens to ए (so रवि → रवे), giving रवये; उ strengthens to ओ (भानु → भानो), giving भानवे. So 'all three add -आय' is false — the इ/उ datives end in -ए, not -आय. The 'silent deletion to shorten' story has no basis; there is no -आय lurking in रवये. The 'lengthen to ई/ऊ then add -आय' claim confuses गुण (a different vowel grade, ए/ओ) with vṛddhi-style lengthening, and invents the non-form रवीआय. And रवि/भानु are bare nominal stems (the nominatives are रविः/भानुः), certainly not already-formed datives. The unifying point: same CASE (dative), same kind of OBJECT (saluted by नमः), but stem-class-specific morphology — गुण then -ए for इ/उ, bare -आय for अ.

3. (D) आचार्या (with final -आ) is a FEMININE आ-stem noun ('female teacher'). The error is double-rooted in one mistaken move: applying the masculine अ-stem dative ending -आय to a feminine आ-stem. The feminine आ-stem forms its dative singular with -ायै, so 'salutation to the female teacher' is आचार्यायै नमः. The claim that आचार्या has no dative and cannot be saluted is false — every noun has a dative; only the ending differs by gender/stem. 'आचार्याय is correct but word order is wrong' accepts the wrong form and invents a word-order rule नमः does not impose (नमः routinely follows its dative, as in मित्राय नमः). 'आचार्या is masculine → accusative आचार्यम्' is doubly wrong: the -आ marks feminine (contrast masculine आचार्यः), and नमः governs the dative, never the accusative. So the only sound mantra is आचार्यायै नमः.

4. (B) एकार्थक means 'having one (the same) referent' — रवि, भानु, आदित्य, सविता, पूषा, मित्र all point to the single Sun. That is referential identity, not poverty of meaning: each name carries its own shade (मित्र = the friendly Sun, सविता = the impeller, पूषा = the nourisher, and so on). Distributing one name per posture therefore layers twelve facets of the one Sun across the year's twelve months — a unified discipline, not pointless repetition, and certainly not twelve different suns. The student's 'use मित्राय for all twelve' flattens this deliberate richness. 'Twelve genuinely different suns' contradicts एकार्थक outright — there is one Sun with many names. 'Antonyms' is the opposite of the truth: synonyms share a referent; antonyms oppose, and saluting the 'opposite deity' each posture would be incoherent. The design's logic is one Sun, many equivalent-yet-flavoured names, one per posture/month.

5. (D) A defining rule of समास is that every member EXCEPT the last drops its vibhakti (case ending). So सूर्य enters the compound bare — no visarga, no -आय, nothing. The single visarga at the very end belongs to the compound treated as one word, and is carried by its final member नमस्कार, which alone inflects for the case of the whole. Hence सूर्यनमस्कारः ('the Sun-Salutation', nominative) — the -ः is the compound's, via नमस्कार. The claim that it is सूर्य's surviving visarga because सूर्य is the 'head' is wrong on two counts: the prior member always loses its ending, and the grammatical case-bearer of this तत्पुरुष-type compound is the LAST member, not सूर्य. 'Both keep visargas but one is silent' invents a phantom: there is no सूर्यःनमस्कारः form. 'A dative -आय reduced to -ः' is incoherent — endings are dropped, not transmuted into a different case marker. One ending, on the last member, for the whole word.

6. (A) The prefix निः (निर्-) is privative — it strips away or negates the following noun: निर् + भय = निर्भय 'fearless', निर् + जल = निर्जल 'waterless'. Applied to रोग ('disease'), नीरोग = 'free from disease' = healthy, which is exactly why it can gloss स्वस्थ ('self-abiding', hence well). It does NOT intensify; 'very-diseased' inverts the prefix's force. The variant 'निः intensifies but रोग means health' compounds two errors (wrong prefix sense AND wrong noun sense) to reach a coincidentally-near answer for the wrong reasons — रोग unambiguously means disease. 'रोग means posture' is simply false; the posture word here is आसन. And 'नीरोग and स्वस्थ are unrelated' contradicts the chapter's own gloss, which equates them precisely because both name health — स्वस्थ from the inner ('self-abiding') side, नीरोग from the negative ('disease-free') side.

7. (C) स्वस्थं शरीरम् names the sound, disease-free body itself — the structural/physical fruit. वीर्यम् (active strength/vigour) and तेजः (shining radiance) are both manifestations of vital ENERGY — one inward-active, one outward-glowing — so they cluster as the energetic tier. प्रज्ञा (wisdom/discernment) is squarely COGNITIVE/mental. This tripartite reading (body, energy, mind) is what makes the list an 'all-round well-being' claim. 'All four are bodily' erases the mind, yet प्रज्ञा is explicitly wisdom and the chapter also speaks of स्वस्थं मनः (a healthy mind). 'स्वस्थं शरीरम् and प्रज्ञा bodily; वीर्यम्/तेजः mental' miscategorises both energy-words as mental and wisdom as bodily — reversed. The last option scrambles the assignments arbitrarily (वीर्यम् is energy not cognition; तेजः is radiance not bodily structure; प्रज्ञा is mind not energy). The coherent map is body / energy×2 / mind.

8. (D) The -आय ending is the dative singular of MASCULINE अ-stems specifically (मित्र, सूर्य, आदित्य, छात्र, देव). The three words here are NOT अ-stems: सवितृ is an ऋ-stem (dative सवित्रे), पूषन् is an न्-stem (dative पूष्णे, with the न् surfacing in the weak/oblique form), and रवि is an इ-stem (dative रवये via गुण). So 'regularising' any of them to -आय produces a non-form. 'रवि → रवाय because all Sun-names use -आय' is the classic over-generalisation the chapter warns against — the इ-stem dative is रवये. 'पूषन् drops न् and becomes अ-stem पूषाय' invents a stem-change that does not occur; the न्-stem dative is पूष्णे. 'सवितृ adds -आय → सवित्राय' confuses the ऋ-stem dative (सवित्रे) with अ-stem morphology. The lesson: same case across all, but the ending is stem-class-bound; -आय is exclusive to masculine अ-stems.

9. (A) A तत्पुरुष compound encodes a case relation between its members internally — here सूर्य stands to नमस्कार in the dative sense 'salutation TO the Sun', which is exactly why the two-word expansion uses सूर्याय (dative). But that internal relation is dissolved into the meaning; it does NOT fix the case of the whole compound. The compound is a single noun that inflects freely: सूर्यनमस्कारः (nominative), सूर्यनमस्कारम् (accusative), सूर्यनमस्कारेण (instrumental), etc., with the ending on the last member नमस्कार. As printed, सूर्यनमस्कारः is nominative. 'Locked permanently in the dative' confuses the internal relation with the external case — they are different layers. 'The relation is genitive' is wrong: नमस्कार/नमः govern the dative ('to'), not the genitive ('of'). 'No internal relation, just side-by-side' would make it a mere द्वन्द्व list, but the sense is plainly 'salutation-to-the-Sun', a governed relation. So: dative INSIDE, free case OUTSIDE.

10. (B) Context and paradigmatic position disambiguate. नमः is offered in worship; मित्राय stands as the first of a fixed series of twelve Sun-names (मित्र, रवि, सूर्य, भानु, आदित्य ...); together these pin मित्र to the masculine Sun-deity 'the friendly one'. The dative मित्राय is itself the masculine (and the deity) form. 'Nothing fixes it' ignores how Sanskrit, like any language, resolves polysemy by context — the worship setting and the Sun-name list leave no real ambiguity. 'Different spelling मीत्र vs मित्र' fabricates an orthographic distinction that does not exist; both senses share the spelling मित्र. '-आय only attaches to deities' is false: छात्राय (to the student), देवाय (to a god), नराय (to a man) all take -आय — the ending marks the dative of masculine अ-stems generally, not divinity. The disambiguator is meaning-in-context, not a special ending or spelling.

11. (B) नमः invariably governs ONE case — the dative. All five saluted words are therefore dative singulars: गुरवे (उ-stem), सूर्याय (अ-stem), आचार्यायै (feminine आ-stem), रवये (इ-stem), पूष्णे (न्-stem). The surface endings differ because dative morphology is stem- and gender-specific, but the CASE is identical. 'Different ending = different case' is the misconception: many distinct ending-shapes realise the same case across stem-classes (just as English plural can be -s, -es, -en, -i). 'Only सूर्याय is dative, rest are vocatives' is wrong — vocatives address ('O Sun!') and would not follow नमः; and नमः demands the dative of each. 'All instrumentals' misreads 'salutation' as an instrument-using action; नमः is an अव्यय taking a dative addressee, never an instrumental. One case, five stem-driven spellings.

12. (A) The design is a tidy bijection: twelve postures, twelve Sun-names, twelve months. Its integrity rests on the CLOSED set of twelve canonical names. A thirteenth posture would have no matching name unless that closed set were arbitrarily enlarged — which the tradition does not warrant. So the strain is structural (a mismatch in cardinality), not magical or legal. 'The Sun would manifest a new name' projects a metaphysical reflex; names are a linguistic/traditional set, they do not spawn to fit exercise routines. 'Thirteen is grammatically forbidden' is invented — Sanskrit forms त्रयोदश ('thirteen') perfectly well; there is no numeral taboo. 'Adding a posture deletes a name' assumes a conservation law that does not exist; nothing forces the name-count down. The real reason: a fixed twelve-element set cannot one-to-one pair with thirteen items without either a leftover or an unjustified expansion.

13. (D) मित्राय is unambiguously the DATIVE singular of the masculine अ-stem मित्र — the recipient of the salutation, governed by नमः. The vocative (address) of मित्र is the bare stem मित्र (optionally हे मित्र), NOT मित्राय. So the pupil errs twice: he misreads the CASE (dative→vocative) and implicitly misforms the vocative (a vocative would never look like -आय). 'मित्राय is vocative if नमः becomes a finite verb' still keeps the false claim that -आय is a vocative ending — it is not; and नमः is an अव्यय, not a finite verb. 'Only the case is wrong; vocative of मित्र is मित्राय' explicitly endorses the false vocative form. 'नमः is the vocative, मित्राय the subject' is incoherent: नमः ('salutation') is not an addressable being, and मित्राय (dative) cannot be a nominative subject. Correct reading: 'salutation to Mitra (the Sun)'.

14. (A) स्वस्थ is an ordinary adjective; it inflects to match its noun in gender, number and case. It looks identical in स्वस्थं शरीरम् and स्वस्थं मनः precisely BECAUSE both nouns are neuter singular — so the agreeing adjectival form is स्वस्थम् in each. With a masculine noun it would be स्वस्थः (e.g. स्वस्थः नरः), with a feminine स्वस्था — proving it is not invariable. Calling it an अव्यय like नमः is the misconception: नमः truly never changes, but स्वस्थ does (स्वस्थः/स्वस्था/स्वस्थम्). 'मनः is masculine' is factually wrong — मनस् is a neuter (s-stem) noun, मनः in the नपुंसकलिङ्ग, which is exactly why agreement gives the same neuter form. 'It is really one compound स्वस्थशरीरमनः' invents a word the chapter does not use and contradicts the two-phrase structure. The truth: same form by neuter agreement, not by being frozen.

15. (B) Links one and two are sound: नमः does govern the dative, and the dative does prototypically answer 'to/for whom' (the सम्प्रदान कारक). The slip is at the final link. The classic सम्प्रदान definition — 'the one whom the agent intends as recipient of a thing GIVEN' (e.g. विप्राय गां ददाति, 'gives a cow to the brahmin') — presupposes a verb of giving. नमः is not such a verb (it is an अव्यय meaning 'salutation') and denotes no transfer of a thing. Its dative object is licensed by IDIOM, a recognised non-कारक or extended use, not by an act of giving. So translating नमः as 'I give to' over-reads the सम्प्रदान machinery. The other options misplace the error: नमः does NOT take the accusative (link one is fine), and the dative answers 'to/for whom', not 'from whom' (that is अपादान/ablative — link two is fine). And the chain is not fully correct: 'I give a salutation to' smuggles in a giving-verb that नमः is not.

16. (C) The question turns on morphology, not sense — all are the Sun. पूषा is the nominative singular of पूषन्, a consonant (न्-)stem with an irregular strong/weak paradigm (oblique पूष्णे, पूष्णः, etc.) — quite unlike the smooth vowel-stem behaviour of the others. मित्रः and अर्कः are masculine अ-stems; भानुः is a masculine उ-stem; these inflect regularly (and सविता is an ऋ-stem, also distinct, but पूषन् is the most irregular consonant-stem of the listed options). So पूषा is the declensional outlier here. 'मित्रः is feminine' is false — मित्र as the Sun-name is masculine (मित्रः). 'भानुः is an indeclinable' is false — it is a fully declinable उ-stem (dative भानवे). 'अर्कः is a compound of two roots' is unfounded — अर्क is a simple noun. Only पूषा carries the irregular न्-stem declension that sets it apart in form.

17. (C) Mastery of WHY नमः takes मित्राय rather than मित्रः requires distinguishing the dative from the other cases. Drilling the full paradigm of a familiar word (छात्र, 'student') sets छात्राय (dative) side by side with छात्रः (nom.), छात्रम् (acc.), छात्रेण (inst.), छात्रात् (abl.), छात्रस्य (gen.) — so the learner isolates -आय as the dative marker and understands that नमः selects THAT case, not the nominative. A single form in isolation would not teach the contrast. 'The mantras use forms of छात्र' is false — the mantras use Sun-names (मित्राय etc.), not छात्र; छात्र is a teaching model. 'छात्र is a Sun-name' is plainly wrong — it means 'student'. 'Sanskrit forbids teaching a case in isolation' invents a non-rule; the reason is pedagogical contrast, not a legal ban. The paradigm makes the dative legible by opposition.

18. (D) स्वाहा is one of the small idiomatic set (with नमः, स्वस्ति) that governs the DATIVE. सोम is a masculine अ-stem, so its dative singular is सोमाय, exactly parallel to मित्र→मित्राय and सूर्य→सूर्याय. Hence 'to Soma, svaha' = सोमाय स्वाहा; the nominative सोमः is wrong because स्वाहा does not take a nominative. 'सोमम् स्वाहा — accusative of the thing offered' misanalyses स्वाहा as a transitive verb taking a कर्म; स्वाहा is an अव्यय governing the dative addressee, not an accusative object. 'सोमः स्वाहा is correct, स्वाहा takes the nominative' repeats the original error — स्वाहा never licenses a nominative addressee. 'सोमेन स्वाहा — instrumental' reads स्वाहा as 'by means of', but the offering is made TO Soma, a dative recipient. The rule mirrors नमः exactly: idiomatic dative government, अ-stem ending -आय.

19. (C) String (i) सूर्याय नमस्कारः is genuinely two independent, separately-inflected words: सूर्याय stands in the dative (overtly marking the 'to the Sun' relation) and नमस्कारः stands in the nominative. String (ii) सूर्यनमस्कारः is a single compound word: सूर्य has DROPPED its case ending (no -आय), the two members have fused, and only the final नमस्कार bears the case of the whole. So they differ both in word-count (two vs one) and in whether the dative relation is overtly marked (it is, in (i); it is internalised and unmarked, in (ii)). 'Just removing the space between the same inflected words' is false — you cannot simply delete a space, because सूर्याय's -आय is gone in the compound. 'The reverse — (i) is the compound' inverts the facts: the spaced, ending-bearing version is the phrase. 'Both are compounds, (i) misspelt' denies that (i) is a perfectly correct two-word phrase. One word vs two; ending dropped vs retained.

20. (D) प्रज्ञा (wisdom), तेजः (radiance) and वीर्यम् (vigour) are inner, cultivated qualities of the person who practises — mental, energetic, vital. धनम् (wealth) is an external, material possession and is NOT listed among the chapter's fruits; including it commits the category error of mixing an outward good with inward excellences. 'तेजः is a material substance (sunlight)' misreads तेजः: in this list it is the person's own radiance/brilliance, an inner quality, not literal sunbeams. 'प्रज्ञा doesn't come from exercise' contradicts the chapter, which explicitly counts प्रज्ञा among the fruits of daily practice. 'वीर्यम् duplicates तेजः' is the synonym-trap the papers repeatedly refute: वीर्यम् is active strength, तेजः is radiance — distinct fruits. Only धनम् is both absent from the list and categorically external.

21. (A) नमः is an अव्यय used as a fixed salutation-formula. With its dative addressee (मित्राय) it forms a complete, idiomatic utterance — 'salutation to Mitra' — needing no copula or action verb. That self-sufficiency is the deep point. One COULD recast it with an action verb (मित्राय नमः करोमि, 'I make salutation to Mitra'), but that introduces a finite verb (करोमि) and its own syntax — so it would not be a no-change addition; it changes the construction. 'Incomplete without अस्ति, means nothing' is false — nominal salutation-formulae are complete without a copula in Sanskrit. 'करोमि required, नमः cannot stand alone' contradicts the very mantra, which stands as नमः + dative. 'नमः is the finite verb' miscategorises the अव्यय as a verb. The right reading: a complete nominal formula by idiom; verbs are optional reformulations, not silent equivalents.

22. (D) नमः governs the dative regardless of gender. The word देवी is a feminine ई-stem; its dative singular is देव्यै. So 'salutation to the goddess' = देव्यै नमः, parallel in case (dative) to मित्राय नमः though the ending differs by stem/gender. 'देवीय नमः' wrongly grafts the masculine अ-stem -आय onto a feminine ई-stem — the over-generalisation the chapter cautions against (compare the feminine आ-stem आचार्या → आचार्यायै, not आचार्याय). 'देव्याः नमः — genitive' substitutes the wrong case; नमः takes the dative, not the genitive (देव्याः would be 'of the goddess'). 'देवीम् नमः — accusative' again gives नमः a कर्म it cannot govern; the saluted one stands in the dative. The feminine ई-stem dative is देव्यै.

23. (A) The reading collapses because it picks the wrong sense of the polysemous मित्र. The chapter places मित्राय नमः as the FIRST of the twelve Sun-salutation mantras; here मित्र is a name of the Sun ('the friend' of all, the friendly Sun), so the mantra means 'salutation to the Sun (as Mitra)', a worship formula, not a maxim about human friendship. The friendship-moral imports the common-noun sense that the worship context excludes. 'नमः does not mean bow, sentence meaningless' is false — नमः does mean salutation/bow; the sentence is meaningful, just about the Sun. 'मित्र means friend, so the moral is correct' is exactly the trap — it ignores the Sun-name reading that the chapter fixes. 'Grammatically wrong, no meaning' is false — मित्राय नमः is impeccably grammatical (dative + नमः). The miss is semantic: friend vs Sun-name.

24. (C) Map each described stage to its word in the stated sequence: 'free of illness' = स्वस्थं शरीरम् (the disease-free body, glossed नीरोगम्); 'physically energetic' = वीर्यम् (active vigour/strength); 'visibly glowing' = तेजः (radiance/brilliance); 'mentally sharp' = प्रज्ञा (wisdom/discernment). The correct order is therefore स्वस्थं शरीरम् → वीर्यम् → तेजः → प्रज्ञा. The first distractor reverses the sequence entirely (mind first, body last). The second misplaces वीर्यम् ahead of bodily health and swaps the last two. The third scrambles all four with radiance first and vigour last. Only the body→vigour→radiance→wisdom ordering matches each clause to the right fruit in the given progression.

25. (C) The chapter teaches a clean, reliable idiom: नमः, स्वस्ति and स्वाहा govern the DATIVE — मित्राय नमः, स्वस्ति प्रजाभ्यः, अग्नये स्वाहा. That is the construction a Class-7 learner should internalise. 'They all govern the genitive' over-generalises wrongly — मित्रस्य नमः is not the taught idiom; the saluted/blessed/offered-to word stands in the dative, not the genitive of possession. 'स्वस्ति always takes the accusative' is simply false; the standard is dative (प्रजाभ्यः). 'All three take the nominative' is the very error the chapter exists to correct (मित्रः नमः is wrong). Dative is the safe, taught rule for all three.

26. (A) Vowel-sandhi needs two adjacent VOWELS. At the सूर्य|नमस्कार seam, सूर्य ends in the vowel अ but नमस्कार begins with the CONSONANT न्, so no vowel coalescence is triggered — the members simply abut and the अ is unchanged. By contrast महा (ending आ) + आत्मन् (beginning आ) puts आ next to आ; like vowels coalesce into one long आ, yielding महात्मन्. So the contrast is: consonant-initial second member (no vowel-sandhi) vs vowel-initial second member (coalescence). 'Both undergo आ + आ → आ' is false — सूर्य's seam has no आ+आ junction at all (the second member starts with न्). 'महात्मन् is wrong, stays महाआत्मन्' denies a basic savarṇa-dīrgha sandhi (आ+आ→आ). 'न् → ण् giving सूर्यणमस्करः' invokes ण-retroflexion, which is not triggered here in this simple junction. The decisive factor is vowel-vs-consonant at the seam.

27. (B) Referential synonymy (both = the Sun) is a fact about MEANING; inflection is a fact about STEM-CLASS. मित्र is a masculine अ-stem: its dative is मित्राय, the stem plus bare -आय. रवि is a masculine इ-stem: its dative is रवये, formed by गुण-strengthening इ→ए then the suffix. Same case (dative, demanded by नमः), different morphological route. 'रवि's dative is रवाय' is the over-generalisation error — इ-stems do not take -आय. 'Synonyms must differ in case' is nonsense: meaning and case are independent; synonyms can share any case. 'रवि borrows मित्राय's form' is impossible — words inflect from their own stems, not by borrowing another word's ending. The lesson: same case, stem-specific formation.

28. (A) Match each fault to the fruit that is its natural opposite. आलस्यम् (laziness, inertia) is most directly overcome by वीर्यम् (active vigour/energy) — energy is the antidote to inertia. भयम् (fear), which dims and shrinks a person, is most directly opposed by तेजः (radiance/boldness) — a bright, bold spirit dispels timidity. So वीर्यम्↔आलस्यम् and तेजः↔भयम्. 'प्रज्ञा counters laziness, body counters fear' misassigns: wisdom is not the prime antidote to inertia, and a sound body is not specifically a fear-remedy. 'तेजः counters laziness, वीर्यम् counters fear' swaps the two correct pairings. 'धनम् ... नमः ...' is doubly invalid — धनम् is not even a fruit of the practice, and नमः is the salutation-word, not a quality that counters fear. The cleanest fit is vigour-vs-laziness, radiance-vs-fear.

29. (B) नमः governs the DATIVE of WHATEVER it salutes — the rule is about case, not about which deity is named. चन्द्रमसे is a correct dative singular of the s-stem चन्द्रमस् ('moon'). So नमः चन्द्रमसे is grammatically impeccable; it is merely off-topic in a chapter about the Sun. 'नमः governs only Sun-names' confuses the chapter's CONTENT (Sun-salutations) with the GRAMMAR rule (dative government, deity-neutral). 'The moon must be nominative चन्द्रमाः' repeats the core nominative error नमः forbids. 'नमः governs the accusative चन्द्रमसम्' misassigns the case — नमः never takes an accusative object. Judge grammar by the rule (dative is correct), judge fit-to-chapter by the topic (Sun, not moon).

30. (B) The fully correct sentence gets every element right: सूर्यनमस्कारः is ONE compound (समास), not two words; it names a TWELVE-posture practice; each posture salutes the ONE Sun under a distinct but equivalent (एकार्थक) NAME; that name stands in the DATIVE after नमः (an अव्यय/indeclinable); and the fruits are sound body, vigour, radiance and wisdom — no wealth. The first distractor errs by calling the compound two words and by inventing 'wealth and twelve different suns'. The second errs on case (नमः does NOT take the nominative मित्रः) and on fruits (धनम् is not listed). The third errs on three points: सूर्यनमस्कारः is a compound NOUN, not a verb; मित्र here is a Sun-name, not a human friend; and there are twelve, not thirteen, postures/months. Only the first option survives every check.

31. (D) नमः governs the dative because a salutation is directed TO/FOR its addressee — the dative encodes that recipient/beneficiary relation, giving 'salutation to Mitra'. The genitive मित्रस्य would express possession ('of Mitra' = Mitra as owner), which is not what a salutation does. English 'salutation of Mitra' is loose and ambiguous and should not steer the Sanskrit case. 'Either case interchangeable' is false — they mean different relations and नमः fixes the dative. 'नमः selects the genitive, so मित्रस्य is proper' inverts the rule; मित्राय is correct, मित्रस्य नमः is not the salutation idiom. 'Genitive impossible to form' is false — मित्रस्य exists; it is simply the wrong RELATION here, not an impossible form. Dative for the addressee, genitive for possession.

32. (B) सविता ('the impeller', the Sun who sets things in motion) and भास्करः ('the light-maker', भास् + कर) are both genuine names of the Sun. गिरिराजः means 'king of mountains' — an epithet of the Himalaya, a mountain, not the Sun; it is the intruder, paralleling the हिमालयः/पर्वतराजः impostor pattern. 'सविता is feminine, so it cannot be a Sun-name' is wrong: सविता is the masculine ऋ-stem nominative (सवितृ), and it IS a Sun-name. 'भास्करः means maker of pots' is false — भास् means 'light', so भास्करः is 'light-maker', i.e. the Sun (the pot word is घटः). 'All three genuine' overlooks गिरिराजः's mountain meaning. The mountain-epithet is the odd one out.

33. (C) The governing rule of (ordinary) समास is that the non-final member drops its vibhakti — the dative -आय is removed, even though the meaning 'salutation TO the moon' is exactly the dative relation. That relation lives in the compound's sense; it is not spelled out by an ending. So the form is चन्द्रनमस्कारः, with bare चन्द्र. 'Compounds keep the first member's dative ending' contradicts the basic rule and reproduces the चन्द्रायनमस्कारः error. 'Use the genitive चन्द्रस्य inside' just substitutes a different retained ending, still violating ending-drop (and the relation is dative, not genitive anyway). 'Keep the nominative visarga चन्द्रः-' likewise illegally retains an ending. All retained-ending forms break the same rule: prior members go bare.

34. (D) स्वस्थं शरीरम् and स्वस्थं मनः share the adjective but apply it to DIFFERENT nouns: शरीरम् is the physical body, मनः is the mind. So the chapter is making a two-domain claim — the practice benefits BOTH the bodily and the cognitive — not repeating one fact. 'Same thing, redundant' denies the body/mind distinction the pairing exists to assert. 'मनः means body too' is false — मनः is specifically the mind (a neuter s-stem), contrasted with शरीरम्. 'स्वस्थम् changes to wealth in one phrase' invents a meaning shift; स्वस्थ means health/self-abiding in both (the wealth word would be धनम्). The deliberate parallel — same adjective, two distinct domains — is the point.

35. (C) Sanskrit marks grammatical roles primarily by CASE ENDINGS, not by position, so word order is comparatively free. In both नमः मित्राय and मित्राय नमः, the dative मित्राय marks the addressee and नमः the salutation — same meaning, 'salutation to Mitra'. The reordering carries at most a stylistic/emphatic nuance, not a change of who salutes whom. 'नमः मित्राय means the salutation belongs to Mitra (possession)' wrongly reads a dative as a genitive. 'Only मित्राय नमः is grammatical' invents an order rule; both orders are fine. 'नमः first makes it the subject' treats नमः as a nominative agent, but नमः is an अव्यय and मित्राय is fixed as dative by its ending regardless of position. Case, not order, fixes the roles here.

36. (A) The correct statement enumerates the stem-class datives accurately: अ-stem masc. → -आय (मित्राय, छात्राय); इ-stem → रवये; उ-stem → गुरवे/भानवे; feminine आ-stem → -ायै (आचार्यायै). 'All masculine nouns use -आय' is false — only अ-stems do; इ/उ stems give रवये/भानवे, not रवाय/भानाय. 'Feminine आ-stems use -आय like masc.' is false — they take -ायै (आचार्यायै), the very contrast that makes आचार्याय wrong for a female teacher. 'इ/उ use -ये/-वे only after Sun-names, else -आय' is incoherent — the ending depends on STEM-CLASS, not on whether the word happens to be a Sun-name; गुरु (a non-Sun word) still gives गुरवे. Only the first option states the stem-bound datives correctly throughout.

37. (B) A द्वन्द्व coordinates members ('X and Y'), e.g. रामलक्ष्मणौ ('Rama and Lakshmana'). सूर्यनमस्कारः is NOT 'the Sun and the salutation' — that misreads the relation. It is a तत्पुरुष (determinative) compound in which the prior member सूर्य stands in a case relation (here the dative sense 'to') to the head नमस्कार: 'salutation TO the Sun'. So one member governs the other; they are not coordinated equals. The द्वन्द्व reading 'lists two things' imports an 'and' that is not there. The बहुव्रीहि reading ('one who has sun-salutations', pointing to an external possessor) does not fit — the word names the practice itself, not a possessor. 'Not a compound but two nouns + unwritten च' both denies the compound and re-imports coordination. The right label: तत्पुरुष with an internal dative relation.

38. (D) Two repairs are needed. (1) Sandhi: महा (ending आ) + आचार्या (beginning आ) brings आ next to आ, which coalesce to a single long आ (savarṇa-dīrgha), giving महाचार्या. (2) Case: नमः governs the dative, and the feminine आ-stem dative singular is -ायै, so the noun must be महाचार्यायै. Hence महाचार्यायै नमः. 'Already correct, no sandhi, nominative ok' leaves both defects. 'महाचार्याय — dative -आय' fixes sandhi but wrongly applies the masculine अ-stem -आय to a feminine आ-stem (it must be -ायै). 'महाआचार्यायै — keep आ + आ separate' fixes the case but denies the obligatory आ+आ→आ coalescence. Only महाचार्यायै नमः corrects both the seam and the case.

39. (D) एकार्थक = 'having one (the same) meaning/referent' — a statement about SEMANTICS (all denote the Sun). It says nothing about morphology. The five are separate words from different stem-classes — रवि (इ-stem), भानु (उ-stem), आदित्य/अर्क/भास्कर (अ-stems) — each declining by its own class's rules (e.g. dative रवये vs भानवे vs आदित्याय). So 'one meaning implies one paradigm' confuses meaning with form. 'Synonyms share one paradigm, all decline like रवि' is the same confusion stated baldly — भानु (उ-stem) does NOT decline like रवि (इ-stem). 'Five case-forms of one word' is contradicted by their being five distinct lexemes, not inflections of one. 'Antonyms' is the opposite of synonyms — they share a meaning, so they are not antonyms. Same referent, different paradigms.

40. (B) Check each: वीर्यम् means vigour/valour, NOT radiance — wrong. तेजः means radiance/brilliance, NOT vigour — wrong. प्रज्ञा means wisdom, NOT wealth (wealth is धनम्, which isn't even a fruit) — wrong. So all three glosses are wrong; the correct set is वीर्यम् = vigour, तेजः = radiance, प्रज्ञा = wisdom. 'Only प्रज्ञा wrong, वीर्यम्=radiance and तेजः=vigour correct' endorses the very वीर्यम्/तेजः swap that is wrong. 'Two wrong, वीर्यम्=vigour right' miscounts — the student's stated gloss for वीर्यम् was 'radiance', which is wrong, so vigour being the true sense does not make the student's gloss right. 'None wrong' is plainly false. Three errors; corrected as vigour/radiance/wisdom.

41. (A) The mantra मित्राय नमः is itself a dative construction, so to make sense of the very first thing the student chants, the dative must be explained right there — and once grasped, it generalises to all twelve Sun-name mantras (रवये, सूर्याय, भानवे ...). Front-loading the grammar at the point of need is the pedagogy. 'Dative cannot be taught after a mantra' invents a sequencing taboo; the reason is need-at-first-use, not a prohibition. 'Only the first mantra uses the dative, others use the nominative' is flatly false — every Sun-name mantra is नमः + dative, never nominative. 'To keep grammar and postures strictly separate' contradicts the chapter's whole method, which INTEGRATES grammar with the postures, and the dative is reused throughout. The dative is taught early because it is needed early.

42. (A) The flaw is inferring CASE from a surface ending alone. While -आय is the dative singular of masculine अ-stems, an identical surface string can surface for other reasons (within compounds, derivations, or other forms), so the shape -आय is not a guaranteed, exclusive dative flag. Case is confirmed by stem-class plus the word's syntactic role (e.g. governed by नमः), not by the ending shape in isolation. 'The pupil is correct, -आय is exclusive everywhere' is exactly the over-generalisation being challenged. 'मित्राय is genitive' is wrong — the masculine अ-stem genitive is -स्य (मित्रस्य); मित्राय is dative. 'No word ever ends in -आय except datives' overstates uniqueness and ignores that the string can recur. The disciplined move: read case from stem + syntax, not from a bare ending.

43. (C) Each salutation is its own नमः + dative formula, so 'to Surya and to Soma' is rendered सूर्याय नमः सोमाय नमः — both सूर्य and सोम are masculine अ-stems, dative सूर्याय/सोमाय, each governed by नमः. 'सूर्यः नमः सोमः नमः' puts the deities in the nominative (the saluter), the core error नमः forbids — the saluted ones must be dative. 'सूर्यसोमाय नमः' compounds the two into one stem with a single dative; that would name a fused 'Surya-Soma' entity and salute it once, not salute each separately as 'to Surya and to Soma'. 'सूर्यम् सोमम् नमः' uses the accusative, which नमः never governs. Two datives, two नमः: सूर्याय नमः सोमाय नमः.

44. (C) The final segment is grammatically loaded, not a typo. आचार्या is a FEMININE आ-stem (female teacher); आचार्यः is a MASCULINE अ-stem (male teacher). Different gender AND different stem-class mean different full paradigms — their datives alone diverge (आचार्यायै vs आचार्याय), their nominatives, accusatives, etc. all differ. So they are two distinct words, not one mis-spelled. 'Final vowel decorative, no grammar' ignores that gender and declension ride on exactly that ending. 'Difference is number (plural vs singular)' is wrong — both are singular; -आ here marks feminine, not plural. 'Difference is case (dative vs nominative of one masculine word)' is wrong — they are two genders of two stems, both shown in the nominative, not two cases of one word. Gender and stem, hence whole paradigms, differ.

45. (A) A समास is a single, lexically integrated word; its members are fused and cannot be pried apart by inserting other material — सूर्यनमस्कारः admits no word between सूर्य and नमस्कार. By contrast, सूर्याय नमः is two independent words in a syntactic relation, and Sanskrit's free word order permits other material to intervene between them. So the claim is correct, and it nicely diagnoses the word/phrase difference. 'Compounds freely allow insertion' is false — that is precisely what fusion forbids. 'सूर्याय नमः is also one word, no insertion' wrongly treats a two-word phrase as a single word. 'You can insert into the compound but not the phrase' inverts the truth. Compound = inseparable single word; phrase = separable two words.

46. (C) Consistency requires each named fault to face its true antidote. आलस्यम् (laziness) is opposed by वीर्यम् (vigour) — energy defeats inertia. भयम् (fear/timidity) is opposed by तेजः (radiant boldness) — brightness dispels timidity. The other two fruits, प्रज्ञा (wisdom) and स्वस्थं शरीरम् (sound body), are positive goods with no single named fault opposite in this pairing. The first distractor pairs wisdom with laziness and vigour with fear — neither is the natural opposite (vigour↔laziness, radiance↔fear are). The second pairs radiance with laziness and body-health with fear — again the wrong antidotes. The third uses धनम् and नमः, neither of which is a fruit/quality that opposes a fault (धनम् isn't a fruit; नमः is the salutation-word). Only the first arrangement opposes each fault with its genuine antidote.

47. (A) The 'offering a salutation to' picture is intuitively close, but it breaks on the grammar: a true verb-driven सम्प्रदान needs (a) a verb of giving and (b) the gift in the ACCUSATIVE. Here नमः is an अव्यय (indeclinable 'salutation'), NOT an accusative noun-gift, and there is no giving-verb. So the dative मित्राय is licensed by the IDIOM of नमः, an extended/non-prototypical dative, not by a सम्प्रदान कारक built from a verb + accusative gift. 'नमः is the accusative gift + unstated verb gives' invents both an accusative status नमः lacks and a verb that isn't there. 'नमः is the verb gives' miscategorises the अव्यय as a finite verb. 'नमः governs the genitive' is wrong — it governs the dative (मित्राय). The honest analysis: idiomatic dative, not a fully verbal सम्प्रदान.

48. (B) नमः governs the dative in any number. For the DUAL of अश्विन् ('the two Ashvins'), the dative dual is अश्विभ्याम्, so 'salutation to the two Ashvins' = अश्विभ्याम् नमः. 'अश्विने नमः — only one at a time' wrongly forces the singular; नमः can salute a dual or plural addressee (compare स्वस्ति प्रजाभ्यः, plural). 'अश्विनौ नमः — dual nominative' commits the nominative error नमः forbids — the addressee must be dative, and the dual dative is अश्विभ्याम्, not the nominative dual अश्विनौ. 'अश्विनाय — -आय' treats an न्-stem dual as a singular अ-stem; wrong stem-class AND wrong number. Dative dual: अश्विभ्याम्.

49. (D) The chapter's genuine claim is integrative: body-movement (postures) and meaningful language (the dative mantras) are cultivated together, so physical health and linguistic/cognitive growth advance side by side, yielding the four fruits. The student's 'one without the other is incomplete' is close but overstated; the refinement keeps the integration without claiming a silent posture is grammatically defective. 'Postures impossible without parsing grammar first' is too strong — you can physically move without parsing, but the chapter's POINT is to do both meaningfully. 'Mantras are decorative, only postures matter' contradicts the chapter, which treats the mantras as meaningful (each a dative salutation). 'Grammar must be in a separate lesson' inverts the chapter's whole method of teaching grammar alongside the practice. Integration, not separation or one-sided priority.

50. (B) Check each against 'नमः + dative, ending by stem-class'. (i) गुरवे is the correct उ-stem dative (गुरु→गुरवे). (ii) देव्यै is the correct feminine ई-stem dative (देवी→देव्यै). (iv) अग्नये is the correct इ-stem dative (अग्नि→अग्नये). (iii) मित्रः is the NOMINATIVE — the very error नमः forbids; it should be the dative मित्राय. So (iii) is the wrong one. 'गुरवे wrong, should be गुरवाय' is false — उ-stems give गुरवे, not -आय. 'देव्यै wrong, should be देवीय' is false — the feminine ई-stem dative is देव्यै, and देवीय wrongly applies masculine -आय. 'अग्नये wrong, should be अग्नाय' is false — the इ-stem dative is अग्नये. Only the nominative मित्रः breaks the rule.

51. (D) सूर्यनमस्कारः already has सूर्य as its first member; the 'salutation-to-the-Sun' relation is built INTO the compound. The whole word is now a NOUN naming the practice — it does not reach out and govern another external dative. So prefixing सूर्याय just repeats the Sun ('to-the-Sun Sun-salutation'), an absurd doubling. The governing behaviour of नमः belongs to the two-word phrase सूर्याय नमः, not to the closed compound. 'No error, सूर्याय सूर्यनमस्कारः is proper' endorses the redundancy. 'Should be nominative सूर्यः सूर्यनमस्कारः' still adds a redundant external Sun-word (and in the wrong case). 'Accusative सूर्यम्' likewise doubles the Sun and misassigns case. The compound is self-contained; do not feed it another Sun-word in any case.

52. (A) नीरोग is a privative word — निः + रोग, literally 'without disease' — and so it can only define health by what is ABSENT. स्वस्थ, स्व + स्थ ('self-abiding'), defines health POSITIVELY: being settled, centred and at ease within oneself. So although the chapter equates them (both = healthy), स्वस्थ carries the extra positive, inner dimension a purely negative term lacks. 'स्वस्थ means standing upright (posture)' over-literalises स्थ; here it is 'abiding/settled in oneself', not literal standing. 'नीरोग is richer, health is just no-disease' is exactly the reductive view स्वस्थ transcends. 'Identical in every nuance' ignores the negative-vs-positive contrast that gives स्वस्थ its depth. Equated in reference, स्वस्थ is richer in connotation.

53. (C) सविता is the nominative of सवितृ, an ऋ-stem (agent-noun, 'the impeller'). ऋ-stems form the dative singular in -ए on a strengthened stem, giving सवित्रे. So 'salutation to Savitri (the Sun)' = सवित्रे नमः; the -आय of सविताय wrongly imposes अ-stem morphology. 'सविताय correct, all Sun-names take -आय' is the over-generalisation the papers repeatedly warn against — the ending depends on stem-class, and सवितृ is not an अ-stem. 'सवितृये — ऋ-stems take इ-stem -ये' grafts इ-stem morphology onto an ऋ-stem; the ऋ-stem dative is सवित्रे. 'सविता is already a dative' is false — सविता is the nominative. The principle: -आय is exclusive to अ-stems; ऋ-stems give -ए (सवित्रे).

54. (C) Audit each clause. 'नमः governs the dative' is right (the core rule). 'मित्र is a Sun-name' is right. 'सूर्यनमस्कारः is one compound' is right (a समास). 'twelve postures' is right (twelve postures = twelve names = twelve months). 'fruits include धनम्' is WRONG — wealth is NOT a fruit; the fruits are the inner-quality set स्वस्थं शरीरम्, वीर्यम्, तेजः, प्रज्ञा. So only the last clause is wrong. 'नमः governs the nominative' is itself the classic error, not a correction — the dative clause is right. 'सूर्यनमस्कारः is two words' contradicts its being a compound — the compound clause is right. 'thirteen postures' is wrong — there are exactly twelve. The single remaining defect is the smuggled-in धनम्.

55. (D) The accusative (कर्म) is the case of the DIRECT OBJECT of a verb's action — it presupposes a verb whose action falls on the object. नमः is not such a verb; it is an अव्यय ('salutation') and performs no verbal action whose object the accusative could mark. So even though salutation 'feels' directed at Mitra, the grammar fixes the addressee in the DATIVE by idiom (मित्राय), not in the accusative (मित्रम्). 'मित्रम् is actually nominative, accusative is मित्राय' mislabels both forms — मित्रम् is the accusative and मित्राय the dative. 'Ordinary nouns after नमः take the accusative' is false — नमः takes the dative of any addressee, Sun-name or not (गुरवे नमः, etc.). 'Both acceptable' is wrong — only the dative is correct. No action-verb, so no accusative object; idiom selects the dative.

56. (B) मित्राय is the masculine अ-stem मित्र (the Sun-name) in the DATIVE (चतुर्थी), SINGULAR, functioning as the addressee governed by the अव्यय नमः. Each feature must hold: gender (masculine, the Sun-deity), stem-class (अ-stem, hence ending -आय), case (dative, demanded by नमः), number (singular, one Sun). 'Neuter, accusative' fails on gender (the Sun-name is masculine) and case (नमः takes dative, not accusative — मित्रम् would be accusative). 'Genitive (possessor)' fails on case — the genitive is मित्रस्य, and salutation is to, not of, Mitra. 'Feminine आ-stem' fails on gender/stem — मित्र is masculine अ-stem; the feminine आ-stem dative would be -ायै (like आचार्यायै), which मित्राय is not. The full correct profile: masc. अ-stem, dative, singular.

57. (C) Datively-inflected forms like अग्नये and मित्राय carry endings that belong to THOSE words — अग्नि (इ-stem) inflects to अग्नये, मित्र (अ-stem) to मित्राय. स्वाहा and नमः are अव्यय: invariable, with no endings to lend; they simply GOVERN, requiring the deity-word to be dative. So nothing is transferred. 'स्वाहा and नमः transfer their endings' is the misconception — invariables have no case endings to give. 'They must agree with the deities in case' is wrong — agreement is for adjectives with nouns; an अव्यय neither inflects nor agrees. 'They are nouns in the dative and the deities copy them' wrongly makes the invariables case-bearing nouns. The deity-word owns its dative ending; the अव्यय only demands the dative.

58. (C) स्वस्ति governs the dative (as in स्वस्ति प्रजाभ्यः). For the PLURAL 'to the gods', the masculine अ-stem dative plural of देव is देवेभ्यः, so 'well-being to the gods' = स्वस्ति देवेभ्यः, exactly parallel to प्रजाभ्यः (dative plural). 'स्वस्ति देवाय — singular' gives the dative but in the wrong NUMBER (singular, 'to one god'); the blessing here is to the gods (plural). 'स्वस्ति देवाः — nominative plural' commits the nominative error स्वस्ति's dative government forbids. 'स्वस्ति देवानाम् — genitive plural' substitutes possession ('of the gods') for the dative recipient. Dative plural: देवेभ्यः.

59. (A) The two are different KINDS of linguistic object. सूर्यनमस्कारः is a noun (a compound) that NAMES the practice — you can talk about 'the सूर्यनमस्कारः'. सूर्याय नमः is a two-word salutation utterance — the ACT of saluting the Sun. Naming-a-thing vs performing-an-act is a categorial difference, not the formal/informal or fast/slow variation of one item. 'Formal and informal spellings of one expression' denies that categorial difference. 'Older vs modern Sanskrit' invents a chronological distinction that does not capture the noun/act contrast. 'Differ only in pronunciation speed, same meaning/category' explicitly (and wrongly) asserts identical category. A name vs an act — that is the sharp line.

60. (B) नमः governs exactly ONE case — the dative — so all five saluted words are dative singulars: मित्राय (अ-stem), रवये (इ-stem), भानवे (उ-stem), सवित्रे (ऋ-stem), पूष्णे (न्-stem). The five different surface endings simply realise that single case across five stem-classes. 'Different case per ending' is the misconception that ending-shape determines case — here one case wears five stem-driven shapes. 'All nominative' is the cardinal error नमः forbids — the saluted ones are dative, and the nominatives (मित्रः, रविः, भानुः, सविता, पूषा) are quite different. 'Only -आय is dative, the rest instrumentals' is false — रवये, भानवे, सवित्रे, पूष्णे are datives, not instrumentals (the instrumentals would be रविणा/भानुना etc.). One case, five stem-class spellings: that is the invariant.
