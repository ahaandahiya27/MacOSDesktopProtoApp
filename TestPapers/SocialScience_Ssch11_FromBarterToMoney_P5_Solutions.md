## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** The rule is that a deal stays barter only while every side is a good and nothing functions as a means of payment. Deals 1 and 4 are pure goods-for-goods, so they qualify. The choice claiming three are barter is wrong because cowrie shells, the moment they are used to settle a balance, are acting as commodity money, which disqualifies Deal 3. The choice claiming only Deal 1 is wrong because barter never required goods to be weighed on a scale; Deal 4's by-eye equal swap is still barter. The choice claiming all four is wrong because Deal 2 used silver coins as change, which is money entering the deal, so it is a purchase, not barter.

**2. (A)** Barter ends the instant anything acts as a means of payment, and that is stage (iii) where salt plays the role of money to balance the swap. Shaking hands at stage (i) adds no money, so it remains barter. Agreeing to meet weekly at stage (ii) is just scheduling; no deferred money payment is promised, so it is still barter. Reselling the cloth at stage (iv) is a separate later act and says nothing about whether the original wheat-for-cloth swap used money.

**3. (A)** The chain shows everyone wanting something other than the blacksmith's tools, which is precisely the failure of a double coincidence of wants. The measure-of-value option is wrong because the problem is not disagreement over a price ratio; no partner is even willing to take tools. The indivisibility option is wrong because nothing in the scenario turns on splitting a good. The store-of-value option is wrong because rusting is never mentioned and is not what blocks the deal; the block is mismatched wants.

**4. (B)** The tailor case is pure mismatched wants: a willing trade exists in goods but the one available partner wants something different, with no spoilage, no ratio dispute, and no splitting issue. The curdling-milk option introduces perishability (poor store of value). The bull-worth option introduces the absence of a common measure of value. The grindstone option introduces indivisibility. Only the first isolates the double-coincidence failure cleanly.

**5. (D)** The deadlock is a pure disagreement over relative worth, exactly what a common measure of value fixes by giving each good one priced figure. The medium-of-exchange option targets mismatched wants, which is not the problem here since both already want to trade. The store-of-value option targets perishability or saving, not a pricing dispute. The standard-of-deferred-payment option targets future-dated debts, but the trade fails now over value, not over timing.

**6. (A)** The block is that a whole ox cannot be reduced to the tiny fraction the deal needs, which is the indivisibility problem cured by money's divisibility. Durability is about lasting over time, not about splitting for a small buy. Wide acceptance solves mismatched wants, not the size mismatch here, since the trouble is not finding a willing partner. Portability is about carrying, which is unrelated to splitting a single large good into a small payment.

**7. (B)** His loss comes from papayas rotting before he can trade, a perishability failure that money cures by being a durable store of value. The medium-of-exchange option misstates that function, which is about being accepted by anyone, not forcing buyers to want a good. The common-measure option is irrelevant and absurdly claims money changes the weight of fruit. The standard-of-deferred-payment option wrongly suggests money slows ripening, which it cannot; the issue is storing worth, not preserving the fruit itself.

**8. (C)** Each good fails on one axis: the ox on divisibility, salt on durability, grain on portability; good money has all three at once, so the correct set is durability, divisibility and portability. The edibility option lists qualities money does not need and that do not solve the listed faults. The acceptance option pairs a real money virtue with two defects (perishability and bulk) that money must avoid. The growth option describes interest-like multiplication, which is not a property money has merely by being held.

**9. (B)** The chapter's order is barter first, then money invented to cure barter's faults, which is the correct rebuttal. The same-day option denies the cause-and-effect sequence the chapter draws. The digital-first option reverses the entire timeline, since digital money is the most recent stage. The money-first option flatly inverts history and wrongly treats barter as a fallback for lost coins.

**10. (D)** The chapter presents money as a solution that arose because barter's defects made trade hard, so the cause is barter's faults and the effect is money's invention. The money-first option reverses cause and effect. The simultaneous option denies the causal chain the chapter builds. The no-faults option contradicts the whole premise, since the chapter lists barter's faults as the reason money was needed.

**11. (D)** Pricing for comparison is the common measure of value; taking notes for goods on the spot is the medium of exchange; locking takings away for later is the store of value, giving that exact order. The second option scrambles the order. The third option also scrambles it, putting store of value first. The fourth option mislabels the pricing act as a standard of deferred payment, which involves a future-dated debt, not a comparison made now.

**12. (A)** A promise to pay a stated sum at a future date is the standard of deferred payment, and because no money changes hands now it is not functioning as a medium of exchange, so the first option is right. The second wrongly calls a future promise a present exchange. The third is self-contradicting: fixing a future date is exactly what makes it a standard of deferred payment, not what rules it out. The fourth wrongly says no price was set, when 7,000 rupees clearly is the agreed sum.

**13. (A)** Lying sealed preserves worth, which is the store-of-value function, and then being handed over for rice is the medium-of-exchange function, giving that order. The second option swaps the two roles. The third and fourth options misapply pricing (common measure) and future-debt (deferred payment) functions, neither of which describes a coin simply resting in a box and then buying goods on the spot.

**14. (B)** A price tag is used to compare worth before buying, which is the common measure of value, not a medium of exchange. Handing coins to the driver, tapping a card to take goods, and scanning a QR code to pay are all payments that move money for goods or services on the spot, which is the medium-of-exchange function. Only the price-tag case is a different function.

**15. (A)** These items were everyday useful goods that doubled as payment, which is exactly commodity money. The fiat option fails because no government decree gave them value; their value came from their usefulness. The legal-tender option fails because no central bank issued or guaranteed grain or cowries. The digital option fails because these were physical goods, not electronic balances moving between accounts.

**16. (B)** Early coins were valued for their genuine metal content, while modern notes are fiat money resting on trust and Reserve Bank backing, which is the true contrast. The first wrong option invents gold thread in the note, a classic precious-paper trap. The second reverses the two cases, mislabelling the early coin as fiat and the note as material-valued. The fourth denies that money has worth at all, contradicting the chapter.

**17. (C)** A minted coin's fixed weight ended the need to weigh, and its certifying stamp ended the need to test purity, curing both troubles at once. The more-valuable-metal option does not address weighing or testing and is not why minting helped. The portability option only touches carrying, not weighing or purity-testing. The edibility option is irrelevant and false, since coins are not food and that would not certify purity.

**18. (D)** The contrast is about wear over time, which is durability, and alloys are chosen because they are harder and survive handling. The divisibility option is wrong because the comparison is about wear, not splitting. The portability option is wrong because alloys are not chosen to reduce weight and the scenario says nothing about carrying. The wide-acceptance option is wrong because acceptance comes from trust and law, not from the metal's hardness.

**19. (A)** The main design or emblem side is the obverse (heads) and the other side is the reverse (tails), so the emblem face is the obverse. The second option reverses these names. The third wrongly says both faces are the obverse and miscalls the rim the reverse. The fourth confuses the edge with a face, which is incorrect.

**20. (B)** 'Rupya' meant wrought or stamped silver, matching India's early silver coins. The printed-paper option is wrong because the rupee began as a metal coin, not paper. The cowrie option confuses commodity money with the coin's name and origin. The quick-response option is an anachronistic pun on QR codes and has nothing to do with the Sanskrit root.

**21. (C)** Reading coins as historical evidence is numismatics. Minting is the making of coins, not their study. Bartering is swapping goods for goods and has nothing to do with examining a hoard. Inflation control is about managing prices and is unrelated to studying ancient coins for history.

**22. (D)** Paper money began in China, and its real advantage was lightness and ease of carrying large sums, improving portability. The India option is wrong on origin and falsely claims the paper is precious. The Rome option is wrong on origin and absurdly claims notes cannot be torn or lost. The Britain option is wrong on origin and repeats the precious-paper trap, since a note's worth comes from trust, not rare paper.

**23. (D)** Many issuers would destroy the uniformity and shared trust a single currency needs, which is why the Reserve Bank of India alone issues notes. The precious-metal option is nonsense and wrongly hands the role to the United Nations. The longer-lasting option misstates the consequence and the preventing body. The notes-into-coins option is incoherent and falsely gives shopkeepers an issuing role.

**24. (C)** Acceptance rests on trust grounded in Reserve Bank backing, which the signature and bank name signal, so the first child is right. The first second-child option falsely claims the colours equal the face value. The second second-child option wrongly bases worth on appearance. The neither option repeats the precious-paper trap by reducing the note to its paper's recycling value, ignoring trust and backing.

**25. (A)** A modern note backed by the Reserve Bank is fiat money, valued by trust and legal-tender status, not by its material. The cowrie shell was commodity money valued as a useful object. The ancient silver coin was valued for its actual metal, not by decree. Cattle handed over in payment is commodity money valued as a useful good. Only the modern note is fiat money.

**26. (C)** A coin spending above its metal worth is the hallmark of fiat money backed by legal-tender status and trust. The decoration option wrongly claims the face value is ignored and the coin trades for its metal, which contradicts everyday use. The hidden-metal option invents extra metal to force a metal-equals-face-value rule that fiat money does not follow. The barter option is wrong because a money purchase, not a goods-for-goods swap, is happening.

**27. (B)** Prices rose from 20 to 25 rupees, which is inflation; 200 rupees bought 10 packs before (200 divided by 20) but only 8 packs now (200 divided by 25), so purchasing power fell. The deflation option is wrong because prices rose, not fell, and the pay buys fewer, not more. The barter option misuses the term, since money, not goods-for-goods swapping, is involved. The unchanged option is the key trap: the same rupee figure hides a real fall in what it buys.

**28. (D)** With prices doubling, the same 1,000 rupees buys about half as much, so inflation has cut its real purchasing power even though the figure is unchanged. The doubling option is wrong because cash does not multiply by itself. The legal-tender option is wrong because notes do not lose legal status merely by being stored. The last option is the trap of confusing the unchanged number with unchanged buying power, which inflation has in fact reduced.

**29. (B)** Banks safeguard deposits and enable electronic payments and transfers, but they do not set a shop's prices or pick its customers, so the first option is right. The printing option is wrong because banks do not print currency and certainly do keep money safe. The minting option is wrong because banks do not mint coins and do allow electronic transfers. The barter option is wrong on both counts, since banks do not swap takings for goods and do permit withdrawals.

**30. (A)** A cheque instructs a bank to move a stated sum between accounts, which both defines it and distinguishes it from handing over physical notes. The new-currency option is wrong because a cheque creates no money; it directs existing rupees. The paper-coin option is wrong because a cheque is not itself spendable currency. The barter option is wrong because rupees, not goods-for-goods swapping, settle the payment.

**31. (A)** A demand draft is prepaid at the bank, so the funds are certain, which a personal cheque cannot promise. The new-currency option is wrong because a draft moves existing rupees and is not a separate money. The barter option is wrong because rupees settle the deal, not a goods-for-goods swap of papers. The extra-rupees option is wrong because neither a draft nor a cheque creates new money.

**32. (D)** A debit card spends the holder's own balance at once (Riya), while a credit card borrows money the bank advances and the holder repays later (Sahil). The swapped option reverses the two. The both-debit option ignores that Sahil's money was advanced by the bank, not drawn from his balance. The both-credit option ignores that Riya's own money left her account immediately.

**33. (C)** A transfer only moves money: 600 minus 250 leaves 350 in A, and 100 plus 250 gives 350 in B, keeping the total at 700. The rise option wrongly claims the bank mints new rupees during a transfer. The fall option wrongly claims money is destroyed in transit. The gold option wrongly claims UPI converts rupees into metal; UPI simply moves the same currency.

**34. (D)** Because cash, card, and UPI all spend the same rupee balance in one account, UPI is plainly a method, not a new currency. The days-of-the-week option is false and would not prove anything about currency anyway. The different-unit option is false, since UPI shows rupees. The never-back-to-cash option is false, because UPI balances are ordinary rupees that can be withdrawn as cash.

**35. (D)** All four are rupee payments differing only in method, so the same 50 rupees moves in each case. The cash-only option wrongly brands cheque, card and UPI as barter, though all use money. The different-currency option is false, since all are rupees. The new-rupees option is false, because moving money by any method creates no fresh currency.

**36. (C)** Trade moved from barter to coins and notes and most recently to electronic transfers, giving that order. The first wrong arrow runs the timeline backwards. The second wrongly places barter after coins. The third wrongly puts electronic transfers before coins and notes, though digital payment is the newest stage.

**37. (D)** The order is barter first, then commodity money like cowries, then minted metal coins, then paper currency. The first wrong option puts commodity money before barter and coins after paper. The second wrongly starts with coins and places barter after them. The fourth reverses the whole sequence, starting with paper and ending with barter.

**38. (C)** The correct summary captures the evolution, the trust-and-RBI basis of modern money, and that digital methods just move rupees. The always-existed option triple-fails: money came after barter, modern money is fiat not metal, and UPI is no new currency. The identical option wrongly equates barter with money and repeats the precious-paper trap. The digital-first option inverts the timeline and falsely values notes by metal weight.

**39. (B)** A torn note still spends because, as R states, its value rests on trust and Reserve Bank backing, not on the paper, so both are true and R explains A. The 'does not explain' option is wrong because R is precisely the reason A holds. The 'R is false' option is wrong because trust and backing genuinely are the source of value. The 'A is false' option is wrong because a worn note really does keep its face value.

**40. (A)** A is true because UPI only moves existing rupees, leaving the total unchanged, but R is false because UPI creates no new money, so the answer is A true, R false. The first two options wrongly accept R as true. The last option wrongly rejects A, though a transfer plainly does not alter the country's total money.

**41. (C)** The coin spends at full value despite cheap metal because, as R says, it is fiat money trusted and made legal tender, so both are true and R explains A. The 'does not explain' option is wrong because R is exactly the reason. The 'R is false' option is wrong because modern coins genuinely are fiat money. The 'A is false' option is wrong because such coins truly do spend above their metal worth.

**42. (B)** A correctly describes the double-coincidence problem in barter, and R correctly states a fact about money, but R is about money's cure, not about why barter has the problem, so R does not explain A. The 'correctly explains' option is wrong because R answers a different question (how money fixes it) than A poses (what barter requires). The 'R is false' and 'A is false' options are wrong because both statements are individually true.

**43. (B)** Since fiat money works only on trust, losing trust would make people refuse the notes and break down money-based trade. The precious-metal option is fantasy; paper does not become metal. The worth-more option is backwards, since lost trust lowers, not raises, acceptance. The barter-impossible option is wrong, because if money failed people would more likely turn back to barter, not find it impossible.

**44. (C)** Not finding a willing partner is the double-coincidence failure; failing to agree a pots-per-blanket ratio is the lack of a common measure of value; a large pot unable to pay for a small needle is indivisibility, giving that exact order. The other options reshuffle these labels or introduce store-of-value and portability defects that none of the three woes actually describes.

**45. (A)** Divisibility is the property that lets money be split for a small buy, curing indivisibility, so that row is right. Durability cures perishability, not disputes over value ratios. Portability cures the burden of carrying, not mismatched wants. Wide acceptance cures mismatched wants, not spoilage. Each wrong row pairs a real money feature with a trouble it does not actually fix.

**46. (D)** P is correct, since barter persists in pockets such as some fairs alongside money. Q is wrong because a note's worth comes from trust and backing, not from any gold in the paper. R is wrong because UPI moves ordinary rupees and is no separate currency. S is wrong because barter came first and money was invented afterwards.

**47. (A)** Money's wide acceptance plus its roles as measure and store of value together overcome barter's central troubles, making it the best reason. The precious-metal option is false, since modern money is fiat and can lose value to inflation. The stop-producing option is absurd, as money still buys real goods that must be made. The same-product option describes the opposite of what money does; it frees traders from needing matched wants.

**48. (D)** The correct fix names real barter faults and grounds a note's value in trust and backing, repairing both errors. The first wrong option repeats both mistakes. The second invents a 'cheap paper' problem barter never had and keeps the precious-paper idea. The third clings to 'barter was perfect' and to a gold-paper notion, fixing neither error.

**49. (C)** Legal-tender status means the coin must be accepted to settle payment, so a shopkeeper cannot lawfully refuse it. The foreign-only option contradicts the meaning, since legal tender works within the country. The precious-metal option is wrong because legal tender is about acceptance by law, not metal content. The turn-it-away option is the opposite of legal tender, which removes the right to refuse it.

**50. (D)** Both wants and price are already agreed, so the only remaining block is that a whole camel cannot be split to match a tiny purchase, which is indivisibility cured by divisibility. The double-coincidence option is ruled out because the wants already match. The common-measure option is ruled out because the rate is already agreed. The store-of-value option is wrong because nothing is spoiling; the issue is splitting.

**51. (A)** A cheque shifts the same rupees between accounts, sparing the buyer from carrying and risking cash, which is the real advantage. The extra-rupees option is false, since a cheque creates no money. The more-valuable option is false, because the rupees keep their value. The barter option is false, because money still settles the deal; no goods-for-goods swap occurs.

**52. (C)** Claim (i) correctly defines barter and claim (ii) correctly contrasts early metal-valued coins with modern fiat money, so both are right. Claim (iii) is false because UPI only moves existing rupees and creates none. Any combination that includes (iii), such as the second, third, or 'all three' options, must be wrong, leaving (i) and (ii) only.

**53. (C)** A common measuring-unit lets every good carry one comparable price, so comparisons are instant, while barter forces a separate ratio for each pair, which is why X is faster. The equal-weight option is false and not how a measure of value works. The same-product option describes the opposite of a healthy market. The preserve-forever option confuses a measure of value with durability, which is a different property.

**54. (B)** The root error is assuming a note's worth lies in its material; it does not, because notes are fiat money valued by trust and backing, so there is no precious material to double. The arithmetic option keeps the false premise and only quibbles over the multiple. The single-note option still treats the paper as precious. The no-error option fully accepts the precious-paper trap the chapter warns against.

**55. (C)** Without banks, the account-based services of safe saving and electronic transfer collapse, which is the direct effect. The farming option is wrong because growing crops does not depend on banks issuing seeds. The walking option is absurd, since paths are not bank-funded. The language option is absurd, since speech does not depend on bank records. Only money-handling services hinge directly on banks.

**56. (C)** Minting gave coins a fixed weight and certifying stamp, ending the weigh-and-test chore, and paper notes, being light, solved the burden of carrying many heavy coins. The first wrong option misassigns weighing to commodity money and ignores that paper came before digital for the weight problem. The second reverses the two stages. The fourth wrongly credits barter and commodity money, neither of which addressed these specific problems.

**57. (B)** Comparing priced tags uses money as a common measure of value, and then paying on the spot uses it as a medium of exchange, in that order. The store-of-value and deferred-payment labels in the first option fit neither act, since nothing is saved and nothing is owed for later. The second option mislabels reading a tag as exchange and paying as storing. The fourth wrongly calls comparison a deferred payment and an on-the-spot payment a measure of value.

**58. **59. (B)** Saving over many years exposes the weakness that goats sicken, age, or die, making them a poor store of value, which is the main worry. The too-portable option misuses portability and is not a real saving risk.

**60. (A)** Money was invented to cure several barter faults together, so abolishing it would revive all of them, which is the strongest objection. The perishable option is wrong, since money does not physically preserve goods; it stores value, not the goods themselves. The never-existed option contradicts the chapter, which says barter came first and even survives today. The unchanged option falsely claims barter and money are identical, ignoring all the faults money fixed.
