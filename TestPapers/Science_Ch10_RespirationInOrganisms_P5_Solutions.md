## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** Respiration takes in oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide. Normally the volume would stay roughly the same because each oxygen used is replaced by a carbon dioxide given off. Here the carbon dioxide is absorbed as fast as it forms, so the volume drops by exactly the oxygen consumed — about 5 units. The claim that carbon dioxide leaked through the walls is wrong: the jar is sealed and the loss is the oxygen, not escaped gas. Nitrogen is not produced by respiration, so the idea of 5 units of nitrogen replacing oxygen is false. The absorbing chemical is chosen to soak up carbon dioxide, not oxygen; it does not remove the oxygen itself.

**2. (A)** While sprinting at 32 per minute for half a minute he takes 32 × 0.5 = 16 breaths. While sitting at 16 per minute for one and a half minutes he takes 16 × 1.5 = 24 breaths. The total is 16 + 24 = 40 breaths. The figure of 32 wrongly counts only the sprint rate as if it lasted a whole minute. The figure of 48 wrongly uses the sprint rate for the full two minutes. The figure of 24 counts only the sitting period and forgets the sprint breaths.

**3. (C)** Each breath uses only some of the oxygen, dropping it from about 21% to about 16%; the leftover oxygen in exhaled air is enough to keep a casualty alive — which is the whole point of mouth-to-mouth. Lungs do not manufacture oxygen, so the idea of enriched air is false. The casualty certainly needs oxygen, not just warmth. Exhaled air is not pure carbon dioxide (it is only about 4%), and cells cannot turn carbon dioxide back into oxygen.

**4. (C)** Anaerobic breakdown releases only a small part of the energy locked in each glucose, because the glucose is not broken down completely. To match cell P's total energy, cell Q must consume many more glucose molecules. Using the same amount would leave Q far short of P's energy. The claim that oxygen-free respiration is more efficient reverses the truth — it is far less efficient per glucose. Lactic acid is a leftover, not a fuel the cell burns instead of glucose.

**5. (B)** Both samples turn limewater milky, so both contain carbon dioxide — room air is not carbon-dioxide-free. The faster a sample milkens, the more carbon dioxide it carries, and X (exhaled) does it in 20 seconds versus Y's 8 minutes, so exhaled air is many times richer. Reaching the same final milkiness does not make the concentrations equal; speed reveals the difference. X clearing first means MORE carbon dioxide, not less. Y also milkens, so it is not mere water vapour and does contain carbon dioxide.

**6. (C)** An adult frog takes in oxygen partly through its moist skin (and partly through lungs); underwater the skin stays wet and gas exchange continues, so it is calm. In a dry box the skin dries out and skin-breathing fails, so it is distressed despite the air. Adult frogs do not rely on gills, so 'only gills' is wrong. The frog does not stop respiring out of water — every cell keeps respiring. Lungs do not manufacture oxygen anywhere.

**7. (D)** Air moves only when a pressure difference exists, flowing from high pressure to low. At the held top the pressures are balanced and nothing moves. Relaxing the breathing muscles shrinks the chest, which squeezes the lung air into a smaller space and raises its pressure above the outside; air then flows out. Relaxing does not enlarge the chest — that is inhalation. The lungs are passive, not muscular fists. On relaxing the diaphragm domes UP, not flattens down; flattening is the active inhalation move.

**8. (C)** The products tell the story: alcohol plus a flame-extinguishing gas (carbon dioxide) are the signature of anaerobic respiration of sugar by yeast. Photosynthesis would release oxygen (which feeds a flame) and require light and carbon dioxide as a raw material — the opposite pattern. The other objections invent rules: there is no gas-holding rule about size, photosynthesis does not make lactic acid (anaerobic muscle respiration does), and photosynthesis is not confined to balloons. The decisive point is the alcohol and the flame-killing gas.

**9. (C)** An earthworm breathes through moist skin using the oxygen held in soil air spaces; flooding drives that air out, so it surfaces to reach oxygen. A fish's gills extract dissolved oxygen from water, so it can stay in the puddle. The worm does not grow gills, and the fish does not switch to skin. Both still need oxygen continuously. Flooding LOWERS the available oxygen for the worm's skin, it does not add 'too much.'

**10. (D)** Respiration never stops in a living plant, day or night. In strong light photosynthesis runs faster than respiration, so there is a net surplus of oxygen (the glowing splint flares). In the dark photosynthesis stops but respiration continues, so the leftover gas is mainly carbon dioxide (it snuffs the flame). The plant does not stop respiring by day — that is the central misconception. Oxygen is not made at night, so 'dissolving away' is wrong, and the plant does not 'breathe out oxygen by night.'

**11. (D)** When a rate rises evenly, the total equals the average rate times the time. The average of 14 and 26 is (14 + 26) ÷ 2 = 20 per minute. Over 10 minutes that is 20 × 10 = 200 breaths. Using only the start rate (14 × 10 = 140) ignores the rise. Using only the end rate (26 × 10 = 260) ignores the slow start. The figure 400 doubles the correct count, perhaps by adding both rates instead of averaging.

**12. (A)** All three milken the limewater, so all three release carbon dioxide — burning and respiration share this. The candle and mouse consume oxygen until it falls too low to sustain a flame or an active animal, while seeds respire slowly and quietly but steadily. Seeds do not absorb the carbon dioxide; they produce it. Seeds release carbon dioxide (from respiration), not oxygen. Visible movement or fire is not required for carbon dioxide production — quiet respiring seeds prove it.

**13. (C)** Exercising muscles respire faster and demand more oxygen, so a bigger fraction of each breath's oxygen is taken up — leaving less (14% rather than 16%) in the exhaled air. Lungs do not leak oxygen through the chest wall. Inhaled air stays at about 21% oxygen regardless of effort, so it does not rise. The body does not manufacture oxygen; it consumes it.

**14. (A)** Holding one breath while working hard makes oxygen run short, so the muscles increasingly respire anaerobically. In human muscle the partial breakdown of glucose without oxygen yields lactic acid, whose build-up causes the burning ache. With abundant oxygen the muscle would respire aerobically and not build lactic acid, but the dive limits oxygen. Photosynthesis does not occur in muscle and does not make glucose for fuel here. Alcohol is the anaerobic product in yeast, not in human muscle — human muscle makes lactic acid.

**15. (B)** The rubber sheet is the diaphragm. Pulling it down enlarges the bottle's space, lowering the pressure, so the balloon (lung) fills — this is inhalation, in which the real diaphragm flattens and moves down. Pulling down does not shrink the space, so it is not exhalation. The diaphragm flattens (not domes up) during inhalation; doming up is exhalation. The rib movement described (up and out) accompanies inhalation, not exhalation, so that pairing is wrong too.

**16. (B)** During hard play oxygen lagged, so muscles respired anaerobically and built up lactic acid. After stopping, the body keeps breathing hard to take in the extra oxygen that breaks the lactic acid down — this 'oxygen debt' is exactly why panting outlasts the effort, confirming the muscles ran short of oxygen. Human muscles make lactic acid, not alcohol, so there is no alcohol to blow away. Resting reduces lactic-acid production, it does not speed it. Panting is about gas exchange, not cooling the lungs.

**17. (A)** Carbon dioxide is made by respiration in a body cell, passes into the blood, is carried to the alveoli, then travels out through bronchi, trachea, nasal cavity, and nostrils. The first wrong path starts at the alveoli and ends with the cell, reversing the direction of release. The second jumbles the order, sending blood after the alveoli. The last option is the inhalation path of oxygen IN, the exact reverse of carbon dioxide leaving.

**18. (A)** Yeast respires aerobically when oxygen is available, breaking sugar down more completely with little alcohol. Sealed off from air, it switches to anaerobic respiration, whose products are alcohol and carbon dioxide — hence the strong smell in jar B. It is not that equal alcohol formed and only evaporation differed; the sealed jar genuinely makes more alcohol because of the oxygen shortage. Air does not turn sugar straight into alcohol. Photosynthesis makes sugar and oxygen, not alcohol, and a seal is not 'darkness' that drives it.

**19. (D)** Exhaled air carries more water vapour than inhaled air; when this warm moist air meets cold air it cools and the vapour condenses into tiny visible droplets — the 'mist.' Carbon dioxide is a colourless gas and is not seen, so it is not the white cloud. Oxygen does not freeze into powder at everyday cold-morning temperatures. Nitrogen likewise does not solidify in ordinary cold air. The mist is condensed water, evidence of water vapour, not of carbon dioxide.

**20. (D)** A cockroach draws air in through openings called spiracles into a branching network of fine tubes (tracheae) that carry oxygen straight to its tissues; a fish uses gills to extract oxygen dissolved in water. Swapping them (gills for the cockroach, spiracles for the fish) reverses the organs. Insects have tracheae, not lungs, and fish do not use moist skin. Lenticels and stomata are plant structures, not animal breathing organs.

**21. (B)** Breathing (moving air) and respiration (releasing energy in cells) are linked but different. Breathing faster delivers more oxygen, letting cell respiration speed up — so the first half is broadly right. But holding the breath does NOT stop respiration: cells keep respiring, carbon dioxide keeps building, and that build-up forces the urge to breathe. So they are not the same process that start and stop together. Faster breathing does affect respiration (it aids it), and holding the breath does not halt cell respiration. Neither does faster breathing slow respiration.

**22. (B)** The control isolates the cause: living peas respire and release carbon dioxide (milky limewater), while killed peas, unable to respire, release none (clear limewater). So the carbon dioxide is a product of living respiration. It is not that dead peas make carbon dioxide that is trapped — they make none, since respiration has ceased. Limewater milkens whenever carbon dioxide is present, not only after boiling. Photosynthesis needs light and would consume carbon dioxide, not release it, so the dark flask rules it out anyway.

**23. (A)** The proportion of oxygen in air is roughly 21% at all altitudes; what changes is the air's thinness — fewer molecules of everything per breath, so fewer oxygen molecules too. To gather enough oxygen the climber breathes more often. So his 'smaller share' idea is wrong; it is fewer total molecules, not a lower percentage. The percentage is not higher up the mountain, and cold is not the cause here. Thin air is not enriched with carbon dioxide replacing oxygen.

**24. (D)** Respiration consumes oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide, water, and energy — oxygen is a reactant on the left. Photosynthesis, the leaf's other process in light, builds glucose and releases oxygen — oxygen is a product on the right. The leaf carries out both; they are distinct reactions, not the same equation. The leaf does not stop respiring just because it photosynthesises. Oxygen is not freely placed on either side of respiration — it is strictly a reactant there. Animals do not run the photosynthesis equation, so side placement is not arbitrary.

**25. (B)** A rate per minute scales the count to 60 seconds. Sitting: 9 breaths in 30 s means 9 × (60 ÷ 30) = 18 per minute. Post-run: 9 breaths in 15 s means 9 × (60 ÷ 15) = 36 per minute. Reporting 9 and 9 ignores the different time windows. The pair 30 and 15 simply repeats the time intervals, not rates. The pair 18 and 18 wrongly assumes both windows were 30 seconds; the post-run window was only 15 seconds, doubling its rate.

**26. (D)** The gas-exchanging sac is an alveolus, and its walls are thin so the two gases can diffuse rapidly between the air inside and the blood outside. A bronchus is an air tube, not a gas-exchange sac, and walls do not 'store' oxygen. Alveoli do not manufacture oxygen — they let existing oxygen cross. The trachea is the windpipe and does not pump blood; that is the heart's job.

**27. (C)** Fractional increase compares the rise to each person's own resting rate. A: rise 24 − 12 = 12 on a base of 12, i.e. 12/12 = 1 (doubled). B: rise 30 − 18 = 12 on a base of 18, i.e. 12/18 = two-thirds. So A's fraction (1) is larger than B's (two-thirds), even though both rose by the same 12 breaths — a higher final rate (B) does not mean a larger fraction. Equal raw rise (12) does not mean equal fraction, because the resting bases differ. And here A's fraction is the larger, not the smaller.

**28. (B)** A living beetle respires, taking in oxygen, which lowers the trapped gas volume and draws the drop inward. Once dead it no longer respires, so no oxygen is consumed and the drop is still — proving the earlier movement was respiration. A dead beetle does not release oxygen. Respirometers work on cold-blooded animals too, and the drop did move for the live beetle. Mere wriggling does not consume oxygen the way respiration does; the controlled comparison pins the cause on respiration.

**29. (C)** Exhalation begins with the muscles relaxing: the diaphragm domes up and ribs move down and in (iii); this shrinks the chest space (iv); the smaller space raises the lung pressure above the outside (ii); air then flows out from high to low pressure (i). The first wrong order starts with the chest shrinking before any muscle change. The second starts with the pressure rising before the cause (the chest shrinking) exists. The last begins with air already flowing out, which is the final effect, not the trigger.

**30. (B)** The leaf respires both day and night, always producing carbon dioxide. In bright light, photosynthesis consumes carbon dioxide as fast as (or faster than) respiration makes it, so the limewater stays clear. In the dark, photosynthesis stops but respiration continues, so carbon dioxide accumulates and milkens the limewater. The leaf does not respire 'only in the dark' — it respires constantly. It does not stop making carbon dioxide in light; the carbon dioxide is just re-used. Milky limewater in the dark signals respiration, not night-time photosynthesis (which does not occur without light).

**31. (A)** Inhalation begins with the diaphragm contracting and flattening (iv); this enlarges the chest space (iii); the larger space lowers the lung pressure below the outside (i); air then rushes in from high to low pressure (ii). The first wrong order starts with air already flowing in, the final effect. The second puts the chest enlarging before the diaphragm acts, reversing cause and effect. The last begins with the pressure drop before its cause (the chest enlarging) has happened.

**32. (D)** A fish does not have lungs; its gills extract oxygen already dissolved in water. It depends on oxygen-bearing water flowing past the gills and suffers when that water runs low on oxygen (its version of 'drowning'). It does not have water-filled lungs. It does not manufacture oxygen from water — it only takes the dissolved oxygen. It does not breathe water into air sacs the way we breathe air into alveoli; the gill mechanism is different.

**33. (A)** Photosynthesis needs 10 units of carbon dioxide; the leaf's own respiration supplies 3 of those internally, so only 10 − 3 = 7 units must be drawn from the outside air. The figure 10 wrongly assumes respiration stops in light — it does not. The figure 13 wrongly adds release to uptake, but the released carbon dioxide is re-used, not vented and re-absorbed. The figure 3 ignores photosynthesis, which dominates in bright light. Net uptake from the air is 7 units.

**34. (A)** A flame needs oxygen; the candle burns briefly in exhaled air, proving some oxygen remains (about 16%), though less than room air's 21%, so it dies sooner. Carbon dioxide does not feed a flame, so it cannot be 'pure carbon dioxide that briefly fed it.' Exhaled air has LESS oxygen than room air, not more, so the candle does not last longer there. A flame cannot persist on 'stored heat' without oxygen — its brief life is the oxygen that is present.

**35. (B)** A 40% rise on 15 is 15 + 0.40 × 15 = 15 + 6 = 21 per minute. Calming to 20% above resting is 15 + 0.20 × 15 = 15 + 3 = 18 per minute. So 21 then 18. The pair 40 and 20 mistakes the percentages for the rates themselves. The pair 21 and 12 wrongly subtracts 20% (12 is 20% below 15, not above). The pair 18 and 21 reverses the two results.

**36. (D)** Anaerobic respiration breaks glucose down only partway, releasing far less energy per glucose than the complete aerobic breakdown — so statement 3 is true. Statement 1 is false: breathing is the mechanical exchange of air, respiration is the chemical release of energy in cells. Statement 2 is false: plants respire continuously, day and night, even while also photosynthesising by day. Hence only statement 3 is true; not 1, not 2, and not all three.

**37. (D)** During the sprint, oxygen ran short and lactic acid accumulated; afterward the body keeps breathing fast to take in the extra oxygen needed to clear that lactic acid (the oxygen debt), so the rate stays high before settling. The lungs are not simply 'lagging' by a fixed two minutes; the duration depends on the debt. Stopping reduces lactic-acid production, it does not increase it. A resting body needs LESS oxygen than a sprinting one — the high rate is for clearing the debt, not for meeting a higher resting demand.

**38. (C)** Correct pairings: fish — gills; cockroach — tracheae through spiracles; earthworm — moist skin; woody stem — lenticels. The first wrong set swaps gills and spiracles between fish and cockroach, gives the earthworm lungs, and puts lenticels on a leaf (leaves use stomata). The second set gives the fish lungs, the cockroach skin, the worm stomata, and a stem gills — all wrong. The third set gives the cockroach lungs, the worm spiracles, and a root alveoli (alveoli are in human lungs, not roots).

**39. (A)** Photosynthesis requires light and releases oxygen; in this dark jar oxygen fell and carbon dioxide rose, which is exactly what respiration does — so the seeds were respiring, not photosynthesising. The candle was lowered in after the night and confirms low oxygen; it did not consume the oxygen beforehand. Living seeds clearly changed the gases, so they were not dead and gases did not change 'by themselves.' Photosynthesis does not happen fastest in the dark — it does not happen at all without light.

**40. (A)** Without oxygen, glucose is broken down only partway to lactic acid, so much of its energy stays locked in that lactic acid — far less energy is released than the complete aerobic breakdown to carbon dioxide and water. Lactic acid is a leftover the body must later clear, not a fuel that releases extra energy. Starting from the same glucose does not equalise the yields, because the degree of breakdown differs. The oxygen-free route is not 'more energy released slowly' — it is genuinely less energy per glucose.

**41. (C)** Cells respire continuously and keep producing carbon dioxide; with no breathing to remove it, carbon dioxide builds up in the blood and triggers the powerful urge to breathe — this happens before energy actually runs out. It is not excess oxygen; oxygen is being used up, not piling up. Lactic acid is made by muscles working anaerobically, not 'directly by the lungs,' and it is not the main breathing trigger here. Human cells do not make alcohol, so that is not the signal.

**42. (B)** Warm water carries less dissolved oxygen, so to meet the same oxygen need the fish must pass more water over its gills — it pumps faster. Slowing down would gather even less oxygen, the opposite of what is needed. A fish does not have lungs to switch to and cannot make air-breathing a permanent change. Warming does not abolish the fish's oxygen need; every cell keeps respiring, so the gills cannot simply stop.

**43. (B)** The trachea is the one shared airway that splits into the two bronchi; block it and neither lung receives air. Block just one bronchus and the other lung is still ventilated, so it is less catastrophic. The trachea is an air passage, not the site of energy release (that is inside the cells). Bronchi carry air, not blood. Neither the trachea nor anything else makes oxygen — oxygen is taken from the air, not manufactured.

**44. (D)** Aerobic respiration converts glucose and oxygen into carbon dioxide and water, releasing energy. The matter does not vanish — it becomes carbon dioxide and water. So glucose does not simply 'disappear into energy.' Glucose is not turned into oxygen; oxygen is a reactant used up, not a product. Energy is released, not a material substance you can bottle like carbon dioxide. The correct picture is reactants becoming products plus released energy.

**45. (C)** Total breaths = rate × time = 20 × 90 = 1800. One-third of these are uphill: 1800 ÷ 3 = 600 breaths. The figure 1800 is the whole ride, not the uphill third. The figure 300 wrongly takes one-sixth (or halves the third). The figure 60 confuses minutes with breaths. The uphill total is 600.

**46. (A)** Earthworms exchange gases through moist skin and rely on oxygen in the air spaces between soil particles. Heavy rain floods those spaces, displacing the air, so oxygen underground drops and the worms surface to breathe — a respiration need, not thirst. They do not have special drinking mouths driving the behaviour, and they do not store rainwater as a breathing supply. Rain does not enrich deep soil with oxygen; it does the opposite by waterlogging it.

**47. (C)** In exhalation the breathing muscles relax: the diaphragm domes up, the ribs fall down and in, the chest volume shrinks, and the trapped air's pressure rises above the outside so air leaves. The first wrong set is the full inhalation picture (flatten, up-and-out, enlarge, pressure falls). The second mixes a doming diaphragm with rising ribs and a falling pressure — contradictory. The last pairs a flattening diaphragm (an inhalation move) with a shrinking chest, which cannot happen together.

**48. (B)** Both rely on yeast's anaerobic respiration of sugar, whose two products are alcohol and carbon dioxide. The brewer keeps the alcohol; the baker uses the carbon dioxide to raise the dough (the alcohol bakes off). Aerobic respiration would not produce alcohol, so it cannot give the brewer's prize. Photosynthesis makes sugar and oxygen, not alcohol, and yeast does not photosynthesise. Boiling does not create alcohol from sugar — the alcohol comes from the yeast's respiration.

**49. (A)** A leaf's released oxygen is a product of photosynthesis, which runs in light. But the leaf respires constantly too, taking in oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide — the same respiration animals do. So plants do not 'do the opposite of respiring'; they respire AND photosynthesise. Plants do respire (not never), and they respire by day as well as night (not only at night). 'Breathing out oxygen' confuses the photosynthetic release of oxygen with respiration's release of carbon dioxide.

**50. (D)** Plants release oxygen only through photosynthesis, which needs light. In the dark the plant cannot photosynthesise, so its constant respiration is all that shows — and respiration consumes oxygen, exactly like the mouse. The plant does not photosynthesise faster in the dark (it cannot at all). Plants DO make oxygen, but only in light, so the expectation would be right in bright light, not in the dark. The controlled chamber points to respiration, not a leak.

**51. (C)** In one hour: 12 × 60 = 720 breaths. In 24 hours: 720 × 24 = 17,280 breaths. The figure 720 is just one hour's total, not a day's. The figure 288 confuses minutes (12 × 24) and forgets the per-minute scaling. The figure 1,440 is the number of minutes in a day, not breaths. The full-day total is about 17,280.

**52. (C)** Lungs are elastic bags, not muscles; they cannot suck. The diaphragm and rib muscles do the work, enlarging the chest so the lung pressure drops below the outside, and air then flows in from high pressure to low. So lungs do not contract like a pump. Temperature differences are not the mechanism. The diaphragm flattens DOWN (it does not push air up from the stomach) — its downward move helps enlarge the chest.

**53. (D)** An adult frog takes in oxygen through its moist skin and its lungs; it does not use gills as an adult. So 'gills like a fish all its life' is wrong. Frogs do not have spiracles or tracheae — those are insect structures. And a land-living frog still needs oxygen, because every cell keeps respiring. The correct adult-frog picture is moist skin plus lungs.

**54. (B)** Both the flame and the grasshopper consume oxygen and release carbon dioxide (both milken limewater). A flame uses oxygen rapidly and dies quickly when it falls; the grasshopper respires more slowly, so the oxygen lasts longer before distress. The grasshopper does not manufacture oxygen. The candle does not release oxygen for the grasshopper — it consumes oxygen. The grasshopper certainly uses oxygen; it is just slower, which is why it outlasts the candle.

**55. (B)** Leaves exchange gases mainly through stomata; woody stems through lenticels; roots take oxygen from the air trapped between soil particles. The first wrong set swaps stomata and lenticels and invents soil gills. The second uses spiracles (insect) and alveoli (human lung) for plant parts. The third puts alveoli on a stem and lenticels on a root, mismatching the structures. Only stomata–lenticels–soil-air is correct.

**56. (A)** Rate per minute = 24 × (60 ÷ 48) = 24 × 1.25 = 30 per minute, which sits above the 15–18 resting range. Reporting 24 forgets to scale the 48-second window to a full minute. Reporting 12 wrongly halves instead of scaling up. Reporting 48 confuses the time interval (48 seconds) with a breath count. The correct rate is 30 per minute — above resting.

**57. (D)** The pupil's equation builds glucose and oxygen while using carbon dioxide, water, and energy — that is essentially photosynthesis, the reverse of respiration. Respiration is glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy, with energy released as a product on the right. Energy is not a reactant 'needed to start' on the left in this context — it is released. Water is a genuine product of aerobic respiration, so it should not be removed. Aerobic respiration uses glucose, not alcohol (alcohol is the anaerobic yeast product).

**58. (A)** After jogging the cells are respiring faster and producing carbon dioxide more quickly; with the breath held, carbon dioxide rises in the blood sooner and triggers the urge to breathe earlier — hence the shorter hold. It is not extra oxygen forcing exhalation; oxygen is being used up, and carbon dioxide is the trigger. Jogging speeds respiration, it does not stop it. The trigger is rising carbon dioxide, not blood temperature.

**59. (C)** Oxygen falls from 21% to 16% (about 5% removed) while carbon dioxide rises from 0.04% to 4% (about 4% added); these are roughly balanced, so the overall air volume barely changes — the percentages describe composition, not a growing volume. The body does remove oxygen (about 5%), so 'removed no oxygen' is false. It does remove oxygen as it adds carbon dioxide, so 'removed nothing' is wrong. Carbon dioxide rises in exhaled air, so it is certainly not all removed.

**60. (B)** Both snails respire and release carbon dioxide. In jar 1 the lit weed photosynthesises, consuming that carbon dioxide as fast as it forms, so the limewater barely milkens. In jar 2 there is no photosynthesis (dark, no plant), so carbon dioxide builds up and milkens the paper strongly. The snail does not stop respiring just because oxygen is available. Snails do not photosynthesise. A photosynthesising weed REMOVES carbon dioxide, it does not add an offsetting amount.
