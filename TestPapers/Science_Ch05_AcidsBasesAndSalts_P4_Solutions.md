## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** Blue litmus turns red only in an acid. Of the four, only lemon juice (citric acid) is an acid, so it is the single liquid that turns blue litmus red. Lime water and soap solution are both bases, which leave blue litmus unchanged (they turn red litmus blue instead). Sugar water is neutral and leaves blue litmus unchanged. Since exactly one strip turned red, that liquid is the acid, i.e. lemon juice.

**2. (C)** Turning blue litmus red is an acid property, so removing every such substance removes all the acids. Curd (lactic acid) and vinegar (acetic acid) are acids, so they are removed. Milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) and lime water are bases, which do not turn blue litmus red, so both remain. Keeping only milk of magnesia would wrongly discard the equally basic lime water; keeping vinegar would keep an acid.

**3. (A)** Tomato juice is an acid and milk of magnesia is a base. Mixed in exactly neutralising amounts they cancel, leaving the mixture neutral, so red litmus shows no change (it is already red, and only a base would turn it blue). Adding one extra drop of tomato juice tips the mixture slightly acidic, so it now turns blue litmus red. It would not turn red litmus blue (that needs excess base), and the neutral state is not permanent once more acid is added.

**4. (A)** Leaving both litmus papers unchanged is exactly the result of any neutral liquid. Distilled water, common salt water and sugar water are all neutral, so all three leave red and blue litmus unchanged and give identical results. The test therefore establishes neutrality, not identity; it cannot single out the distilled water. Salt water does not turn blue litmus red and sugar water does not turn red litmus blue (both are neutral, not acidic or basic), and dissolved solids do not make these solutions acidic.

**5. (C)** Turmeric turns red only in a base and stays yellow in acids and in neutral liquids. Soap solution is basic, so the paper turns red. Lemon juice is acidic, so the paper stays yellow. Plain water is neutral, so the paper stays yellow. The order is therefore red, yellow, yellow. Reversing red to the acid (lemon) misremembers turmeric's direction; turmeric does not turn red in acids or in water.

**6. (C)** China rose shows dark pink (magenta) in acids and green in bases. Vinegar is acidic (acetic acid), so it turns china rose magenta. Soap solution is basic, so it turns china rose green. Swapping the two reverses china rose's direction. China rose is not a single fixed colour, and it is not colourless like phenolphthalein in acid; it goes magenta there.

**7. (C)** Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a base. Lime water and milk of magnesia are bases, so both turn it pink, giving a count of 2. Tamarind water is acidic (tartaric acid) and sugar solution is neutral, so both leave phenolphthalein colourless. Counting 1 forgets that both lime water and milk of magnesia are bases; counting 3 wrongly includes an acid or neutral.

**8. (A)** Methyl orange is yellow in bases and red in acids. Lime water is a base, so methyl orange is yellow in it; dilute hydrochloric acid is an acid, so methyl orange is red in it. The order is yellow then red. Reversing them confuses the directions; methyl orange shows neither pink nor colourless (those are phenolphthalein behaviours), and the acid would not also be yellow.

**9. (D)** Bitter taste is a signature of a base no matter how strong or weak it is, and milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) is a base. A mild bitterness simply means a weakly basic taste, not an acid; the intensity of a taste does not switch the chemical family. An acid would taste sour, not bitter, so mild bitterness cannot point to an acid, and a neutral liquid has no bitter taste at all.

**10. (D)** Neutralisation gives a salt plus water, and the salt depends on which acid and base reacted. Here the acid (hydrochloric acid) is the same both times, but the bases differ: sodium hydroxide gives sodium chloride, while potassium hydroxide gives potassium chloride. So the two solids are different salts. They are not both common salt or both potassium chloride, none of the base remains after exact neutralisation, and matter is not destroyed, so a solid salt does form each time.

**11. (D)** Phenolphthalein is pink only in a base and colourless otherwise. While the washing soda solution was basic the indicator was pink; when the pink vanished the base had been neutralised and the solution was about neutral. Adding more vinegar (acid) makes the solution acidic, which is also colourless for phenolphthalein, so it stays colourless. Adding more liquid does not bring back pink, excess acid never turns phenolphthalein pink, and phenolphthalein has no deep-red colour.

**12. (B)** Turmeric turns red only in a base and stays yellow in acids and neutral liquids. P turns red litmus blue, so P is a base and turns turmeric red. Q turns blue litmus red, so Q is an acid and leaves turmeric yellow. R changes neither litmus, so R is neutral and leaves turmeric yellow. S turns blue litmus red, so S is an acid and leaves turmeric yellow. Hence three of the four (Q, R and S) stay yellow, and only P (the base) turns red.

**13. (C)** A salt is named from the metal of the base combined with the acid part of the acid, exactly as the worked example shows (sodium from the base + sulphate from the acid → sodium sulphate). Applying the same rule, hydrochloric acid (acid part: chloride) with potassium hydroxide (metal: potassium) gives potassium chloride. It is not sodium chloride, since no sodium is present — chloride does not 'always' pair with sodium — and the acid is not regenerated by neutralisation.

**14. (C)** Baking soda is a base; lemon juice is an acid, so they undergo a neutralisation that releases carbon dioxide gas, seen as fizzing. Sugar water is neutral, so there is no acid for the baking soda to react with and little gas is produced. Lemon juice is the acid (not a base) and sugar water is neutral (not an acid). The bubbles are a gas produced by a real reaction, not trapped air, which is why they appear strongly only with the acidic lemon juice.

**15. (C)** Each sting is relieved by neutralising it with the opposite family. Stinging nettle injects an acid, so a mild base — baking soda paste — neutralises it. The wasp sting is basic, so a mild acid such as vinegar neutralises it. Using baking soda on both would worsen the basic wasp sting, and lemon juice (an acid) is useless on the acidic nettle sting. Only baking soda for the nettle and vinegar for the wasp pairs each sting with the opposite family.

**16. (B)** Phenolphthalein is a synthetic, lab-made indicator, unlike the three named natural ones. Litmus comes from lichens, turmeric from the turmeric root and china rose from hibiscus flowers, so all three are obtained from plants or living sources. Phenolphthalein is not extracted from red cabbage or tree bark (those would be natural sources) and is not a natural indicator; it is manufactured chemically.

**17. (A)** Quicklime is a base. Added to acidic soil it neutralises the excess acid and improves the field, which is why the first crop improves. Spread on a field that is already basic, it adds more base and makes the soil even more basic, worsening it. Lime is not neutral, so it does not balance any soil; it is not an acid, so it does not lower basicity; and it is not an inert salt, since it clearly changes acidic soil.

**18. (D)** Phenolphthalein is colourless in acids and turns pink only in a base. Starting colourless, the beaker held an acid. Adding sodium hydroxide neutralises that acid, and the moment the liquid turns pink shows enough base has been added to pass the neutral point and make the mixture basic. So the beaker is now basic, not still acidic. Pink does not mark the neutral point (a neutral mixture leaves phenolphthalein colourless), and adding a base neutralises an acid rather than strengthening it.

**19. (D)** Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a base, and a base also turns red litmus blue. A liquid cannot be basic for phenolphthalein yet neutral for litmus at the same time, so the two results contradict each other and the report is inconsistent. A neutral substance leaves phenolphthalein colourless, an acid leaves phenolphthalein colourless too (pink means base, not acid), and salt water is neutral, so none of those explain a genuine pink-with-no-litmus-change result.

**20. (C)** Methyl orange is yellow in bases and red in acids. Solution X turns it yellow, so X is a base. Solution Y turns it red, so Y is an acid. Solution Z changes neither litmus, so Z is neutral. Hence base, acid, neutral. The other orders mislabel the methyl-orange-yellow solution as an acid or treat the methyl-orange-red solution as something other than an acid.

**21. (D)** Acidity is too much hydrochloric acid in the stomach. Magnesium hydroxide is a mild base, and a base neutralises an acid to give a salt and water, removing the excess acid and easing the burning. A base does not make the stomach more acidic; that would worsen the problem. The relief is chemical neutralisation, not a coating that merely hides the acid, and an antacid does not strengthen the acid.

**22. (A)** Red litmus turning blue marks a base. In a base, blue litmus shows no change (it is already blue and only an acid turns it red), phenolphthalein turns pink (it is pink only in a base), and turmeric turns red (it turns red only in a base). So the pattern is blue litmus unchanged, phenolphthalein pink, turmeric red. The other options apply acid behaviours (blue litmus to red, phenolphthalein colourless, turmeric yellow) to what is actually a base.

**23. (D)** Dissolved carbon dioxide makes carbonic acid, so soda water is mildly acidic. An acid turns blue litmus red, and methyl orange is red in acids, so both of those happen. The drink is not basic, so methyl orange would not turn yellow and blue litmus would not stay blue. It is not neutral, so litmus does change. Only 'blue litmus red and methyl orange red' fits an acid on both counts.

**24. (B)** City air carries more sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, which dissolve in rain to form acids, so city rain is more acidic. Marble is calcium carbonate, which behaves as a base, so the acidic rain reacts with and gradually eats away the basic marble — faster in polluted city air than in clean countryside air. The rain is not basic and the marble is not acidic, so the reversed chain is wrong; the rain is acidic, not neutral, and the marble does not react 'with itself'.

**25. (B)** Olfactory indicators such as onion and vanilla keep their smell in an acid but lose it in a basic solution. So a cloth that loses its oniony smell when dipped in a liquid has been dipped in a base. An acid would leave the onion smell intact, and pure water or common salt water are neutral, where the smell would also remain. The disappearance of the smell is the test result that marks a base.

**26. (B)** China rose is magenta in acids and green in bases, so cup A (magenta) is an acid and cup B (green) is a base. Methyl orange is red in acids and yellow in bases, so it turns red in A (acid) and yellow in B (base). Reversing this would treat magenta as basic, which is wrong. Both red would need both to be acids; both yellow would need both to be bases, but A is an acid.

**27. (C)** While pink, the soap solution was basic. The pink vanishing marks the point where the vinegar (acid) has neutralised the base, leaving the mixture about neutral. Continuing to add vinegar makes the mixture acidic, so blue litmus now turns red. The mixture is not locked at neutral once more acid is added; the soap solution has been used up so it is no longer basic; and litmus has no green result.

**28. (B)** Marble is calcium carbonate, which reacts with an acid to release carbon dioxide gas, seen as bubbling. The hydrochloric-acid beaker therefore bubbles. Lime water is basic, and a carbonate does not release gas with a base in this way, so the lime-water beaker does not bubble. Bubbling in both, or none at all, ignores that the reaction needs an acid. Marble is not unreactive towards acids.

**29. (D)** Once litmus shows the acidic waste has reached neutral, the acid has been used up, and the water is safe. Adding excess slaked lime (a base) would push the water past neutral into basic, which is itself harmful to river life, so the colleague's advice is wrong. Adding acid again or adding common salt does not improve safety either; the correct move is to stop at neutral. 'More base is always safer' is the trap, since over-treating swaps one harm for another.

**30. (A)** Methyl orange is red only in an acid, so a liquid that turns it red is an acid. In an acid, blue litmus turns red and phenolphthalein stays colourless (it is pink only in a base). So the liquid is an acid with blue litmus red and phenolphthalein colourless. The base options (blue litmus unchanged with phenolphthalein pink) and the neutral option contradict the methyl-orange-red result, which already fixes the liquid as an acid.

**31. (A)** Bases turn red litmus blue and feel soapy. Soap solution and ammonia solution are bases, giving a count of 2. Vinegar (acetic acid) and lemon juice (citric acid) are acids, and sugar water is neutral, so none of those three is a base. A count of 1 misses one base, while 3 or 4 wrongly counts an acid or the neutral sugar water as basic.

**32. (C)** Red litmus turning blue is a base property, and methyl orange turning yellow is also a base property (it is yellow in bases, red in acids). The two results agree, so the liquid is a base. Methyl orange changing colour does not by itself mean acid — its yellow specifically marks a base. A neutral substance would not turn red litmus blue, so two agreeing base results rule out neutral, and tasting is unnecessary and unsafe.

**33. (D)** Lemon juice (citric acid) is the acid. Ammonia solution and washing soda solution are both bases. Sugar water is neutral. So acids = lemon juice, bases = ammonia solution and washing soda, neutral = sugar water. Sugar water is not an acid, ammonia is not the lone base, and washing soda is not neutral, so the other groupings misplace at least one substance.

**34. (D)** A bee sting is acidic and is relieved by neutralising it with the opposite family — a mild base such as baking soda paste. Vinegar is an acid, so rubbing it on an acidic sting adds the same family and does not neutralise it. Vinegar is not a base, the bee sting is acidic (not basic), and being 'sharp' or 'medicine-like' has nothing to do with neutralising power. The correct remedy is a mild base.

**35. (D)** Phenolphthalein is pink in bases and colourless in both acids and neutral solutions. So a colourless result only rules out a base; it cannot by itself tell an acid from a neutral substance. A second test, such as blue litmus, is needed: if it turns red the solution is an acid, otherwise neutral. Colourless does not 'always' mean neutral, base or acid, since it covers two of the three families at once.

**36. (B)** Litmus separates pure water from a weak acid in one step: water leaves both papers unchanged (it is neutral), while even a weak acid turns blue litmus red. Phenolphthalein is useless here because water leaves it colourless, not pink, so option A's premise is false. Turmeric stays yellow in water (it does not turn red), and tasting is unsafe and wrong — water is not sweet and the acid is sour, not salty. Only the litmus test reliably tells them apart.

**37. (B)** Ammonia solution turns red litmus blue, so it is a base. Adding vinegar (an acid) neutralises it, and when neither litmus changes the mixture has reached neutral, where the acid and base have cancelled. It is not still basic, since red litmus would otherwise still turn blue. It is not yet acidic, because that needs excess acid (blue litmus would then turn red). Neutralisation does not create a stronger base.

**38. (A)** Sour taste and turning blue litmus red are signatures of an acid, and lemon juice is acidic whether concentrated or diluted. Diluting it with water lowers how strongly acidic it is but does not change its family, so both samples still turn blue litmus red. Dilution does not make an acid stop changing litmus, keep litmus blue, or become neutral; it only weakens the acidity, leaving the acid still an acid.

**39. (B)** Red litmus turning blue shows washing soda solution is a base. A base turns turmeric red and turns phenolphthalein pink. So turmeric is red and phenolphthalein is pink. Turmeric staying yellow is the acid/neutral result, and phenolphthalein staying colourless is the acid/neutral result, so options pairing those with a base are wrong. Only 'turmeric red and phenolphthalein pink' matches a base on both counts.

**40. (B)** Acid rain leaves acidic residue on the stone, and a base neutralises an acid, so a dilute basic wash can neutralise that residue and slow further attack on the calcium-carbonate wall. A dilute acid wash would add more acid and worsen the corrosion, so it is unsound. Limestone is a base (calcium carbonate), not acidic, so it is certainly affected by acid rain, and the two washes are not equivalent — only the basic one neutralises the acid.

**41. (C)** Leaving blue litmus blue (no change) plus turning phenolphthalein pink are both base behaviours; only a base does both. Soap solution is the base, so it fits. Orange juice is acidic (citric acid): it turns blue litmus red and leaves phenolphthalein colourless. Sugar water is neutral: blue litmus stays blue but phenolphthalein stays colourless, so it fails the phenolphthalein test. Hence soap solution is the only match.

**42. (B)** Lime water is a base. Adding dilute hydrochloric acid neutralises it, and when neither litmus changes the mixture has reached neutral. Adding a few more drops of acid then puts acid in excess, making the mixture slightly acidic. It is not still exactly neutral once extra acid is added; lime water has been used up so the mixture is not basic; and an acid certainly does affect a neutral mixture by making it acidic.

**43. (D)** Neutralisation always gives a salt and water, but the salt depends on the specific acid and base. Sulphuric acid with potassium hydroxide gives potassium sulphate, not common salt (sodium chloride), because no sodium or chlorine is involved. So labelling it 'common salt' is wrong. No salt-free outcome occurs, the sulphuric acid is not regenerated, and the salt formed is named from this particular acid-base pair: potassium sulphate.

**44. (A)** Turmeric turns red only in a base and stays yellow in both acids and neutral liquids. So a yellow turmeric result could mean either an acid or a neutral substance, and it cannot separate the two; a second indicator such as blue litmus is needed (it turns red in an acid, unchanged in a neutral). Turmeric does not turn red in acids or neutrals, does not give the same colour in acids and bases, and is not limited to changing only in neutral liquids.

**45. (A)** Phenolphthalein and methyl orange are the synthetic, lab-made indicators; litmus, turmeric and china rose are natural. So methyl orange (synthetic) paired with china rose (natural) is one of each. Litmus is natural, not synthetic, and china rose is natural, not synthetic, so the pairings that call litmus or china rose 'synthetic' are wrong, and calling phenolphthalein 'natural' reverses its true kind.

**46. (A)** Neutralisation of an acid by a base releases heat, so the beaker warms up as the reaction proceeds; nothing is boiling away. Acid boiling away would, if anything, need heat put in and would not be caused by the reaction. A base does not freeze the mixture, and the salt being formed stays dissolved rather than boiling off. The warmth is simply the reaction's released heat.

**47. (C)** All four choices are bases, but the question asks for the cheap, bulk option actually used to treat acidic water. Slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) is exactly that — inexpensive and spread in large quantities on acidic land and water. Sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) is corrosive and costly, ammonia solution is not used to treat lakes, and milk of magnesia is a medicinal preparation, not a bulk treatment. So slaked lime fits both 'a base' and 'cheap and commonly used'.

**48. (A)** China rose shows dark pink (magenta) only in an acid. X turns blue litmus red, so X is an acid and turns china rose magenta. Y turns red litmus blue, so Y is a base, and china rose is green (not magenta) in it. Z changes neither litmus, so Z is neutral and leaves china rose its ordinary colour. Hence only X turns china rose magenta; Y, being a base, turns it green instead.

**49. (C)** Turning blue litmus red and turning methyl orange red are both acid behaviours, and leaving phenolphthalein colourless is consistent with an acid (it is pink only in a base). All the results agree on acidity, so the liquid is an acid. Methyl orange turning red is itself the acid result, not a base sign. Colourless phenolphthalein alone could be neutral, but the two acid results rule that out, so the findings are consistent, not in conflict.

**50. (B)** China rose is green only in a base, so the solution is a base, and a base turns red litmus blue — that single prediction is safe. The other predictions are acid behaviours: only an acid tastes sour, only an acid turns blue litmus red, and a base turns phenolphthalein pink (not colourless), so 'leaves phenolphthalein colourless' would be wrong for a base. Hence the one correct prediction is that it turns red litmus blue.

**51. (D)** Brushing the teeth with toothpaste, a mild base, neutralises the acid made by bacteria in the mouth, exactly matching acid + base → salt + water. Dissolving sugar is mere mixing, not an acid-base reaction. Ice melting is a physical change of state, not a chemical neutralisation. An apple browning is a reaction with air, not between an acid and a base. Only the toothpaste example is a genuine neutralisation.

**52. (C)** Exact neutralisation uses up all the acid (the lemon juice) and all the base (the baking soda), leaving a salt dissolved in the water formed. Boiling off the water leaves that solid salt behind, not the original lemon juice — no acid remains after exact neutralisation. The baking soda is also fully used up, and matter is not destroyed, so a solid does form. The correct answer is the salt.

**53. (B)** Common salt (sodium chloride) is neutral, not a base, so 'common salt — base' is the wrong pairing. Lemon juice (citric acid) is correctly an acid, vinegar (acetic acid) is correctly an acid, milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) is correctly a base, and soap solution is correctly a base. So the single error is calling neutral common salt a base.

**54. (A)** Methyl orange is red only in an acid, so a red methyl-orange result agrees with blue litmus turning red and confirms the liquid is an acid. Phenolphthalein turning pink would indicate a base, contradicting the acid finding. Turmeric turning red also indicates a base, again contradicting it. Red litmus turning blue is a base behaviour too. Only methyl orange turning red is a second, independent acid sign.

**55. (A)** Changing neither litmus paper proves a liquid is neutral, but water is only one of many neutral liquids; salt water, sugar water and similar solutions give the same result. So the test cannot single out pure water, let alone prove it is chemically pure. Water does not turn blue litmus red (it is neutral). Acids and bases do change litmus, so they are not the ones leaving it unchanged. Litmus works fine on colourless liquids, so that objection is false.

**56. (D)** In acid + base → salt + water, the water is genuinely a product of the reaction, formed when the acid and base combine, along with the salt. It is not merely the solvent water that was already present; the equation states water as a product. Leftover hydrochloric acid would be excess acid, not water. And water is always one of the two products, so it is wrong to say only salt forms.

**57. (C)** Blue litmus turning red is the test for an acid. Vinegar (acetic acid) is an acid, so it fits. Soap solution and milk of magnesia are bases, which turn red litmus blue, not blue litmus red. Distilled water is neutral and changes neither litmus paper. So vinegar is the only acidic liquid among the four that turns blue litmus red.

**58. (D)** Blue litmus turning red is the test for an acid. Lemon juice and vinegar turn blue litmus red, so two are acids. Soap solution is a base and leaves blue litmus unchanged; lime water is also a base and leaves blue litmus unchanged. So exactly 2 of the four are acids. A count of 1 misses one acid, while 3 or 4 wrongly counts a base as acidic.

**59. (B)** China rose is magenta in acids and green in bases. Cup P (magenta) is therefore the acid, and cup Q (green) is the base. To neutralise the base in cup Q you add the acid, which is cup P, so adding P slowly to Q neutralises it. Saying magenta marks a base reverses china rose's direction; P is not neutral (it would then merely dilute Q), and china rose certainly distinguishes acids from bases, since it shows two different colours.

**60. (B)** Phenolphthalein is colourless across the whole neutral range and the whole acidic range, turning pink only once a solution is basic. So at an exact neutral endpoint it gives no distinctive colour and cannot flag that point precisely. A better confirmation that neither acid nor base is in excess is that both litmus papers stay unchanged — blue stays blue and red stays red. Turmeric red would actually signal excess base, and the other options misstate how phenolphthalein behaves.
