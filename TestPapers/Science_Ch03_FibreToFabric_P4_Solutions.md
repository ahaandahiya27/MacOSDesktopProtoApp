## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** Protein fibres (wool, silk) contain nitrogen and char with a burnt-hair smell; cellulose fibres (cotton, jute) burn with a papery smell and leave soft grey ash; synthetics melt into a bead. P and R both show the burnt-hair signature, so both are animal fibres — exactly two. The idea that grey ash signals protein is wrong: grey papery ash is the cellulose signature, so Q is plant, not animal. Two different threads can certainly both be wool or silk, so 'cannot share the smell' is false. Cotton does NOT smell of burnt hair — it smells like burning paper — so 'both wool and cotton smell of hair' misstates the test that does in fact distinguish them.

**2. (A)** Silk is a protein fibre: it burns with a burnt-hair smell and is fairly strong even when wet. A papery burn smell and loss of strength when wet are the marks of cotton, a plant cellulose fibre, so the first fabric is mislabelled. The claim that silk weakens and burns papery reverses silk's real behaviour. 'Both are silk because both burn' ignores that the two burn smells are diagnostically different. The second fabric (burnt-hair smell, mats in hot water) is in fact wool — a protein fibre — not a synthetic; keeping strength when wet is consistent with wool, so calling it synthetic misreads the evidence.

**3. (D)** The burnt-hair smell is shared because both wool and silk are nitrogen-containing proteins; the difference is that they are DIFFERENT proteins — keratin in wool, fibroin in silk — which is why they feel and behave differently. They are not the identical protein, and dyeing has nothing to do with cotton's chemistry, so the first distractor is wrong twice over. Fibroin is silk, not cotton, and silk is a protein that smells of hair, not the papery cellulose one, so that option scrambles all three assignments. Cotton is cellulose (not protein); its papery smell is the correct, expected result of the test, not an error.

**4. (C)** Warmth from clothing is insulation, not heat generation. Wool's crimped, scaly fibres trap a layer of still air; air conducts heat poorly, so body heat escapes slowly and you feel warm. Wool itself makes no heat, so the 'small heater' idea and the 'slow chemical reaction' option are both false. Colour/dye does not warm a fibre — undyed creamy fleece insulates just as well — so the dye-based explanations are wrong. The corrected chain is: crimped fibres → trapped still air → slow heat loss → feeling warm.

**5. (A)** Insulation depends on trapped still air, not on mass. Lofty, crimped wool (Scarf 1) holds the most air; pressing it into felt (Scarf 2) squeezes out much of that air, so it insulates less; smooth silk (Scarf 3) traps the least and is the coolest. Density does not equal warmth — packing the fibres tight reduces the air pockets, so 'denser is always warmer' is wrong. Being an animal fibre does not make silk warm; its smooth fibroin traps little air, so silk is not warmest. Equal mass does not make them equally warm, because structure (air-trapping), not mass, governs insulation.

**6. (B)** A single fibre cannot meaningfully 'shrink' the way a knitted garment felts; felting needs many scaly wool fibres locking together under heat and agitation, so the shrink idea fails at the single-fibre scale. The burn test on one fibre clearly distinguishes the burnt-hair (protein/wool) smell from the papery (cellulose/cotton) smell, so burning is the better single test. Cotton does not shrink faster than wool — it is wool's scales that cause felting — so that distractor inverts the behaviour. The two fibres do NOT behave identically in fire (different smells), so 'neither works' is false. Wool does not dissolve in hot water; it felts, so the dissolving claim is wrong.

**7. (A)** Fine, soft yarn comes from breeds rich in fine undercoat hairs and poor in coarse guard hairs (the Merino idea). Flock A fits this directly. More guard hairs do NOT mean softer — they make the fibre coarser — so 'more wool is always softer' confuses quantity with quality. Scouring cleans and dyeing colours; neither changes a coarse hair into a fine one, so softness is not decided by those steps. Sorting can remove some guard hairs from Flock B, but it cannot make Flock B's remaining fibre finer than a breed that is naturally fine; you cannot sort your way past a coarse undercoat, so Flock A still wins.

**8. (D)** Fleece is insulation against cold, not a heat-shedding device, so a hot lowland is the wrong environment for thick-fleece wool sheep; this makes the first part wrong. Because cold prompts thick fleece, India rears its wool sheep in cool highlands (J&K, Himachal), not the hot coast, so the second part is wrong too — both parts fail. Saying only one part is wrong leaves the other false claim standing. 'Both correct' accepts the inverted biology that heat thickens fleece, which contradicts why a fleece exists.

**9. (D)** Selective breeding shifts traits within what the animal can already produce — here, finer and more uniform wool — but it cannot make a sheep produce a fibre from a different organism. Wool is keratin from a sheep; silk is fibroin spun by a silkworm larva; no amount of breeding turns one into the other, so the conversion idea is biologically false. Mulberry leaves feed silkworms, not sheep, and diet cannot give a sheep the silk-spinning apparatus, so that option is also wrong. Sheep CAN be bred for fleece quality (that is the whole point of breeds like Merino), so the last option's reason is false even though its 'impossible' verdict is right for the wrong reason.

**10. (A)** The microscopic scales interlock under heat and agitation, which is exactly why wool felts and shrinks — so scales → felting. The crimp gives the fibre bulk and creates air pockets, so crimp → trapped air → warmth. The swap option assigns each cause to the wrong effect. Neither feature is about colour, and a smooth straight fibre would trap less air and not felt, so the 'colour only' option is wrong on both counts. Scales and crimp are features of an animal protein fibre; they do not make a fibre plant-derived, so the last option is nonsense — wool is animal regardless.

**11. (C)** Yak, camel and the Pashmina goat live in cold regions; their thick coats trap air for the animal's own warmth, and harvesting that fibre gives humans a warm wool. None of these fibres is spun in a cocoon — cocoons are silk, made by silkworm larvae, not by yaks, camels or goats. They are animal fibres, not crops, so 'plant fibres from cold fields' is wrong. They are natural fibres, not factory-made synthetics designed to mimic warmth, so the last option misclassifies them.

**12. (A)** Scouring removes grease and dirt; only after washing can you reliably sort the wool by texture, length and colour, so scouring must precede sorting — the listed order swaps them. Dyeing comes AFTER burr-picking (you colour clean, burr-free wool), so claiming dye must precede burr removal reverses the real order. Shearing is the first step and spinning is the last, so swapping them is absurd — you cannot spin yarn before you have the fleece. Sorting greasy, unwashed fleece is exactly the mistake the swap creates, so 'nothing is swapped' is false.

**13. (B)** Dye soaks evenly only into clean fibre, so dyeing greasy unscoured fleece gives blotchy colour — the first problem. Sorting separates fine from coarse hairs for quality; if you dye before sorting, both hair types end up dyed together in one batch, undermining the later sorting needed for fine yarn — the second problem. Dyeing does not clean or grade wool (scouring and sorting do that), so the 'no problem' option is false. Dye does not create a burnt-hair smell, and dye cannot transmute keratin into silk fibroin, so the last two options are nonsense.

**14. (B)** Scouring is a wash that removes greasy and dirty material; burrs are small bits of plant matter caught in the fleece and are picked out by hand in a separate step, so scouring does not eliminate them — the worker is wrong. Warm water does not dissolve woody plant burrs, so the 'dissolves completely' claim is false. Burrs are plant debris, not grease, so they are not washed away with the grease. Burr-picking is done on sheared, scoured fleece, never before shearing (there is no fleece to pick burrs from before shearing), so the last option's reason is wrong even though its 'worker is wrong' verdict is right by accident.

**15. (B)** You must first remove the fleece (shearing), then wash it (scouring), then grade it (sorting), then remove plant burrs, then add colour (dyeing), and finally twist it into yarn (spinning). The first wrong order sorts before scouring, but you grade clean wool, not greasy fleece. The second wrong order scours before shearing, which is impossible (you cannot wash a fleece still on the sheep) and also dyes after spinning, which misplaces dyeing. The third wrong order dyes before sorting and burr-picking, but colour is added to clean, sorted, burr-free wool, so dyeing belongs near the end.

**16. (A)** Sorting separates coarse guard hairs from the fine undercoat; skip it and both end up spun together, so the yarn is uneven. Burr-picking removes embedded plant matter; skip it and burrs remain, making the yarn prickly — these are the two direct defects. Grease and poor dye uptake are caused by skipping SCOURING, which was done here, so that distractor blames the wrong step. Burr-picking has nothing to do with smell, flammability or anthrax spores, so that option is irrelevant. No step turns wool into cotton; wool stays keratin regardless, so the last option is impossible.

**17. (D)** Clean wool is the prerequisite for two separate later jobs: sorting (you can only grade texture, length and colour reliably on washed fibre) and dyeing (dye soaks evenly only into clean fibre). So scouring gates both — that is the full explanation. Scouring neither dyes nor grades wool, so it is not a 'repeat' of those steps. Sorting greasy fleece is unreliable, so the claim that sorting works equally well on greasy wool is false. Dye does NOT soak better into greasy wool — grease repels even dyeing — so the last option inverts the chemistry.

**18. (B)** Burrs are easiest to see and remove on clean wool; on greasy, dirty raw fleece they are hidden, so picking burrs first means many are missed and remain even after scouring. Removing a few visible burrs first does not make scouring more effective — the hidden burrs are the problem — so 'cleaner than usual' is wrong. Step order clearly matters here (clean-then-pick vs pick-then-clean give different results), so 'no difference' is false. Burrs are inert plant debris, not scrubbing agents, so they do not speed grease removal.

**19. (A)** Scouring removes 30% of 4 kg, leaving 70% of 4 = 2.8 kg of scoured wool. Sorting then discards 10% of that 2.8 kg, leaving 90% of 2.8 = 2.52 kg ≈ 2.5 kg. The 2.8 kg figure stops after scouring and forgets the sorting loss. The 1.6 kg figure wrongly subtracts both 30% and 10% from the ORIGINAL 4 kg (4 − 1.2 − 0.4 = 2.4, and over-subtracting more), mishandling the second percentage. The 4.0 kg figure ignores both losses, treating raw fleece as if no mass were removed.

**20. (D)** Dyeing's purpose is purely to change appearance (colour); it does not clean, grade or spin — so it is the unique fit. Scouring's PURPOSE is cleaning; any change in look is incidental, so it fails the 'without cleaning' condition. Scouring does not add colour — dyeing does — so the student's reason is also factually wrong. Sorting arranges fibres by quality, not by appearance in the colour sense, so it is not the appearance step. Only dyeing satisfies all the constraints, so there are not two correct answers.

**21. (A)** Shears are tools that work in any season; cold does not blunt them or make winter cutting impossible, so that is the invalid (false) reason and therefore the correct choice. It is genuinely true that winter shearing would expose the sheep to cold, that spring shearing lets a new fleece regrow before winter, and that in warm months the fleece is not needed for warmth — all three are valid justifications, so they are not the answer. The question asks for the one that does NOT hold up, which is the mechanical-impossibility claim.

**22. (C)** Scouring is a wool step that washes grease and dirt out of the fleece; reeling is a silk step that softens the sericin gum so the single cocoon filament can be unwound. The first distractor swaps the two — scouring is not for unwinding silk, and reeling is not for washing fleece. Neither step dyes the fibre; colour is added in a separate dyeing step, so 'add the final colour' is wrong. The twist that makes yarn is a spinning/throwing step, not scouring or reeling, so 'only to add the twist' is false.

**23. (B)** Felting is caused by heat plus agitation locking the fibre scales together; cool washing, not wringing, and drying flat each remove one source of heat or agitation, so all three target the single felting/shrinking problem. Wool does not DISSOLVE in hot water — it felts — so the 'dissolving' explanation is wrong. The instructions protect the fibre structure, not merely the dye; heat and agitation deform the fibre itself, so the dye-only reason is too narrow and partly false. Heating wool does not convert it into cotton (keratin cannot become cellulose), so the last option is impossible.

**24. (C)** Wool felting requires heat AND mechanical agitation; the scrubbing of Sweater A provides the agitation that drives the scales to interlock, so A shrinks more. A still soak (B) has heat but little agitation, so it felts less. The claim that still water 'penetrates deeper' to shrink more ignores that agitation, not penetration depth, drives felting. Heat alone is not sufficient — agitation matters — so 'shrink equally' is wrong. Shrinkage is promoted by hot water, not cold, so 'requires cold water' is the opposite of the truth.

**25. (D)** Each observation maps to a distinct property: warmth comes from crimp trapping air; felting comes from the surface scales interlocking; the burnt-hair smell comes from wool being a nitrogen-rich protein. The first wrong set scrambles these (e.g., it blames warmth on protein chemistry and the burn smell on scales). The second wrong set scrambles them differently. Dye explains none of these — undyed creamy fleece is still warm, still felts, and still smells of burnt hair when burnt — so the dye option is false.

**26. (B)** Both fabrics reach room temperature, so they start equally warm; the difference is how fast each conducts the hand's heat away. Wool's air-trapping structure conducts slowly, so the hand loses less heat and wool feels warmer. The fabrics are not at different temperatures — they shared the room — so the 'higher temperature' option is false. Wool stores no heat to release; being an animal fibre does not make it a heater. Cotton is a natural plant fibre, not a synthetic, so calling it synthetic and heat-absorbing is doubly wrong.

**27. (C)** Felting interlocks the fibre scales irreversibly; soaking (hot or cold) does not unlock them, so the shrinkage is essentially permanent — the sweater cannot be returned to size. Cold water does not 'relax' the scales to slide apart; that is the opposite of how felting works, so the first 'yes' is wrong. Heat does not un-shrink wool — it promotes more felting — so the second 'yes' is false. The wool has not turned into cotton; it is still keratin, just matted, so the last option's reason is incorrect even though 'no' is right.

**28. (C)** Wool insulates by trapping still air; when water replaces that air, and since water conducts heat much better than air, the insulation collapses and the jumper feels clammy and cold. Water is a far better heat conductor than air, so 'water is a better insulator' is exactly backwards. Soaking does not convert keratin into cellulose, so the chemistry-change option is impossible. Dye does not provide warmth, and wetting does not necessarily wash out dye, so the dye explanation is wrong.

**29. (D)** The larva (silkworm) is the voracious feeding stage, eating mulberry leaves to grow, and it is the larva that spins silk as it transforms into a pupa — so feeding and spinning both centre on the larva. The adult moth does not feed heavily nor spin silk (it lays eggs), so that pair is wrong. The pupa is the resting transformation stage inside the cocoon; it neither eats most nor spins at hatching, so that pair is wrong. The egg does not eat, and the pupa does not spin during emergence (the cocoon is already made), so the last pair is wrong.

**30. (A)** Silk is spun by the larva (caterpillar) as it prepares to become a pupa; the cocoon already exists before the adult moth forms, so the moth never spins it — the narrator is wrong. The pupa does not spin the cocoon; it is the stage that rests inside the cocoon the LARVA already spun, so 'pupa spins it' misplaces the event. An egg cannot spin silk and is not 'wound around the pupa,' so that option is biologically meaningless. Only the larva-spins-the-cocoon statement is correct.

**31. (A)** The true order is egg → larva → pupa → adult moth. The false statement reverses larva and pupa, claiming the pupa hatches from the egg and the larva forms later — that is backwards, so it is the correct choice for a 'false' question. It is true that the egg hatches into a larva (silkworm) that eats mulberry leaves, that the larva spins the cocoon as it becomes a pupa, and that the adult moth emerges and lays eggs — so those three are valid and not the answer.

**32. (B)** A cocoon is one long continuous filament; an emerging moth bites through it, cutting it into short pieces. Treating the intact cocoons with heat before emergence preserves the unbroken thread, so that is the best rescue. Letting the moths emerge leaves chewed, broken cocoons — the long thread is already cut — so 'empty cocoon is the silk' is wrong. Adult moths do not eat mulberry leaves (the larvae do), so feeding them changes nothing. Boiling AFTER the moths leave is too late; the continuous thread is already broken, so it cannot be recovered whole.

**33. (C)** Mulberry leaves are the silkworm larva's food; abundant fresh leaves let the larva grow and spin a better cocoon, so the first half is true. Sheep do not eat mulberry leaves to improve wool, and no diet converts wool keratin into silk, so the second half is false — making the split judgement correct. 'Both true' wrongly assumes mulberry helps every fibre animal. 'Both false' denies that the silkworm's diet matters, which contradicts well-known sericulture practice. The last option inverts both halves, since mulberry helps silkworms (not sheep).

**34. (A)** The mature larva stops eating and spins its protective silk cocoon as it becomes a pupa; this is the moment the continuous filament exists intact, so farmers harvest cocoons then. The egg → larva transition is hatching, not silk-spinning, so harvesting eggs yields no silk. Waiting for the moth to emerge breaks the thread (the moth chews out), so harvesting after emergence ruins the silk. The moth → egg phase is reproduction, not silk production, so collecting from the egg-laying moth gives no usable thread.

**35. (C)** Fibroin is the silk filament that is woven into fabric; sericin is the sticky gum coating that is softened and removed during reeling. Throwing away the fibroin throws away the thread, so weaving with only sericin fails. The claim that sericin is the strong thread and fibroin the throwaway gum reverses their roles. They are not equally good threads — only fibroin is the woven fibre, so 'both equally good' is false. Fibroin is a fibre, not a gum, so 'both are gums, neither weavable' wrongly classifies fibroin.

**36. (C)** Hot water softens the sericin gum so the single filament releases and unwinds smoothly. Without it, the gum holds the thread fast, so pulling breaks it into fragments rather than yielding the long strand. Dry thread does not slide off faster — the gum makes it stick — so 'unwinds even faster' is wrong. Hot water in reeling frees the thread by softening gum, not for dyeing, so the dye claim is false. A cocoon is made of one long fibroin filament coated in sericin gum, so 'no thread, only gum' denies the fibroin filament that is the whole point of silk.

**37. (A)** A single silk filament is extremely fine and weak, so several are reeled together to make a usable, stronger thread — that is the real reason. Mulberry silk is naturally whitish, so cocoons are not blended to make white, and colour-mixing is not the purpose. A single filament is too thin, not too thick, so 'several thin it down' inverts the problem. Reeling combines silk filaments with each other, not silk with wool, so 'mix wool with silk' is wrong — there is no wool in reeling.

**38. (B)** Reeling unwinds the cocoon's filaments and combines them into thread; weaving interlaces warp and weft threads on a loom to make cloth — so reeling makes thread, weaving makes fabric. The first distractor swaps the two definitions. Weaving does not unwind cocoons, so 'both unwind, neither makes fabric' is wrong — weaving clearly makes fabric. Boiling belongs to reeling (to soften gum), not to weaving, and heat does not 'form cloth' by itself, so the last option is nonsense.

**39. (C)** Sericin is a natural gum; while it coats the filament it binds the thread to itself, so the thread cannot be unwound or woven smoothly — that mechanical reason is why it is softened and removed in reeling. It is not removed for toxicity, so the 'poisonous' claim is false. Sericin is not silk's colour source, so 'only coloured part' is wrong. Removing the gum does not make the filament too thin — the gum is an extra coating, not the strand's body — so the thinness reason is also wrong.

**40. (D)** Tasar, eri and muga are all natural silks produced by silkworms other than the common mulberry one, so they share a natural-silk origin; nylon is a synthetic made from petroleum, so nylon is the odd one out. Muga is a non-mulberry SILK (famous from Assam), not a goat wool, so 'muga is wool' is wrong. Eri is a natural silk, not a synthetic, so grouping eri with nylon is false. Tasar is a silk, not sheep's wool, so 'tasar is a wool grade' is wrong.

**41. (A)** Different silkworm types give different natural silks; muga's golden sheen is a natural property of its silkworm and is the pride of Assam. Muga is spun by a silkworm, not a goat, and goats do not spin silk, so the 'Assam goat eating golden leaves' option is biologically impossible.

**42. (D)** The larva first feeds on mulberry leaves to grow, then spins its cocoon, the cocoon is reeled into thread, and the thread is finally woven into cloth. Feeding must precede cocoon spinning (a fed larva spins the cocoon), so any order putting the cocoon before feeding is wrong. Reeling and weaving are the last two steps, so beginning with them inverts the chain. Weaving is the final step, so it cannot start the sequence.

**43. (D)** Silk fabric originates as the long fibroin filament the silk-moth larva spins into its cocoon; reeling recovers that filament as thread. A goat's undercoat is Pashmina WOOL, an animal fibre but not silk, so that origin is wrong. Sericin is the gum that is REMOVED in reeling, not the woven thread, so the fabric is fibroin, not sericin. Seed hairs from plant bolls are cotton, a plant fibre, not silk.

**44. (C)** Wool (sheep) and silk (silkworm) are animal protein fibres; cotton (seed hairs) and jute (plant stem) are plant cellulose fibres — the correct split. Cotton is a plant fibre, not animal, so pairing it with wool as 'animal' is wrong. Plants are living too, but their fibres are still plant fibres, so 'all four animal' is false. Silk is animal and jute is plant, so calling them both animal — and calling wool a plant fibre — misclassifies three of the four.

**45. (B)** The silk moth's larva spins silk, and the Pashmina goat gives a fine wool — both correct. Sheep give wool (not silk) and the silk moth gives silk (not wool), so that pairing swaps the two. The mulberry plant only FEEDS the silkworm (silk comes from the worm, not the plant), and sheep give wool not cotton, so that pairing is doubly wrong. The yak gives wool (not silk) and the camel gives wool (not cotton), so the last pairing is wrong on both counts.

**46. (A)** Pashmina is a fine goat WOOL (animal fibre from a goat's undercoat); muga is a golden SILK spun by a non-mulberry silkworm in Assam — different fibre classes from different animals. They do not both come from silkworms — Pashmina is goat wool — so that option is wrong. They do not both come from goats — muga is silkworm silk — so that option is wrong. The last option swaps the two sources entirely, which is exactly backwards.

**47. (B)** A cocoon is built from one extremely long filament, often hundreds to over a thousand metres; this enormous continuous length is why reeling recovers one long thread and why one cocoon is so valuable. 'A few centimetres' wildly underestimates the length. The length is not a fixed one metre — it varies and is far longer. The filament is vastly longer than a human hair, so 'shorter than a hair' is the opposite of the truth; many cocoons are combined for STRENGTH, not because each is tiny.

**48. (B)** Sorter's disease (anthrax) is contracted by inhaling spores in raw, unwashed fleece dust; the sorter handles exactly that material, so the sorter is at highest risk. Once wool is scoured, the spores are removed, so a shopper's finished sweater carries essentially none, and body heat does not 'activate' spores. Spinning happens after scouring, so the spore-laden dust is already gone — the spinner is low risk. The recognised route is inhalation of raw-fleece spores, not drinking milk, so the shepherd-via-milk option misstates transmission.

**49. (D)** Inhaling anthrax spores from raw fleece causes sorter's disease, a form of anthrax due to the bacterium Bacillus anthracis. The common cold is a mild viral illness unrelated to fleece dust, and dye carries no cold virus. Malaria is mosquito-borne by a parasite, not from scouring water. Tooth decay is caused by mouth bacteria on teeth and has nothing to do with inhaled fleece spores, so only the anthrax answer fits the inhaled-spore route.

**50. (C)** Anthrax spreads by inhaling spores from raw fleece, so disinfecting the fleece and protecting the airway with masks, gloves and ventilation attacks the actual route — the most effective single measure. Bright dye does not reveal or kill spores, so it adds no protection. More frequent shearing does not remove spores from the fleece a sorter handles. 'Selling only finished sweaters' ignores that someone must still process raw wool, where the real exposure lies, so it does not protect the workers.

**51. (D)** Processing — especially scouring — washes the spores out of the fleece, so a finished sweater is essentially spore-free and safe. Anthrax CAN infect humans (that is what makes sorter's disease an occupational hazard), so 'only sheep' is false. Dye is a colourant, not an antibiotic, so it cannot cure anthrax. Woollen sweaters are made of wool, not cotton, so the cotton explanation is simply wrong; the real reason is spore removal by processing.

**52. (B)** Sorter's disease (anthrax) threatens the wool sorter, who handles raw, unwashed fleece and inhales its spores — the correct match. The shepherd's recognised exposure would be to fleece-borne spores, not 'only from milk,' so that line misstates the route.

**53. (B)** The correct chain starts with spores in raw fleece, dust raised during sorting, inhalation, and then anthrax in the lungs. Anthrax here is bacterial and inhaled, not a water-borne parasite drunk in scouring water, so the last chain misidentifies both the agent and the route.

**54. (D)** Polyester is a tough synthetic that adds strength, shape-retention and wrinkle resistance; the wool supplies the warmth — so the blend combines wool's warmth with polyester's durability. Blending cannot convert wool's protein into a plant fibre; wool stays keratin, so that option is impossible. A burnt-hair smell comes from burning protein, not from adding polyester, and is not a design goal. Warmth in this blend comes from the wool's air-trapping crimp, not from polyester, so 'polyester supplies the warmth' is wrong.

**55. (A)** Nylon and polyester are manufactured chemically from petroleum; they are durable and wrinkle-resistant but do not biodegrade and are not grown by any organism. They are not harvested from plants or animals — that describes natural fibres — so the first distractor is wrong. No silkworm eats petroleum or spins synthetic fibre, so that option is biologically absurd. Synthetics are not 'always warmer and softer' than wool — wool's air-trapping crimp often makes it warmer — so newness does not guarantee superiority.

**56. (D)** In standard reeling the cocoon is boiled while the pupa is still inside, killing it so the moth cannot emerge and cut the continuous thread — that is the ethical objection, and it is tied to preserving the unbroken filament. The killing is not of egg-laying adult moths, so the 'crushed while laying eggs' option misplaces the stage. Eggs are not boiled — they must hatch into the silk-spinning larvae — so that option is wrong. Larvae are FED mulberry leaves (well-fed larvae spin good cocoons), not starved, so the starvation option contradicts sericulture practice.

**57. (C)** Scarcity plus desirability raises price; an animal yielding only a little fine fibre per year makes that fibre rare and dear, so the trader's logic holds. Vicuña and Pashmina are animal fibres (from a llama relative and a goat), not mass-produced plant fibres, so the first distractor mis-sources them. Animal fibres vary widely in price by quality and quantity, so 'all cost the same' is false. They are natural fibres, not cheap synthetics, so the last option misclassifies them.

**58. (B)** Softness comes from a fine-wool breed plus careful sorting; safety comes from disinfecting fleece and protecting the airway with masks, gloves and ventilation — so this plan hits both goals directly. A coarse breed with no sorting hurts softness, and unmasked sorters are endangered, so that plan fails both. Skipping scouring leaves greasy, dirty wool (not soft) and does not remove spores, while bright dye neither reveals nor kills spores — so that plan fails both too. Mulberry leaves are silkworm food (sheep cannot be made silky by diet) and a fleece has no pupa to boil, so that plan is irrelevant to wool.

**59. (D)** Boiling has two real purposes: softening the sericin gum for reeling and killing the pupa so the continuous thread stays whole; cleaning is incidental, so the student is wrong. Cleaning is not the only reason, so 'fully correct' is false. Boiling does not dye the silk — muga's gold is natural, not from boiling — so the dye claim is wrong. Cocoons ARE boiled in commercial silk production, so 'never boiled' is false.

**60. (C)** Weakening when wet, a papery burn smell, grey ash, and no felting are all cotton (plant cellulose) traits, so an 'animal-fibre' label is false — the yarn is cotton. Wool keeps strength when wet and burns with a burnt-hair (not papery) smell, and it DOES felt, so the wool option contradicts three clues. Silk is a protein that burns with a burnt-hair smell, not papery with grey ash, so the silk option is wrong. Pashmina is a wool and would mat in hot water, so 'never shrinks' plus a papery burn does not fit Pashmina either; every clue converges on cotton.
