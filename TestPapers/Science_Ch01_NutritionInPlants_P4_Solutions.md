## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** Both processes run at once in the lit leaf. Photosynthesis pulls 18 units of CO2 out of the air; respiration puts 6 units back. The meter sees the NET effect on the air: 18 removed minus 6 added = 12 removed, i.e. a net fall of 12 units. The claim that respiration switches off in light is the day-respiration misconception — respiration never stops, so 18 is wrong. The 'net rise of 6' answer counts only the respiratory output and forgets the much larger photosynthetic uptake from the same air. 'No change/always cancel' is only true at the exact compensation point where the two rates happen to be equal; here uptake clearly exceeds release, so the gases do not cancel.

**2. (D)** A zero NET reading means CO2 taken in by photosynthesis equals CO2 given out by respiration — the compensation point. Lowering light reduces photosynthesis until its uptake rate drops to match the steady respiratory output, giving zero net change while both processes still run. Total darkness does NOT halt both processes: respiration continues, so the meter would show a net RISE, not zero. Maximum brightness drives photosynthesis far above respiration, giving a large net FALL, not balance. Removing chlorophyll stops photosynthesis entirely while respiration continues, again giving a net rise — and it does stop a process, which the question forbids.

**3. (C)** The carbon of plant food comes from carbon dioxide, which enters the LEAF through stomata (not the roots/soil), is fixed into glucose during photosynthesis, then travels as food through the PHLOEM to storage organs where surplus glucose is stored as starch. The first wrong option mislocates CO2 uptake at the roots and uses xylem (which carries water, not food). The minerals option confuses soil minerals with the carbon source — minerals are needed for health but are not the carbon backbone of glucose. The oxygen option names the wrong gas: oxygen is a PRODUCT released, not the raw material that supplies carbon.

**4. (D)** The bulk of a plant's dry body is made of glucose/starch and related substances whose carbon comes from carbon dioxide fixed in photosynthesis — so the new dry mass is mostly carbon captured from CO2, not soil minerals. Water is taken in, but it is the source of hydrogen, not the bulk solid dry mass attributed here, and 'turning water directly into solid body' misstates the process. Oxygen is a product RELEASED by photosynthesis, not a building block absorbed to add mass. The student's root-feeding idea is the classic soil-as-food misconception the chapter explicitly overturns: roots supply water and minerals, but food (and thus most dry mass) is made in the leaves from CO2.

**5. (A)** A controlled experiment isolates one factor by changing ONLY that factor between two otherwise-identical set-ups. To prove carbon dioxide specifically is needed, keep light, water, warmth and the plant identical and differ ONLY in CO2: one leaf's air has its CO2 absorbed by potassium hydroxide while the other, on the same plant, stays in ordinary air. Any difference in the starch test can then be pinned on CO2 alone. Wrapping one leaf in foil changes LIGHT, not CO2. A plant kept two days in the dark differs in light/destarching history, not CO2. A variegated leaf (green vs white) tests CHLOROPHYLL, not carbon dioxide.

**6. (C)** Starch forms only where ALL requirements are met. Photosynthesis needs chlorophyll (so white tissue fails) and light (so foil-covered tissue fails). The only region satisfying both is green tissue that was also lit — it alone turns blue-black. Comparing the lit-green region with the lit-white region shows chlorophyll is needed; comparing the lit-green region with the covered-green region shows light is needed — so one leaf demonstrates both. 'Entire green border regardless of foil' ignores the light requirement on the shaded green. 'Whole uncovered half including white' ignores the chlorophyll requirement on the white tissue. 'No region at all' is wrong because the lit-green tissue still has everything it needs.

**7. (B)** The decisive factor is the presence of chlorophyll, the light-capturing pigment. A red leaf only LOOKS non-green because a red pigment masks the chlorophyll that is still present, so it photosynthesises and grows. A white patch contains no chlorophyll at all, so it cannot make food and tests negative for starch — exactly the difference observed. Calling red pigment 'a kind of chlorophyll' is false; colour alone does not equal chlorophyll. The soil-feeding answer revives the food-from-soil misconception. 'White tissue makes food using white light' is backwards — colourless tissue without chlorophyll cannot photosynthesise no matter the light.

**8. (B)** Light reaching the plant weakens sharply with distance, and the bubble rate (a measure of photosynthesis) drops in step — strong evidence that light intensity controls the rate of photosynthesis. Only light was varied; water, temperature and the plant were held constant, so those cannot be the cause. Chlorophyll is not destroyed by simply dimming the light; the chlorophyll is intact and works again when the lamp returns. The plant is sitting in water throughout, so it cannot 'run out of water.' The bubbles are oxygen (a product released in light), not carbon dioxide — CO2 is taken in, not bubbled out, during photosynthesis.

**9. (A)** Xylem (intact) carries water UP to the leaves, so the leaves keep their water and keep photosynthesising. Phloem carries the FOOD made in the leaves DOWN/out; with the ring broken, that food cannot pass the cut and accumulates as a swelling just above the ring. The wilt-and-die option confuses the two tissues — water travels in the untouched xylem, so water supply is fine. 'Grows faster' is baseless; removing phloem hinders, not helps, the plant. The carbon-dioxide option is irrelevant: CO2 enters through stomata on the leaves, which the ringing does not touch.

**10. (A)** Glucose is soluble; large amounts dissolved in cells would upset the cell's water balance by drawing in water. Converting it to insoluble starch lets the plant stockpile food compactly without that osmotic disturbance — which is why surplus glucose is stored as starch. Starch cannot be made directly from sunlight; glucose is made first in photosynthesis, then converted. Starch is a solid food store, not a gas the plant breathes out — that confuses it with carbon dioxide. Starch is not a pigment; the green pigment is chlorophyll, a different substance entirely.

**11. (B)** Boiling the leaf in alcohol is the step that removes the green chlorophyll; skipping it leaves the chlorophyll in place. Iodine still turns the starch blue-black, but that dark colour is hidden behind the strong green of the leaf, so it is very hard to see — the starch is really there, just masked. Skipping alcohol does NOT destroy the starch; alcohol removes pigment, it does not break down food. Boiling in water alone does not wash out the starch, so 'nothing left to detect' is wrong. Iodine reacts with starch directly and needs no alcohol to 'trigger' it; alcohol's only job is to strip the masking chlorophyll so the colour can be seen.

**12. (C)** The plant is kept in the dark first so its leaves use up their stored starch (destarching). Then any starch found at the end must be NEW starch made during the experiment, so a positive iodine result can be credited to the conditions being tested. Skip the dark period and the leaf may still hold old, stored starch, which would also turn blue-black — a false positive that ruins the conclusion. The dark period has nothing to do with iodine absorption, and it does not 'supply carbon dioxide' — CO2 comes from the air during the lit phase. It does not turn the leaf white either; darkness empties starch, it does not destroy chlorophyll or the ability to photosynthesise.

**13. (D)** A seed carries its own food store, which fuels early growth even in the dark — that is why the cupboard seedling grows at first. But it cannot make NEW food without light (photosynthesis needs light), so once that store runs out it yellows and dies. The second pea, moved into light on day 3, starts photosynthesising and survives. So the pair shows: stored food powers early dark growth, but only light lets the seedling make food and survive once the store is gone. Light does not 'make seeds germinate' here — both germinated; the difference is survival afterwards. Seedlings do not photosynthesise faster in the dark — they cannot photosynthesise at all without light. The dark seedling did not die merely from lack of water; the lit one had the same water and lived.

**14. (C)** Cuscuta dies on a non-living wire but thrives on a living shrub — proof it needs a LIVING host, the mark of a parasite. The mushroom thrives on DEAD wood and harms nothing alive — the mark of a saprotroph that digests dead, decaying matter. Calling Cuscuta a saprotroph contradicts its death on the lifeless wire (it cannot feed on dead matter). Lumping both as parasites ignores that the mushroom needs no living host. The symbiont/autotroph option is doubly wrong: Cuscuta harms its host (not mutual benefit), and the mushroom has no chlorophyll, so it cannot be an autotroph making food in its cap.

**15. (C)** In a parasitic relationship one partner is harmed; in the lichen, the fungus gains food while the alga gains water, minerals and shelter — a mutually beneficial partnership, i.e. symbiosis, not parasitism. The 'alga harms the fungus' option invents harm where the description states mutual benefit. 'No living organisms, only minerals' is false — both the fungus and the alga are living. The role-swap option is factually wrong: the ALGA (with chlorophyll) makes the food, not the fungus, so it misstates who feeds whom and still misses the key point that both benefit.

**16. (A)** Carbon dioxide RISES wherever an organism only respires (gives out CO2) and does not photosynthesise to take it back. Jar 2 holds a germinating seed wrapped in foil — no light, so no photosynthesis; it only respires, so its CO2 rises. Jar 1 (a lit green pea seedling) and Jar 3 (a lit, cut-but-living green leaf) both photosynthesise in the light, taking in more CO2 than they release, so their CO2 FALLS, not rises. 'None of them' is wrong because the foil-wrapped seed clearly raises CO2. Among the labelled options offered, only Jar 2 fits.

**17. (B)** Each plant solves a nitrogen shortage by a different route. The bean cannot use nitrogen gas directly; Rhizobium bacteria in its root nodules fix air nitrogen into a usable compound for it. The Venus flytrap, in nitrogen-poor bog, digests the insects it traps to obtain nitrogen. Neither pulls nitrogen gas straight from the air through its leaves — plants cannot use N2 directly. The roles are not swapped: the flytrap traps insects, the bean uses nodule bacteria. And nitrogen is not 'made during photosynthesis' — photosynthesis builds glucose from CO2 and water, not nitrogen.

**18. (D)** Both pots sit in the same nitrogen-rich AIR, yet only pot A (given nitrogen-fixing bacteria) thrives — proof that plants cannot use atmospheric nitrogen gas directly. The bacteria fix that nitrogen into a soil form the plant's roots can absorb, so pot A greens up while pot B stays starved. Stomatal size has nothing to do with nitrogen uptake. The bacteria did not 'remove the plant's need for nitrogen' — they supplied it in usable form. And the two pots do NOT show nitrogen gas is a raw material of photosynthesis; photosynthesis uses CO2, water and light — nitrogen is for growth, not the day's food-making.

**19. (B)** Photosynthesis on a given day needs carbon dioxide, water, light and chlorophyll — NOT soil nitrogen. So as long as plant B's leaf still has some chlorophyll, it can make starch that day and turn blue-black, just like plant A. The long-term paleness and stunting show nitrogen matters for healthy growth and green colour, but the same-day starch test shows it is not a direct raw material of photosynthesis. 'Only A turns blue-black' wrongly treats nitrogen as a photosynthetic raw material. 'Neither' overstates nitrogen's role — a green-ish leaf still photosynthesises. 'Only B, forced to make extra starch' is backwards: nitrogen shortage hampers growth, it does not boost starch-making.

**20. (A)** An autotroph makes its own food using chlorophyll and light. The single fact that an organism has NO chlorophyll and sends threads into a host to draw off ready-made food shows it cannot make its own food — it is a parasite, which directly overturns the autotroph claim. Bright green stems that grow faster in stronger light actually SUPPORT being an autotroph. Releasing oxygen bubbles when lit underwater is a hallmark of photosynthesis, again supporting autotrophy. Losing water vapour on warm afternoons is ordinary transpiration and says nothing about how the plant feeds.

**21. (C)** An autotroph makes its own food, which needs chlorophyll. (i) A leafless Cuscuta has no chlorophyll and steals food from a living host — a parasite, so its autotroph label is wrong. (iii) A mushroom has no chlorophyll and feeds on dead matter — a saprotroph, so its label is wrong too. The green-stemmed cactus (ii), green duckweed (iv) and photosynthetic blue-green microbes (v) all contain chlorophyll and make their own food, so they ARE autotrophs. The two wrong labels are therefore (i) and (iii). Options blaming the cactus, duckweed, microbes, or claiming 'only large leafy trees' are autotrophs misjudge which organisms truly photosynthesise.

**22. (D)** A food chain's energy traces back to the green plant, which captured sunlight and stored it as food; that energy passes up the chain as grass → grasshopper → frog → snake. So the first student is right: the snake's energy comes ultimately from the sun via the grass. Oxygen (the second student's answer) is only used to RELEASE energy already stored in food during respiration — it is not the source of that energy. Energy does not have two separate sources, and no animal 'makes energy from nothing' — that would break the conservation the chapter teaches.

**23. (A)** The 'feeds on' arrows the student drew (eagle → frog → insect → plant) point from eater to eaten. ENERGY flow points the OTHER way — from the producer that captures sunlight up to the top consumer: plant → insect → frog → eagle. So the corrected energy-flow chain starts at the plant. Energy flow and 'feeds on' do NOT point the same way. Energy does not flow 'from the eagle downward' — the eagle is the end of the line, not the source. Food chains certainly do carry energy, so the arrow direction is not meaningless.

**24. (B)** The phytoplankton are the producers at the base of the chain. Kill them and the shrimp that eat them lose their food first and decline; the herring that eat the shrimp then run short and decline next. So the collapse passes UP the chain from the base. The shrimp cannot start photosynthesising to replace the lost producers — they are animals. The herring do not increase from 'clearer water'; losing the base of the chain harms everything above it. And the order is not herring-first — the impact begins at the producers and rises.

**25. (C)** In light the sealed globe balanced because photosynthesis used the CO2 from respiration and respiration used the O2 from photosynthesis. Wrapped in black cloth, photosynthesis stops but respiration continues, so the plant keeps consuming oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide with nothing to recycle them: O2 falls, CO2 rises, and the plant weakens. Oxygen cannot rise in the dark — there is no photosynthesis to make it. 'Nothing changes' wrongly assumes a sealed globe freezes its gases; respiration keeps altering them. A green plant does not become a saprotroph feeding on the soil.

**26. (A)** In bright light the plant photosynthesises and adds oxygen, helping the mouse. In the dark the plant makes NO oxygen and, like the mouse, respires and uses oxygen up — so now two organisms compete for the same limited air, and the mouse becomes distressed SOONER than in the light. Plants do not release 'extra oxygen in the dark' — photosynthesis needs light. The plant does not keep the air fresh regardless of light. And the plant certainly does affect the jar's air: in the dark it actively consumes oxygen.

**27. (B)** The dark-kept disc is the NEGATIVE CONTROL. Forty-eight hours of darkness lets the leaf use up its stored starch, so it tests negative — establishing a starch-empty baseline. The disc taken after 6 hours of sunlight has made fresh starch and tests positive, so its blue-black is unambiguously NEW starch. Iodine does not 'fail on dark leaves' — the dark disc was negative because it had no starch, not because iodine stopped working. Two days of dark do not permanently destroy chlorophyll — the lit disc proves the leaf still photosynthesises. And the dark disc is not the source of the lit disc's starch.

**28. (D)** Stomata are the pores through which both water vapour leaves and carbon dioxide enters. Shutting them to save water therefore ALSO cuts off the CO2 supply, and since CO2 is a raw material of photosynthesis, food-making slows for want of it. Sunlight is not blocked by closing the tiny pores — light passes through the leaf surface regardless. Closing stomata does not destroy chlorophyll. It reduces water LOSS, but it does not make the roots stop taking up water or kill the leaf that afternoon — the real consequence is the CO2 cut-off slowing photosynthesis.

**29. (C)** The plant is sealed at the soil, so the only route for the steady weight loss is water vapour escaping from the leaves through the stomata — transpiration. Coating every leaf surface with petroleum jelly blocks the stomata, so the loss almost stops, confirming the leaves were the exit. The plant is not 'burning its own mass' in photosynthesis — photosynthesis adds mass. The soil cannot leak from a pot sealed at the top. And the lost weight is escaping water, not stored starch converted into vapour.

**30. (D)** The shaded leaf loses little water (low transpiration) but still makes starch in light (photosynthesis works) — so the two can vary independently and are distinct processes. The 'required/always together' option is contradicted by the very observation: low transpiration with successful food-making. 'Photosynthesis causes transpiration, so low loss means no food' is disproven because the low-loss leaf DID make starch. They are not the same process under two names — transpiration is water vapour leaving the stomata, photosynthesis is food being made; the data show one can be low while the other proceeds.

**31. (A)** Transpiration is the loss of water vapour through stomata, which then condenses as droplets in the bag. A living leaf's stomata work, so it transpires and droplets form; a dead leaf's cells no longer regulate or drive this loss, so far fewer droplets appear. The rain idea is excluded — the bag is sealed and identical for both, so any difference is the leaf itself. Xylem carries water inside the plant but does not 'squirt' water out of the surface; loss is as vapour through stomata. The dead-leaf option is backwards: a dead leaf does not MAKE water; it loses less because its transpiration has effectively stopped.

**32. (B)** Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals UPWARD from the roots, so claim 1 is correct. Phloem carries the FOOD made in the leaves outward and largely downward — it does NOT carry water upward, so claim 2 is wrong. Hence only claim 1 is correct. 'Both correct' accepts the false claim 2. 'Only claim 2' wrongly says xylem carries the food. 'Neither / both carry food' ignores that xylem's job is water transport. Exactly one claim — claim 1 — stands.

**33. (D)** The correct word equation is carbon dioxide + water → (sunlight, chlorophyll) → glucose + OXYGEN. The pupil wrote 'carbon dioxide' on the right where oxygen belongs — oxygen is the gas released. So the one fix is changing the right-hand carbon dioxide to oxygen. Changing left-hand water to oxygen breaks a correct raw material. Removing chlorophyll deletes a needed condition. Changing glucose to starch is wrong — glucose is the immediate product; starch is the later storage form. Only the right-hand-gas error needs correcting.

**34. (A)** Green plants are the great regulators of atmospheric gases. In photosynthesis they take in carbon dioxide and release oxygen — exactly reversing what animals' breathing and the burning of fuels do — so the proportions of the two gases stay roughly steady over the years. Respiration in plants does the OPPOSITE of balancing: like animals, respiring plants take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide, so it cannot be what removes the excess CO2. Transpiration is the loss of water vapour through stomata; it does not 'wash carbon dioxide out of the air.' And soil does not soak up the air's carbon dioxide through roots — roots take up water and minerals, not atmospheric CO2.

**35. (C)** Photosynthesis needs light as its energy source. Plant A under the lamp has light and makes starch (turns blue-black); plant B in the dark box has CO2, water, warmth and chlorophyll but NO light, so it makes none. Since light is the only factor that differed, the experiment isolates LIGHT. 'Both blue-black' ignores that B had no light. 'Only B, darkness concentrates starch' is backwards — no light means no starch. 'Neither' is wrong: one lit day is ample for plant A to make detectable starch.

**36. (C)** Water, warmth and carbon dioxide were held identical while only light differed, and growth climbed steadily with light (40 g in full sun down to 0 g near-dark). So more light drives more photosynthesis and hence more food and growth — light is the limiting factor here. 'No clear effect' ignores the obvious trend. 'Darkness grows plants fastest' misreads the 0 g near-dark plot. Carbon dioxide cannot be the cause because it was kept the same across all four plots — only light varied.

**37. (D)** The day's photosynthesis needs carbon dioxide, water, light and chlorophyll — not soil nitrogen — so the spinach was photosynthesising even while pale. The paleness and stunting, cured by nitrogen-rich manure, show nitrogen is needed to build proteins and the green pigment for healthy growth — but it is not a direct raw material of that day's food-making. So (D) is right. Nitrogen is not the raw material turned into starch, nor the carbon source (carbon comes from CO2), and it does not replace sunlight.

**38. (A)** In the legume–Rhizobium partnership the BACTERIA fix air nitrogen into a usable form for the plant; in return the plant — a food-maker — gives the bacteria sugary food and a protected home in the root nodules. So what the bacteria receive is food and shelter. 'Usable nitrogen from the plant' reverses the deal — nitrogen flows from bacteria TO plant. The plant does not give chlorophyll — bacteria do not photosynthesise. And it does not hand them carbon dioxide to build glucose; the bacteria get ready-made sugars from the plant.

**39. (C)** Repeatedly cropping maize drains soil nitrogen. The natural, fertiliser-free remedy is to grow a leguminous crop (such as beans) for a season: the Rhizobium bacteria in its root nodules fix atmospheric nitrogen, and ploughing the crop back returns that nitrogen to the soil. Watering more heavily adds no nitrogen. Planting maize closer together uses the soil harder, draining nitrogen faster. Leaving the field bare for a year does not restore nitrogen by itself — nothing fixes the air nitrogen without the legume's bacteria.

**40. (B)** In a lichen the ALGA has chlorophyll and photosynthesises, making the food the pair lives on. So the food-maker you would point to is the alga. The fungus does not photosynthesise — it supplies water, minerals and shelter and takes food from the alga, so the options crediting the fungus are wrong. 'Neither, lives only on minerals' is wrong too — a lichen does need a food-maker, and that is the alga.

**41. (D)** Iodine turns blue-black only where STARCH is present. Boiled rice and raw potato are starch-rich, so they react strongly; an apple (mostly sugar) and boiled egg yolk (mostly fat and protein) have little or no starch, so they barely change. The reaction is about starch, not about being 'living/uncooked' — boiled rice is cooked yet reacts. The blue-black shows starch, not chlorophyll. And it has nothing to do with sweet taste — apple is sweet yet gives almost no colour.

**42. (A)** A broad, flat blade presents the largest possible surface to sunlight, and packing the chlorophyll-rich cells just beneath the lit upper surface puts the pigment where the light is strongest — both features serve efficient photosynthesis (light capture). The thin, flat shape is not built to store large amounts of water — water-storing organs are thick and fleshy. The broad shape catches wind rather than resisting it, so 'stays heavy so the wind cannot move it' is wrong. And leaf shape is not an anti-insect toughness adaptation.

**43. (C)** The pores are stomata. Their chief role in nutrition is to let carbon dioxide — a raw material of photosynthesis — enter the leaf, while oxygen and water vapour pass out through them. Veins are not these surface pores; the food made in the leaf travels in the phloem inside the veins, not out through the stomata. Root hairs absorb water from the soil, not gases at the leaf surface. And stomata do not 'absorb mineral nutrients from the air' — minerals come from the soil through the roots; the stomata's job is gas exchange. Both 'stomata' options name the structure correctly, but only one states the correct role.

**44. (B)** Food is made only in the green, chlorophyll-containing parts — mainly the leaves — never in a carrot's root or a potato's stem-tuber. The food made in the leaves is carried through the phloem to these storage parts, where the surplus is laid down as insoluble starch. So a storage organ does not make its own food (the green leaves did); the roots do not absorb ready-made food from the soil (that is the food-from-soil misconception); and water carried up in the xylem is a raw material, not something that 'turns into' stored food by itself.

**45. (B)** A brown (no-colour) iodine result simply means no starch is present; it says NOTHING about whether the tissue ever contained chlorophyll — so (B) is the FALSE statement. A blue-black colour does indicate starch is present — true. Destarching the plant beforehand does let any new starch be credited to the experiment — true. Boiling the leaf in alcohol does remove chlorophyll so the colour change is easy to read — true. Only (B) is false.

**46. (D)** Alcohol is highly flammable, so heating it directly over a flame risks catching fire. Standing the alcohol tube in a beaker of hot water heats it safely and evenly, which is why the test is done this way. Water is not 'added to the alcohol to dissolve chlorophyll' — the alcohol itself removes the chlorophyll. Heating does not turn alcohol into starch. And it is the heat, not any 'light from the flame', that the step needs — the point is simply to avoid a naked flame on flammable alcohol.

**47. (C)** The essential difference is the MODE of nutrition. The banyan is an autotroph: it builds its own food from carbon dioxide and water using sunlight. The goat is a heterotroph: it cannot make food and must consume ready-made food. The banyan does not eat small soil creatures, nor does it make food 'inside its roots' — food is made in green tissue from CO2 and water. 'Both take ready-made food from their surroundings' is false for the banyan, which manufactures its own.

**48. (B)** The bread mould has no chlorophyll, so it cannot make its own food by photosynthesis — ruling out the kitchen-light and the warmth-and-moisture options. Instead it feeds as a saprotroph: it secretes digestive juices onto the bread, which break the food down outside the fungus, and then it absorbs the dissolved food. It does not trap and digest insects — that is an insectivorous plant's method, not a bread mould's. So option (B) correctly describes saprotrophic nutrition by external digestion and absorption.

**49. (D)** Cuscuta thrives on a LIVING hibiscus but shrivels and dies on a lifeless plastic stake — exactly the behaviour of a parasite, which can feed only by drawing food from a living host. A saprotroph feeds on dead, decaying matter, which a plastic stake is not, and it would not depend on a living shrub. A green autotroph would make its own food and not die for lack of a host; Cuscuta is genuinely chlorophyll-poor, not 'briefly' discoloured. A symbiont benefits its partner; Cuscuta harms its host. So it is a parasite.

**50. (B)** Both facts must fit one explanation. The sundew's green leaves photosynthesise to make its energy-rich food; it traps and digests insects mainly to get the scarce NITROGEN its boggy soil cannot supply. So it is a green food-maker that supplements nitrogen by catching insects. The green leaves are not merely ornamental — they genuinely photosynthesise. It is not a parasite — it does not draw food from a living host. And it is not a saprotroph — it does not live on dead, decaying matter; its green leaves are functional.

**51. (D)** With photosynthesis clearly outpacing respiration in steady bright light, the plant releases more oxygen than it uses and takes in more carbon dioxide than it releases. So by the end oxygen has RISEN and carbon dioxide has FALLEN. 'O2 fallen, CO2 risen' describes net respiration (darkness), the opposite case. 'Both risen' is impossible when CO2 is being consumed. Nitrogen is not taken in or released by either process, so it does not fall sharply.

**52. (C)** A green plant begins a food chain because it captures sunlight to create brand-new food, introducing fresh energy into the chain — it is a producer. A mushroom only breaks down food (dead matter) that already exists; it adds no new energy, so it cannot be the starting point. The green plant is not 'the only thing that respires' — all the organisms respire. The mushroom does not eat the plant to begin the chain. And size has nothing to do with which organism is the producer.

**53. (D)** A tiger eats only meat, but the deer it eats fed on plants — so the energy in the tiger's food was first captured from sunlight by green plants and passed up the chain. Plants also release the oxygen the tiger needs to release that energy by respiration. That is the deep, universal dependence. Occasional grass-nibbling when ill is trivial and not the reason. Plants do not pass soil minerals 'directly' to the tiger. And plants release most of their oxygen in daylight, not specially at night.

**54. (C)** Light is the energy source for the algae's photosynthesis. Persistent shade starves them of light, so they make little food and decline (producers fail first). The snails, which depend on the algae for food, then run short and decline too — the impact passes up the chain from the base. 'Snails thrive on softer algae' invents a benefit; less light means less algae, not better food. Algae cannot 'feed on silt' — they are autotrophs that need light, not particles, to make food. 'Algae don't need light' is flatly false; without light photosynthesis stops.

**55. (A)** Autotrophs make their own food, but not from nothing — they still need raw materials and energy from outside: carbon dioxide, water and especially light. Locked in a dark cupboard a green plant cannot photosynthesise, so it cannot feed itself and will starve once its reserves are gone. 'Need nothing once grown' is exactly the misconception being corrected. The plant cannot survive by eating the cupboard's wood, nor by taking ready-made food from the air — those are not how a green plant feeds.

**56. (B)** Carbon dioxide is a raw material of photosynthesis. The KOH removes it from one leaf's air, so that leaf cannot make starch and stays brown; only the normal-air leaf makes starch and turns blue-black. The student's error was treating CO2 as something whose removal HELPS, when in fact CO2 is essential and its removal stops food-making. 'Both turn blue-black, light alone is enough' ignores that CO2 is needed too. 'Neither, destarching destroyed the ability' misunderstands destarching, which only empties stored starch and does not disable photosynthesis. 'KOH feeds the leaf' inverts KOH's role — it ABSORBS CO2, it does not provide it.

**57. (D)** The leaf releases net oxygen by day (photosynthesis exceeds respiration) and takes in net oxygen by night (only respiration). At the two zero-crossing instants around dawn and dusk, the rate of oxygen made by photosynthesis exactly equals the rate of oxygen used by respiration — the compensation point — so the NET exchange is zero while both processes still run. The leaf has not stopped both processes — respiration never stops. It is not 'only respiring' or 'photosynthesis completely stopped' at those instants — those describe the night, which would give a net oxygen UPTAKE, not zero.

**58. (A)** Saprotrophs such as fungi and bacteria feed on dead leaves, wood and animal remains by breaking them down — and in doing so they release the nutrients locked inside that dead matter back into the soil, where green plants can absorb and reuse them. That recycling is why decomposers matter even though they make no food themselves. They do not photosynthesise or pass on sun-made food (they have no chlorophyll), and they are not the source of plants' oxygen — green plants make their own oxygen in photosynthesis. 'No real importance' ignores their essential role in returning nutrients to the soil.

**59. (A)** A plant's body is largely made of food (glucose/starch and related substances) whose carbon comes from carbon dioxide fixed in the leaves during photosynthesis — so the great bulk of new solid mass is built from a gas in the air, not solid soil. Roots take up water and dissolved minerals, not whole solid soil that hardens into wood, so the first wrong option misdescribes how roots work. Oxygen is a PRODUCT released by photosynthesis, not a raw material the tree turns into solid wood. The girl's whole-soil idea is the food-from-soil misconception the chapter overturns: the trunk's mass traces mostly to carbon dioxide, with water supplying hydrogen.

**60. (B)** Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals UPWARD from the roots; phloem carries the food (sugars) made in the leaves AWAY from them, largely downward. So the correct match is water/minerals = xylem/up and food = phloem/away-and-down. The first wrong option swaps the two tissues. 'Both in the xylem' is false — the plant has two distinct transport tissues. The last option reverses both directions (water down, food up). Only this row keeps each tissue with its correct cargo and direction.
