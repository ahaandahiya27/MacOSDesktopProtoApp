## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** The chapter's central claim is that sacredness reaches beyond buildings into Nature and journeys, so revered rivers, hills, forests and the act of pilgrimage would keep the land sacred even with no temples. Rebuilding one tall temple, or a single great stone shrine, stays inside the 'buildings only' view the chapter explicitly widens. A government register of buildings is administrative, not a source of sacredness, and again fixes sacredness to structures.

**2. (D)** The chapter takes 'tirtha' (literally a river-ford) and extends it metaphorically to a crossing from the ordinary toward the higher, which is the deeper sense it stresses. 'River' captures only the literal ford and wrongly implies every tirtha must lie on water. 'Peak' confuses tirtha with sacred mountains, a separate idea. 'Nectar' belongs to the Kumbh's pot-of-amrita legend, not to the meaning of tirtha.

**3. (B)** The chapter applies 'tirtha' to confluences, hilltops and seaside temples alike because all are imagined as crossing-points toward the sacred, so the metaphor, not the literal ford, governs. Restricting tirthas to riverbanks ignores that extension. Hills and oceans are revered across the same traditions, not split by religion. Hilltop shrines plainly exist in the chapter, so denying them is false.

**4. (D)** 'Tirthankara' literally means 'maker of a tirtha (crossing)', so the title frames the teacher as one who enables the passage to a higher, liberated life, matching the chapter's crossing-metaphor. Building a tall shrine confuses holiness with size. Granting tax-free land belongs to royal land-grants, a different chapter's theme. Forming a river describes a sangam, not a Tirthankara.

**5. (C)** The char dham are deliberately placed at the country's four directional extremes, so a single pilgrimage loop sweeps across the entire land and ties distant regions together. A northern valley, a single city, or a tight radius would cluster the sites and defeat the integrating effect the chapter highlights.

**6. (A)** The chapter fixes char dham at 4, jyotirlingas at 12 (Shiva), and Shakti pithas at 51 (the goddess Sati). Swapping char dham to 12 and jyotirlingas to 4-Vishnu fails both count and deity. Putting 51 on the jyotirlingas and 12 on the pithas swaps the two large networks. Assigning the goddess to the jyotirlingas and Shiva to the pithas swaps the deities.

**7. (D)** Spreading 51 sites across the land creates a single goddess-geography linking distant regions, which is exactly the integrating effect the chapter draws. Confining worship to one shrine, or sealing regions off, is the opposite of scattering. The pithas never become jyotirlingas; that conflates two distinct networks.

**8. (D)** The chapter stresses that faiths originating in India and those arriving from outside both mark sacred places here, so dargahs and churches do count. Crediting only the oldest religion ignores that breadth. Claiming outside faiths merely renamed temples misreads how their followers built and honoured their own sites. Government recognition is irrelevant to a place being sacred.

**9. (C)** The chapter names Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism as the faiths that arose in India. Islam, Christianity and Zoroastrianism arrived from outside, so each of the other groups smuggles in a non-Indian-origin faith and fails.

**10. (D)** Worshippers of several faiths at one shrine directly refutes the idea that a site serves only one religion, which is the chapter's 'shared sites' point. The antiquity of pilgrimage, the sacredness of Nature, and the pilgrimage-trade overlap are all true chapter themes but are not what a single shared dargah is being used to disprove here.

**11. (C)** The chapter reads the demanding climb as a mirror of inner spiritual effort, giving the hardship purpose. Keeping pilgrims away contradicts the welcoming aim of pilgrimage. Calling it accidental denies the symbolism the chapter draws. Treating it as a strength contest reduces a spiritual idea to athletics.

**12. (B)** Integration in the chapter comes from long pilgrim journeys mixing people and customs across regions; clustering the sites removes those long journeys and so removes the integrating effect. River links, festival attendance and government recognition are not the mechanism the chapter credits for integration, so they are weaker explanations.

**13. (C)** The chapter explains that pilgrims needed food and lodging, so traders worked the same busy roads, making the route maps coincide. Separate roads contradict that overlap. Building trade routes only after pilgrimage ended is invented and reverses the chapter's point. Calling it coincidence ignores the stated cause.

**14. (C)** If pilgrims and merchants never shared roads, the overlap the chapter rests on would collapse, so that finding undermines the claim. Shrines at crossroads, festival crowds and long pilgrim journeys all reinforce the intertwining rather than threaten it.

**15. (D)** A confluence is a place where waters cross and join, which directly enacts the tirtha idea of a crossing. Rivers do not stop at a sangam, and that misreads moving water. Bathing is in fact central at sangams, not forbidden. A tax boundary is a political, not sacred, notion.

**16. (C)** The Kumbh follows a roughly twelve-year sky-based cycle (so 'every year' is wrong) and rotates among four cities (so 'same town' is wrong) — exactly two errors. 'None' ignores both. 'One' misses the rotation error. 'Three' wrongly adds that the festival does not exist, which is false.

**17. (B)** The chapter fixes the Kumbh by astronomical positions of Jupiter and the Sun on a ~12-year cycle across four cities. Monsoon rainfall, a kings' vote and a new temple's completion are all invented triggers that contradict the sky-based, multi-city rotation.

**18. (B)** 'Kumbh' means pot and recalls the pot of amrita (nectar) of legend, paired with 'mela' (fair). A mountain-source, a coronation crown and a fig-tree proclamation are all distractors built from other chapter themes (sacred mountains, kingship, sacred trees) and do not explain 'kumbh'.

**19. (A)** Bathing at the sangam ties the Kumbh to the tirtha-as-crossing idea, explaining the huge gatherings. Saying the festival has no river link contradicts the bathing itself. Banning bathing at confluences is the reverse of the practice. Placing the Kumbh on a hilltop ignores its riverbank character.

**20. (B)** Calling a peak a gateway to heaven shows physical landscape and spiritual meaning merging, the chapter's fusion idea. Saying only plains can be sacred contradicts the very example. Keeping geography and religion apart is the opposite of fusion. Treating mountains only as borders strips away their sacred meaning.

**21. (A)** The chapter dates river and confluence worship to at least Vedic times, underscoring its antiquity. The last hundred years, the post-Gupta centuries and the modern-city era all wrongly make an ancient practice recent.

**22. (D)** A holy tree shows sacredness extending into living Nature beyond buildings, the chapter's point. Restricting holiness to fig trees overgeneralises from one example. Limiting it to buildings contradicts the very tree. Saying the Buddha forbade natural reverence reverses the example, since the Bodhi tree is itself revered.

**23. (C)** A trade-derived name for a revered tree links commerce with cultural and sacred life, the chapter's interwoven theme. Claiming the tree was sacred only to traders narrows a broadly revered tree. Forbidding traders from resting there contradicts the very story of the name. Naming trees after kings is invented.

**24. (B)** The banyan spreads because aerial roots descend and become new trunks, so one tree covers a large area. Golden leaves, a stone trunk and Kumbh-timed flowers are all invented and have nothing to do with the tree's spreading habit.

**25. (A)** Sacred groves survive mainly through community belief that they are a deity's dwelling, which is why no fence or law is needed. State laws and police, a walled enclosure, and royal payments all replace belief with external force, contradicting the chapter's account of belief-driven protection.

**26. (B)** Belief-protected groves show culture conserving Nature without law, the chapter's lesson. Requiring police contradicts the very example of unguarded groves. Saying belief always harms Nature reverses the finding. Limiting protection to cities ignores rural groves entirely.

**27. (A)** The belief simultaneously voices reverence and protects the ecosystem, hence 'double duty.' Turning the grove into policed state land replaces belief with law. Inviting logging and mining is the opposite of protection. Claiming only buildings are sacred contradicts the grove being sacred at all.

**28. (D)** A punyakshetra is a stretch of land where landscape, culture and the sacred merge, exactly a revered riverbank or forest drawing pilgrims. A coin, a land-grant plate and a praise inscription are administrative or political objects with no fusion of geography and spirituality.

**29. (B)** The chapter notes sacred geography appears elsewhere too, so the 'only India' claim is mistaken. Saying no other country has holy places is plainly false. Crediting modern Europe with inventing it is invented. Denying India any sacred geography contradicts the whole chapter.

**30. (D)** The chapter's overall argument is that sacred geography fused faith, Nature and pilgrim movement to unify a diverse land over millennia. Reducing it to traders' profit ignores its devotional and natural sides. Saying it divided India reverses its integrating role. Calling it purely modern contradicts the 3000-year span.

**31. (C)** A 3000-year span signals a deep, continuous force, not a passing event. A recent campaign, a single dynasty and a one-century span all shrink the timeframe and miss the chapter's point about endurance.

**32. (C)** Outside faiths gained sacred sites because their followers settled and built and revered places over generations, integrating them into the sacred landscape. A single royal decree, mere renaming of temples, or government grants all misdescribe this organic, centuries-long process.

**33. (D)** A relic is a revered bodily part or object of a holy figure, while a sangam is a river confluence. The other options swap the two, or substitute unrelated terms (land grant, dating era, festival cycle, fig tree) that belong to different ideas entirely.

**34. (B)** A shrine draws its holiness from the divine, a relic, or a spiritual figure. A battle, a market town or a coronation are secular associations and do not, on their own, make a place a shrine in the chapter's sense.

**35. (B)** By tying heroes' journeys and deeds to many real places, the epics made those places sacred and spread them across the land. Forbidding pilgrimage, naming a single place, or describing only imaginary lands all contradict the scattering effect the chapter credits to the epics.

**36. (A)** Scattered shrine-networks, revered Nature, and long pilgrim journeys all spread sacredness across the land. A coin advertising a ruler's army is a political object that does nothing to make the land sacred, so it breaks the pattern.

**37. (A)** A sangam is a river confluence, not a living organism, so it does not belong with the trees. The peepal and banyan are living fig trees, and the Bodhi tree was itself a peepal — all living — so calling them non-living, a building, or a stone pillar is false.

**38. (B)** Long journeys to scattered sites mixed people and customs, which integrates rather than divides. The distances did not isolate regions; they were bridged by pilgrims. Saying only one site was visited, or that ordinary travel was forbidden, both contradict the busy pilgrim movement the chapter describes.

**39. (A)** Pilgrim crowds created demand that traders met, so towns grew up — the reason directly explains the assertion, and both are true. Denying the explanatory link ignores the cause-and-effect the chapter draws. The assertion and the reason are both genuinely true, so the options calling either false are wrong.

**40. (C)** A dargah revered by several faiths is the textbook shared site. A single-sect temple serves one tradition only. A royal palace and a market square are not sacred and not shared across faiths, so none of the distractors fits the 'more than one tradition' test.

**41. (D)** All three are tirthas because each is conceived as a crossing toward the sacred. Rivers beginning their flow describes a source, not a crossing. A boundary stone is political. A royal-only site contradicts the open, devotional nature of a tirtha.

**42. (D)** Fusion means the landform itself is divine, so worshipping a mountain or river directly shows it. Ignoring the landscape, worshipping only indoors, or valuing Nature only for farming all keep geography and spirituality apart, the opposite of fusion.

**43. (B)** Because sacred places blend Nature and culture as a shared heritage, the chapter implies a shared duty to protect them. Calling them useless relics, valuing them only for tourism, or treating them as traders' property all ignore the heritage-and-conservation argument the chapter builds.

**44. (C)** Conservation here means safeguarding Nature and heritage for the future. Clearing forests for profit, building over rivers, and discarding traditions are all forms of destruction, the opposite of conservation.

**45. (A)** The grove example shows belief protecting forests cheaply and lastingly without law, so reinforcing sacred belief fits the chapter's logic. Armed guards and fines, walls and locks, and annual payments all rely on external force or money, which the chapter shows is not what kept groves safe.

**46. (B)** Shared roads carried both pilgrims and traders, so a route-junction became a busy, important spot for a shrine. Forbidding shrines except at empty crossroads is invented. Paying traders to stay away, or barring pilgrims and traders from meeting, both contradict the overlap the chapter describes.

**47. (C)** Shared regions led people of different faiths to revere the same Nature, hence 'across many communities.' Government assignment, a monopoly for the oldest religion, and yearly reassignment are all invented mechanisms that contradict the organic, shared reverence the chapter describes.

**48. (A)** A Tirthankara is a supreme Jain teacher, 'maker of a crossing.' A sangam is a river confluence (not a relic), a relic is a holy figure's part or belonging (not a confluence), and a jyotirlinga is one of 12 Shiva shrines (not one of the 51 goddess sites), so the other pairings are swapped or miscounted.

**49. (C)** From smallest to largest: char dham 4, jyotirlingas 12, Shakti pithas 51. Putting jyotirlingas before char dham breaks the order. Putting the pithas before the jyotirlingas reverses 12 and 51. Listing the pithas first is largest-to-smallest, the reverse of what is asked.

**50. (C)** Worshipping and trading on one journey shows religious and economic life intertwined, the chapter's overlap theme. Forbidding pilgrims money, keeping sites and markets apart, and confining trade to temples all contradict the merging of devotion and commerce.

**51. (B)** The jyotirlingas are twelve Shiva shrines. Fifty-one with the goddess describes the Shakti pithas; four with Vishnu echoes a miscount of the char dham; seven with the Buddha is invented. Only the twelve-Shiva pairing is right.

**52. (A)** All three branches share the crossing-metaphor: a ford crosses water, a Tirthankara makes a crossing, a hilltop shrine is a crossing toward the divine. Requiring flowing water fits only the ford. Tax-free land and single-religion rules are unrelated to the shared root and are invented.

**53. (D)** The Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya is central to Buddhism. The jyotirlingas are Shaiva (Hindu), not Jain; the Shakti pithas are goddess sites in the Hindu tradition, not Buddhist; the char dham are Hindu abodes, not Sikh. Only the Bodhi-tree pairing is correct.

**54. (C)** Seeing Nature as holy gives people a motive to protect it, which is precisely how sacredness has aided conservation, so the reason explains the assertion and both are true. Denying the link ignores the chapter's causal point, and both statements are genuinely true, so the options calling either false fail.

**55. (A)** The char dham are Badrinath (north), Dwarka (west), Puri (east) and Rameswaram (south). Haridwar, Prayagraj, Nashik and Ujjain are the four Kumbh cities. Bodh Gaya, Sarnath, Kushinagar and Lumbini are Buddhist sites. The last list mixes in southern temple towns that are not the char dham.

**56. (A)** The chapter's thesis is that pilgrimage, site-networks and revered Nature together make the whole land sacred, far beyond shrines. A single holy temple, a king's declaration, or denying any link to geography each contradict that central message.

**57. (D)** A map dense with revered Nature, routes and shared shrines across the country demonstrates one connected sacred geography where the land itself is holy. Confining sacredness to grand buildings, sealing each site to one faith, or concentrating sites in one region all contradict the chapter's spread-out, shared, Nature-inclusive picture.

**58. (B)** The chapter's shared-site theme means both pilgrims are rightly there, since many holy places draw several faiths together. Reserving sites by law for one religion, restricting worship to the founding faith, and claiming a site loses holiness when shared all contradict the chapter's account of cross-faith reverence.

**59. (A)** The Shakti pithas number 51 and the jyotirlingas 12, so the student reversed the two figures. The mistake is not about the char dham, which number 4. The deities are correctly matched but the counts are swapped, so 'right numbers, wrong deities' is the reverse of the actual error. The Kumbh's four cities are unrelated to either count.

**60. (A)** Protecting revered Nature as shared heritage honours reverence, conserves the ecosystem and treats landscape as spiritually meaningful — the chapter's full package. Turning sites into fee attractions reduces them to tourism income; assigning features to one religion denies their shared character; replacing landscapes with buildings reverses the chapter's move beyond mere structures.
