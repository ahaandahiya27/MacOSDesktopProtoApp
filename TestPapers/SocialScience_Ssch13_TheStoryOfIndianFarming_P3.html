Worked Solutions

1. (C) What names a farming type is its main purpose. Here the household grows mainly to eat, so it is subsistence farming; an occasional tiny exchange does not change the purpose. Selling in the market for income is what would make it commercial. The size of the plot or the intensity of cultivation is a separate idea from purpose, and rice is a cereal grown in a field, not the fruit-vegetable-flower cultivation that horticulture means.

2. (A) How heavily land is worked per unit area is the intensive-versus-extensive distinction: X farms intensively, Y extensively. Whether the output is eaten or sold (subsistence vs commercial) is about purpose, not about effort per hectare. Kharif and rabi name seasons, not intensity, and neither farmer is described as avoiding or using chemicals, so the organic/Green-Revolution contrast does not apply.

3. (B) The defining shift is purpose: growing chiefly to sell makes it commercial farming. Intensity (effort per area) may or may not change and is a different axis. Whether the field is watered by rain or by irrigation is unrelated to whether the crop is sold, and stopping food-growing says nothing about whether chemicals are avoided, which is what organic would require.

4. (B) Subsistence versus commercial is decided by why the crop is grown, and a farmer's purpose can shift between years, so the same field can fall under either label. Soil type does not flip yearly, and a field does not move. The two terms are opposites, not synonyms: one means growing to eat, the other growing to sell.

5. (A) Labour is one of the key inputs of farming, and hired workers like her are a large part of it; in rural India most such work is in fact done by women. She is not a subsistence farmer because she keeps none of the crop, and not a commercial farmer because she owns and sells nothing. Far from irrelevant, the work of landless labourers is essential to the harvest.

6. (B) Growing fruits, vegetables and flowers is horticulture, and selling all of it makes the purpose commercial, so both labels apply. She is not growing cereals, and she is not keeping the produce to eat, so subsistence does not fit. Nothing tells us the plot is large and thinly worked (extensive) or that it relies only on rain, and neither the Green Revolution nor hillside terracing is described.

7. (D) The chapter treats subsistence and commercial farming as suited to different situations, not as one being universally superior: commercial farming brings cash but also market and cost risks, while subsistence farming guarantees a family's own food. Saying selling always beats eating ignores food security. Subsistence farms are usually smaller and need not out-produce commercial ones. And the two are clearly different in purpose, so the comparison is meaningful.

8. (D) Spreading limited effort thinly over a large area for a low yield per hectare is extensive farming, and depending on the rains makes it rain-fed. Intensive irrigated farming would mean heavy inputs and artificial watering on each unit of land, the opposite of what he does. He is growing a field crop, not fruits and flowers, and the Green-Revolution package specifically means high-yield seeds, irrigation and chemicals, which he is not using.

9. (A) Indian farming varies along many axes at once: soil and climate differ by region, crops follow different seasons, some land is rain-fed and some irrigated, and purposes range from subsistence to commercial, so the practices are far from uniform. Claiming every farm uses identical seeds and tools, or that all farms are commercial and irrigated, simply restates the false uniformity. And farming has changed greatly, including through the Green Revolution, so it did not freeze in ancient times.

10. (A) Kharif crops are sown with the incoming southwest monsoon in the hot, rainy season, so they can live on the natural rains. Rabi crops grow in the cool dry winter and usually need irrigation because there is little rain then. Zaid crops fill the short hot gap between rabi and kharif and again lack monsoon rain. Rainfall in India is highly seasonal, not even across the year, so the season choice matters.

11. (C) Rabi crops like wheat are matched to the cool, comparatively dry winter and are harvested in spring, so sowing wheat in the hot, wet monsoon mismatches its needs and the crop suffers. Crops are not season-neutral; that is why kharif, rabi and zaid exist. Naming it a kharif or zaid crop does not change wheat's growing requirements, and no crop ripens in two weeks.

12. (D) The northeast monsoon brings rain mainly in the October-to-December window, so that is when she can count on it. June to September is the period of the southwest monsoon, a different system. March to May is the hot pre-monsoon stretch, and Indian rainfall is concentrated in monsoon periods rather than spread evenly through the year.

13. (A) Rain-fed farms rely on the monsoon as their only water source, so a weak monsoon hurts them most. Irrigated farms can draw on canals, wells or pumps and are buffered against a poor monsoon. Rabi crops are sown in winter, not during the monsoon, so a weak monsoon affects them less directly, and crop rotation is a soil practice that does not itself demand heavy rain.

14. (B) The short, hot stretch between the rabi harvest and the kharif sowing is the zaid season, so a quick crop there is a zaid crop. A rabi crop occupies the cool winter, and a kharif crop is sown with the southwest monsoon, neither of which is the in-between window. A multi-year perennial is the opposite of a quick short-season crop.

15. (D) After spring comes the short hot zaid window, then the southwest monsoon brings the kharif season, and after the rains the cool rabi winter follows, so the order is zaid, kharif, rabi. Starting with winter or placing zaid after kharif breaks that calendar. The seasons follow one another rather than running all at once.

16. (B) Sowing with the June monsoon rains and harvesting as the cool season begins is the kharif pattern. Rabi crops are sown in the cool season and harvested in spring, the reverse timing. Zaid crops occupy the short hot gap, not the monsoon. Crops follow seasons closely, so the timing is a real clue to which season it belongs to.

17. (C) Kharif crops grow during the wet monsoon and can live on rain, whereas rabi crops grow in the dry winter when little rain falls, so they often need irrigation. Rabi crops need water like any crop; irrigation supplies it, it does not trick the plant. The monsoon falls in the rainy season, not winter, and kharif crops in such regions rely largely on monsoon rain rather than on canals.

18. (A) Because irrigation supplies water by artificial means, it cushions farms against a weak or late monsoon, so expanding it directly reduces monsoon-driven crop failures. Banning monsoon crops would waste the season's rain rather than help. Pushing more reliance on rainfall would increase, not reduce, vulnerability. And growing one crop every year drains soil nutrients and does nothing for water supply.

19. (C) Alluvial soil is fertile silt deposited by rivers and supports good yields because it carries nutrients and retains moisture. Even fertile soil supplies nutrients to plants; that is precisely why it is fertile. Alluvium is river-borne silt, not wind-blown desert sand, and rather than blocking roots it makes a productive, workable bed for them.

20. (D) Humus is decomposed plant and animal matter; mixing it in supplies nutrients and improves the soil's ability to hold moisture, so the crop does better. It does not dry out the soil; it helps retain water. It loosens and enriches soil rather than hardening it into rock, and it supports, not destroys, the living matter that keeps soil healthy.

21. (D) Crop rotation means growing different crops in turn so that no single nutrient is exhausted, which is exactly the fix for falling yields from monocropping. Repeating the same hungry crop would drain the soil further. Withholding humus removes a source of nutrients rather than helping, and replacing fertile topsoil with sand destroys the very thing that grows crops.

22. (B) Rotation works because different crops draw on and return different nutrients, keeping the soil's nutrient balance from being drained, which is why the following cereal does better. Rotation does not mean spinning the field; it means changing the crop grown. It conserves nutrients rather than stripping them all, and it keeps the field productive rather than ending cultivation.

23. (C) Terrace farming cuts a slope into level steps so that rainwater is held on each step and soil is not washed away, letting crops grow on steep ground. The steps still need soil and water; they manage them better. They conserve soil rather than turning land to sand, and they do nothing to remove the need for seeds, which every crop requires.

24. (A) The honest comparison is a trade-off: saved seeds cost little and fit local conditions, while bought high-yield seeds can lift yields but bring extra cost and usually more water and chemicals, which is why many farmers blend the two. Saved seeds do not always out-yield modern ones, bought seeds are not free and often do demand more inputs, and seeds clearly differ, so the choice does matter.

25. (D) A rain-fed farm depends entirely on the monsoon, so a weak or late monsoon is its chief threat. The other risks all assume irrigation equipment she does not have: there is no canal to flood the field, no pump to break and no drip system to clog. That is exactly why irrigation, where available, makes a farm less vulnerable.

26. (B) A bund is a low raised embankment that traps water in the field and reduces soil erosion, which is why farmers build them as part of water and soil management. It is not a seed, since it does not grow; not a chemical, since it does not kill pests; and not a crop, since it is an earthen structure, not a plant.

27. (C) The mix of ancient tanks and bunds with modern canals and wells shows that storing and supplying water for crops has been a concern across the ages, tackled by both old and new means. It was not invented in the Green-Revolution era, and the very existence of these structures proves farmers thought hard about water. Water management matters even in regions that get seasonal rain, not only where it never rains.

28. (C) The chapter stresses that in rural India most agricultural operations are carried out by women, so a survey of who does the hands-on field work would mostly find women. It is not done by men only, nor by machines only, since much of this labour is manual, and it is local rural labour rather than imported foreign workers.

29. (D) Since women do most of the farm work in rural India, picturing the typical farmer only as a man misrepresents who actually farms, so a woman is at least as representative. The correction is not that tractors are never used or that men never farm, both of which overstate the case, and Indian farming is far from fully mechanised, so machines have not replaced people.

30. (B) If much of women's work is unpaid and unrecorded, the records understate it, yet women still perform a large share of the real work, so their contribution is undercounted rather than small. Unpaid work is still genuine farm labour, so it is wrong to say only paid male labour counts, and farming plainly still depends heavily on human labour.

31. (C) Such a policy fails because a large part of farm work is done by women and by landless labourers who may not own land, so assuming every farmer is a male landowner leaves them out. It is not because women contribute nothing, which is false, nor because all farmers are wealthy owners, and farm labour is still essential, so support for workers matters.

32. (B) If women do most of the work, then training, tools and support aimed at them will reach the people actually farming, which is the sensible response. Withdrawing support from farms with women, or assuming women cannot benefit from better tools, would ignore the very workers carrying the load, and replacing them with imported labour neither follows from the fact nor reflects how rural farming operates.

33. (B) The correction is twofold: Indian farming is far from fully mechanised, and women continue to do most of the actual field work, so their role has not ended. Saying machines do everything overstates mechanisation, claiming women never worked the fields contradicts the chapter, and asserting men always did all the work is the very misconception being challenged.

34. (A) Women are central to rural farming because they do the majority of the physical field tasks, from sowing to harvest. It is not because they own most farmland, since land ownership is often not in their name, nor because they alone run heavy machinery. And they are very much in the fields doing the work, not merely managing from a distance.

35. (D) The Green Revolution paired high-yielding seed varieties with irrigation, chemical fertilisers and pesticides, and farm machinery to lift food-grain production. It was precisely not a return to saved seeds without chemicals, nor a no-new-seed system of hand tools and rain. And it concerned farming inputs and yields, not a change in how goods were traded.

36. (D) Its great success was helping India feed itself by sharply raising food-grain output, reducing dependence on imports. It did not remove the need for water; in fact it relied heavily on irrigation. It did not make all farmers wealthy, and many later faced debt, and it boosted farming rather than ending it.

37. (A) Output did rise, so the assertion is true, but the claim that there were no harmful side effects is false: the Green Revolution also degraded soil, depleted groundwater, polluted soil and water, harmed health and pushed many farmers into debt. So the reason is wrong while the assertion stands, which rules out the options that call the assertion false or the reason true.

38. (B) The accurate verdict is mixed: yields rose, but at the cost of degraded soil, falling groundwater, chemical pollution, health harms and farmer indebtedness. It did not reduce the food supply, since output rose, and it certainly had measurable effects. It did not benefit soil and groundwater; those were among the things it harmed.

39. (B) Drawing water from underground faster than it is replenished lowers the water table, so heavy pumping depletes groundwater and threatens future supply. Pumping removes water, so it cannot raise the water table or flood the region, and it certainly affects groundwater rather than leaving it unchanged. It does not manufacture new underground rivers.

40. (A) Sustained heavy chemical use pollutes soil and water and wears down the soil's natural fertility, which is the chain the chapter describes. It does not make soil richer and cleaner; that is the reverse of what happens. Chemicals do not simply vanish without effect, and they have no power to increase rainfall.

41. (C) The pattern ties together: the intensive Green-Revolution methods lifted yields but also drained soil and groundwater and raised input costs, so the very same approach produced both the gains and the later troubles. The problems are clearly linked to the farming method, the region did adopt the revolution, and the debt traces to the high cost of seeds and chemicals, not to low yields.

42. (C) The push for sustainable methods comes from the long-term harm that chemical- and water-heavy farming did to soil, water and health, despite its yield gains. It is not because those methods produced too little food, since they raised output, nor because chemicals proved harmless, which is the opposite of what was found, and India has not abandoned farming.

43. (A) The balanced reply is that modern high-yield seeds can lift output but bring real costs in money, water and soil damage, which is exactly why many farmers blend traditional and modern methods. Modern inputs do have downsides, so 'no downsides' is wrong; modern seeds can raise yields, so 'never' is wrong; and the methods can be mixed, so a farmer is not forced to choose one forever.

44. (C) The fair lesson is a trade-off across time: chemical-boosted yields can give short-term output gains while imposing long-term costs on soil, water and health. Saying it brings only benefits ignores the documented harms, saying only costs ignores the real rise in food output, and it is plainly connected to the condition of soil and water, which it degraded.

45. (C) Organic farming answers the chemical problem by swapping chemical fertilisers and pesticides for natural ones such as compost, manure and neem. Using more chemicals is the opposite of organic. Organic farming still uses soil and supplies nutrients, just naturally, and it certainly does not abandon food production; it grows food differently.

46. (D) Choosing a natural input like neem over a synthetic pesticide is a hallmark of organic farming. The Green-Revolution package is defined by chemical inputs, the reverse of this choice. Selling crops abroad is unrelated to which kind of pesticide is used, and she is still controlling pests, just by natural means rather than abandoning the effort.

47. (C) Sustainable farming means meeting present needs while protecting soil, water and the capacity to farm in the future. Using up resources as fast as possible, or ignoring the future for this year's profit, is the very opposite of sustainability, and farming that produces no food today fails the 'meets today's needs' part of the definition.

48. (B) Blending traditional and modern methods lets a farmer earn decent yields while guarding the soil and limiting costs, which is exactly the balance the chapter highlights. The aim is to protect the land, not harm it; there is no law banning all modern tools; and the point is not to disprove chemicals but to combine the strengths of both approaches.

49. (D) Practices that protect and maintain soil health, such as rotation, adding humus and stopping erosion, are together called soil conservation. Irrigation is about supplying water, not protecting soil; exporting soil is not a farming practice; and mechanisation refers to using machines, not to conserving the soil itself.

50. (C) The defining difference is that the organic farm shuns synthetic fertilisers and pesticides and uses natural inputs instead. Organic wheat still grows in soil, and the organic farm does produce wheat, so the no-soil and no-wheat claims are wrong. Using more chemical pesticide would make it the chemical-intensive farm, not the organic one.

51. (A) Cutting chemical inputs and easing back on heavy irrigation reduces water pollution and slows groundwater depletion, so over time the groundwater situation can improve. Organic farming still uses water and soil, so 'no water, no soil' is wrong; natural inputs do not refill aquifers overnight; and organic crops do grow and use water, just more sustainably.

52. (A) A sensible middle path is to combine natural methods with measured modern inputs, balancing yield, soil health and cost while she transitions, which is just the blended approach the chapter endorses. Piling on chemicals worsens the long-term harm she is trying to escape, stopping farming sacrifices her livelihood, and ignoring soil health to chase yield repeats the Green Revolution's mistake.

53. (C) Organic and natural farming are framed as a response because they consciously cut chemical inputs after the Green Revolution exposed the damage those inputs caused. Older traditional farming did not rely on heavy chemicals, so the chemical era is the new thing being responded to; organic methods draw on long-known natural inputs rather than being invented in the 1960s; and natural inputs like manure and neem have been used for ages.

54. (B) Machines speed up heavy tasks and reduce manual labour, which is most useful on larger holdings, and that is the core benefit of mechanisation. It does not remove the need for soil, seeds or water, which crops still require; it does not guarantee wealth, since machines also cost money; and it does not restrict farming to one season.

55. (B) Heavy machinery fits larger holdings and can be costly or impractical on very small, fragmented plots, so the small farmer's caution is reasonable. Mechanisation is not equally easy and cheap for every farm, it is certainly used in India, so it is not impossible, and it reduces rather than wholly removes the need for human labour, which farms still rely on.

56. (A) Today's farmers face shrinking, fragmented land, climate change, unstable market prices, water stress and debt, and naming these is the accurate answer. The other options are unreal: farmers do not enjoy abundant land and perfect prices, they clearly do face difficulties, and India grows a wide range of crops, so 'no crops' is false.

57. (D) Earning little despite a good harvest because prices crash is the problem of uncertain market prices, a real challenge for farmers. It is not a weak monsoon, since the harvest was good, nor barren soil, since the field produced well, and the issue is the low price, not a total absence of buyers.

58. (D) Erratic rains, heatwaves and shifting seasons are signs of climate change, which makes the timing of farming unreliable. It is not an excess of irrigation water, nor soil becoming too fertile, which would not disrupt planning, and it is not a sudden loss of farm labour, which is a separate matter.

59. (D) Repeated division shrinks and scatters holdings, which makes efficient farming and the use of machinery harder, a real challenge the chapter notes. It does the opposite of enlarging farms, it does not improve soil fertility, and it has no power to guarantee higher crop prices, which depend on the market.

60. (A) The accurate summary holds three threads together: the Green Revolution's record was mixed, women do most rural farm work, and many farmers combine old and new methods. Calling the Green Revolution purely good ignores its harms and misnames the typical worker, claiming all farming is identical denies its real variety, and saying women do no work while machines do everything contradicts the chapter on both counts.
