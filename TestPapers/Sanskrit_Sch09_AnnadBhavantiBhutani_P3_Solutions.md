## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** Trace the four links: भूतानि come from अन्न; अन्न from पर्जन्य; पर्जन्य from यज्ञ; यज्ञ from कर्म. Rain (पर्जन्य) is the product of यज्ञ (यज्ञाद्भवति पर्जन्यः) and at the same time the source of food (पर्जन्यादन्नसम्भवः). So पर्जन्य is genuinely both a product and a source. भूतानि are only an end-product (the verse never makes them a source of कर्म within these four lines). कर्म is the source of यज्ञ, not the product of यज्ञ. अन्न is the source of भूतानि and the product of पर्जन्य — not the source of पर्जन्य. Only rain occupies both roles in the stated chain.

**2. (A)** पर्जन्यात् is पञ्चमी (ablative), 'from rain', and अन्नसम्भवः is 'the coming-into-being of food'. So पर्जन्यादन्नसम्भवः = 'from rain (comes) the arising of food' — food comes from rain. The ablative marks the source, so the direction is rain → food, never the reverse (that would require अन्नात् ... पर्जन्यसम्भवः). It is a sequence of dependence, not a simultaneous joint arising, and सम्भव means 'coming-to-be', the opposite of destruction.

**3. (C)** समुद्भव means 'origin / source of arising', and the compound कर्मसमुद्भवः (a बहुव्रीहि describing यज्ञः) means 'whose origin is action'. So यज्ञः कर्मसमुद्भवः = 'yajna has action as its origin' → yajna arises from action (कर्म → यज्ञ). The reverse direction (action from yajna) inverts the compound. The two are distinct stages of the cycle, not synonyms, and समुद्भव is about origination, not destruction.

**4. (A)** The true order (head to tail) is कर्म → यज्ञ → पर्जन्य → अन्न → भूतानि. The student's chain कर्म → भूत → पर्जन्य → अन्न → यज्ञ has put भूत where यज्ञ belongs and यज्ञ at the end where भूतानि belong: the two ends are swapped. कर्म is correctly the head (यज्ञः कर्मसमुद्भवः makes action the ultimate source), so it should not start from अन्न. पर्जन्य→अन्न is correct (rain → food), so saying rain should come from food is itself wrong. The chain is not correct as written.

**5. (D)** In the chain कर्म → यज्ञ → पर्जन्य → अन्न → भूतानि, yajna directly produces पर्जन्य (यज्ञाद्भवति पर्जन्यः). Remove yajna and the immediate next link, rain, fails first; the failure then propagates down to food and beings. Beings and food are downstream effects, so they fail later, not first. कर्म is upstream of यज्ञ (its source), so कर्म is not what fails when यज्ञ stops — it is what stops producing यज्ञ.

**6. (D)** भवन्ति ('they come to be'), सम्भवः and समुद्भवः ('arising, coming-into-being') are all built on भू ('to be / become'). Their shared sense is that each stage comes-into-being from the previous one — a chain of becoming/arising. It is not destruction (भू is 'be', not 'destroy'), the words are inflected forms with distinct meanings (not mere repetitions of the bare root), and nothing in the root concerns counting.

**7. (B)** Tracing back from the end: भूतानि←अन्न←पर्जन्य←यज्ञ←कर्म. The chain bottoms out at कर्म (action), which is the source of यज्ञ and thereby of everything downstream — so action is the ultimate root. भूतानि merely appear first in the verse's wording but are the END product, not the root. अन्न and पर्जन्य are mid-chain links, each depending on a further source (rain, then yajna), so neither is the root. The head of the chain is कर्म.

**8. (D)** An unbroken series of ablatives ('beings from food, food from rain, rain from yajna, yajna from action') makes every member depend on its source. The teaching is interdependence — nothing is self-standing. It is the opposite of self-sufficiency (each thing needs a source). The ablative marks origin, not command, so it is not a chain of orders. And a fixed, ordered chain is precisely not randomness.

**9. (C)** भवन्ति is 3rd-person plural ('they come to be'), so its subject must be a nominative plural — here भूतानि ('beings'), which agrees in number. अन्नाद् (= अन्नात्) is ablative ('from food'), an adverbial source, not the subject, despite standing first. भवन्ति is the verb itself, not a subject. अन्न is only the stem inside the ablative अन्नात् and is not a free nominative here. The subject is भूतानि.

**10. (A)** The rule is: a final unvoiced त् becomes the voiced द् before a voiced consonant such as भ्. Applying it to जगत् + भवति gives जगद्भवति, exactly parallel to अन्नात्→अन्नाद् before भ्. No-change (जगत्भवति) ignores the sandhi. Becoming the aspirate थ् is not what voicing does (थ् is unvoiced-aspirate). Becoming the nasal न् would only happen before a nasal (e.g. before न्/म्), not before भ्.

**11. (A)** Final त् voices to द् only before a VOICED consonant. त् itself is unvoiced, so before त् the original त् need not voice (e.g. तत् + तत्र → तत्तत्र, no voicing). ग्, द् and ज् are all voiced consonants and WOULD trigger the change (तत्+गच्छति→तद्गच्छति, etc.). The odd one out — the sound that does not trigger voicing — is the unvoiced त्.

**12. (D)** The ablative singular of the अ-stem वृक्ष is वृक्षात् (just as अन्न → अन्नात्). Before the voiced भ् its त् voices to द्: वृक्षात् + भवति → वृक्षाद्भवति, exactly parallel to अन्नात्→अन्नाद्. वृक्षम् is accusative ('the tree' as object), वृक्षस्य is genitive ('of the tree'), and वृक्षे is locative ('in the tree') — none is the ablative 'from the tree'.

**13. (B)** Splitting the sandhi reverses the voicing: the द् in अन्नाद् was originally the unvoiced त् of the ablative अन्नात्. So the underlying words are अन्नात् + भवन्ति. The द् is a sandhi product, not the real ending, so 'अन्नाद् + भवन्ति' mis-states the underlying form. There is no word अद्भवन्ति. And the ablative ending is -आत् (long ā), not -अत्, so अन्नात (short a) is wrong.

**14. (B)** Sandhi is purely a sound change at word-junctions; it never alters a word's grammatical case. अन्नात् stays ablative ('from food') even after it appears as अन्नाद्. So the claim that the sandhi turns it nominative is FALSE — and is the answer. The other three are all TRUE: त् does voice to द्, the trigger is the voiced भ्, and the ablative meaning is preserved.

**15. (B)** Final त् before the voiced भ् voices to द्, so सत् + भावः → सद्भावः (a real Sanskrit word meaning goodwill/good-disposition), exactly the rule behind अन्नात्→अन्नाद् before भ्. Leaving it unchanged (सत्भावः) ignores the sandhi; the nasal न् would appear only before a nasal sound; and च् would arise only before palatal च्/छ्, not before भ्.

**16. (D)** The ablative अन्नात् expresses origin/source: beings emerge OUT OF food. It is dependence, not agency — so the potter-and-pot image (an agent deliberately making) misreads the ablative as if it were an instrumental/agentive construction. Ownership would be genitive (अन्नस्य), and 'giving as a gift' would be a dative (अन्नाय) idea. Only 'emerge out of food as their source' matches the ablative.

**17. (C)** ग्रामात् in 'comes from the village' is ablative (point of origin); अन्नात् in 'from food beings arise' is likewise ablative (point of origin). They match. ग्रामम् (accusative) marks the destination one goes TO; ग्रामे (locative) marks where one IS; ग्रामस्य (genitive) marks possession ('of the village'). None of these matches the 'from' sense of अन्नात्.

**18. (B)** 'For / for the sake of' is the dative case, which for अन्न would be अन्नाय. But the verse has अन्नात्, the ablative ('from food'), so the meaning is that beings arise FROM food, not 'for food'. Hence the rendering is wrong. It is not genitive (that would be अन्नस्य, 'of food') nor accusative (अन्नम्, 'food' as object); the form अन्नात् is unmistakably ablative.

**19. (A)** 'Fall from the tree' needs the ablative of source/separation, वृक्षात् ('from the tree') — exactly the case used in अन्नात्. वृक्षम् is accusative (object/goal), वृक्षे is locative ('in/on the tree'), and वृक्षाय is dative ('for the tree'). Only वृक्षात् gives the 'from' meaning the sentence requires.

**20. (D)** The ablatives present the cycle as one of impersonal dependence, and the chapter grounds it in ब्रह्म — the neuter, all-pervading Absolute (सर्वव्याप्तः परमेश्वरः), not a personal maker. ब्रह्मा is precisely the personal creator-god the ablative framing avoids invoking. A human priest (ब्रह्मन्) is a ritual officiant, not the cosmic ground. And the chapter presents an ordered cycle, the opposite of random chance.

**21. (D)** भूत is the past passive participle of भू ('to be/become'), so it literally means 'become / having come to be'; a 'being' is thus 'one that has become'. It is past in sense, not future. It has nothing to do with a root meaning 'destroy'. And though Hindi भूत can mean 'ghost' (a thing feared), the Sanskrit derivation is from भू 'to be', not from भी 'to fear'.

**22. (A)** भवन्ति is a finite present-tense verb (3rd pl., 'they come to be'); भूतानि is a noun (neuter plural 'beings'), formed from the past participle भूत. So one is a verb and the other a noun, though both share the root भू. They are not both verbs, not both nouns, and the roles are not reversed (भवन्ति is the verb).

**23. (C)** भूत has two attested uses — 'being/creature' (Vedanta/Gita) and the folk-Hindi 'ghost'; CONTEXT decides which. In a Gita verse about how life arises from food, 'living beings' is the only sensible reading, so 'ghosts' is wrong here. The plural ending -आनि marks number, not which meaning applies. It overstates the case to say भूत can NEVER touch a ghost-sense (folk usage does). And भूत/भूतानि is neuter, not feminine, so the gender argument is also mistaken.

**24. (B)** भूतानि means all living beings — 'those that have become' — so the verse's claim embraces humans, animals and plants together. Restricting it to humans ignores the universal scope. Excluding plants 'because they don't eat' misreads the verse: plants too depend on food/nourishment in the cycle, and भूतानि includes them. 'Souls of the dead' is the ghost-sense, which the living-cycle context rules out.

**25. (A)** भूत is neuter, and neuter nouns share one form for nominative and accusative plural: भूतानि. So 'the beings' as object is still भूतानि. भूतान् is the masculine accusative plural (e.g. देवान्), wrong for a neuter. भूताः is the masculine nominative plural. भूतेन is singular instrumental ('by a being'). Only भूतानि fits the neuter accusative plural.

**26. (D)** The correct mapping is: ब्रह्म (neuter) = the impersonal all-pervading Absolute; ब्रह्मा (masculine) = the personal creator-god; ब्रह्मन् = a priest/Brahmin. The first distractor scrambles all three roles; the second swaps creator-god, priest and Absolute; the fourth wrongly flattens the gender and meaning distinctions the chapter insists on. Only the stated row is right.

**27. (D)** सर्वव्याप्तः = सर्व ('all') + व्याप्त ('pervaded/spread through'), i.e. 'pervading everything' — present everywhere. That is a statement about PRESENCE (omnipresence), not about power (सर्वशक्तिमान्), not about knowledge (सर्वज्ञ), and not about mercy (सर्वकरुणामय). The gloss highlights ब्रह्म's all-pervasiveness.

**28. (A)** The cycle is presented as impersonal interdependence grounded in the all-pervading Absolute, ब्रह्म (neuter). ब्रह्मा (masculine) is a specific personal creator-god, which would reframe the cycle as one being's handiwork — not what the chapter intends. ब्रह्मा is masculine, not feminine. A priest is ब्रह्मन्, not ब्रह्मा. And the two words are emphatically distinct, not identical.

**29. (C)** The one who conducts a ritual and chants is the priest — ब्रह्मन्. The neuter ब्रह्म is the impersonal Absolute, not a ritual officiant. ब्रह्मा is the creator-god, not a working priest. रसायन means chemistry and is irrelevant. Only ब्रह्मन् fits 'the priest who chanted'.

**30. (B)** Sanskrit neuter here marks ब्रह्म as beyond the personal he/she of masculine/feminine — an impersonal, all-pervading principle. Neuter does not mean 'weaker' (grammatical gender is not strength). It does not reduce ब्रह्म to a lifeless object; ब्रह्म is the conscious all-pervading power (चेतनशक्तिः). Nor is it plural — ब्रह्म is one Absolute, singular in number.

**31. (C)** The optative is marked by an -ई/-ईय- sign before the personal ending: ज्ञा's stem जानी- plus the optative gives जानीयात्, whereas the present is जानाति (stem जाना- + -ति). So the tell-tale of the optative is the -ईय- element. The initial जा- comes from the same stem and appears in both forms. A final -त् also appears in many present 3rd-singular forms (e.g. अभवत् etc.) and is not unique to the optative. Neither form takes a prefix, so 'absence of a prefix' distinguishes nothing.

**32. (A)** अवगच्छेत् is the optative of अव+गम् ('should understand'), the same mood as जानीयात् ('should know'). The gloss therefore preserves the MOOD (the 'should/ought' sense). Both are optatives, not past tenses. Both are 3rd-person SINGULAR, not plural. And both are active, not passive. The deliberate match is mood-to-mood.

**33. (B)** 'Should know' needs the optative जानीयात्, giving छात्रः उत्पत्तिक्रमं जानीयात् ('the student should know the order of creation'). जानाति is the plain present ('knows'), अजानात् is past ('knew'), and ज्ञास्यति is future ('will know') — none expresses the 'should/ought' advice that जानीयात् carries.

**34. (C)** वदेत्, चरेत्, जानीयात् are all optatives giving advice/rules ('should speak', 'should follow', 'should know') — moral counsel about what one OUGHT to do. The optative does not report past events (that is past tense), does not narrate the present moment (present tense), and is gentler than a blunt command: a direct 'know!' is the imperative जानीहि / जानीतु, not the optative.

**35. (B)** The optative जानीयात् expresses gentle advice/obligation ('one should know'), while the imperative जानीहि is a direct order ('know!'). So the optative is the softer of the two — the distractor that reverses them is wrong. The forms differ (-ईयात् vs -ईहि) and the forces differ, so they are not identical. Neither is past tense; both concern what should/must be done, not what happened.

**36. (C)** जानीयात् (with the -ईय- optative sign) = 'should know'; जानाति (present stem जाना- + -ति) = 'knows'; ज्ञास्यति (with the future -स्य-) = 'will know'. The first distractor and the second both misassign the moods/tenses; the third wrongly makes all three optatives, ignoring the distinct present and future markers. Only the stated triple is correct.

**37. (C)** The gerundive ज्ञेय ('to be known') stands beside the past participle ज्ञात ('known') exactly as कार्य ('to be done') stands beside कृत ('done'). The -य suffix turns 'X-ed' into 'to-be-X-ed'. It is not a plural of the present जानाति, not a negative of जिज्ञासा, and not a future of अजानात्. The right counterpart is the participle ज्ञात.

**38. (B)** The gerundive pattern turns a past participle into a 'to-be-X-ed' word: कृत→कार्य, so ज्ञात→ज्ञेय ('to be known'). ज्ञानम् is the abstract noun 'knowledge'. ज्ञाता is the agent noun 'a knower / one who knows'. ज्ञात्वा is the absolutive/gerund 'having known'. Only ज्ञेय is the gerundive parallel to कार्य.

**39. (C)** The gerundive ज्ञेयः means 'to be known / ought to be known', taking the doer in the instrumental — 'उत्पत्तिक्रमः सर्वैः ज्ञेयः' ('the order of creation is to be known by all') uses it correctly. 'He knew...' would need a finite past verb (अजानात्), not the gerundive. The third option misuses it as an abstract 'knowledge'. The fourth treats ज्ञेयः as a proper name, which is not its grammatical function here.

**40. (D)** ज्ञेयः is a passive gerundive: it describes the OBJECT as 'that which ought to be known'. जानन् is an active present participle describing the DOER ('knowing / one who knows'). So they differ in voice and focus. ज्ञेयः is not past-completed (that is ज्ञात), nor a certain-future agent (no such certainty is implied) — its core sense is 'to-be-known'.

**41. (B)** Sanskrit has two gerundive suffixes, -य (ज्ञेय) and -तव्य; both mean 'to be X-ed'. So 'should be understood' is बोद्धव्यः (from बुध् + -तव्य), a true gerundive parallel to ज्ञेयः. बुध्यते is a finite present ('understands'), अबुध्यत is past ('understood'), and बोधः is a noun ('understanding') — none carries the 'ought-to-be-done' gerundive force that matches ज्ञेयः.

**42. (C)** In the Gita the food-cycle makes यज्ञ the link between कर्म and पर्जन्य, and the chapter reads it broadly as selfless, well-directed action (of which the literal fire-ritual is just one instance). Image-based devotional worship is पूजा, a different tradition. रसायनशास्त्रम् (chemistry) is unrelated to यज्ञ. And eating a meal is not what यज्ञ denotes. The broad sense is selfless action.

**43. (C)** By inserting यज्ञ between कर्म and पर्जन्य, the verse specifies that it is selfless, well-directed action (yajna), not just any action, that ultimately sustains rain → food → beings. Drop यज्ञ and the chain implies any कर्म whatever produces rain — the moral point (selflessness) is lost. It is therefore not merely 'shorter but identical'. Removing यज्ञ does not reverse पर्जन्य and अन्न, nor sever the food→beings dependence (अन्नात् भवन्ति still holds).

**44. (D)** The chapter distinguishes them by tradition: यज्ञ = the Vedic fire-offering (अग्नि-centred), पूजा = devotional worship often centred on an image/मूर्ति. The distinction is about the form of ritual, not about silence-vs-song, nor about the gender of the performer, nor a mere spelling difference (they are genuinely different practices).

**45. (D)** Both are forms of worship, but they belong to different ritual traditions: यज्ञ is the fire-offering ritual; पूजा is devotional, often image-based worship. So 'identical in every respect' is wrong. Neither word means chemistry (रसायनशास्त्रम्) or a herb (ओषधि). And both are nouns naming rituals, so the verb/noun objection is mistaken. The correction is the tradition-based distinction.

**46. (A)** The genitive plural ओषधीनाम् means 'of (the) herbs', marking the relation 'growth OF herbs'. 'From herbs' would be the ablative (ओषधिभ्यः); 'to herbs' the dative (ओषधिभ्यः as recipient sense); 'by herbs' the instrumental (ओषधिभिः). The -आनाम् ending is unmistakably genitive plural, so 'of herbs' is correct.

**47. (B)** ओषधि is a feminine i/ī-stem, and the ending -ईनाम् (with the lengthened ī before -नाम्) is the genitive plural ('of herbs'), giving ओषधीनाम्. It is not an a-stem (those make -आत् ablative singular / -ानाम् genitive plural differently) nor a consonant-stem neuter nominative plural, nor an u-stem accusative singular (that would be -उम्). The -ईनाम् pattern pins it as i/ī-stem genitive plural.

**48. (D)** The accusative case marks the direct object (कर्म), so उत्पत्तिक्रमम् ('the order of creation') is WHAT is to be known by जानीयात्. The subject (कर्ता) would be nominative (e.g. छात्रः). The instrument (करण) would be instrumental. The source (अपादान) would be ablative. The accusative ending fixes its role as the object.

**49. (B)** उत्पत्ति ('arising') + क्रम ('order/sequence') = 'the order of arising', i.e. the step-by-step sequence in which things come into being — exactly the chain action→yajna→rain→food→beings. It is about coming-to-be, not destruction. It denotes a sequence, not a single act. And it is precisely an order WITH a starting point (कर्म), not one lacking an origin.

**50. (D)** Herbs/plants (ओषधि) sit between rain and food: rain nourishes the plants, and plants/herbs become the food from which beings arise — they connect पर्जन्य to अन्न/भूतानि. They are not the source of yajna (that is कर्म). They are not a name for ब्रह्म (the Absolute). And they are not chemistry (रसायनशास्त्रम्). Herbs are a natural rain→food link.

**51. (A)** शास्त्रम् means a science, systematic discipline or authoritative treatise; so रसायन-शास्त्रम् is 'the science of रसायन' = chemistry. It does not mean a herb (ओषधि), a fire-ritual (यज्ञ), or the Absolute (ब्रह्म). The -शास्त्रम् component is what names it a 'science'.

**52. (C)** Reusing रसायन (an old Ayurvedic word) for the modern science shows Sanskrit's living power to coin precise new terms from old roots — exactly how English reaches for Greek/Latin to name sciences. It does NOT show Sanskrit borrowing from English (the term is Sanskrit-built). The two senses are linked through 'rasa/essence', not coincidental. And the point is terminological adaptability, not a claim that modern chemistry already existed in antiquity.

**53. (A)** रसायनशास्त्रम् = chemistry, and शास्त्रम् = science/treatise — both correct. Astronomy is खगोलशास्त्रम् and शास्त्रम् is not a herb. Grammar is व्याकरणम् and शास्त्रम् is not a ritual. रसायनशास्त्रम् is chemistry (not 'medicine only'), and शास्त्रम् is not the Absolute (that is ब्रह्म). Only the first pair is right on both counts.

**54. (A)** All three are ज्ञा-derivatives with distinct functions: जिज्ञासा is the desiderative noun 'wish to know'; जानीयात् is the optative finite verb 'should know'; ज्ञेयः is the gerundive 'to be known'. They are not all present-tense verbs. The second distractor mis-labels each. And they do share one root (ज्ञा), so 'three different roots' is false.

**55. (C)** जिज्ञासा (curiosity/the wish to know) is the spark; जानीयात् (one should know) is the recognised duty that follows — curiosity leads to the resolve to learn the उत्पत्तिक्रमम्. The sequence is wish → duty → knowing, not the reverse 'know first then lose curiosity'. Duty does not destroy curiosity; it grows from it. And both describe one learner's path, not two unrelated people.

**56. (A)** Checking each: (1) अन्नाद् is the ablative अन्नात् after sandhi, NOT nominative — wrong. (2) भूतानि here means 'beings/creatures', not ghosts — wrong. (3) जानीयात् is the optative 'should know', not past tense — wrong. So all three claims are mistaken, making 'all three are wrong' the answer; 'only one', 'only two', and 'none' all undercount the errors.

**57. (C)** In कर्म → यज्ञ → पर्जन्य → अन्न → भूतानि, a drought removes पर्जन्य (rain) first; that starves अन्न (food); and only when food fails do भूतानि (beings) suffer — beings are at the chain's END, so they are the last to be hit. Rain is removed first, not last. यज्ञ and कर्म are UPSTREAM of rain (its sources), so they are not what fails as a consequence of the drought.

**58. (D)** The correct parse: अन्नाद् is ablative ('from food'), भवन्ति is plain present ('they come to be'), भूतानि is nominative plural 'beings' — together 'from food, beings come to be'. The distractors each pile up errors: calling अन्नाद् nominative/accusative/dative (it is ablative), calling भवन्ति optative/past/future (it is present), and mistranslating भूतानि as ghosts/sacrifices/herbs (it is 'beings'). Only the first line is right on every count.

**59. (B)** Linking the verse to chemistry shows that ancient insight (the food-life cycle) and modern science can speak about one and the same world from different angles — they complement, not contradict. It does not claim chemistry refutes the verse, nor that the verse is secretly a chemistry text, nor that the two domains can never meet (the whole point is that they CAN converse).

**60. (A)** The full, accurate summary chains भूतानि←अन्न←पर्जन्य←यज्ञ←कर्म, grounds it in the all-pervading ब्रह्म, and frames the learner's duty with the optative जानीयात् ('should know'). The first distractor denies the dependence the verse asserts. The second smuggles in the ghost-misreading and a past-tense error. The third turns food into a personal creator-god (ब्रह्मा) and ignores the impersonal-ablative framing. Only the first option captures grammar and meaning together.
