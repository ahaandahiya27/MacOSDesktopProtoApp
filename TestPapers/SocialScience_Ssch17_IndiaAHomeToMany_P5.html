Worked Solutions

1. (A) The chapter's repeated pattern is identity-preserving integration: synagogues, fire temples and churches were kept WHILE the community became part of Indian society, so a separate house of worship signals belonging-with-identity, not enforced apartness. The option about being forced to abandon faith inverts the evidence, since the very survival of the building proves the faith was kept. The 'ruled from its own quarter' option imports conquest, which the chapter does not attach to these settled communities. The 'only modern times' option contradicts the premise the student already granted, that India received the community for over a thousand years.

2. (A) Arab merchants reached the west coast from the 7th century and the Siddis were brought enslaved from roughly the 7th century onward, both ancient; the Tibetan refugees are a 20th-century (1950s) arrival, so the modern case comes last. The first wrong option puts a 1950s arrival first, reversing the order. The second misreads the Siddi span by treating its 19th-century end as its start. The fourth places the Siddis after the 1950s Tibetans, contradicting their 7th-century-onward arrival.

3. (A) The 'world is one family' ideal is matched in the chapter to the concrete enrichment of Indian life by borrowed foods, words, crafts, music and faiths, directly answering the 'purity' argument. The atithi-devo-bhava option misstates the evidence, since newcomers KEPT their customs rather than dropping them. The tolerance option falsely says the Siddis were held apart, when they were drawn into Indian society and constitutionally protected. The pluralism option invents expulsions the chapter never describes.

4. (D) The Parsis fled religious persecution and the Polish children fled war, so both fall under refuge; the Armenians came chasing trade, which is opportunity; and the Siddis fit neither path because they were brought enslaved. Treating all four as traders erases the refugee and enslaved cases entirely. Pairing Armenians with danger misfiles a trading community. The last option correctly groups Parsis and Polish children but wrongly turns the enslaved Siddis into traders.

5. (B) The chapter dates the peaceful Arab-merchant arrival to the 7th century, far earlier than the military invasions, which is exactly why they are distinct events. Saying the invasions came first reverses the timeline. Denying the peaceful arrival contradicts India's oldest mosque, the physical proof of that early trade. Calling them the same moment in two descriptions collapses the centuries-long gap the chapter insists on.

6. (B) The mosque marks where Arab merchants peacefully settled in the 7th century, evidencing that faith spread through trade and settlement before any invasions. The conquest option contradicts the peaceful nature of that settlement. The Siddi option misattributes the mosque to a different, enslaved community. The Parsi option confuses Zoroastrian refugees with the Arab-merchant Muslims and invents a conversion the chapter never mentions.

7. (D) The Mappila community formed peacefully as Arab merchants settled and married into local families, while the Siddis were brought against their will as enslaved people, so the contrast is free settlement versus enslavement. The first wrong option simply swaps the two. The 'armies' option imports conquest into communities the chapter describes as forming through trade or enslavement. The 'refugees' option misfiles both, since neither began as religious refugees.

8. (C) A community brought enslaved later gained constitutional recognition and protection, showing acceptance reached even those who came by force, which is the strongest version of the chapter's openness theme. The 'only freely arriving' option excludes exactly the enslaved case that makes the point. The 'permanently outside' option is the opposite of scheduled-tribe recognition. The 'usefulness in trade' option invents a transactional test the chapter never applies.

9. (C) Scheduled-tribe status is constitutional recognition giving a community special support and protection, which fits the Siddis. The trading-guild option confuses protection with commercial privilege. The 'barred from settling' option is the reverse of recognition and protection. The 'ruling dynasty' option mistakes a protected community for a governing power.
