Worked Solutions

1. (A) A 30-year January holds about 30 x 31 = 930 daily maxima. Swapping one 19-degree day for a 31-degree day adds 12 degrees spread over 930 values, so the mean moves by about 12/930, near 0.01 degrees C — essentially nothing. That is exactly why climate, an average over many years, barely twitches at one freak day; treating the extreme as the new average, or splitting the difference, both ignore the huge denominator, and the record stays fully usable, refuting the 'meaningless' claim.

2. (B) Stocking fuel for a whole year is a long-term decision, so it must track the recurring climate (Report 2's reliable four cold months), not a one-off weather event. A twenty-minute hailstorm is weather and cannot anchor a yearly plan; cost matters operationally but the question is which climate-or-weather signal to use; and averaging a single afternoon into a multi-month climate pattern mixes incompatible time-scales and dilutes the real seasonal signal.

3. (C) Two consecutive Julys are two samples of weather; a real climate trend needs many years averaged together, so one wet July and one dry July prove nothing permanent. Rainfall genuinely is a climate element when averaged, so that distractor is false; whether July is wettest varies by region and is not the error here; and counting rainy days is a perfectly valid summary — the flaw is the leap from two years to 'for good,' not the choice of measure.

4. (C) Wait — S averages near 26, not 18; only T's wildly scattered values (8 to 27) average near 18, and equal-looking means can mask opposite climates: S is remarkably stable, T is highly variable. So a shared mean does not imply a shared climate. T's variability is itself a climate feature, not an absence of climate; and S still has daily weather despite its steady yearly mean — a stable climate is not a dead one.

5. (A) A single morning's fog is the day-to-day state — weather; a pattern repeating 'every year' is the multi-year average — climate. The two describe different time-scales of the same place and are fully compatible. Naming the place does not make a statement climate; not everything observable outdoors is mere weather (the yearly pattern is climate); and fog on one morning sits comfortably inside a snowy-winter climate, so there is no contradiction.

6. (A) Extreme single days are weather; a 20-year mean is climate, and the mean can stay flat while individual days roam far from it. There is no need for the two records to 'cancel' — hundreds of ordinary days hold the average steady regardless of two outliers; a flat mean does not make a climate unstable, it shows the opposite; and genuine extremes are normal weather, not necessarily instrument errors.

7. (C) An imminent personal choice tracks short-term weather (the half-hour forecast), while a recurring yearly budgeting choice tracks the repeating climate (which months usually flood). Not every decision is long-term, so (i) is not climate; not every decision is immediate, so (ii) is not the present forecast; and reserving a yearly fund is inherently about the seasonal pattern, not whether rain starts in the next thirty minutes.

8. (B) A desert's defining feature is low rainfall on the multi-year climate average; an occasional rainy day is weather and does not disqualify the climate. Deserts can and do receive rare rain, so 'ever receives rain' is wrong; low rainfall, not temperature alone, defines a desert (cold deserts exist); and rainfall is part of weather day-to-day and climate when averaged, so that final option misstates the concept.

9. (C) A pattern averaged and repeated over many years is climate; a single day's minute-to-minute changes are weather. Being measured does not make data climate — the time-span and averaging do. Covering more dates does not flip weather into climate by itself unless it is averaged over years, but here the long repeating curve IS the climate and the single day is weather, so the swap is wrong. And although temperature is a weather element, a ten-year averaged seasonal cycle is a climate description.

10. (A) Climate is the long-term average; weather is the daily variation that still occurs around it. A flat fifteen-year mean does not freeze every day at 27 — individual days swing above and below. Climate does not 'replace' weather; the two describe different time-scales of the same place. And a steady average is the signature of a stable climate, the opposite of rapid change.

11. (C) Ranges are Mon 29-18=11, Tue 26-19=7, Wed 37-16=21, Thu 34-28=6, Fri 30-20=10. The smallest is Thursday at 6 degrees. Wednesday's 21 is the LARGEST, not the smallest, and the highest maximum is irrelevant to range; Tuesday's 7 is close but still larger than Thursday's 6, and the lowest maximum does not by itself give the smallest range; Monday's 11 is far from the smallest, and being the earliest day is incidental. The smallest range is found only by computing max-minus-min for every day.

12. (D) Rain fell on Tue, Fri, Sat, Sun = 4 days; total = 8+12+36+4 = 60 mm; average per RAINY day = 60/4 = 15 mm. The first distractor counts dry days as rainy; the second computes the correct totals but wrongly divides by 7 instead of 4 for 'per rainy day'; the third drops a genuine rainy day and miscounts the total. The phrase 'per rainy day' fixes the divisor at the number of days it actually rained.

13. (A) Daily ranges: 27-17=10, 31-19=12, 36-22=14, 30-18=12; their average is (10+12+14+12)/4 = 48/4 = 12. Averaging the maxima alone gives a temperature, not a range; subtracting the lowest min from the highest max gives one giant span across all days, not the average daily range; and using only two days ignores half the data. The average daily range is the mean of each day's own max-minus-min.

14. (A) The max-min thermometer uses index markers left behind at the highest and lowest temperatures, so it stores both extremes unattended. A reading at one instant cannot reveal the whole day's extremes; the instrument records, it does not need hourly human sampling and averaging; and rainfall has nothing to do with temperature extremes — it is measured by a separate rain gauge.

15. (D) Once the jar overflows, the excess is lost, so the recorded figure is only the minimum that fell — the real total could be far higher. 'Exactly 120' treats a saturated instrument as exact; 'at most 120' is backwards, since the true amount is at least that; and an overflow does not signal that rain stopped — water kept arriving and simply spilled. An instrument can only certify within its capacity.

16. (A) A hygrometer measures humidity (68%); an anemometer measures wind speed (24 km/h). Temperature comes from a thermometer and sunshine from a sunshine recorder, not the hygrometer or anemometer; rainfall comes from a rain gauge, not a hygrometer, and humidity is not measured by an anemometer; and the final option simply swaps the two correct instruments. Each element has its own dedicated tool.

17. (C) Relative humidity falls as air warms because warm air can hold more moisture, so the same vapour is a smaller percentage of its larger capacity — the afternoon heat (which lags the noon Sun) drives the 14:00 dip. The dip need not mean less actual vapour escaped 'upward'; hygrometers do not self-reset on a schedule; and wind does not empty an instrument of its reading. The cause is rising temperature lowering relative humidity.

18. (C) For the same storm, a wide mouth catches more litres than a narrow one, but the DEPTH (volume divided by mouth area) is identical, so depth is mouth-size-independent and comparable everywhere. Depth measures amount, not speed; litres certainly can be measured, depth is just the better invariant; and a wide gauge collects MORE litres, not fewer — the point is that depth removes that dependence entirely.

19. (D) Even past noon, incoming heat exceeds outgoing heat for a while, so stored heat in the ground keeps the air warming until balance is reached near 2 p.m. The Sun is not nearer the Earth at 2 p.m.; a properly working thermometer does not lag by hours; and humidity does not fall to zero at 2 p.m., nor would dry air alone explain a timed peak. The lag comes from the delay between peak sunlight and peak stored-heat release.

20. (B) All night the ground radiates away heat with nothing coming in, so cooling accumulates and the minimum arrives after the longest cooling period — just before dawn. The Earth's distance from the Sun does not cycle within one night; the Moon does not draw heat from the ground; and a sound thermometer does not drift down on its own. The lag of the minimum past midnight comes from continued heat loss without resupply.

21. (A) Same time, different sunlight, different temperature — so sunlight, not the clock, drives the heating. The clock is identical for both and cannot be the cause; cold air does not selectively avoid sunlit ground; and the difference is real, not a fault — two correct thermometers SHOULD differ when one is sunlit and one is shaded. Holding time constant isolates sunlight as the heating agent.

22. (B) Slanting rays spread their energy thin over a larger patch of ground, weakening the heating — this is true both at the poles and at a winter noon, where the Sun sits low. Distance to the Sun barely changes seasonally and is not the cause; winter days are shorter, not longer; and air is not seasonally 'thicker' in a way that blocks sunlight. The shared cause is ray angle, not distance, hours, or air thickness.

23. (A) Uneven solar heating makes warm air rise, creating pressure differences that move air as wind, and it evaporates water that condenses and rains — one energy source, two effects. Rays do not mechanically push air to make wind; the Sun WARMS, not cools, water to evaporate it; and the Moon does not drive weather. The whole engine runs on uneven heating plus evaporation.

24. (D) Direct overhead equatorial rays pack energy into a small area (hot); slanting polar rays spread the same beam over more area (cold) — the per-area intensity is the key. The two do not receive equal energy per area; long polar summer days cannot overcome the steeply slanting, weak rays; and slanting rays carry LESS energy per area than direct ones, not more, so the final option inverts the physics.

25. (B) The atmosphere is chaotic: small errors amplify, so a specific distant day is unpredictable, but the long-run averaged climate (the season) is stable and forecastable. The Sun does not weaken a year ahead; climate is very much studied, indeed it is what makes the seasonal forecast possible; and thermometers work fine beyond a day — the limit is the growth of uncertainty, not the instruments.

26. (C) Hot deserts are very hot by day with wide swings and minimal rain — City B (46/7, 90 mm) fits. City A's constant heat and 2400 mm of rain is a tropical rainforest, not a desert; City C with sub-freezing temperatures is a COLD/polar type despite low rain; and City D is a moderate, semi-humid place, not a hot desert. Both 'hot' and 'dry' must hold together, which only B satisfies.

27. (B) Steady heat, very high humidity, heavy spread-out rain and a tall canopy are rainforest signatures; grasping limbs suit climbing and loud calls beat poor visibility among leaves. Polar regions are freezing, not 24-29 deg C; deserts are dry, not 2300 mm, and tails do not store water; and snowfields lack a dense warm canopy. Only the rainforest reading ties every clue together.

28. (A) Fat insulates, a compact body has low surface-area-to-volume to retain heat, and white fur camouflages in snow — all cold-climate, heat-conserving traits, so a rainforest would overheat the animal and its white coat would betray it against green. They do not shed heat (the opposite); white is camouflage for snow, not green leaves; and fat is energy and insulation, not a water store. The shared purpose is surviving cold.

29. (C) A compact body has less surface per unit volume, so it radiates heat away more slowly — ideal for the cold; the long, large-tailed body of Y loses heat too quickly. In freezing cold, soaking up faint Sun cannot offset rapid heat loss; shape strongly governs heat loss; and a compact body exposes LESS surface, not more. Surface-area-to-volume is the deciding rule.

30. (D) Wide paws do double duty — distributing weight on snow and paddling in water — while fur and fat together insulate against cold air and water. Fat insulates and stores energy, not primarily for floating to ambush; dense fur conserves warmth rather than cools; and swimming has nothing to do with climbing trees, which polar bears do not do. Each feature's main job is heat conservation or movement on ice and water.

31. (D) The white coat camouflages the bear against snow so it can approach prey unseen; it is not a heat-absorber. White surfaces reflect rather than absorb the most heat, so the first option is physically wrong; the coat is not for being spotted by other bears; and camouflage exists precisely so prey does NOT see the bear coming. Its job is concealment in a snowy climate.

32. (C) Packed together, each bird shields the others, cutting the surface exposed to wind and cold, so the group conserves heat. The aim is to lose less heat, not shed excess; huddling on ice has nothing to do with herding fish; and trapping COLD air to cool themselves would be self-defeating in freezing weather — they conserve warmth, not invite chill.

33. (C) Ear size tunes heat exchange via surface area: big thin ears radiate heat away in the heat; small ears minimise heat loss in the cold. Hearing is not the climatic point here; large ears in the heat SHED heat, they do not trap it; and ear size tracks climate, not merely body size. Surface area for heat exchange is the unifying rule.

34. (D) Both colourings are camouflage matching the background — green leaves, white snow — to hide from predators or prey. Colour here is about concealment, not warming the body; neither animal glows in the dark; and camouflage works by blending in, the opposite of being the brightest, most startling colour. The shared principle is background-matching.

35. (C) Grasping limbs, a balancing tail and loud calls are all rainforest tools — for climbing trees and signalling through foliage — useless on treeless ice. None of these traits aids cold survival; they are not sand-burrowing tools either; and the monkey cannot fly. Its whole body plan is matched to a warm forest, not the polar climate.

36. (D) A warm, wet, year-round-stable climate offers plentiful, diverse food and shelter, so many species can live together — that is why rainforests are so biodiverse. Cold does not speed reproduction; rainforests are densely PLANTED, which is the basis of their abundance, not bare of plants; and biodiversity is set by climate and resources, not by tourist numbers.

37. (B) A slender body and big thin ears maximise surface area, and short fur gives little insulation — all good for losing heat, so a hot climate. Large ears RELEASE heat, they do not hold warmth; a slender body loses heat fast, the opposite of conserving it; and shape and ear size strongly affect heat loss. The whole plan is built for shedding heat.

38. (B) Water heats and cools slowly and supplies moisture, so a coast has a gentle range and high humidity, while the inland site, lacking that buffer, swings widely and stays dry. Altitude is not stated and is a separate factor; being inland does not bring a place closer to the Sun; and the contrast is a year-long climate difference, not an artefact of which days were sampled.

39. (A) Big day-to-day jumps in sunshine over six days are weather variability, not a climate shift — six days cannot show a climate change. The figures are obviously not constant; and they are clearly NOT the same each day, so cloud cover plainly varied. The lesson is the rapid changeability of weather over a short span.

40. (D) Scorching days, sharp night cooling, big swings and almost no rain are hot-desert signatures — clear dry air heats and cools fast. Heat does not imply wet, so it is not a rainforest; polar climates are persistently cold, not 48 deg C by day; and a coast would show a SMALL range and more humidity, the opposite of wide swings and dryness.

41. (D) Insulating fat suits cold but overheats a rainforest animal; grasping limbs suit forest trees but find nothing to grip on open ice — each is a help in one climate, a hindrance in the other. A grasping tail does not grip ice for warmth; fat is not rain-cushioning; and both features are very much climate-dependent, not universal.

42. (C) Low surface-area-to-volume conserves heat (compact polar body); high surface-area-to-volume sheds heat (lean, big-eared desert body) — one rule, opposite directions. The first option inverts both: compact bodies lose heat SLOWER, lean ones FASTER; shape is about heat, not just apparent size; and the lean-versus-round contrast is driven by climate heat needs, not food abundance.

43. (B) Migration tracks the predictable seasonal climate — escaping the northern winter, returning as it warms — not the weather of one chosen day. The same individuals return year after year, so they do not die out and respawn; and animals do not transform into different species with the season. The recurring seasonal cycle (climate) governs the journey, not a single day's weather.

44. (D) The repeating seasonal climate cycle drives the yearly round trip, and birds usually leave on cues like shortening days and dwindling food, departing ahead of the worst cold. A single day's random weather does not govern a yearly cycle; the birds do not settle permanently — they return each spring; and the cue is a broad seasonal signal, not one tree's leaves nor a never-repeating event.

45. (D) Depth equals collected volume divided by the funnel's mouth area; a wide funnel catches more volume over a larger area, a narrow one less over a smaller area, and the depths come out equal. So the wide gauge does not read deeper; the narrow gauge does not either; and far from being incomparable, equal depths are precisely what makes cross-station comparison fair — that is the whole point of using depth.

46. (C) Climate (the multi-year averaged wettest month) is predictable, while one specific distant day is not, because small uncertainties amplify in the chaotic atmosphere. The Sun is not selectively unpredictable on certain days; rainfall does not obey the calendar like a rule with random exceptions; and the limitation is the growth of uncertainty, not faulty instruments. Averaged pattern is forecastable; a single far-off day is not.

47. (D) Place 1 hovers near 26-27 (small swings) while Place 2 leaps between 16 and 45 (large swings); a shared average conceals this real climatic difference in variability. A matching mean does not make climates identical; high variability is itself a climate feature, not an absence of climate; and a steady place still has daily weather — small swings are not zero swings.

48. (B) Humidity (71%) is precisely the water-vapour content of the air. Rainfall of 0 mm only says no rain fell, not how much vapour is present; a high temperature raises the air's CAPACITY for vapour but does not by itself state the amount held; and wind speed measures air motion, not its moisture. Humidity is the dedicated vapour measure.

49. (B) Freezing temperatures, snow and a very long night signal a polar climate; warm-and-wet with a dense forest signals tropical rainforest. A wide range does not make the freezing report a hot desert, and precipitation alone does not make the second polar; precipitation does not make both tropical (snow is polar); and a max just above zero with a 19-hour night is polar, not a temperate coast, while a warm forest is rainforest, not desert.

50. (A) A single cold day is weather; a polar climate requires year-round cold, so one freezing day proves nothing. Cold days occur in many non-polar places, so the first option is false; one cold day cannot establish a climate, so the child is not right; and temperature is central to defining a polar region, so the last option is wrong. Weather versus climate is the key distinction.

51. (A) A heritable colour that consistently matches the leafy background across generations and improves survival is an adaptation, not chance. Green colour does not warm the frog; it is not a desert reflectance trait; and frogs do not glow to lure prey in the dark. The reliable, inherited background-match with a survival benefit is the hallmark of adaptation.

52. (A) An adaptation is heritable, long-term and climate-matched — polar bears inheriting fur and fat suited to a cold climate fit exactly. Feeling warm one afternoon is a momentary experience, not a heritable feature; learning to open fruit is a one-time learned behaviour, not inherited; and flying indoors once is a single act, not a heritable trait. Only the inherited, climate-fitting feature qualifies.

53. (D) A light reaching beak lets the toucan feed at the tips of thin branches; thick fur insulates the bear in cold. A beak is not a fat store, and fur conserves warmth rather than cools; toucans do not dig burrows, and the bear lives among ice, not green leaves; and a beak does not heat the head, nor does fur improve swimming speed. Each feature's real job is feeding and insulation respectively.

54. (B) Four months (Jun-Sep) carry the bulk of the rain while the rest are dry — a strongly seasonal monsoon-type pattern, not even spread. A large yearly total alone does not make it a rainforest, since rainforest rain is spread through the year, not crammed into four months; small monthly figures in a warm region do not make it polar; and 'at least some rain each month' does not mean evenly spread, since June's 260 dwarfs March's 4. The defining feature is concentration into a season.

55. (A) The within-day variation is the range = max - min = 33 - 19 = 14 deg C. The maximum alone is just the peak, not the variation; the minimum alone is just the floor; and adding the two gives a meaningless 52, not a variation. Variation within a day is the difference between the extremes, the range.

56. (C) Direct equatorial sunlight heats and drives evaporation feeding wet rainforests, while slanting polar sunlight leaves the poles cold and icy — the three facts cohere. The poles are cold, not hot, and ice forms there, not at the equator; the equator is hot, not cold, and rainforests need warmth and rain; and the placement of rainforests and ice caps is clearly tied to sunlight intensity, not accidental.

57. (B) For a while past noon, incoming heat still exceeds heat lost, so the ground and the air above keep warming until balance, producing the afternoon peak. The Sun is overhead at noon, not mid-afternoon; air does not 'travel down from the Sun'; and clouds do not reliably gather at noon every day. The lag is a heat-storage effect, not a Sun-position or cloud effect.

58. (B) Solar heating evaporates water faster where it is warmer, so the sunlit puddle vanishes first — and that same Sun-driven evaporation is the first step in cloud and rain formation. The shaded puddle did not selectively sink while the other was pushed sideways; sunlight does not have weight that presses water out; and the puddles plainly lost different amounts, since one is dry and one is wet. Evaporation rate rises with the Sun's heat.

(B) A genuine coast has high humidity and a small range because the sea adds moisture and damps temperature swings; L's low humidity and wide range mark it as inland, contradicting the 'coastal' label. Nearness to the sea strongly affects both humidity and range, so the first option is false; mountains behind L block sea moisture rather than add it; and the sea supplies moisture rather than removing it, so the last option inverts the physics.
