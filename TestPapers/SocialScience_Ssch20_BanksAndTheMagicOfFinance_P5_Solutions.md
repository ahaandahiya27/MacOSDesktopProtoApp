## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** A bank and a stock exchange both move and manage money, so both belong to financial infrastructure; the railway (moving goods) is physical and the hospital (a social service) is social. Pairing the railway with the bank is wrong because a bank does not carry freight. Calling all four financial is wrong because only the money-handling facilities qualify; cost of building does not decide the type. Putting the hospital with the stock exchange is wrong because a hospital serves people directly (social), not money.

**2. (B)** Finance complements physical infrastructure: it supplies the money to build and use ports and grids, so Region X gets fuller use of what it has. Demolishing the physical assets is wrong because finance funds and operates them rather than replacing them. 'No advantage' is wrong because the chapter's whole point is that the two reinforce each other. 'Only the richest' is wrong because inclusive banking (Jan Dhan) is meant to reach ordinary people too.

**3. (C)** A pipeline physically moves a resource (physical infrastructure); UPI moves money between bank accounts (financial infrastructure) — different categories. 'Same kind' is wrong because what is moved (a resource vs money) decides the type, not the speed. Swapping the labels is wrong: UPI moves money, not a physical resource. 'Only roads count' is wrong because schools, banks, markets and grids are all infrastructure of different types.

**4. (C)** A fixed deposit locks money for a set period at higher interest; a savings account suits everyday saving with easy withdrawals; a current account suits a business making many transactions. The second option misplaces every account. The third reverses the lock-in and everyday roles and treats a current account as a savings tool. Using a fixed deposit for all three fails because its lock-in prevents the easy withdrawals and frequent payments the other two purposes need.

**5. (B)** Banks keep only part of deposits as reserves and rely on a steady flow of new deposits and repayments, so withdrawals can be met without holding every rupee idle. 'Every rupee untouched' contradicts the fact that 80,000 was lent. Printing notes is the RBI's job, not a commercial bank's. Recalling the whole loan in a day is not how loans work — they are repaid over time, and the reserve-plus-flow system is what keeps withdrawals safe.

**6. (C)** Simple: 10% of 1,000 is 100 per year, so 300 over 3 years. Compound: 1,000 to 1,100 to 1,210 to 1,331, an interest of 331. The difference is 331 minus 300 equals 31. '0' wrongly assumes equal rates give equal interest. '100' is just one year's interest. '331' is B's whole interest, not the excess over A.

**7. (B)** Interest earned is 9% of the 1,50,000 lent equals 13,500; interest paid is 4% of all 2,00,000 deposits equals 8,000; net gain is 5,500. The first wrong option charges 9% on the full 2,00,000 and pays the wrong figure. The second pays only on the lent portion (6,000) rather than on all deposits. The last forgets the bank pays interest on deposits and lends only part of them.

**8. (C)** Doubling from square 1 gives 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024 on the 11th square. '22' wrongly adds 2 each time. '121' assumes a square-of-the-number pattern. '2048' is the 12th square, one doubling too far.

**9. (A)** Compounding means each year's interest is added and then itself earns interest, so the untouched sum grows much faster. 'Grows more slowly' confuses leaving money to compound with losing value. 'Same total' is wrong because time and compounding both matter, not the rate alone. 'Shrinks' is false — banks do not cut untouched balances; they add interest.

**10. (B)** Compounding: 1,000 to 1,200 (year 1), to 1,440 (year 2), to 1,728 (year 3). '1600' wrongly adds a flat 200 each year (simple interest). '1440' stops at two years. '1200' applies the interest only once.

**11. (A)** A bank charges borrowers more than it pays depositors and shares some of that gap with you as your interest. The government does not pay your deposit interest, and your money is not idle — it is lent out. Commercial banks do not print notes (only the RBI issues currency). Unrepaid loans are a loss to the bank, not a source of your interest.

**12. (B)** The original sum borrowed (1,00,000) is the principal; the extra 8,000 paid for the use of the money is the interest. Swapping them is wrong because the principal is what was borrowed, not the largest number. 'Dividend' applies to company profit shared with shareholders, not a borrower's payment. 'Benchmark rate' and 'reserve' are RBI/banking terms, not the parts of this loan.

**13. (C)** Deposits do not lie idle; banks lend most of them out for productive uses, so the money works in the economy. Buried cash earns nothing — deposits, not buried cash, earn interest. Banks do not burn money. Deposits fund local lending; they are not simply sent abroad.

**14. (B)** Jan Dhan brings savings, payments and credit within reach of those left out, so they can save safely and later borrow on fairer terms than a moneylender's. Forcing them into shares is wrong because shares carry risk and are not the scheme's aim. Banning borrowing is the opposite of widening access to credit. The scheme is about inclusion, not handing out free monthly money.

**15. (D)** The RBI was established in 1935 and the Jan Dhan Yojana launched in 2014; 2014 minus 1935 is 79 years. '50 years' miscomputes the gap. '1950' is the wrong founding year for the RBI. Swapping the years (RBI 2014, Jan Dhan 1935) reverses the two events.

**16. (B)** The RBI is the banker to banks and the government, so individuals bank at commercial banks instead. It is not where citizens keep savings, so the first option is wrong. There is no adults-only RBI savings account — individuals do not bank there regardless of age. The RBI is central to money and banking (it issues currency and supervises banks), so saying it has no role is false.

**17. (D)** Issuing currency is a job of the RBI, not commercial banks. Accepting individual deposits, giving home loans and allowing cash withdrawals are all everyday commercial-bank activities, so none of those distinguishes the RBI.

**18. (C)** Banks bank with the RBI the way people bank with commercial banks — that is the meaning of banker to banks. The RBI does not compete for individual savers; it does not take personal accounts. It is the central bank, not owned by a commercial bank. It works with Indian banks, not only foreign ones.

**19. (B)** Supervision means making rules and monitoring banks so the system stays safe. The RBI does not run banks' daily branch work, does not compete for their customers, and does not issue company shares (that happens on the stock market).

**20. (C)** A higher benchmark rate raises banks' cost of funds, so they charge borrowers more and borrowing slows. 'Lend more cheaply' reverses the direction — a rise makes borrowing dearer, not cheaper. The RBI does not set every shop price directly. Share prices keep moving with companies and the market; they are not frozen by the rate.

**21. (D)** A rate cut lowers banks' cost of funds, so loans tend to get cheaper. 'Must rise' reverses the link. The RBI does not fix house prices. A dividend is a company-profit payment to shareholders, not something a borrower receives.

**22. (B)** A general rise in prices is inflation; raising the benchmark rate makes borrowing dearer, eases spending and helps check it. The second option uses the wrong tool direction — a cut, not a rise, speeds borrowing. Compounding is interest on interest, not a price rise. A dividend is company profit shared with shareholders, unrelated to prices.

**23. (D)** Stable prices keep money's buying power steady, protecting savings and incomes. Rising prices erode buying power rather than enriching everyone. Buying power very much affects daily life — that is why it matters. Higher inflation harms savers by reducing what their money buys, so it is not a benefit.

**24. (D)** A sharp rate rise raises loan costs, which cools borrowing and spending — the tension with affordability. Loans never become free; fighting inflation does not abolish interest. Borrowing is closely tied to rates, so it is affected. The RBI does not close the stock market to fight inflation.

**25. (D)** Cheques, electronic transfers and UPI are all ways to move existing rupees between accounts — only speed and method vary. None creates new money (only the RBI issues currency). They move money, not shares. No physical notes are mailed; the movement is between accounts.

**26. (C)** Electronic transfers settle almost instantly, while a cheque must clear, which is slower. Neither a cheque nor a transfer creates new money. Cheques are still legal to use, so a transfer is not the only legal method. Both a cheque and a transfer move money, not shares.

**27. (A)** A cheque orders your bank to pay a named person a set amount from your account. A unit of ownership in a company is a share, not a cheque. The RBI does not promise to print notes on a cheque. A record of added interest is a statement entry, not a cheque.

**28. (B)** UPI moves existing rupees from her account to the stall-owner's account in seconds. The phone does not print notes. UPI moves money, not shares. The stall-owner receives payment, not the buyer receiving a cheque.

**29. (A)** UPI is an Indian innovation for instant smartphone payments that others have taken up — hence a gift to the world. It was built in India, not copied from abroad. It is digital, not paper-based or older than the cheque. India's central bank is the RBI, not UPI.

**30. (C)** UPI is a payment method that moves rupees between accounts, not a new currency. It has not replaced the rupee. It is not barter — money is still used. It is not a share traded for goods.

**31. (B)** Twenty shares out of one lakh is a tiny fraction, so you are a very small part-owner. Owning the whole company would need all the shares. A shareholder is an owner, not an employee. A shareholder is a part-owner, not a lender owed fixed interest — that describes a depositor or bondholder, not a shareholder.

**32. (D)** A dividend is the portion of profit a company pays its shareholders. Interest is what a bank pays on a deposit, not what a company pays a shareholder. A shareholder is an owner, not a lender being repaid a loan. The payout is profit, not a refund of the purchase price.

**33. (A)** Shareholders gain through dividends and through a rise in the share's price (selling higher than the buy price). They do not earn fixed interest or a guaranteed refund — shares are not deposits. They are owners, not salaried employees. They are not repaid principal with interest like a depositor — that is the depositor's deal, not a shareholder's.

**34. (D)** Banks and the stock market both move and manage money, so both are financial infrastructure. Transport infrastructure moves goods and people. Social infrastructure serves people directly (schools, hospitals). Energy infrastructure carries power. None of these matches the money-handling role.

**35. (A)** A deposit gives Asha a safe, known return; shares suit Bina's appetite for risk and higher possible gains. Giving Asha shares ignores her wish for safety. 'Shares are always safe' is false — shares carry real risk. 'Deposits can lose value' is wrong — a deposit's return is known, unlike a share's.

**36. (C)** A deposit's interest is set by the bank (known), but a share's value moves with the company and market (uncertain). The first distractor reverses the two. Deposits pay interest, not a dividend, and a share's dividend is not fixed and equal. The RBI does not guarantee share gains.

**37. (A)** Shares carry real risk and can fall, unlike a deposit that earns fixed interest — so they are not 'sure to grow'. 'Never lose value' is false. A savings account is safer, not riskier, than shares. Shares and savings are not identical, so the claim is not correct.

**38. (D)** A genuine bank never asks for your OTP, so refuse and report to 1930. Sharing the OTP — or worse, the OTP and PIN — hands a fraudster the keys to your money. Sending a 'test payment' simply gives the fraudster your money.

**39. (B)** OTP, PIN and password are the keys to your money; a genuine bank never asks for them, so anyone who does intends fraud. Banks do not 'regularly need' them from you. Fraud is not limited to foreigners. These secrets protect ordinary bank accounts, not just share dealings.

**40. (D)** 'Bank' is from the Italian 'banca' (bench) where moneylenders worked; 'banca rotta' (broken bench) gave 'bankrupt'. Neither comes from a 'gold vault' word. They do not come from 'cheque' and 'currency'. 'To trust' is the root of 'credit', not of 'bank' and 'bankrupt'.

**41. (A)** Finance now means managing money through saving, lending and investing. Building roads and ports is physical infrastructure, not finance. Printing notes is one RBI job, not the whole meaning of finance. Barter is exchange without money, the opposite of money management.

**42. (B)** Simple: 10% of 5,000 is 500 per year, so 1,000 over two years. Compound: 5,000 to 5,500 to 6,050, an interest of 1,050. The difference is 1,050 minus 1,000 equals 50. '0' assumes equal rates give equal interest. '100' doubles the real gap. '500' is one year's simple interest, not the difference.

**43. (A)** Each act has its own nature: the deposit is safe with a known return, the share is risky, the loan must be repaid with interest, and UPI moves existing rupees. They do not all grow at one fixed rate — a share is not guaranteed. UPI creates no new money. A loan costs the borrower interest; it does not earn them interest like a deposit.

**44. (C)** Earned: 11% of 3,00,000 lent equals 33,000; paid: 5% of all 4,00,000 deposits equals 20,000; net gain 13,000. The first option wrongly charges 11% on the full 4,00,000. The second pays interest only on the lent portion (15,000) rather than on all deposits. The last ignores the interest paid to depositors entirely.

**45. (A)** Simple: 25% of 8,000 is 2,000 per year, so 4,000 interest over two years, ending at 12,000. Compound: 8,000 to 10,000 to 12,500, an interest of 4,500, ending at 12,500. The compound investor ends 500 ahead. '0' assumes equal results. '2000' is one year's interest. '4000' is the simple-interest total, not the difference.

**46. (A)** A bank, a stock market and a digital payments system all move and manage money (financial), while a power grid carries electricity (physical) — so the grid is the odd one out. The stock market is about money (shares represent money invested). A payments system moving money is exactly financial infrastructure. A bank's role is financial, not a social service like a school or hospital.

**47. (A)** A deposit earns interest and is lent out to work in the economy, unlike idle mattress cash. A bank deposit is safe, not riskier than home cash. A deposit does not turn into shares on its own. Banks do not print notes — that is the RBI's role — so the deposit is not 'printed into new notes'.

**48. (B)** All three are wrong: banks lend out most deposits (not untouched), the RBI serves banks and government (not personal accounts), and shares carry real risk (not sure to grow). 'Only one' and 'only two wrong' understate the errors. 'None wrong' endorses three misconceptions.

**49. (C)** A fixed deposit stays put for a set period, so the bank can lend it for longer and rewards that with higher interest. Wealth of the holder does not set the rate. A fixed deposit is a bank deposit, not a company share paying a dividend. The RBI does not fix every deposit rate by order; banks set deposit rates within the rate environment.

**50. (C)** A deposit means you give the bank money and may earn interest; a loan means you take the bank's money and repay it with interest — opposite directions. The first distractor reverses who gives and who takes. The second swaps which one is repaid and which earns. The third wrongly claims neither involves interest.

**51. (A)** Banks lend out depositors' money, so an unpaid loan endangers those savings and the bank. Banks do not print loan money (only the RBI issues currency). Loans are not gifts — they must be repaid with interest. The RBI does not automatically cover defaulted private loans.

**52. (C)** Lending your deposit to a farmer puts the money to productive use — that is working in the economy. A sealed locker, gold in a vault, or cash mailed back are all idle uses that do not circulate the money into loans.

**53. (D)** Lending on credit means giving money in trust that it will be repaid — matching 'credere'. The borrower handing money to keep safe is a deposit, not credit. Printing notes is the RBI's currency role, not lending in trust. Selling a unit of ownership is a share sale, not a loan.

**54. (D)** Compounding: 10,000 to 11,000 to 12,100 to 13,310. '13,000' adds a flat 1,000 each year (simple interest). '11,000' applies interest only once. '10,000' wrongly assumes no growth.

**55. (A)** The bend comes from interest earning interest, so each year's addition is bigger. The rate is not secretly raised. No extra deposits are made — the growth is from compounding alone. Compound interest builds on the principal plus added interest, not by ignoring the principal.

**56. (B)** Roads move goods, but raising and channelling money for factories needs financial infrastructure — the two complement each other. Banks do not need to own roads for roads to be useful. A town with banks still needs roads (finance complements, not replaces). Factories are funded through banks and markets, not built by the RBI.

**57. (D)** A loan must be repaid with interest, unlike a gift. Saying a loan need never be repaid erases the very feature that makes it a loan. A loan costs the borrower interest; it does not earn them interest. A loan is money received from the bank, not given to it (that would be a deposit).

**58. (D)** A shareholder may gain from a rising price and a dividend, but these are not guaranteed and can fall — unlike the deposit's known interest. The shareholder is not guaranteed fixed interest plus a guaranteed dividend. A shareholder is not repaid principal plus fixed interest like a depositor. A shareholder is an owner, not a salaried employee.

**59. (A)** Deposits are lent out; Bina pays 10% while Asha earns 5%, and the bank keeps the gap — Asha's money works rather than lying idle. The deposit is not locked untouched, and Bina's loan is not newly printed money. The rates are not reversed — the borrower pays more than the depositor earns, so the bank gains, not loses. Loans are not free gifts, and Asha's interest comes from the bank's lending, not the government.

**60. (D)** The RBI issues currency, is banker to banks and government, supervises banks and sets the benchmark rate — all correct. It is not where individuals keep savings (that is a commercial bank). It does not run the stock market or fix share prices. It does not build roads and ports — that is physical infrastructure.
