## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** शृगालः names the fox/jackal, and the descriptive word लोमशा ('hairy/furry') tells us about its coat — so the protagonist is a hairy four-legged animal. लोमशा describes the FOX, not the vine; the fox is no bird and is not hairless, so those readings fail.

**2. (A)** वने is the locative singular of वनम् and means 'in the forest', marking the place where the fox wanders. It is not a dative ('to'), not a genitive ('of'), and not a plural; the locative answers 'where?', which is exactly what the question needs.

**3. (C)** A protagonist is the character whose choices and actions carry the plot; here the fox wanders, leaps again and again, and utters the face-saving verdict, so it is central. The vine does appear but only as a passive goal; द्राक्षालता is the grape-vine, never the fox; and it is the fox, not the vine, who voices the excuse.

**4. (D)** Both श्रान्तः and its gloss क्लान्तः denote bodily weariness from effort. Sadness is carried separately by खिन्नः, so 'mentally sad' confuses two different words; the fox is neither angry nor pleased at this point. The two synonyms reinforce the single sense 'tired'.

**5. (B)** श्रान्तः = tired, खिन्नः = dejected/sad, and पिपासायुक्तः = full of thirst, so the trio is 'tired, dejected and thirsty'. The 'rested/cheerful' set reverses every term; 'proud/wealthy' is off-topic; and 'hungry' wrongly swaps thirst (पिपासा) for hunger (बुभुक्षा).

**6. (A)** पातुम् is the infinitive 'to drink' (from पा) and इच्छा is 'wish/desire', so पातुम् इच्छा = 'the wish to drink' — i.e. thirst. It is not 'to eat' (that would build on भुज्), not a completed past act, and not an inability; the gloss is a desire-to-drink phrase.

**7. (D)** खिन्नः = dejected/sad, an inner mental state; श्रान्तः = physically tired, a bodily state. The reversed pairing swaps them; hunger/thirst belong to बुभुक्षा/पिपासा, not these two; and left/right are direction words. So the order is mind then body.

**8. (A)** Eating addresses hunger (बुभुक्षा, from भुज् 'eat') only; it does not by itself slake thirst (पिपासा, from पा 'drink') nor erase the fatigue श्रान्तः. So a full meal removes hunger while tiredness and thirst can persist. The distractors wrongly let eating cancel thirst, fatigue, or everything.

**9. (A)** तृषा and पिपासा are two words for the same idea, thirst — the desire to drink — hence they are glossed together. बुभुक्षा is hunger (eating), so pairing तृषा with it would be wrong; तृषा is neither tiredness nor a direction. Synonyms of thirst belong together.

**10. (B)** शृगालः is the noun naming the kind of animal (fox); श्रान्तः, खिन्नः and पिपासायुक्तः are adjectives describing its momentary state (tired, sad, thirsty). None of those three names a species — they are feelings — so only शृगालः answers 'what is it?'.

**11. (D)** युक्त means 'joined with / possessed of', so पिपासायुक्तः = 'possessed of thirst' — the fox still craves drink, the opposite of having drunk. It is not a completed action, not hunger (that is बुभुक्षा), and not a direction word. The form marks an ongoing state, not a past deed.

**12. (A)** The compound splits द्राक्षा 'grape' + लता 'creeper', naming the whole grape-vine. There is no morpheme द्राक्ष meaning 'sour' (sour is आम्ल), no privative अ-लता here, and द्राक्षाल does not mean 'fox' (that is शृगालः). Only the grape + creeper split is real.

**13. (D)** द्राक्षालता fuses द्राक्षा and लता into one word, showing Sanskrit's love of compounding (समास). It clearly CAN name plants and clearly DOES join nouns, so 'never' and 'forbids' are false; and using one word for two is fewer, not always more, words.

**14. (A)** द्राक्षा is the grape itself, द्राक्षाफलम् the grape-fruit the fox wants, and द्राक्षालता the whole vine. The first wrong set swaps grape and vine; the second injects 'sour/fox/forest'; the third puts the plant-sense on फलम् and shrinks the vine to one grape. Only the correct triple keeps fruit-fruit-plant in order.

**15. (C)** द्राक्षाफलम् names the grape-fruit, the very thing he tried to reach; calling THAT 'sour' is his excuse. He is not commenting on the vine (द्राक्षालता), the forest, or his own state — the verdict targets the desired fruit, so फलम् is the right word in the punchline.

**16. (D)** उत् ('up') prefixes the root पत् ('fall/fly'), and the ending -ति is present third-person singular, giving 'he leaps up' now. The past would be उदपतत् (with the augment), the future उत्पतिष्यति, and the command पततु; none of those is the form here, which is plainly present.

**17. (A)** उत् is a verbal prefix (उपसर्ग) meaning 'up', steering पत् toward 'leap up'. It is NOT the past augment (that is the अ- seen in उदपतत्), not a case ending (those attach to nouns), and not a desiderative marker (that pattern gives पिपासा/बुभुक्षा). Prefix is the only correct label.

**18. (B)** उदपतत् = उत् + अ (augment) + पतत्; the अ- augment is the mark of the past (लङ्), and उत्+अ sandhies to उद. No new prefix is added (उद is just उत् before a vowel), there is no -ष्य (that is future), and no -तु (that is imperative). The augment is the added piece.

**19. (C)** Past (लङ्) requires the augment अ- before the stem; with the prefix, उत् + अ + पतत् → उदपतत्. The prefix is not dropped (it surfaces as उद), the root पत् is not replaced by पठ् 'read', and -ति is a PRESENT ending, not past. The missing augment is exactly what उत्पतत् lacks.

**20. (D)** उदपतत् (augment) = past, उत्पतति (-ति) = present, उत्पतिष्यति (-ष्यति) = future; पततु (-तु) is a command and sits outside the past-present-future axis. The other orderings misassign the tenses, e.g. treating the present उत्पतति as past or the imperative पततु as a tense.

**21. (B)** Tense is signalled by the augment and the endings, not by a prefix. उत् merely adds 'up'; the absence of the augment अ- and the present ending -ति make उत्पतति present. A prefix never forces past tense, उत् never marks tense on any root, and the form is present, not future.

**22. (B)** उत्/उद् 'up' joins पत् to give 'leap up' and गम् to give उद्गच्छति 'goes up', showing one prefix reused across roots with a consistent 'up' sense. Prefixes are not tied to one root, they shade MEANING (not tense), and they are not nouns taking case endings.

**23. (D)** एकवारम् = once, द्विवारम् = twice, पुनः पुनः = again and again; together they show the fox trying more and more times. They are not one attempt re-labelled, not directions (those use -तः), and not times of day. The sequence depicts mounting, repeated effort.

**24. (C)** He leaps again and again (पुनः पुनः) but cannot reach the grapes, so he saves face by declaring them sour (आम्लम्). He neither succeeds, nor quits after a single jump, nor obtains the grapes — the failure is precisely what triggers the sour-excuse.

**25. (B)** Because he tried repeatedly, we cannot accuse him of not trying; his error is purely the after-the-fact lie that the grapes were sour. The repetition refutes 'lazy', does nothing to make the grapes actually sour, and the moral does not commend giving up early.

**26. (C)** The humour and lesson rest on the fox branding desirable, sweet-seeming grapes as 'sour' (आम्लम्) only because he failed; मधुरम् 'sweet' is the implied truth. He does not call them sweet, ripe, or poisonous — the verdict is specifically the false 'sour'.

**27. (C)** चेत् is the conditional particle 'if', so न लभ्यते चेत् = 'if it is not obtained'. It is not another negative (न already supplies 'not'), not an adjective 'sweet', and not the noun 'fox'. The particle sets up the 'if … then' structure of the punchline.

**28. (B)** The passive न लभ्यते keeps the subject vague ('it is not obtained'), letting the fox avoid saying 'I could not get them' — fitting his face-saving mood. The grapes are not made active agents, the verb stays present, and आम्लम् remains an adjective, not a verb.

**29. (B)** Word for word: न लभ्यते 'is not obtained', चेत् 'if', आम्लम् 'sour', द्राक्षाफलम् 'the grape-fruit' → 'if the grape-fruit is not obtained, (it is) sour'. The sweet/obtained version inverts the condition; the others smuggle in 'fox', 'on the left', or 'tired' that the line does not contain.

**30. (A)** By phrasing it as a rule ('if it is not obtained, it is sour'), the chapter generalises the fox's lie into a recognisable human habit. It does not restrict the lesson to grapes, does not have him eat them, and certainly does not assert that unreachable fruit is truly sour.

**31. (C)** Trying and failing is blameless; the ethical lapse is the dishonest re-labelling of the grapes as 'sour'. Jumping is not itself wrong, thirst is a state not a fault, and the height of the grapes is a circumstance, not a moral failing — only the false excuse is.

**32. (A)** The fox devalues what he could not obtain to soothe his pride — exactly like dismissing the sport as 'boring' after being left out. Practising and getting chosen, training again, or congratulating a rival are honest, healthy responses that do NOT belittle the prize, so they are not parallels.

**33. (B)** The moral targets the dishonest excuse, not effort — so 'never attempt difficult things' contradicts it and is the odd one out. The other three each capture the true lesson: don't belittle the unreachable, be honest about failure, and beware pride-driven excuses.

**34. (C)** By granting that vain striving can be noble, the chapter isolates the lie as the sole target of blame; effort is never condemned. It does not counsel quitting, does not equate effort with the excuse, and does not praise the fox — who in fact gave up AND lied.

**35. (D)** The fable neither recommends giving up early nor endorses belittling the goal — it warns AGAINST the belittling excuse. So both halves of the friend's summary are wrong, giving two errors. Treating either half as correct understates the mistakes.

**36. (D)** By voicing 'they're sour', the fox externalises an excuse he could have kept silent — that spoken rationalisation is what the fable spotlights. Speaking does not prove he could reach them, does not make the grapes sour, and says nothing about his thirst, which the opening already established.

**37. (B)** The तसिल् suffix -तः forms fixed adverbs of direction, so all four are indeclinable adverbs meaning 'on the … side'. They are not nouns taking case endings, not verbs, and not adjectives that agree with a noun in gender/number; -तः marks adverbs.

**38. (A)** -तः derives an indeclinable adverb (वामतः 'on the left'), whereas वामम् is an inflected case form that changes with role and number. वामतः is not a plural, not a verb's past tense, and not identical to वामम् — the suffix changes its whole category to adverb.

**39. (B)** The lesson's pattern is base + -तः → direction adverb (वामतः, अग्रतः …); applied to an 'upper' base it predicts a -तः adverb, not a noun case. -आय is dative, -ति is a verb ending, and -स्य is genitive — none of which express direction the way तसिल् -तः does.

**40. (A)** वामतः is a direction adverb, so it modifies the action अपश्यत् ('looked'), telling us the fox looked to the left. It is not the object (that would be an accusative noun), not the subject (शृगालः is), and not a time word; -तः answers 'in which direction?'.

**41. (A)** Left (वामतः) ↔ right (दक्षिणतः) and front (अग्रतः) ↔ behind (पृष्ठतः) are the natural opposite pairs. The other groupings cross front with left or back with right, which are perpendicular, not opposite; वनम् and द्राक्षा are not directions at all.

**42. (C)** अग्रतः 'in front' and पृष्ठतः 'behind' are opposites, so if the vine is ahead (अग्रतः), then पृष्ठतः points the opposite way — behind, away from the vine. It is not the same as अग्रतः, and behind is neither the right (दक्षिणतः) nor the left (वामतः).

**43. (C)** दक्षिणतः = to the right and अग्रतः = in front/ahead, so the instruction is 'turn right, then look ahead'. The wrong versions swap right for left and front for behind, mis-rendering the two -तः adverbs whose meanings are fixed by the chapter.

**44. (C)** दक्षिण underlies दक्षिणतः 'on the right side', consistent with its sense. It is not 'left' (that is वामतः), not a verb, and not a synonym of 'behind' (पृष्ठतः). The -तः form simply turns the base into a 'right-side' adverb.

**45. (B)** पिपासा derives from पा 'drink' (so thirst = wish to drink) and बुभुक्षा from भुज् 'eat' (so hunger = wish to eat). The first distractor swaps the roots; the second wrongly gives both the same root; the third invents roots पठ्/गम् that have nothing to do with thirst or hunger.

**46. (D)** Desiderative forms (सन्) express 'wishing to do' the root's action — पा → पिपासा 'wish to drink', भुज् → बुभुक्षा 'wish to eat'. This is not the past (augment अ-), not a command (imperative), and not a case (locative shows place, not desire).

**47. (D)** Desiderative stems reduplicate the root's first syllable (पि-, बु-) to express longing to do the action — hence thirst and hunger. It is not a plural marker, not the past augment (which is अ-, not a reduplicated syllable), and not a direction prefix like उत्.

**48. (A)** पिपासा and बुभुक्षा are desideratives ('wish to drink / eat'), so the same pattern on श्रु 'hear' yields 'wish to hear'. It would not be a completed past act, not a direction adverb (that needs -तः), and not a plural; the desiderative encodes DESIRE for the root's action.

**49. (C)** Since a desiderative = 'wish to do [root's action]', the root fixes WHICH desire it is: drinking (पा) → thirst, eating (भुज्) → hunger. The root encodes the action, not a direction, a count, or a colour, so those distractors miss how the word is built.

**50. (B)** Because पिपासा (thirst, ← पा) and बुभुक्षा (hunger, ← भुज्) name DIFFERENT desires, using पिपासा foregrounds thirst at that point. It does not claim the fox can never be hungry, the two words are not synonyms, and बुभुक्षा is a hunger-word, not a non-fox marker.

**51. (A)** श्रान्तः (= क्लान्तः) means physically exhausted, exactly the runner's state. आम्लम् 'sour' describes taste, दक्षिणतः is a direction, and while the runner might also be thirsty, the word for the can't-stand fatigue itself is श्रान्तः.

**52. (D)** Heavy exertion → fatigue श्रान्तः; going without drink → thirst पिपासायुक्तः. The reversed pairing swaps cause and word; आम्लम् is a taste, not a bodily state; and बुभुक्षा is hunger, which neither exertion nor lack of DRINK specifically names.

**53. (D)** उत्पतति is the present 'leaps up', which is correct. आम्लम् means 'sour', not 'sweet' (मधुरम्); खिन्नः means 'sad/dejected', not 'wealthy'; and लता means 'creeper', not 'fox' (शृगालः). Only the verb pairing holds.

**54. (A)** वामतः is indeed a -तः direction adverb — both word and label fit. उदपतत् is PAST, not present; पिपासा is a desiderative noun (thirst), not a past tense; and अग्रतः is an adverb, not a dative noun. Only the वामतः row is fully correct.

**55. (D)** द्राक्षालता is the whole grape-VINE, not a single grape (that is द्राक्षा), so this statement is false. The other three are all true: शृगालः = fox, आम्लम् = sour, पृष्ठतः = behind. The vine-vs-grape confusion is the planted error.

**56. (B)** The fable warns against 'sour-grapes' thinking: belittling what we couldn't get to protect our pride. That is unrelated to over-preparing, to generosity with credit, or to thrift — those are not the dishonest self-excuse the fox models.

**57. (C)** The grammar the chapter spotlights is the -तः direction words (वामतः etc.) and the words for thirst/hunger/tiredness (पिपासा, बुभुक्षा, श्रान्तः). It is not a study of the dative, not a future-tense drill, and not Sun-names, which belong to other chapters.

**58. (C)** The lesson rewards honesty about failure, so the wise fox simply admits he could not reach them. The other replies all belittle or deflect — calling the grapes worthless, blaming the forest, or denying he ever wanted them — which is exactly the sour-grapes excuse condemned.

**59. (C)** Three claims must all hold: the moral is 'don't belittle what you fail to get' (true), उत्पतति is present (true), and पिपासा derives from पा 'drink' (true) — only the first option keeps all three right. The others corrupt the moral, mis-tense उत्पतति, or mis-root पिपासा as भुज्.

**60. (D)** द्राक्षाफलम् is the object (the grapes), न लभ्यते चेत् the condition ('if not obtained'), and आम्लम् the verdict ('sour'). The distractors substitute the fox/vine/forest for the fruit, swap the condition for unrelated states, and replace the sour verdict with a direction or 'sweet' — none matches the line.
