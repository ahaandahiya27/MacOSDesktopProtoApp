**Marking scheme:** **+4** for each correct answer · **−1** for each wrong answer · **0** if unattempted.

**Instructions:** Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Classic traps: confusing the state (four parts: population, territory, government, sovereignty; endures) with the government (one part; changes at elections); treating police/army or the bureaucracy as 'the government'; assuming every democracy is a republic (the UK keeps a monarch); assuming the majority may do anything (rights and the rule of law shield minorities); mixing up the three organs (legislature makes, executive enforces, judiciary interprets), the three roles (protector, provider, regulator), and the three tiers (union, state, local). Do not write on this paper — mark answers on your answer sheet.

1. In 2019 a country held an election and a new party formed the government; in 2024 yet another party won. Through both changes, the same people, the same borders, and the same power to make its own laws stayed in place. Which conclusion is best supported?
   (A) The state changed twice, but the government stayed exactly the same throughout.
   (B) The government changed twice, but the state - its population, territory and sovereignty - endured.
   (C) Both the state and the government were completely replaced at each election.
   (D) Neither the state nor the government can ever change once formed.

2. A textbook lists four things and says 'a state must have all of these.' Three of the four it lists are correct parts of a state. Which item is the IMPOSTER that does NOT belong among the four parts of a state?
   (A) A permanent population
   (B) A defined territory
   (C) Sovereignty (full power to make its own laws)
   (D) An independent judiciary

3. Using the chapter's analogy, a school keeps running while its teachers come and go over the years. If the school is matched to the state, what does the analogy most precisely warn us NOT to conclude?
   (A) That the teachers (the government) can change while the school (the state) continues.
   (B) That when the teachers (the government) leave, the school (the state) ceases to exist.
   (C) That the school (the state) is more lasting than any one set of teachers (any one government).
   (D) That different teachers (governments) can run the same school (state) over time.

4. Two friends argue. Riya says 'the state is just the buildings where ministers sit.' Sam says 'the state is whichever party is currently in power.' Who is right, and why?
   (A) Riya is right; without the ministers' buildings there is no state.
   (B) Sam is right; the state is simply the party that won the last election.
   (C) Both are right, since buildings and the ruling party together make up the whole state.
   (D) Neither; the state is the political organisation of a population on a territory with sovereignty, not buildings and not the ruling party.

5. A new nation is recognised. It has a settled population and clearly marked borders, but a stronger neighbour must approve every law it wishes to pass. Which essential part of a full state is this nation MOST clearly missing?
   (A) Population
   (B) Territory
   (C) Government
   (D) Sovereignty

6. Which statement correctly captures how 'making, enforcing and interpreting laws' relates to the state and the government?
   (A) It is the government's work, done through its three organs; the population, territory and sovereignty are the state's other parts and do not do this work.
   (B) It is the work of the population, which directly writes and judges every law itself.
   (C) It is done by the territory, since laws apply to the land.
   (D) It is the work of sovereignty acting on its own, separate from any organ of government.

7. A citizen claims, 'If the ruling party loses, the country loses its government, its laws and its sovereignty all at once.' How many of these three would actually be lost when a party loses an election?

   (B) Only sovereignty is lost; the government and laws continue.
   (C) None of the three is lost - a new government forms, the laws continue, and sovereignty stays with the state.
   (D) Only the laws are lost, while the government and sovereignty continue.

8. Which pairing correctly distinguishes the state from the government?
   (A) The government has four parts and endures; the state is one part that changes with elections.
   (B) Both have four parts and both change identically at every election.
   (C) The state is one part of the government and disappears between elections.
   (D) The state has four parts and endures; the government is one of those parts and changes with elections.

9. A diagram shows a large circle labelled X containing four smaller shapes; one of the four shapes is itself a box containing three sub-parts labelled 'makes laws', 'enforces laws', 'interprets laws'. The large circle X and the inner box are best labelled, respectively, as:
   (A) The government (X) and the state (the box inside it).
   (B) The territory (X) and sovereignty (the box inside it).
   (C) The population (X) and the territory (the box inside it).
   (D) The state (X) and the government (the box of three organs inside it).

10. Country P passes its laws freely, but agrees by treaty to coordinate trade rules with neighbours while keeping the final power to accept or reject any rule. Country Q must obey every order issued by a foreign capital. Which statement about their sovereignty is correct?
   (A) Both fully lack sovereignty, because any cooperation with others ends sovereignty.
   (B) P retains sovereignty because it keeps the final power over its own laws; Q lacks full sovereignty because an outside power controls its laws.
   (C) Both are fully sovereign, since signing any treaty automatically proves sovereignty.
   (D) Q is sovereign and P is not, because P chose to coordinate with neighbours.

11. Which scenario best demonstrates a state EXERCISING its sovereignty?

   (B) Its parliament passes and its courts enforce a tax law that no outside power can overrule.
   (C) Citizens cast their votes in a general election.
   (D) The state marks the boundary line of its territory on a map.

12. A pamphlet says 'the police and the army are the real government, because they hold the weapons.' The chapter's view corrects this how?
   (A) The police and army are state machinery that the government directs; holding weapons does not make them the government.
   (B) The police and army truly are the government, since force decides who governs.
   (C) The police and army are part of the population and have no role for the state.
   (D) The police and army are a foreign power placed over the state.

13. After an election, a defeated party complains that 'the same officers, police and soldiers are still in place, so nothing really changed.' What is the accurate response?
   (A) Nothing changed at all, because the police and soldiers should have been replaced with the new party's supporters.
   (B) Everything changed, including all police and soldiers, who are dismissed whenever a party loses.
   (C) The government (the political executive) changed; the permanent state machinery, including police and soldiers, rightly continues serving the state regardless of who wins.
   (D) The complaint proves the police and army are themselves the new government.

14. Which feature most sharply separates the armed forces from the elected government?

15. India is described as a 'democratic republic.' A student asks what each word adds. Which explanation is correct?
   (A) 'Democratic' means the people rule by choosing the government in free elections; 'republic' means the head of state is elected, not a hereditary monarch.

   (C) Both words mean exactly the same thing, so the phrase just repeats itself.
   (D) 'Democratic' refers to the size of the army; 'republic' refers to the size of the territory.

16. The United Kingdom has an elected parliament that runs the country, but its head of state is a hereditary monarch. Sweden and Canada are similar. What does this prove about the claim 'every democracy is a republic'?
   (A) The claim is true, because having a parliament automatically makes a country a republic.
   (B) The claim is false: these countries are democracies yet keep monarchs, so they are not republics.
   (C) The claim is true, because a hereditary monarch makes a country a republic.
   (D) It proves these countries are neither democracies nor republics.

17. Imagine a country whose people elect their lawmakers in free elections, but whose head of state is a king who inherited the throne. How should it be classified?
   (A) A republic but not a democracy.
   (B) Both a democracy and a republic.
   (C) Neither a democracy nor a republic.
   (D) A democracy but not a republic.

18. A country abolishes its hereditary monarchy and now elects its head of state, but only one party is allowed to stand and citizens cannot freely choose. Which label fits best?
   (A) A democracy but not a republic.
   (B) Both a full democracy and a republic.
   (C) A republic but not a true democracy.
   (D) Neither a republic nor anything with an elected head.

19. Which combination of (democracy?, republic?) is IMPOSSIBLE to find described in the chapter's framework for a country with a hereditary monarch as head of state?
   (A) Democracy = yes (a country with a monarch can still elect its lawmakers).
   (B) Republic = yes (because a hereditary monarch is, by definition, not an elected head of state).
   (C) Democracy = no (a country with a monarch might also lack free elections).
   (D) Republic = no (a hereditary monarch means it is not a republic).

20. A village council passes a rule that members of a small minority community must do extra unpaid work that others are spared. Even though most villagers voted for it, a court strikes it down. Which principle best explains why the majority's vote did not settle the matter?
   (A) Guaranteed fundamental rights and the rule of law protect minorities, so a majority cannot trample on their rights.
   (B) The minority always rules over the majority in a democracy.
   (C) Majority votes are never valid for any decision.
   (D) Only the majority has rights, so the rule was simply badly written.

21. A speaker argues, 'Democracy means the majority decides, so whatever 51% want must become law.' What is the strongest objection grounded in this chapter?
   (A) The rule of law and guaranteed rights set limits the majority cannot cross, so 51% cannot override a minority's protected rights.
   (B) There is no objection; in a democracy 51% can indeed pass any law at all.
   (C) The objection is that minorities, not majorities, must always decide every law.
   (D) The objection is that democracies have no laws limiting anyone.

22. How does protecting minority rights against the 'dominance of the majority' show the relationship between the rule of law and mere numbers?
   (A) It shows the majority's wishes always override the law whenever they are large enough.
   (B) It shows the rule of law stands above the mere wishes of the majority, since even a numerical majority must obey constitutional rights.
   (C) It shows minorities are left with no protection in a democracy.
   (D) It shows the Constitution can be freely ignored by whoever has more votes.

23. An old saying warns that without law and order, society becomes 'matsya nyaya,' the law of the fishes. A leader then argues, 'So we need a government precisely to prevent this.' Which reading is correct?
   (A) Matsya nyaya means the strong devour the weak; government and the rule of law exist to prevent exactly that.
   (B) Matsya nyaya means everyone is automatically equal, so no government is needed.
   (C) Matsya nyaya means fish should make the laws, which the government prevents.
   (D) Matsya nyaya means the weak always defeat the strong, so the strong need protection.

24. Which situation is the clearest real-world picture of matsya nyaya taking hold?
   (A) A court fairly punishes a powerful person who broke the law.
   (B) Citizens peacefully vote out a government they dislike.
   (C) After all police and courts collapse, the most powerful gangs seize others' property and no one can be held to account.
   (D) A regulator fines a large company for breaking safety rules.

25. Why is the rule of law described as the opposite of matsya nyaya?
   (A) Because the rule of law binds even the most powerful to follow the law, protecting the weak instead of letting the strong prey on them.
   (B) Because the rule of law lets the powerful ignore the law whenever convenient.
   (C) Because the rule of law applies only to weak and poor people.
   (D) Because the rule of law abolishes the government entirely.

26. A minister says, 'I am part of the government, so the rule of law does not apply to me.' This claim is wrong because the rule of law means:
   (A) Only ordinary citizens, not officials, must obey the law.
   (B) The government is above the law and writes its own exceptions.
   (C) Everyone, including the government and its ministers, must follow the law.
   (D) Laws apply only to minorities and the weak.

27. Without any government or law, the chapter says society would tend to slide toward which condition, and what does this reveal about why government exists?
   (A) Toward perfect equality requiring no effort, revealing government is unnecessary.
   (B) Toward a fully working Constitution that appears on its own, revealing law needs no enforcer.
   (C) Toward the rule of law forming naturally, revealing the powerful police themselves.
   (D) Toward matsya nyaya (the strong preying on the weak), revealing that government exists to maintain order and protect the weak.

28. A body of elected representatives meets, debates a proposed bill, and votes it into law. A second body then issues orders to carry the new law out across the country. A third body later decides whether someone broke that law. These three actions belong, in order, to the:
   (A) executive, judiciary and legislature.
   (B) judiciary, legislature and executive.
   (C) legislature, executive and judiciary.
   (D) legislature, judiciary and executive.

29. The word 'legislature' traces to Latin roots for 'law' and 'a bringing or proposing.' Which organ's defining task does this origin best fit, and why is the match exact?
   (A) The legislature's, because its defining task is precisely to propose and make laws.
   (B) The executive's, because its task is to carry out laws.
   (C) The judiciary's, because its task is to judge and interpret laws.

30. A state assembly spends a session amending the wording of a draft education bill and finally votes it into law. Which classification of this work is correct?

   (C) Judicial work, because the assembly is interpreting the meaning of a law.

40. A school principal acts in three ways during one term: she keeps students safe (role 1), runs the library and meals (role 2), and sets and enforces fair rules for clubs (role 3). If the principal models the government, which labels fit roles 1, 2 and 3?
