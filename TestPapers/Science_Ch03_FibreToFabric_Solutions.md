## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** Animal fibres come from animals: wool (sheep, goat, yak, camel) and silk (silkworm). Cotton, jute and flax are plant fibres. Only "wool and silk" has two animal fibres.

**2. (D)** Animal fibres (wool, silk) are made of protein; plant fibres (cotton) are made of cellulose. The different chemistry gives the different feel. (Option C reverses the two.)

**3. (C)** Protein fibres smell like burning hair when burnt; cellulose fibres (cotton, jute, flax) smell like burning paper. Of the choices, only wool is a protein fibre, so the smell of burning hair points to wool.

**4. (D)** The silk moth gives silk, not wool. Yak, camel and goat all yield wool, so the odd one out is the silk moth.

**5. (A)** The thick coat of hair on a sheep is the fleece. A cocoon belongs to a silkworm, sericin is silk gum, and a burr is a bit of plant debris.

**6. (D)** The fleece insulates the sheep by trapping a layer of air, keeping it warm in cold weather. (Providing wool for humans is a use we make of it, not the animal's reason for growing it.)

**7. (C)** Pashmina is the very fine, warm wool obtained from a special kind of goat (the pashmina/changthangi goat), not from sheep, silkworm or camel.

**8. (A)** Wool fibres are crimped and trap a lot of still air both between and within the fibres; trapped air is a poor conductor, so it keeps you warm. Smooth plant fibres trap far less air. (Colour and weight have nothing to do with warmth.)

**9. (C)** In India, wool sheep are reared in the cold highlands such as Jammu & Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh (also Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Arunachal). Warm coastal states like Kerala are not wool regions.

**10. (B)** The processing order begins shearing → scouring → sorting. You must first cut the fleece (shear), then wash it (scour), then grade it (sort). The other orders put washing or sorting before the fleece is even removed.

**11. (B)** If the fleece were sheared off in winter, the sheep would lose its insulation in the cold and could fall ill. Shearing in spring/summer leaves time for new wool to grow before the next winter.

**12. (A)** Scouring is the thorough washing of sheared wool in warm water to remove grease, dust and dirt. Cutting it off is shearing; colouring is dyeing; twisting into yarn is rolling/spinning.

**13. (D)** Scouring removes grease and dirt. Skip it and the wool stays greasy and dirty, which spoils sorting, dyeing and spinning later. (It can still be dyed, just badly; it does not become short or flammable because of skipping.)

**14. (A)** Sorting grades the cleaned wool by physical features of the fibre — texture, length and colour. The price of the sheep or the country alone are not sorting criteria.

**15. (C)** Burrs are small bits of plant material (seeds, leaf fragments) caught in the fleece; picking them out removes this plant debris. Grease is removed by scouring, not burr-picking.

**16. (A)** Raw, undyed fleece is naturally a creamy white, which is why it can then be dyed into many colours. It is not naturally red, black or blue.

**17. (B)** The final (sixth) step rolls/combs the cleaned, dyed fibres into yarn ready to be woven into fabric. A fleece is the raw start; cocoons and sericin belong to silk.

**18. (B)** Burrs are easiest to see and remove on clean wool. Doing burr-picking before scouring means the grease and dirt hide the burrs, so many are missed — order matters here.

**19. (A)** Wool sorters who breathe in spores from raw fleece are at risk of sorter's disease (a form of anthrax). The other illnesses listed are unrelated to wool sorting.

**20. (A)** Sorter's disease is caused by spores of the bacterium Bacillus anthracis that can be present in raw fleece — it is bacterial, not viral, and not caused by cold weather or by eating wool.

**21. (B)** A sorter catches the disease mainly by inhaling dust and spores from the raw fleece while handling it. Dyed yarn and finished sweaters have already been cleaned, and scouring water is not the route.

**22. (C)** The risk is highest when handling raw, unwashed fleece (the sorting stage), because that is when the spores are still present. Finished/processed wool and shop-bought sweaters carry little risk.

**23. (C)** By the time wool reaches a shop it has been scoured and processed, removing the spores, so the buyer of a finished sweater is at very low risk. (Sweaters here are wool, not cotton, and the disease is not "sheep-only.")

**24. (D)** Sensible safety means protecting the worker: masks, gloves, good ventilation and disinfecting the fleece before sorting. Working without masks or skipping cleaning steps would increase risk.

**25. (A)** Fine, soft wool comes from breeds with very few coarse guard hairs. Merino has very few guard hairs, so Merino gives the finer, softer wool; Lincoln (many guard hairs) is coarser.

**26. (C)** A fleece has coarse outer guard hairs and a fine inner undercoat. The fine inner hairs form the undercoat. (Cocoon and sericin belong to silk; burrs are plant debris.)

**27. (D)** A single fleece mixes coarse and fine hairs of different lengths and colours. To spin fine, even yarn you must first sort and separate these — that is why sorting precedes spinning. (Sorting does not add colour or remove grease.)

**28. (B)** Hot water plus rough agitation makes wool fibres' tiny scales interlock, so the garment felts and shrinks permanently. Cool water and gentle handling prevent this. (Wool does not dissolve in cold water.)

**29. (D)** Very hot water and hard scrubbing felt the fibres together, so the sweater shrinks and becomes matted — it does not grow, turn into cotton, or stay unchanged.

**30. (A)** Sericulture is the rearing of silkworms for silk. Rearing sheep is for wool; weaving cotton and dyeing wool are different activities.

**31. (C)** The silkworm is the larva (caterpillar) of the silk moth — not of a bee, butterfly or housefly.

**32. (C)** The silk moth's life cycle is egg → larva (silkworm) → pupa (in the cocoon) → adult moth. The other orders place the stages out of sequence.

**33. (B)** The insect spins silk while changing from larva into pupa, forming the cocoon around itself. That larva-to-pupa transition is the cocoon-spinning stage; the other transitions do not produce silk.

**34. (C)** The common mulberry silkworm feeds mainly on mulberry leaves — that is why it is called the mulberry silkworm. Oak leaves feed tasar worms; roses and wheat are not its food.

**35. (A)** A cocoon is a single continuous silk thread spun around the pupa — it is this one long thread that makes silk valuable, not many short glued pieces.

**36. (C)** The cocoon is steamed/boiled before the moth emerges because an emerging moth would bite through the cocoon and break the single long thread into many short, less useful pieces. (It is not done to feed or grow the insect.)

**37. (B)** Reeling is unwinding the silk thread from the softened cocoon. Cutting from a sheep is shearing (wool); dyeing and weaving are later, separate steps.

**38. (B)** Hot water softens the sericin, the gum holding the silk thread together, so the thread can be found and unwound. It does not colour the silk, hatch eggs or feed the worm.

**39. (C)** A single silk fibre is extremely thin and weak, so during reeling several cocoons' threads are combined into one stronger strand. (It is not too thick, nor the wrong colour, nor cotton.)

**40. (D)** Weaving interlaces lengthwise warp threads with crosswise weft threads on a loom to make fabric. Washing, boiling and egg-laying are not weaving.

**41. (D)** The journey is: the worm spins a cocoon → reeling unwinds the thread → weaving makes cloth → fabric. So cocoon → reeling → weaving → fabric is correct.

**42. (C)** Besides mulberry (white) silk, India is famous for the non-mulberry silks tasar, eri and muga. Nylon/polyester are synthetic; cotton/jute are plant fibres; wool/pashmina are not silk.

**43. (D)** Muga silk, with its natural golden sheen, is the speciality of Assam. It is not associated with Punjab, Kerala or Gujarat.

**44. (A)** The ethical objection is that the pupae inside the cocoons are killed when the cocoons are boiled to keep the thread whole. The other options are not the reason for the objection.

**45. (B)** The correct match is: silk comes from a silkworm's cocoon and wool from an animal's fleece. The other options swap or misassign the sources.

**46. (D)** Synthetic fibres like polyester and nylon are man-made from petroleum: durable and wrinkle-resistant but not biodegradable. They are not from plants/animals, not warmer than wool, and not spun by silkworms.

**47. (C)** A little polyester added to wool adds strength and helps the garment keep its shape (resist stretching). It does not turn wool into cotton or make it smell of hair.

**48. (D)** Both are proteins, but different ones arranged differently — crimped, scaly keratin makes wool bulky and warm, while smooth fibroin makes silk glossy. Silk is not a plant fibre and wool is not man-made, so they are not identical.

**49. (A)** A single mulberry cocoon's thread is remarkably long — roughly hundreds to over a thousand metres — which is what makes one cocoon so useful. The other lengths are far too short.

**50. (A)** The clearest sign that wool is an animal fibre is that it is sheared from the body of a living sheep. Being woven, dyed or warm are true of plant fibres too, so they do not prove animal origin.

**51. (B)** Tracing "100% silk" back to its origin, the fabric began as the cocoon spun by the larva (silkworm) of the silk moth — not seed hair, goat fleece or jute stems.

**52. (B)** Among the steps, dyeing improves the look (adds colour) but does not clean the wool (scouring does) or align/spin it (rolling does). So dyeing is the step that fits.

**53. (A)** Sericin is a stiff gum that glues the thread to itself and to the cocoon; while it is on, the thread cannot be unwound or woven smoothly, so it is softened/removed during reeling. (It is not removed because it is poisonous or the wrong colour.)

**54. (B)** The reasoning is sound: a fibre that an animal makes only in tiny amounts each year is naturally scarce, which raises its price. Vicuña is an animal (a llama relative), its wool is natural, and wools certainly do not all cost the same.

**55. (A)** To get the finest, softest yarn you want a fine-wool breed (few guard hairs) and careful sorting of the fleece. A coarse breed with no sorting gives rough yarn, skipping scouring spoils it, and a silkworm gives silk not wool.

**56. (D)** Correct pairing: yak → wool. Sheep give wool (not silk), the silk moth gives silk (not wool), and a mulberry plant is the silkworm's food, not a silk source.

**57. (C)** The student is wrong: boiling mainly softens the sericin for reeling and kills the pupa so the long thread is not broken — cleaning is not its main purpose. (The cocoon is indeed boiled, and boiling does not dye it.)

**58. (B)** The six steps run shearing, scouring, sorting, picking burrs, dyeing, rolling. After shearing, scouring and sorting, the fourth step is picking out the burrs. (Reeling and weaving belong to silk.)

**59. (D)** The correct wool chain is shearing → scouring → sorting → picking burrs → dyeing → rolling into yarn. The other chains scramble this order.

**60. (B)** The most effective control attacks the source and protects the worker: disinfect the raw fleece and provide masks, gloves and good ventilation. Dyeing colours, shearing frequency and selling finished sweaters do not reduce a sorter's exposure to spores.
