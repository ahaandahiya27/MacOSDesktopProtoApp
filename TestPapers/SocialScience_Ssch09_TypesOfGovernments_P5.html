1. A new state writes a constitution that lists three named bodies: one drafts and votes on statutes, one runs the daily administration and commands officials, and one hears disputes about whether actions break those statutes. 5. A country's written rules say all three organs exist, but in practice the head of the executive personally appoints and dismisses every judge and can rewrite any law overnight. Judicial review of elections is a narrow court power, not the broad structural division being dismantled here.

Swapping legislative and executive labels misnames the assembly's lawmaking as administration. Calling enforcement judicial and the court ruling executive reverses the carry-out and judge roles. Lumping all three as legislative ignores that only the debate-and-vote step is lawmaking while the others are enforcement and judging.

3. (B) Letting one body both legislate and judge removes the independent judicial check, so the assembly could rule in its own favour and abuse power; that is why separation of powers matters. Democracy does not forbid efficiency; it limits how power is concentrated. A legislature making laws is normal and proper, so that is not the objection. The judiciary's job is to judge and the legislature's is to make laws, so the last option reverses their roles.

4. (B) An independent court that can rule against the government keeps even rulers bound by law and protects rights, which is why independence is the ideal.
