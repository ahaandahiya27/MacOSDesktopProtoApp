Worked Solutions

1. (B) Standard convention: long thin line = positive terminal, short thick line = negative terminal; the student called the short thick line positive, so it is reversed. The lines are terminals of the cell, not wires (wires are plain lines), so the 'connecting wires' option fails. Thickness is purely a drawing convention for marking the negative side, not a measure of cell strength. The symbol's meaning does not depend on whether it is drawn left or right, so position cannot decide the sign.

2. (C) Voltages add only when cells are in series, i.e. + of one to − of the next, giving 2+2+2 = 6 V. Joining all positives together and all negatives together is a parallel arrangement that keeps the voltage at one cell (2 V), not 6 V. Joining + to + and − to − makes cells oppose, so their pushes partly cancel rather than add. Air gaps break the conducting path entirely, so no current and no battery action.

3. (B) When cells oppose (+ to +), their voltages subtract; two equal cells leave roughly zero net push, so the bulb stays dark — that is the correct reason. The cells are not individually too weak; the issue is cancellation, not weakness. Opposing cells do not double the voltage — that would require series aiding (+ to −), so the 'burns out' option is wrong. Two correctly arranged cells routinely light torch bulbs, so 'a torch must use one cell' is false.

4. (B) Series-aiding cells add their pushes, so each extra cell raises the voltage and current, making the bulb brighter — and excess current can burn the filament, which is the full correct statement. Series cells do not 'share' current; current is the same all round the loop, so the dimmer option is wrong. Brightness is set by the total voltage, not capped by the first cell, so 'stays the same' fails. The number of cells being odd or even has nothing to do with completing a loop.

5. (A) Conventional current leaves the positive (long thin) terminal, travels the external loop, and returns to the negative (short thick) terminal — the correct path. Starting at the negative terminal reverses this, so that option is wrong. Current does not emerge from the 'middle' of a cell in two directions; it follows the single loop. Current must complete the whole loop back to the cell, so 'never back to the cell' is impossible for a working circuit.

6. (C) A small circle with a cross is the standard symbol for a bulb; with a cell and a closed switch the loop is complete, so the bulb glows — the correct reading. A cell is drawn as long-thin and short-thick lines, not a crossed circle, so 'cell' is wrong. An open switch is a raised arm leaving a gap, not a crossed circle. A fuse is usually shown as a thin line in a rectangle, not a crossed circle, so the fuse reading is also wrong.

7. (D) An arm lying flat across both contacts bridges the gap, making the switch closed and the loop complete, so the bulb can glow — correct. An open switch is the raised-arm form leaving a gap, not the flat form, so 'open' is wrong. A switch arm lying flat is its normal closed position, not a melted fuse. A switch arm has nothing to do with recharging a cell, so that option is irrelevant.
