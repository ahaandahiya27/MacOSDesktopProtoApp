**Instructions:** Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Classic Paper-5 traps: forgetting respiration runs in the light too, so a net gas figure is read as one process; treating the compensation point as 'both stopped' rather than 'equal rates'; assuming soil nitrogen or air nitrogen is a same-day photosynthesis raw material; reading a positive starch test as proof of chlorophyll, or a negative one as proof food was never made when it was just locked behind an unsoftened waxy leaf; confusing xylem (water, up) with phloem (food, out) and confusing which controlled set-up isolates light versus chlorophyll versus carbon dioxide; calling a host-dependent parasite a saprotroph or a mutualist; crediting most new dry mass to soil rather than to carbon dioxide; and assuming an insectivorous or nodulated plant is not a true autotroph. 1. In one bright hour a sealed-chamber leaf fixes carbon dioxide at 24 units per hour by photosynthesis while respiration releases it at 5 units per hour. Cloud then halves the light, and photosynthetic fixing falls in proportion to the light while respiration is unchanged. What does a meter reading only the NET carbon-dioxide change of the surrounding air now show per hour?
   (A) A net fall of 19 units, because respiration switches off whenever any light still reaches the leaf.
   (B) A net fall of 12 units, because only the photosynthetic uptake affects the air.
   (C) A net rise of 5 units, because halving the light makes respiration the only process that still runs.
   (D) A net fall of 7 units, because photosynthesis now fixes 12 while respiration still releases 5.

2. A leaf in a sealed chamber sits at its exact compensation point, where the meter shows zero net carbon-dioxide change. The experimenter slowly RAISES the light from this point. Before any other change, what does the meter begin to show, and why?
   (A) Still zero, because the compensation point is fixed and does not shift when light changes.
   (B) A net RISE in carbon dioxide, because brighter light makes the leaf respire faster to cope.
   (C) A net FALL in carbon dioxide, because more light speeds photosynthesis above the steady respiration rate so uptake now exceeds release.
   (D) A net RISE in carbon dioxide, because at high light the leaf stops photosynthesising to protect its chlorophyll.

3. A young tree adds 30 kg of dry mass in a year. A student's accounting: 18 kg from carbon dioxide, 6 kg traced to water supplying hydrogen, 1 kg of soil minerals, and the rest 'lifted as solid soil through the roots.' Using only this chapter's ideas, which single correction makes the accounting sound?
   (A) Roots do not lift solid soil; the remaining mass also traces to carbon dioxide fixed in photosynthesis, not to whole soil carried up.
   (B) The carbon-dioxide share is far too high; almost all dry mass actually comes from minerals dissolved in soil water.
   (C) Water cannot supply any mass at all, so the 6 kg should be reassigned to oxygen the tree absorbed.
   (D) The minerals figure is too low; soil minerals normally make up most of a tree's dry weight.
