## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** The angles sum to 180°, and 3 + 5 + 7 = 15 parts, so one part is 180/15 = 12°. The largest interior angle is 7 parts = 84°. An exterior angle is the supplement of its adjacent interior angle: 180° − 84° = 96°. Reading 84° confuses the interior angle with its exterior partner; 108° supplements the 5-part (60°) wrongly; 72° supplements a value that does not occur.

**2. (C)** The exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles: ∠P + ∠Q = 124°. With ∠Q = ∠P − 26°, we get 2∠P − 26° = 124°, so 2∠P = 150° and ∠P = 75° (then ∠Q = 49°). Picking 49° gives ∠Q instead; 62° just halves 124° ignoring the difference; 98° wrongly treats 124° as adjacent to ∠P.

**3. (C)** The third side x satisfies 13 − 6 < x < 13 + 6, i.e. 7 < x < 19. The whole numbers strictly between are 8, 9, …, 18, which is 18 − 8 + 1 = 11. Counting 12 wrongly includes 7 or 19; 13 counts both endpoints; 7 stops short at 14.

**4. (D)** The two base angles are equal and together with the 44° vertex angle sum to 180°, so each is (180° − 44°)/2 = 136°/2 = 68°. The bisector detail does not affect the base angles. Choosing 44° repeats the vertex angle; 88° doubles a base angle; 46° comes from a faulty (180−88)/2 step.

**5. (A)** The angles sum to 180°: (2x+10) + (3x−20) + (x+40) = 6x + 30 = 180, so 6x = 150 and x = 25°. The angles are 60°, 55° and 65°, all below 90°, so the triangle is acute. It is not right-angled (no 90°), not obtuse (no angle over 90°), and not equiangular (the three differ).

**6. (B)** Three equal sides make a triangle equilateral, and an equilateral triangle has three 60° angles, so it is acute. The second has two equal angles (40°, 40°), so two equal sides — it is isosceles — and its 100° angle makes it obtuse. The first cannot be right (no 90°) or scalene (sides are equal); the second is not acute (100° > 90°) or right (no 90°).

**7. (D)** When the altitude and median from the same vertex coincide, that line is both perpendicular to BC and bisects it, so the two halves of the triangle are mirror images, forcing AB = AC. This need not be equilateral (only two sides are forced equal), need not be right-angled at A, and says nothing about BC being longest.

**8. (D)** In any triangle the largest angle lies opposite the longest side. The longest side is BC = 9 cm, and the angle opposite BC is ∠A (the vertex not on BC). So ∠A is the largest. ∠B faces CA = 5 cm (shortest, so smallest), ∠C faces AB = 7 cm (middle); they are not equal since the sides differ.

**9. (B)** Let ∠B = b. Then ∠A = b + 18° and ∠C = 2b. The sum is (b + 18) + b + 2b = 4b + 18 = 180, so 4b = 162 and b = 40.5°. Then ∠C = 2b = 81°. Picking 40.5° reports ∠B; 60° assumes equal split; 63° is ∠A wrongly named.

**10. (A)** The third angle is 180° − 35° − 85° = 60°. Larger angles face longer sides, so ordering sides by their opposite angles 35° < 60° < 85° gives shortest to longest exactly. The reverse order is backwards; sides are unequal since the angles differ; the side opposite 85° (not 60°) is the longest.

**11. (B)** The exterior angle at B equals the sum of the two remote interior angles: ∠CBE = ∠A + ∠C. So 3∠C = 50° + ∠C, giving 2∠C = 50° and ∠C = 25°. Then ∠CBE = 75°. Choosing 75° reports the exterior angle; 50° just copies ∠A; 37.5° halves 75° incorrectly.

**12. (B)** The third side x satisfies 15 − 8 < x < 15 + 8, i.e. 7 < x < 23, so the smallest whole-number value is 8. Perimeter = 8 + 15 + 8 = 31 cm. Using 7 (which fails the strict inequality) gives 30; 23 wrongly adds only the two given sides; 38 uses the largest allowed value 15 instead of the smallest.

**13. (C)** Let the third angle be t; each equal angle is t + 15, and there are two of them. Angle sum: (t + 15) + (t + 15) + t = 3t + 30 = 180, so 3t = 150 and t = 50°. Each equal angle is t + 15 = 65°. Choosing 50° reports the third angle; 70° adds 20 instead of 15; 55° comes from a wrong 3t = 165 slip.

**14. (D)** Since ∠C = 80°, the other two sum to ∠A + ∠B = 100°. With ∠A : ∠B = 2 : 3, the parts total 5, so one part is 100/5 = 20°. Then ∠B = 3 parts = 60° (and ∠A = 40°). Choosing 40° reports ∠A; 50° splits 100° evenly; 100° is the combined sum, not ∠B alone.

**15. (D)** In a right triangle the two legs are themselves the altitudes from the acute-angle vertices, and the third altitude from the right-angle vertex falls inside; all three altitudes pass through the right-angle vertex, so that is their common point. They do not meet at a midpoint, and for a right (or acute) triangle the meeting point is not outside.

**16. (C)** Since AB = AC, the base angles are equal: ∠B = ∠C = 64°, so the vertex angle ∠A = 180° − 64° − 64° = 52°. The bisector from A splits ∠A in half, so ∠BAD = 52°/2 = 26°. Choosing 52° gives the whole vertex angle; 32° halves 64°; 64° copies a base angle.

**17. (A)** The triangle inequality needs the two smaller sides to sum to more than the largest: 2k + 3k = 5k, which exceeds 4k for every positive k. So the set always works regardless of k's exact value. The inequality does not depend on k being whole or greater than 1, and it never fails for positive k.

**18. (C)** Interior ∠A = 180° − 140° = 40° and interior ∠B = 180° − 110° = 70°. Then ∠C = 180° − 40° − 70° = 70°. Alternatively the three exterior angles sum to 360°, so the exterior angle at C is 360° − 140° − 110° = 110°, making interior ∠C = 70°. Choosing 110° reports C's exterior angle; 60° and 40° come from arithmetic slips.

**19. (D)** The other two angles add to 180° − 96° = 84°. Since their total is under 90°, each must be below 90°, so both are acute. Neither can be 90° (that alone would exceed 84°), neither can be obtuse for the same reason, and there is no information forcing them to be equal.

**20. (C)** With two equal sides 10 and 10, the third side x satisfies |10 − 10| < x < 10 + 10, i.e. 0 < x < 20. The whole numbers are 1, 2, …, 19, giving 19 values. Counting 20 includes x = 20 (degenerate); 10 only counts up to 10; 21 includes both 0 and 20.

**21. (C)** Sum: (x+30) + 2x + (x−10) = 4x + 20 = 180, so 4x = 160 and x = 40°. The angles are 70°, 80° and 30°; the largest is ∠B = 2x = 80°. Choosing 70° gives ∠A; 60° comes from a wrong x = 30; 30° gives the smallest angle ∠C.

**22. (C)** The exterior angle at B is the supplement of the base angle ∠B, so ∠B = 180° − 115° = 65°. Since AB = AC, ∠C = ∠B = 65°, hence ∠A = 180° − 65° − 65° = 50°. Choosing 65° reports a base angle; 115° just repeats the exterior angle; 80° wrongly uses 180 − 2(50).

**23. (B)** A triangle is isosceles when at least two angles are equal. (45,45,90) has two 45°, (70,70,40) has two 70°, and (60,60,60) has all equal (equilateral, which is also isosceles) — that is 3. The triple (100,50,30) has three different angles, so it is scalene. So exactly 3 qualify, not all 4 and not just 1 or 2.

**24. (A)** The exterior angle at C (125°) equals the sum of the two remote interior angles ∠A + ∠B. So ∠A = 125° − 65° = 60°. Choosing 55° subtracts wrongly; 65° just copies ∠B; 115° treats 125° as supplementary to ∠A instead of using the remote-angle rule.

**25. (A)** The third side x must satisfy 12 − 7 < x < 12 + 7, i.e. 5 < x < 19. The value 5 cm fails because 7 + 5 = 12 only equals (does not exceed) the third side, so the points would be collinear. The values 8, 15 and 18 all lie strictly inside the range and are valid.

**26. (B)** A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side, so QM = MR; the two triangles PQM and PMR share the same height from P and have equal bases, giving equal areas. A median is perpendicular only in special (isosceles) cases, it bisects the angle only in special cases, and there is no rule making it half of QR.

**27. (D)** The third side x satisfies 14 − 9 < x < 14 + 9, i.e. 5 < x < 23. The even whole numbers strictly between 5 and 23 are 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 22 — exactly 9 of them. Choosing 8 or 10 miscounts the list; 17 is the total number of all (not just even) whole-number values, ignoring the even restriction.

**28. (C)** Equal angles face equal sides. Since ∠B = ∠C, the sides opposite them are equal, so the side opposite ∠C equals the side opposite ∠B = 10 cm. Choosing 6 cm uses the side opposite ∠A; 8 cm averages the two; 16 cm adds them.

**29. (B)** Exterior angles sum to 360°: 2y + 3y + 4y = 9y = 360, so y = 40°. The exterior angles are 80°, 120° and 160°. The largest exterior angle (160°) sits at the smallest interior angle, which is 180° − 160° = 20°. Choosing 40° gives y; 80° gives the smallest exterior angle; 60° = 180−120 is the middle interior angle.

**30. (C)** In a right triangle the altitude from the right-angle vertex drops perpendicularly onto the hypotenuse and its foot lies inside that side, strictly between the two endpoints. It does not reach a vertex, does not fall outside the triangle, and is shorter than the hypotenuse, not equal to it.

**31. (D)** ∠B + ∠C = 180° − 40° = 140°, so the half-angles sum to 70°. In triangle BIC the angle at I is 180° minus the two half-angles: ∠BIC = 180° − 70° = 110°. (Equivalently 90° + half of ∠A = 90° + 20° = 110°.) Choosing 70° forgets to subtract from 180°; 140° uses the full angles; 90° drops the ∠A/2 term.

**32. (A)** The three angles are in arithmetic progression with common difference 20°: they are ∠B + 20°, ∠B, ∠B − 20°. Their sum is 3∠B = 180°, so ∠B = 60°. (The angles are 80°, 60°, 40°.) Choosing 40° gives ∠C; 80° gives ∠A; 50° comes from a faulty split.

**33. (A)** Two equal sides (5, 5) make it isosceles. To check the angles, the base 6 is shorter than the sum-square comparison would need for a right or obtuse angle; in fact with sides 5,5,6 the largest angle is opposite 6 and is acute (a 5-5-6 triangle is close to a 5-5-5 equilateral one, whose angles are all 60°, and lengthening one side slightly keeps that angle below 90°). It is not equilateral (sides differ) and not scalene (two sides equal).

**34. (C)** Let ∠C = c. Then ∠A = 2c and ∠B = 2c − 20. Sum: 2c + (2c − 20) + c = 5c − 20 = 180, so 5c = 200 and c = 40°. (Then ∠A = 80° and ∠B = 60°.) Choosing 80° reports ∠A; 60° reports ∠B; 50° comes from a wrong 4c = 200 slip.

**35. (D)** A line through A parallel to BC creates alternate angles equal to ∠B and ∠C on the two sides, so those two angles are 55° and 65°, summing to 120°. This is exactly 180° − ∠A, consistent with ∠A = 60°. Choosing 60° gives ∠A itself; 110° doubles 55°; 130° doubles 65°.

**36. (D)** The median's foot M is the midpoint of BC; the altitude's foot H is where the perpendicular from A meets BC. They coincide only when AB = AC, i.e. when ∠B = ∠C. Here ∠B = 40° ≠ 70° = ∠C, so AB ≠ AC and M ≠ H. H is not the midpoint, and M is not the foot of the perpendicular.

**37. (A)** For an isosceles triangle the third side equals one of the given sides, so it is 16 or 9 (giving sides 16,16,9 or 16,9,9). Check validity: 16,16,9 works (16+9>16); 16,9,9 works (9+9=18>16). Both are valid, so there are 2 values. Saying 1 misses one case; 3 invents an extra; 0 ignores both.

**38. (C)** The exterior angle at C equals ∠A + ∠B = 150°. With ∠A = 4∠B, we get 4∠B + ∠B = 5∠B = 150°, so ∠B = 30° and ∠A = 120°. Choosing 30° reports ∠B; 96° and 100° are faulty splits of 150°.

**39. (A)** Parts: 3 + 4 + 5 = 12, so one part is 36/12 = 3 cm, giving sides 9, 12 and 15 cm; the longest is 15 cm. A 3-4-5 (here 9-12-15) triangle has 9² + 12² = 81 + 144 = 225 = 15², a Pythagorean relation, so it is right-angled. Choosing 'acute' misreads the angle; 12 cm picks the middle side; 20 cm misuses the parts.

**40. (A)** ∠B + ∠C = 180° − 70° = 110°. With ∠B = 3∠C, we get 3∠C + ∠C = 4∠C = 110°, so ∠C = 27.5° and ∠B = 82.5°. Choosing 27.5° reports ∠C; 55° splits 110° evenly; 110° is the combined sum, not ∠B.

**41. (C)** Let each base angle be b; there are two of them and the vertex angle is 4b. Sum: b + b + 4b = 6b = 180°, so b = 30° and the vertex angle is 4b = 120°. Choosing 30° gives a base angle; 60° and 90° come from wrong relationships.

**42. (D)** Validity needs 20 − 13 < x < 20 + 13, i.e. 7 < x < 33, which every value from 13 to 20 already satisfies. The whole numbers from 13 to 20 inclusive are 13, 14, …, 20, that is 20 − 13 + 1 = 8 values. Choosing 7 forgets an endpoint; 6 undercounts; 33 confuses the count with the upper bound.

**43. (A)** The bisector of ∠A splits it into 25° on each side, so in triangle ABD the angle ∠BAD = 25°. With ∠ABD = 60°, the third angle ∠ADB = 180° − 25° − 60° = 95°. Choosing 85° forgets to halve ∠A; 70° uses the full 50°; 65° is an unrelated subtraction.

**44. (A)** All three angles different means all three sides differ, so the triangle is scalene. The largest angle 95° exceeds 90°, so it is obtuse. It is not acute (an angle over 90° exists), not isosceles (no two angles equal), and not right (no 90° angle).

**45. (B)** An altitude's foot falls outside the opposite side only when the angle at the near vertex is obtuse; here the foot is beyond B, so ∠B is obtuse. It is not ∠A that is obtuse (that altitude is from A and still drops onto BC's line), it is not a right angle (then the foot would be at B), and an equilateral triangle has all feet inside.

**46. (D)** The third side x satisfies 25 − 18 < x < 25 + 18, i.e. 7 < x < 43. The largest whole-number value is 42 and the smallest is 8, so the difference is 42 − 8 = 34. Choosing 43 subtracts the loose bounds; 35 uses 43−8; 33 uses 42−9 or similar slips.

**47. (B)** Let ∠A = a; then ∠B = 3a and ∠C = 5a. Sum: a + 3a + 5a = 9a = 180°, so a = 20°, giving angles 20°, 60° and 100°. The largest angle, ∠C = 100°, exceeds 90°, so the triangle is obtuse. It is not acute (an angle over 90° exists), not right (no 90°), and not equiangular (the angles differ).

**48. (A)** An exterior angle and its adjacent interior angle are supplementary, so the adjacent interior angle is 180° − 105° = 75°. The bisector detail does not change this value. Choosing 105° copies the exterior angle; 37.5° halves 75° (the bisected halves, not the whole); 52.5° halves 105°.

**49. (A)** Sum: 4x + 3x + 2x = 9x = 180°, so x = 20° and the angles are 80°, 60° and 40°. The longest side lies opposite the largest angle; ∠A = 80° is largest, so side a is longest. Side b faces 60° (middle) and side c faces 40° (shortest); the sides are not equal since the angles differ.

**50. (B)** ∠B + ∠C = 180° − 60° = 120°, so the half-angles sum to 60°. In triangle BIC, ∠BIC = 180° − 60° = 120° (equivalently 90° + ∠A/2 = 90° + 30° = 120°). Choosing 60° forgets the 180° subtraction; 150° miscomputes the half-sum; 90° drops the ∠A/2 term.

**51. (D)** Because the triangle has an obtuse angle (120°), the altitudes from the two acute (30°) vertices land outside the triangle, on the extensions of the sides adjacent to the obtuse angle. They do not fall inside (that happens in acute triangles), they coincide with sides only at a right angle, and there is no rule equating them to that median.

**52. (B)** The two equal sides together measure 32 − 12 = 20 cm, so each equal side is 20/2 = 10 cm. Check validity: 10 + 10 = 20 > 12, valid. Choosing 6 cm wrongly subtracts 12 from 20 and halves; 12 cm copies the base; 20 cm gives the total of the two equal sides, not one.

**53. (D)** The exterior angle at A equals the sum of the remote interior angles ∠B + ∠C = 145°. So ∠C = 145° − 80° = 65°. Choosing 35° subtracts 145 from 180; 100° supplements 80°; 80° copies ∠B.

**54. (C)** With two sides 6 and 6, the third side L satisfies 0 < L < 12, so the largest whole number is 11. (Indeed 6 + 6 = 12 must exceed L, so L = 11 works but 12 fails.) Choosing 12 cm violates the strict inequality; 6 cm is too small to be the maximum; 10 cm is allowed but not the largest.

**55. (A)** By definition an altitude is the perpendicular segment from a vertex to the opposite side (or its extension), so the altitude from R is perpendicular to PQ. An angle bisector splits the vertex angle, not necessarily meeting the side at a right angle; a median hits the midpoint, not necessarily perpendicularly; so not all three are perpendicular.

**56. (B)** Parts: 1 + 2 + 6 = 9, so one part is 180/9 = 20°. The angles are 20°, 40° and 120°. The 120° angle exceeds 90°, so the triangle is obtuse. It is not acute (an angle over 90° exists), not right (no 90°), and not equiangular (angles differ).

**57. (C)** Three consecutive even numbers summing to 30 are 8, 10 and 12 (8+10+12 = 30). They satisfy 8 + 10 = 18 > 12, so a valid triangle forms, and since all three sides differ it is scalene. It is not isosceles or equilateral (no equal sides), and it does exist.

**58. (D)** The bisector makes ∠BAD = 40°/2 = 20°. In triangle ABD the angles sum to 180°, so ∠B = 180° − 100° − 20° = 60°. Choosing 80° forgets to halve ∠A; 40° copies ∠A; 20° gives ∠BAD instead of ∠B.

**59. (B)** Interior angle at the first vertex = 180° − 130° = 50°; at the second = 180° − 112° = 68°. The third interior angle = 180° − 50° − 68° = 62°. Choosing 118° gives the third exterior angle (360 − 130 − 112 = 118); 50° and 68° are the other two interior angles, not the third.

**60. (B)** In an isosceles triangle the perpendicular (altitude) from the apex A to the base BC also bisects the base, so D is the midpoint and DC = BD = 7 cm. Therefore BC = BD + DC = 7 + 7 = 14 cm. Choosing 7 cm gives only half; 21 cm trebles; 3.5 cm halves the wrong way.
