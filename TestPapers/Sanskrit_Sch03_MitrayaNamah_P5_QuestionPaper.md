1. A grammarian states: 'नमः, स्वाहा and स्वस्ति all force a following word into the dative, yet none of them is a verb.' The single deepest reason their dative government CANNOT be explained the way a verb like 'दा' (to give) governs a dative recipient is that:
   (A) they are nouns standing in the dative themselves, and the following word merely agrees with them in case
   (B) they are the imperative forms of three roots meaning 'bow', 'offer' and 'bless', so the dative is the कर्म of each
   (C) they govern the dative only because the words after them happen to be Sun-names, which always take -आय
   (D) they are अव्यय (indeclinables) whose dative object is fixed purely by idiomatic convention, with no verbal action whose recipient (सम्प्रदान कारक) the dative could mark

2. Compare the dative-building of three Sun-names: अ-stem आदित्य → आदित्याय, इ-stem रवि → रवये, उ-stem भानु → भानवे. A student says 'all three just add -आय in disguise'. The precise morphophonemic truth is that:
   (A) all three add -आय, but a vowel is silently deleted in रवये and भानवे to shorten the word
   (B) only the अ-stem takes the bare ending -आय; रवि and भानु first strengthen their final vowel to its गुण grade (इ→ए, उ→ओ) before the dative suffix, so रवये and भानवे end in -ए, not -आय
   (C) the इ and उ stems lengthen to ई and ऊ and then add -आय, so रवये is really रवीआय contracted
   (D) रवि and भानु are themselves already datives, so no ending is added at all

3. A pupil writes the salutation to the female teacher as आचार्याय नमः, reasoning 'मित्र→मित्राय, so आचार्या→आचार्याय'. Two independent things are wrong with this. They are:
   (A) नमः does not govern आचार्या at all, and आचार्या has no dative form, so the salutation cannot be made
   (B) आचार्याय is correct, but नमः should come first, giving नमः आचार्याय
   (C) आचार्या is masculine, so its dative is आचार्यम्, and नमः requires the accusative
   (D) the stem is treated as if it were a masculine अ-stem (giving -आय), whereas आचार्या is a feminine आ-stem whose dative singular is -ायै, so the correct form is आचार्यायै नमः

4. The chapter pairs twelve postures, twelve Sun-names and twelve months. A clever student argues: 'If the names are mere synonyms (एकार्थक) of one Sun, then assigning a DIFFERENT name to each posture is pointless repetition.' The best resolution is that:
   (A) the student is right; the chapter should use मित्राय for all twelve postures and drop the other names
   (B) the names are equivalent in reference (all denote the one Sun) yet each evokes a distinct aspect/quality of that Sun, so pairing a different name with each posture enriches the single yearly discipline without implying twelve suns
   (C) each of the twelve names denotes a genuinely different sun, so there is no repetition at all
   (D) the names are antonyms, so each posture salutes the opposite of the previous one's deity

5. In सूर्यनमस्कारः the final visarga (-ः) appears. A student claims it is सूर्य's own nominative visarga, surviving inside the compound. The correct account is that:
   (A) the -ः is सूर्य's nominative visarga, preserved because सूर्य is the head of the compound
   (B) both सूर्य and नमस्कार keep their own visargas, but the first is silent in pronunciation
   (C) the -ः is a dative ending of सूर्य reduced from -आय inside the compound
   (D) the prior member सूर्य surrenders ALL case marking inside the समास; the final -ः belongs to the whole compound, carried by its last member नमस्कार, which alone takes the case of the compound as a unit

6. नीरोगम् glosses स्वस्थम्. नीरोग analyses as निः + रोग. A pupil insists 'निः intensifies, so नीरोग means very-diseased, hence स्वस्थ secretly means terminally ill.' The decisive refutation is that:
   (A) निः is a privative/negating prefix meaning 'free from / without', so नीरोग = 'free from disease' = healthy, exactly matching स्वस्थ; निः does not intensify the noun it attaches to
   (B) निः does intensify, but रोग here means 'health', so नीरोग means very-healthy by accident
   (C) निः negates, but रोग means 'posture', so नीरोग means 'free of postures', i.e. resting
   (D) नीरोग and स्वस्थ are unrelated; the gloss in the chapter is an error

7. The four fruits are स्वस्थं शरीरम्, वीर्यम्, तेजः and प्रज्ञा. A student sorts them into 'bodily' vs 'non-bodily'. The MOST defensible three-way grouping the chapter supports is:
   (A) all four are bodily, since the practice is physical and the mind is not addressed
   (B) स्वस्थं शरीरम् and प्रज्ञा are bodily; वीर्यम् and तेजः are mental
   (C) bodily-structural (स्वस्थं शरीरम् = sound body), energetic (वीर्यम् = vigour and तेजः = radiance, both forms of vital energy), and cognitive (प्रज्ञा = wisdom)
   (D) वीर्यम् is cognitive, तेजः is bodily, and प्रज्ञा and स्वस्थं शरीरम् are energetic

8. A learner builds three matched mantras for a single posture experiment: नमः सवित्रे (for the ऋ-stem सवितृ, dative सवित्रे), नमः पूष्णे (for पूषन्, dative पूष्णे), and नमः रवये (for the इ-stem रवि). He then 'regularises' all three to -आय. Which regularisation is the ONLY grammatically defensible one — i.e. which word actually takes -आय?
   (A) रवि takes -आय, giving रवाय, because all Sun-names ultimately use -आय
   (B) पूषन् takes -आय, giving पूषाय, because न्-stems drop the न् and behave like अ-stems
   (C) सवितृ takes -आय, giving सवित्राय, because ऋ-stems add -आय directly
   (D) none of the three takes -आय; सवितृ, पूषन् and रवि belong to stem-classes (ऋ/न्/इ) whose datives are सवित्रे, पूष्णे and रवये respectively, so 'regularising' to -आय would corrupt all three

9. Expanding सूर्यनमस्कारः into two words gives सूर्याय नमस्कारः, with सूर्याय in the dative. A pupil concludes 'therefore the compound सूर्यनमस्कारः is itself a dative-meaning word.' The accurate correction is that:
   (A) the dative relation between सूर्य and नमस्कार is INTERNAL to the compound's meaning ('salutation to the Sun'), but the compound as a whole word carries its own case ending on नमस्कार and is not itself in the dative — सूर्यनमस्कारः here is nominative
   (B) correct — the compound is permanently locked in the dative case and can never be nominative
   (C) wrong — the internal relation is genitive ('salutation of the Sun'), not dative
   (D) the compound has no internal relation at all; the two members are simply listed side by side

10. मित्र means both 'human friend' (a common noun, often neuter मित्रम्) and the Sun-name मित्र (masculine). In the mantra मित्राय नमः, what fixes the masculine Sun-reading rather than the neuter 'to the friend'?
   (A) nothing fixes it; मित्राय is ambiguous and the mantra could equally mean 'salutation to friendship'
   (B) the salutational context with नमः plus the parallel position among the other eleven Sun-names fixes मित्र as the masculine Sun-deity; the dative मित्राय is the masculine form here
   (C) the spelling differs — the Sun-name is मीत्र with a long ई, the friend is मित्र
   (D) the -आय ending only ever attaches to deities, so any -आय word must be a god

11. A student chants नमः गुरवे, नमः सूर्याय, नमः आचार्यायै, नमः रवये and नमः पूष्णे, then says 'every one ends in a different sound, so they must be different cases.' The unifying fact is:
   (A) they are five different cases, since Sanskrit assigns one case per ending shape
   (B) all five are dative singulars — the single case नमः governs — and the varying endings (-वे, -आय, -ायै, -ये, -ष्णे) merely reflect different stem-classes and gender, not different cases
   (C) only नमः सूर्याय is dative; the rest are vocatives addressing each deity
   (D) they are all instrumentals, because नमः is performed 'by means of' each name

12. If a thirteenth posture were genuinely added to the salutation, the chapter's design would strain. The MOST precise statement of WHY — distinguishing the contingent from the necessary — is:
   (A) the one-to-one pairing relies on there being exactly twelve canonical Sun-names (and twelve months) to match the postures; a thirteenth posture would lack a partner name unless the closed set of names were also expanded, which is not warranted
   (B) the Sun would be metaphysically compelled to manifest a thirteenth name to fill the gap
   (C) thirteen is grammatically forbidden in Sanskrit numeral compounds, so the posture is illegal
   (D) adding a posture would automatically delete one Sun-name to keep the total at twelve

13. A pupil renders मित्राय नमः as 'I salute, O friend' — taking मित्राय as a vocative address. Two things are wrong. They are:
   (A) मित्राय is indeed vocative, but नमः should be a finite verb 'I salute' for the reading to hold
   (B) only the case is wrong; the vocative of मित्र genuinely is मित्राय, so the form is fine
   (C) नमः is the vocative here and मित्राय is the subject, so the roles are merely swapped
   (D) मित्राय is dative (the one saluted), not vocative (the one addressed); and the vocative of मित्र would be the bare मित्र / हे मित्र, not the -आय form — so both the case and the implied form are mistaken

14. The chapter glosses स्वस्थम् as नीरोगम् and uses it for both शरीरम् and मनः (स्वस्थं शरीरम्, स्वस्थं मनः). A student concludes 'स्वस्थम् is an indeclinable that never changes.' The accurate grammatical reason it looks the same in both phrases is:
   (A) स्वस्थ is a declinable adjective that AGREES with its noun; both शरीरम् and मनः are neuter singular, so the agreeing adjective is स्वस्थम् in both — identical by agreement, not by indeclinability
   (B) स्वस्थम् is an अव्यय like नमः and therefore has only one fixed form
   (C) मनः is masculine and शरीरम् is neuter, but the adjective ignores gender, so it stays स्वस्थम्
   (D) the two phrases are really one neuter compound, स्वस्थशरीरमनः, so only one ending appears

15. Consider the inference chain a student offers: 'नमः governs the dative → the dative answers "to/for whom" (सम्प्रदान) → सम्प्रदान is the recipient of a giving → therefore नमः literally means "I give to".' Where exactly does this chain first go wrong?
   (A) at the first link: नमः actually governs the accusative, not the dative
   (B) at the last link: नमः takes a dative-form object by idiom, but it does not denote an act of GIVING, so the सम्प्रदान-as-recipient-of-giving rationale, valid for verbs like दा, is misapplied to the non-verbal नमः
   (C) at the second link: the dative answers 'from whom', not 'to/for whom'
   (D) the chain is entirely correct; नमः does mean 'I give a salutation to'

17. A teacher asks why the chapter drills the WHOLE छात्र paradigm (छात्रः, छात्रम्, छात्रेण, छात्राय, छात्रात्, छात्रस्य ...) and not merely the dative छात्राय before introducing मित्राय नमः. The best pedagogical rationale is:
   (A) the mantras are literally composed using inflected forms of छात्र, so all its cases must be memorised
   (B) छात्र is one of the Sun's twelve names, so its full paradigm is needed for the postures
   (C) seeing छात्राय contrasted against छात्रः, छात्रम्, छात्रेण etc. lets the learner pick out -आय as the DATIVE specifically (versus other cases), so they grasp not just the form but why नमः selects the dative and rejects the nominative मित्रः
   (D) Sanskrit forbids teaching any single case in isolation, so the full table is legally required

18. A pupil writes the offering 'to Soma, svaha' as सोमः स्वाहा (using सोम, an अ-stem). Using the same logic that fixes मित्राय after नमः, the corrected form and the principle are:

19. Two strings: (i) सूर्याय नमस्कारः and (ii) सूर्यनमस्कारः. A student says they have the same number of independent words and the same syntax. The sharpest distinction is that:

26. सूर्यनमस्कारः joins सूर्य + नमस्कार with NO vowel change at the seam. But the compound महा + आत्मन् shows that sandhi CAN occur at a seam. Which statement correctly contrasts these two seams?
