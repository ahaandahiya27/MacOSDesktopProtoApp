57. (B) A forest influences temperature, moisture and rainfall, soil stability, flood and drought patterns and carbon dioxide together, so losing it changes all of these, not just temperature. Shade is only one of many roles, so 'only temperature' is too narrow. Forests affect water, soil and climate as well as gases, so 'only carbon dioxide' is too narrow. Forests have measurable effects, so 'nothing at all' is wrong.

58. (D) Energy falls at each transfer, so the organism furthest from the producer, the top hawk, traces back to the smallest share. Body size does not set the energy share, so the grasshopper is not chosen for being small. Middle animals do not always get the least; the top gets the least, so the frog is wrong. The grass is the producer with the most captured energy, not the least.

59. (C) Killing grasshoppers starves the frogs (decline), pushing the snake onto its other prey, the mice; the hawk still has snakes, so it is only mildly affected. The snake also eats mice, so it does not starve. Mice eat grass, not grasshoppers, so losing grasshoppers does not remove the mice's food. Grasshoppers are low in the web and do not compete with the hawk for snakes, so the hawk does not increase.

60. (A) Decomposers are the step that breaks dung and dead bodies into humus, returning nutrients to the soil; cut them and the nutrients stay locked in undecayed matter, so this break most directly stops the return. Grazing animals pass nutrients on but do not make humus, so removing them is not the key break. Rain does not carry nutrients out of dead matter into roots; decomposers release them into the soil first. Sunlight powers plant growth but is not the link that returns nutrients to the soil, so cutting it does not directly stop the return.
