Worked Solutions

1. (D) Heat is the total thermal energy; it depends on both temperature and the amount (mass) of substance. A small amount of very hot water can hold less total heat than a large amount of moderately warm water, so the litre at 30 °C can contain more heat than the tablespoon at 90 °C. The claim that heat depends only on temperature ignores the amount of substance. Heat and temperature are not the same quantity, so they need not match. And a larger amount is not automatically hotter — temperature is set by how vigorously the particles move, not by how much there is.

2. (C) Heat flows from the hotter body to the colder one until both reach the same (equilibrium) temperature; with identical blocks that common value lies between, near 50 °C. Metals readily conduct, so the blocks do not stay separate. Heat stops flowing once temperatures are equal — the cold block cannot overshoot past the hot one. And the hot block warms the cold block, so the pair does not simply drop to the cold value of 20 °C.

3. (B) Objects left together in one room reach the same temperature. Metal feels colder because it is a good conductor and pulls heat from the skin quickly, cooling the touch point fast; the sensation is about heat flow, not a lower temperature. The claim that metal always cools lower ignores thermal equilibrium. Plastic does not produce its own heat. And the hand judges both surfaces by the same rule — the difference is conduction speed, not a flaw special to metal.

4. (C) Both liquids read 60 °C, so they are at the same temperature, and each is hotter than the room air; heat therefore flows from each liquid into the cooler air. Heat does not flow between the two cups, since they are not in contact and are at the same temperature anyway. Two things at the same temperature are equally hot regardless of how much heat each stores. And being equal in temperature to each other does not stop them losing heat to the colder surroundings.

5. (B) A thermometer reads correctly only after its bulb liquid reaches the same temperature as what it touches; the thread then holds steady, and that mark is the temperature. The contents always have a temperature — a steady reading does not mean the temperature vanished. The thermometer is not 'out of heat'; it simply matched the sample. And a thermometer shows temperature, not the total heat content, which would also depend on the amount of substance.

6. (A) Temperature does not depend on amount, so a thermometer reads 5 °C in both. The pool holds more heat energy only because it contains so much more water — that is a difference in total heat, not in temperature. More water does not raise the temperature reading. Metal does not 'concentrate' temperature. And 5 °C is well within an ordinary laboratory thermometer's range, so both can be read.

7. (D) Clinical thermometers have a kink that traps the thread so the reading stays fixed once the thermometer is removed, letting the nurse read it later at the desk. A laboratory thermometer has no kink, so its thread would drop as it cooled on the way to the desk, and its range is overkill for a fever. The kink is essential, not something to remove. So the laboratory thermometer is the wrong tool for a reading taken away from the body.

8. (C) A clinical thermometer reads only about 35–42 °C and its kink locks the thread, so it can neither reach boiling temperatures nor follow a freely changing reading — both fatal for this task. The liquid expands just as readily; the narrow range is a design choice, not a limit of the liquid. Cost is not the governing reason — accuracy over the body range is. Both thermometers use Celsius, so a unit difference does not explain the wider laboratory scale.

12. (B) A laboratory thermometer has no kink, so its thread moves freely and the reading can shift if conditions or position change; a clinical thermometer's kink locks the thread so its reading is held. Not all thermometers trap their threads — only the clinical kink does. So it is false that both lock or both move freely. 19. (A) In a solid metal rod, heat passes from particle to particle by conduction, reaching and melting the nearer wax first and the farther wax later — exactly the observed order. 22. Gases can hold and carry heat (chiefly by convection), so it is false that they hold none. Solids are not inherently colder than gases — temperature is unrelated to the packing. 23. (A) Metal is a good conductor, carrying heat from the hot curry up to the handle; wood is an insulator, so little heat reaches its handle and it stays cool. 28.
