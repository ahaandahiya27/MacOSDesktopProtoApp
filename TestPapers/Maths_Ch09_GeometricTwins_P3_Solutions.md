## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** For SAS the equal angle must sit between the two equal sides. Here AB and BC meet at B, but the given equal angle is at C — it is opposite side AB, not included between AB and BC. So the data is Side-Side-Angle (SSA), which is the ambiguous case: two non-congruent triangles can fit the same SSA data. Calling it SAS ignores the word 'included.' ASA needs two angles, but only one angle is given, so that path fails too. SSS would need the third side to be forced, but SSA does not force the third side uniquely. Hence congruence cannot be claimed.

**2. (B)** Congruence written as DEF ≅ XYZ matches vertices in order: D↔X, E↔Y, F↔Z. So side EF corresponds to side YZ. Since EF = 7 cm, YZ = 7 cm. DE corresponds to XY (= 5) and FD to ZX (= 9), so those are wrong. The letter order is exactly what tells us the correspondence, so 'cannot be decided' is false.

**3. (B)** Angles A and B are at the ends of side AB, so AB is the side included between the two equal angles. Two angles and the included side give ASA, which guarantees congruence. AAA never proves congruence (it only gives similarity), so even though the third angle is also 70° in both, that alone would not be enough — what makes it work is the equal included side. SAS is mislabelled here; we have two angles, not two sides.

**4. (A)** A rhombus has all four sides equal but its angles are not all 90° (otherwise it would be a square). Its two diagonals are its axes of reflection: folding along either diagonal maps the figure onto itself. The mid-lines joining midpoints of opposite sides are NOT axes for a rhombus (they are for a rectangle/square but not a rhombus), so it is not 4. It clearly has more than one and certainly more than zero.

**5. (D)** A regular n-gon has rotational symmetry of order n: it coincides with itself after each rotation of 360°/n. For a regular pentagon, 360°/5 = 72°, and there are exactly 5 positions (at 72°, 144°, 216°, 288°, 360°) where it maps onto itself, so the order is 5. The figure also has 5 lines of symmetry, but the question asks for rotational order, not total symmetries (which would tempt 10). Order 1 means no rotational symmetry, which is false for a regular polygon.

**6. (D)** Compare triangles ABD and ACD: AB = AC (given), angle BAD = angle CAD (AD bisects A), and AD is common. That is two sides and the included angle, so SAS gives congruence. By CPCT, BD = DC (so D is the midpoint) and angle ADB = angle ADC; since these two add to the straight angle 180°, each is 90°. So BOTH results hold. They are genuinely congruent (same size), not just similar, because AD is shared, fixing the size.

**7. (A)** SSS states three pairs of equal sides guarantee congruence — the triangle's shape and size are then fully determined, so any 3-4-5 triangle is congruent to any other. With only two sides given, the third vertex is not pinned down: the included angle can vary, giving infinitely many different triangles, so two sides are not enough. The uniqueness has nothing to do with the triangle being right-angled (every SSS triple is unique, right-angled or not) nor with the sides being coprime.

**8. (C)** We need a letter with both a vertical line of symmetry and 180° rotational symmetry. H has a vertical axis (left/right mirror) and also looks identical after a half-turn, so it satisfies both. A has a vertical line of symmetry but no rotational symmetry (upside-down it is not an A). S and Z have 180° rotational symmetry but no line of symmetry (their mirror image is reversed). Only H meets both conditions.

**9. (D)** A line of symmetry would fold the triangle onto itself, which requires at least two equal sides on either side of the fold. In a scalene triangle all three sides differ, so no fold maps it onto itself — it has 0 lines of symmetry. An isosceles triangle has exactly 1 and an equilateral has 3, but scalene has none.

**10. (A)** A regular hexagon has 6 equal sides, so its rotational order is 6. The smallest mapping rotation is 360°/6 = 60°. After every 60° it lands on itself, giving 6 distinct positions in a full turn. Order 3 with 120° would be true for a figure that only repeats every third of a turn (like an equilateral triangle pattern), not a regular hexagon. There is no order 12 for a plain regular hexagon.

**11. (B)** All three pairs of sides are equal, which is precisely the SSS criterion, so the triangles ARE congruent — and being all 6 cm, both are equilateral. The student's reasoning is wrong because SAS needs a stated included angle; here we cite SSS. Equal sides do force the triangle (so it is not 'only similar' — congruent triangles are the same size), and no angle is needed for SSS, so the last option is also wrong.

**12. (D)** Triangles PQS and PRS: PQ = PR, angle QPS = angle RPS (PS bisects P), PS common — SAS, so they are congruent. By CPCT, QS = SR and angle PSQ = angle PSR; these are equal and sum to 180°, so each is 90°. Thus PS is both the median and the altitude — i.e. the perpendicular bisector of QR. All three conclusions hold simultaneously, regardless of the size of angle P (the isosceles condition alone is enough, not a right angle at P).

**13. (C)** An isosceles triangle that is not equilateral has exactly one axis: the line through the apex and the midpoint of the base. A non-square rectangle has 2 axes (the two mid-lines), an equilateral triangle has 3, and a circle has infinitely many. So only the isosceles triangle has exactly one.

**14. (C)** From the perimeter, AC = 30 − 8 − 11 = 11 cm. Under ABC ≅ DEF the correspondence is A↔D, B↔E, C↔F, so DF corresponds to AC (both join the first and third vertices). Hence DF = AC = 11 cm. DF is not AB (=8, that corresponds to DE) and is certainly not the whole perimeter. The angles are not needed — corresponding sides are equal under congruence.

**15. (C)** In the first triangle the hypotenuse is √(81+144) = √225 = 15 cm. So both triangles have a right angle, a hypotenuse of 15 cm, and a leg of 9 cm — exactly the RHS criterion (Right angle, Hypotenuse, Side). RHS guarantees congruence. The second's other leg is in fact forced to be 12 cm by Pythagoras, so it is not unknown. RHS is a valid rule and is NOT the same as the ambiguous SSA — the right angle plus hypotenuse removes the ambiguity. Being congruent, they are more than just similar.

**16. (B)** A square has rotational symmetry of order 4: at 90°, 180°, 270° and 360° it coincides with its start. That is 4 matching positions in a full turn. The number 8 confuses rotational order (4) with total symmetries including its 4 reflection lines (4+4=8), but the question asks only about rotation. So the answer is 4.

**17. (B)** A reflection is a rigid motion (an isometry): it preserves all lengths and angles, so the image is congruent to the original. However a reflection flips orientation — a left hand reflects to a right hand. It does not enlarge anything (that would be wrong about size and similarity), it does not keep the same orientation (rotation/translation do, reflection does not), and it certainly does not change the area of the figure itself.

**18. (C)** The right angle at A (and D) is included between the two legs AB and AC (DE and DF). Two sides and the included angle is exactly SAS, the most direct rule here. RHS is for when you know the hypotenuse and one leg plus the right angle — but here both legs are given, so SAS is cleaner (RHS would still work after computing the hypotenuse, but it is not the most direct). ASA fails because only one angle is given, and AAA never proves congruence.

**19. (A)** Order of rotational symmetry counts how many positions in a full turn map the figure onto itself. Order 1 means only the complete 360° turn works — i.e. the figure has no genuine rotational symmetry. It does not look the same at 90° (that would be order 4) or at 180° (that would be order 2 or more). Rotational order is unrelated to the COUNT of mirror lines, so 'exactly one line of symmetry' is a different property.

**20. (D)** Angles A and B sit at the two ends of side AB, so AB is the included side between the two equal angles — this is exactly ASA, which guarantees congruence. Two angles plus a side DO fix a triangle (the third angle is forced, and a side fixes the size), so the second option is false. It is not 'only similar': the equal side fixes the size, making them congruent. SAS is the wrong label because we have angles, not two sides.

**21. (D)** By definition of reflection, the mirror line is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining a point to its image. So m meets PP' at its midpoint and at 90°, and P, P' are equidistant from m regardless of orientation. The mirror line is not parallel to PP' (it is perpendicular to it), and it does not pass through P' itself unless P were on the line. Equal distance from the mirror always holds, not only for horizontal segments.

**22. (A)** Under ABC ≅ DEF, angle B corresponds to angle E, so angle B = 67°. The three angles of triangle ABC sum to 180°, so angle C = 180° − 48° − 67° = 65°. Angle C equals angle F by correspondence, but its value comes from the angle sum. 67° is angle B, 48° is angle A, so neither is C, and it certainly can be found.

**23. (C)** S looks the same after a 180° turn (order 2 rotational symmetry) but has no mirror line — its reflection is reversed. O has infinite rotational symmetry and many lines of symmetry, so it fails the 'no line' condition. T has one vertical line of symmetry but no rotational symmetry. E has a horizontal line of symmetry but no rotational symmetry. Only S has order-2 rotation with no mirror line.

**24. (A)** SSS says three pairs of equal sides are by themselves enough to guarantee congruence — no angle needed. So the child's premise is mistaken. Three equal sides fix BOTH shape and size (each angle is determined by the sides via the triangle's rigidity), so 'only similar' is wrong. It is SSS, not SAS, because we are matching three sides, not two-sides-and-an-included-angle.

**25. (D)** Both transformations are symmetries of the equilateral triangle: a reflection in any of its three axes maps it onto itself, and a 120° rotation (since order = 3) also maps it onto itself. Applying two such symmetries still leaves the triangle covering its original region — the result coincides with the original. Rigid motions never change size, so it is not larger. Because each step is a self-symmetry, the figure does coincide (not a stuck mirror image, not displaced).

**26. (A)** List the three pairs: AC = AD (given), BC = BD (given), and AB = AB (shared side). That is three pairs of equal sides — SSS — which proves congruence. No angle is stated, so SAS and ASA cannot be invoked directly. There is no given right angle, so RHS does not apply; the kite shape does not force a right angle here.

**27. (B)** For a regular n-gon, both the number of lines of symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry equal n. A regular octagon (n = 8) therefore has 8 lines of symmetry and rotational order 8 (smallest turn 360°/8 = 45°). The other options mismatch the two counts, but for a regular polygon they are always equal to each other and to n.

**28. (A)** A non-square rectangle has 2 lines of symmetry — the two lines joining midpoints of opposite sides (the diagonals are NOT axes for a non-square rectangle). It also has rotational symmetry of order 2: a 180° turn maps it onto itself. A square would have 4 of each, but a plain rectangle does not. Order 1 would mean no rotational symmetry, which is false since the half-turn works.

**29. (B)** Since AB = AC, base angles B and C are equal; each = (180 − 36)/2 = 72°. BD bisects angle B (72°), so angle DBC = 36°. Mistaking the full base angle gives 72°; halving 36° wrongly gives 18°; 54° comes from an unrelated computation. The correct value is half of 72°, i.e. 36°.

**30. (C)** Reflection is an isometry: every length is preserved, so the perimeter (a sum of lengths) is unchanged, and the enclosed area is unchanged too. Congruent figures always share equal area and equal perimeter; a mirror image is still congruent. Nothing is halved or altered — only the left/right orientation flips.

**31. (A)** The correspondence A↔P, B↔Q, C↔R fixes every matching part. So angle B = angle Q (not angle R), and side AC (joining A and C) corresponds to side PR (joining P and R), giving AC = PR. The other options mismatch the order: AB corresponds to PQ (not QR), angle A to angle P (not Q), angle C to angle R (not P), BC to QR (not PR). Only the first option respects the correspondence throughout.

**32. (B)** Order 3 means it coincides at 360°/3 = 120° (and 240°, 360°). 60° would map it to an upside-down position that is NOT the same triangle, so 60° is wrong. A regular polygon's lines of symmetry equal its rotational order, so an equilateral triangle has 3 lines (one through each vertex and the opposite midpoint), not 1. A square's 90°/4 figures are irrelevant here.

**33. (A)** Two triangles can have the same area while having completely different shapes (e.g. a tall thin triangle and a short wide one), so equal area does NOT force congruence — that statement is false. The others are true: congruent triangles are a special case of similar (with ratio 1); AAA fixes shape only (similarity), not size; and reflection is an isometry, giving a congruent image.

**34. (A)** N has no vertical line of symmetry, so reflecting it in a vertical mirror produces a reversed N (the diagonal stroke slants the other way). It does not stay a normal N (that would require a vertical axis it lacks). A vertical reflection does not turn N into Z (that is a different transformation) nor into M (M and N are not mirror images of each other). N does, however, have 180° rotational symmetry — a different property from the vertical reflection asked about here.

**35. (C)** In triangles ABM and ACM: BM = MC (given), angle AMB = angle AMC = 90° (AM ⟂ BC), and AM is common. Two sides and the included right angle give SAS, so the triangles are congruent. By CPCT, AB = AC, so the triangle is isosceles. The midpoint condition is not coincidental — it forces the isosceles property. There is no reason angle A is 90°, and equilateral would require all three sides equal, which is not shown.

**36. (C)** We have a Right angle, equal Hypotenuses, and an equal Side (leg) — exactly RHS, which guarantees congruence. Although the given angle is not between the two given sides (so superficially it resembles SSA), the special fact that it is a RIGHT angle, paired with the hypotenuse, kills the ambiguity: Pythagoras forces the other leg to be √(13²−5²)=12 cm in both. The right angle is not the included angle between the hypotenuse and the leg (those two sides meet at an acute vertex), so SAS is mislabelled; ASA needs two angles, which are not given.

**37. (D)** A general parallelogram has rotational symmetry of order 2 (a 180° turn maps it onto itself) but no line of symmetry (its diagonals and mid-lines do not fold it onto itself). A non-square rectangle has 2 lines of symmetry, so it fails the 'zero lines' test; an isosceles trapezium has 1 line of symmetry; a regular pentagon has 5 lines. Only the general parallelogram pairs rotational symmetry with no axis.

**38. (C)** If a 90° rotation is a symmetry, then repeating it gives symmetries at 180°, 270° and 360° as well — four positions in a full turn — so the order is at least 4. It cannot be 2 (180° only), because a 90° symmetry forces order divisible by 4. Order 90 is meaningless here (order counts positions, and 360/90 = 4). Order 1 means no symmetry, contradicting the given 90° symmetry.

**39. (C)** The altitude from the apex of an isosceles triangle is also the perpendicular bisector of the base (by the SAS congruence of the two halves), so BM = MC = 6 cm. In right triangle ABM, AM = √(AB² − BM²) = √(100 − 36) = √64 = 8 cm. The value 6 cm is BM, not AM; 10 is the slant side AB; 2 comes from a wrong subtraction. The altitude is 8 cm.

**40. (B)** AAA alone gives only similarity. But the moment ONE pair of corresponding sides is equal, two equal angles plus a corresponding side become ASA (or AAS), which proves congruence — the equal side locks the scale. So they ARE congruent. It is the equal side, not 'AAA', that does the work, so 'congruent by AAA' is the wrong justification even though the conclusion of congruence is right. Combining angle and side data is perfectly legitimate.

**41. (B)** A kite with AB = AD and CB = CD is symmetric about the diagonal joining the vertices between the equal-length pairs — that is AC. Reflection in AC therefore swaps the two off-axis vertices B and D, so B maps to D. A and C lie on the axis and stay fixed (B cannot map to a fixed vertex), and B does not map to the midpoint of BD (reflection maps a vertex to a vertex here).

**42. (A)** The correspondence ABC ↔ ACB pairs AB with AC, the common angle A with itself, and AC with AB — two sides and the included angle, i.e. SAS. By CPCT this yields angle B = angle C, the base-angle theorem. ASA would illegitimately assume the very equality (angle B = angle C) we are trying to prove. While 'a triangle matches itself' is trivially true side-for-side, the elegant proof of equal base angles uses the SAS self-map, not a trivial SSS, and no right angle is involved so RHS does not apply.

**43. (C)** Rotational symmetry and reflection symmetry are independent. A figure such as a pinwheel or an S-shaped four-armed swastika-like motif can have rotational order 4 yet zero lines of symmetry. So order-4 rotation does NOT force any mirror line. (A square happens to have 4 lines too, but that is a property of the square, not a consequence of order-4 rotation.) Hence the guaranteed minimum number of lines is zero.

**44. (B)** Sides AB and BC meet at vertex B, and the equal angle is at B (and E). So the angle IS included between the two equal sides — this is SAS, which guarantees congruence. It is not SSA: SSA would be when the equal angle is NOT between the two equal sides, but here it is. ASA requires two equal angles (only one is given). RHS requires a right angle and a hypotenuse, neither stated.

**45. (D)** A regular n-gon has rotational order n. So the orders are: triangle 3, square 4, hexagon 6, dodecagon 12. The largest is the dodecagon at 12. The hexagon (6) is smaller, the square's right angles do not maximise anything, and regular polygons do NOT all share the same order — order grows with the number of sides.

**46. (A)** Equal base angles A and B force the opposite sides equal: CA = CB (converse of the isosceles base-angle result). So the triangle is isosceles with apex C, and its axis of symmetry passes through C and the midpoint of AB, i.e. it is the perpendicular bisector of AB. Side AB is a side, not an axis; the perpendicular bisector of CA is not an axis here; and since the triangle is isosceles it certainly does have an axis.

**47. (C)** AB and BC are included at vertex B (DE and EF at E). For SAS — the only rule available with two sides and one angle — the equal angle must be that included angle at B/E. If instead the equal angle were at A/D or C/F, it would be a non-included angle, giving SSA, which does NOT guarantee congruence. So the position very much matters; only the included angle works.

**48. (D)** Repeating every 72° means 360°/72° = 5 positions, so rotational order is 5. Because the construction uses rotation only and no reflection, the motif need not be mirror-symmetric, so the whole pattern has no line of symmetry — 0 lines. Order is the count of positions (5), not the angle (so 'order 72' is wrong). Rotational symmetry alone does not create mirror lines, so 5 or 1 lines are wrong.

**49. (A)** DE = DF means base angles E and F are equal: angle E = angle F. With angle D = 3·angle E, the angle sum gives 3E + E + E = 180°, so 5E = 180°, E = 36°. Then angle F = 36° and angle D = 108°. Choosing 45° (from 4E = 180) miscounts the equal base angles; 60° and 30° come from other miscounts. The correct base angle is 36°.

**50. (D)** SSA — two sides and an angle that is not between them — is the ambiguous case: two non-congruent triangles can share the same SSA data, so it does not determine a unique triangle. SSS, ASA and SAS each fix a triangle uniquely (they are the valid congruence criteria). Hence SSA is the odd one out.

**51. (D)** A semicircle has a single axis: the perpendicular to the diameter through its midpoint (folding the curved part onto itself). It has NO rotational symmetry other than the trivial 360° (order 1) — a 180° turn would put the flat edge on top, not matching. It is not like a full circle (infinite symmetry) because the straight edge breaks most symmetry. A half-turn does not map it onto itself, so order is 1, not 2.

**52. (D)** Reflection is an isometry: it preserves every length and angle, so a triangle and its mirror image have all corresponding parts equal — they are congruent. Congruence permits reflections (you may flip a figure to superimpose it), so left/right 'handedness' is irrelevant. This holds for ALL triangles, not just isosceles or equilateral ones; an isosceles or equilateral triangle additionally coincides with its own reflection by symmetry, but even a scalene triangle is congruent to its mirror image.

**53. (B)** Angles A and B sum to 180° − 80° = 100°. With angle A = 2·angle B, we get 2B + B = 100°, so 3B = 100°, B = 100/3 ≈ 33.3°. Under ABC ≅ DEF, angle E corresponds to angle B, so angle E ≈ 33.3°. The value 66.7° is angle A (twice B), 80° is angle C, and 50° would be the answer only if A equalled B, which it does not. Correspondence sends B to E, giving ≈ 33.3°.

**54. (C)** Any diameter of a circle folds the circle exactly onto itself, and there are infinitely many diameters, so a circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry — true. A general (irregular) quadrilateral need have none, so 'every quadrilateral' is false. A scalene triangle has zero, not one, lines of symmetry. An angle DOES have a line of symmetry: its bisector folds one ray onto the other.

**55. (B)** AD a median gives BD = DC; AD perpendicular to BC gives angle ADB = angle ADC = 90°; AD is common. Triangles ABD and ACD therefore match by SAS (two sides and the included right angle), so AB = AC by CPCT — the triangle is isosceles. This says nothing about angle A being 90°, and it is more than 'no consequence' — a median that is also an altitude forces the isosceles property. Equilateral would need all three sides equal, which is not implied.

**56. (C)** A regular n-gon has n lines of symmetry: the equilateral triangle has 3, the regular hexagon has 6. So the hexagon has 6 − 3 = 3 more. They are not equal, fewer sides does not give more symmetry, and the difference is 3, not 6 (6 is the hexagon's total, not the difference).

**57. (D)** Sides AB and AC meet at vertex A, and the given equal angle is at A — so it is the included angle. Two sides with the included angle is SAS, which guarantees congruence. The student's 'ASA' is wrong because ASA requires two angles, but only one angle is given. It is not SSA (the angle IS between the two sides), and AAA is irrelevant since we are not relying on angles to match.

**58. (D)** Composing the reflection in the horizontal axis with the reflection in the vertical axis (which are perpendicular and meet at a point) produces a rotation of 180° about that point — a symmetry of the figure. So the figure must have at least order-2 rotational symmetry. It need not be order 4 (that would require diagonal mirrors too, as in a square). Order 1 or 0 would mean no rotational symmetry, contradicting the forced 180° symmetry.

**59. (B)** The correspondence ABC ↔ BAC pairs A↔B, B↔A, C↔C. So side CA (from C to A) corresponds to side CB (from C to B), forcing CA = CB — the triangle is isosceles with apex C, and the base angles A and B are equal. It does NOT make AB = BC (that would be a different correspondence), and the swap of just two vertices does not force all three sides equal (equilateral) nor a right angle at C.

**60. (A)** Reflection symmetry and rotational symmetry are independent. (i) is false: an isosceles (non-equilateral) triangle has 1 line of symmetry but only order-1 rotation (no genuine rotational symmetry). (ii) is false: a general parallelogram has order-2 rotational symmetry but no line of symmetry. Since each property can occur without the other, both claims fail. So neither implication holds — both are false.
