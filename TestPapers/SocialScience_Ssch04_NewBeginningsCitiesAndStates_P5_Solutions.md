## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** The correct order is Harappan order, then the long non-urban gap of roughly a thousand years, then the spread of iron, then the mahaajanapadas, then the first empires; so the gap is wrongly placed at the very end. The Harappan order is genuinely first, not after iron. The mahaajanapadas come late, well after the Harappans, not before them. The first empires are the final stage, growing out of the mahaajanapadas, so they cannot precede them.

**2. (B)** Copper seals with undeciphered script are hallmarks of the Harappan First Urbanisation, while silver punch-marked coins belong to the later Second Urbanisation, and the higher layer being later fits that long separation. The Harappans did not use coins of any metal, so the first distractor is false. In an undisturbed mound a higher layer is younger, so the coin is not older than the seal. Seals are precisely a Harappan feature, so claiming the Harappans never made seals is wrong.

**3. (D)** A revival would require the same people, place and features carrying on, but the Ganga wave was a fresh rise in a new region with iron and coins the Harappans lacked, which settles it as a Second Urbanisation. It did not reuse the Indus citadels or continue the undeciphered script. The gap was about a thousand non-urban years, not a few decades. The chapter describes no Harappan migration east rebuilding the old cities.

**4. (A)** The claim is that a whole working system, not just walls, was lost; only the survival of trade, writing and administration with continued urban life would undermine it. People returning to village life with trade and writing fading actually supports the claim of lost order. A thousand-year gap before cities returned also supports it. The later cities' iron and coins concern the second wave's novelty and say nothing against the earlier loss.

**5. (B)** Two independent lines converging is convincing precisely because they are unlikely to share the same mistake. Texts do not preserve ordinary people's exact spoken words, and dismissing archaeology overstates the texts. Treating texts as always wrong discards a whole source unfairly. The Harappan First-Urbanisation people did not write the later, differently-scripted texts, so that guarantee is impossible.

**6. (C)** Where 'the people set foot' signals a community that settled and tied itself to a fixed land, exactly the shift that made early states possible. An army on the march contradicts settling down. A footprint coin confuses a territory with money. An empire overshoots the scale of a single clan's home territory.

**7. (D)** The chapter's marks of a state are settled population, fixed territory and an organising authority, and janapadas met all three under a rajaa, so size does not disqualify them. Requiring rule over many regions imposes an empire-sized test the chapter never sets. Having no leader is false, since a rajaa led the janapada. Iron coins from the start is an anachronism, as coined silver money belongs to a later stage.

**8. (B)** The prefix maha- means great, marking scale: mahaajanapadas were the larger states into which earlier janapadas merged. Neither word names a coin or a route. The direction of change ran from small to large, not large to small. Both monarchies and ganas/sanghas existed among the mahaajanapadas, so the republic-versus-monarchy contrast is false.

**9. (A)** The chapter credits fertile soil, available iron and connecting rivers and routes; a cold, dry, riverless plain is the opposite, since rivers were a key advantage, so that is the odd one out. Fertile soil with surpluses is a genuine reason. Access to iron is a genuine reason. Rivers and routes linking the region is a genuine reason.

**10. (C)** Sixteen is a useful traditional grouping from the textual tradition, not a precise modern headcount, so a little caution is fair. Asserting it was certainly one hundred invents a precise figure the chapter never gives. Denying the mahaajanapadas existed contradicts well-attested states. The Harappans vanished long before these lists, so they could not have compiled them.

**11. (C)** The sixteen were many distinct, sizeable states existing around the same time and spread over a broad region from Gandhaara to Anga. Calling them successive empires turns contemporaries into a sequence. Calling them villages in one valley shrinks states into a hamlet cluster. Calling them names for one kingdom collapses many states into one, which the wide span contradicts.

**12. (A)** Walls, a water-filled ditch and a pinch-point gate form one coherent defence-and-control package, exactly what a rich state with rivals would build. Beauty for festivals ignores that all three are defensive. Storing rainwater is contradicted by the rampart and narrow gate, which are not water tanks. Free wandering is directly contradicted by the narrow gate, whose point is to restrict movement.

**13. (A)** Narrowing the gate concentrates movement at one controllable point, letting few guards watch, inspect and even collect tolls. Draining rainwater confuses a gateway with a drain. Two elephants side by side would need a wide gate, the opposite of narrow. Looking graceful treats a security feature as mere decoration.

**14. (D)** Continuous occupation for 2,500 years points to enduring site advantages such as fertile surroundings, river access and trade position. Continuity is not the same as being frozen, so 'nothing changed' is wrong. These were Second-Urbanisation capitals, not Harappan foundations. The chapter describes them as ramparted and moated, so 'never fortified' is false.

**15. (B)** Punch-marked coins appeared in the later Ganga-plains wave; the Harappans had no coinage of any kind, so dating a coin to their period is the error. They used no gold coins either. Coins were used in ancient India, just later, so 'never anywhere' is false. Punch-marked coins were silver, not iron, so naming the metal iron is wrong.

**16. (A)** A medium of exchange exists to ease buying and selling, so its widespread use signals expanding commerce beyond barter's reach. Treating coins as jewellery ignores their role as money. Treating them as grave goods reduces a trade tool to a burial item. Saying coins made barter more popular is self-contradictory, since coins mark a move away from barter.

**17. (D)** Coins drifting far from home is the natural signature of long-distance trade carrying them along the routes, and one cause explains both finds. 'Made on the spot' contradicts the coins being identified with distant regions. 'One identical coin' is false, since regions usually struck their own, which is how these can be traced to Avanti and Anga. 'Forbidden to leave' would make such finds impossible.

**18. (A)** The double difficulty of matching wants is what makes barter clumsy, and a common coin removes it. A sunset restriction is invented. A bank in every village is anachronistic, since barter predates banks. Needing iron tools falsely links swapping goods to a particular technology.

**19. (D)** Although each region usually made its own coins, neighbouring coins also circulated along trade routes, even abroad. 'Never accepted another region's coins' contradicts the finds of foreign coins. 'Only the Harappans minted' is impossible, since coins postdate the Harappans entirely. 'Banned outside the capital' contradicts the cross-regional circulation described.

**20. (C)** Hereditary means power is inherited by birth, so the throne usually went to the king's son or kin, the trait that separates a monarchy from a gana/sangha. The assembly voting each time describes a republic. The wealthiest merchant ignores birth as the basis of succession. A foreign conqueror confuses conquest with inheritance.

**21. (D)** A monarch's duties included keeping order, collecting revenue and maintaining defence; succession was hereditary, not decided by public election, so the election option is the duty that does not belong. Keeping law and order is genuine. Collecting taxes is genuine. Maintaining an army and fortifications is genuine.

**22. (D)** In a monarchy the king held power yet was supposed to govern with the advice of the sabhaa and samiti and his ministers, sharing counsel but not rule. 'Assemblies governed directly, king a figurehead' describes a republic. 'Ignored entirely' erases the advisory role the chapter affirms. 'Fresh king every year by vote' contradicts hereditary kingship.

**23. (B)** Older Vedic words for assemblies alongside later republican terms point to a continuous, evolving tradition of shared decision-making. 'Vedas composed after the mahaajanapadas' reverses the time order the layering implies. 'No earlier roots' ignores that the Vedic terms show exactly such roots. 'Gana and sangha mean a hereditary king' is backwards, since those terms describe republics.

**24. (A)** In a gana/sangha the assembly chose the ruler by debate and, if necessary, a vote, and that locus of decision is the defining republican trait. Inheritance by the eldest son describes a monarchy. Harappan priests appointing the ruler is impossible, since the Harappans were long gone. A foreign empire imposing the ruler misdescribes how these early republics chose their leaders.

**25. (C)** These republics spread decision-making across many family and clan heads, broader than one king's will yet short of universal suffrage. 'Every adult voted equally' overstates inclusion into a modern democracy. 'Single richest individual' narrows power to one person, contradicting shared decision-making. 'Foreign rulers decided' removes self-rule, the opposite of a republic.

**26. (C)** The essential contrast is WHO decides: a single inheriting king versus a decision-making assembly that also picks the ruler. Both relied on farming, so the farming contrast is false. Both maintained armies, so the army contrast is false. Both belonged to the iron age, so the iron-versus-bronze contrast is false.

**27. (B)** The line shows the king governed WITH advice, not that he was powerless or yearly-replaced. 'No real power, assemblies governed' overshoots into a republic. 'Elected every year' contradicts hereditary kingship. 'No army at all' is unrelated and contradicts the monarch's duty of maintaining an army.

**28. (A)** Taxes funded the costly running of a state: soldiers, walls and officials. 'Replace farming' is false, since taxes were often levied on farm produce rather than replacing it. 'Discourage trade' is false, since states benefited from trade. 'Abolish the need for a ruler' is self-contradictory, since collecting revenue is itself one of the ruler's functions.

**29. (B)** Walls, tax collection and armies require an authority able to mobilise resources, the hallmark of organised states. Wandering bands contradict the fixed, organised authority such features imply. Purely religious settlements ignore the very governing apparatus described. Reconstructions of Harappan cities is false, since these were new Ganga-age states, not copies of the long-collapsed order.

**30. (B)** The First Urbanisation worked copper and bronze; iron became widespread only in the later Ganga-plains wave, which it powered. 'Harappans used iron, second wave bronze' reverses the two. 'Both depended on gold tools' is false for both waves. 'Harappan iron coins' is doubly wrong, since the Harappans had no coins and did not work iron.

**31. (D)** 'Necessary but not sufficient' means iron was required yet had to act together with soil, routes and rulers to yield cities. 'Iron alone instantly produced cities' claims sufficiency, the opposite point. 'Never used in the Ganga plains' denies iron's necessity. 'Cities appeared before iron' contradicts the chapter's sequence, in which iron came first and enabled the growth.

**32. (C)** An iron plough breaks tougher soil and grows more, generating the surplus that supports town-dwellers, the engine of urban growth. 'Harvests fall' reverses the expected effect. 'Trade routes close' is wrong, since more surplus means more trade. 'Need for rulers and taxes disappears' is wrong, since a larger settled population needs more administration, not less.

**33. (B)** Iron weapons made armies more effective, helping powerful states conquer and merge rivals into larger kingdoms. Warfare disappearing is the opposite of what stronger armies imply. Farming ending is false, since farming expanded with iron ploughs. Leading to writing links two unrelated developments.

**34. (C)** Since iron tools enabled the land-clearing and farming behind the surplus, removing iron would have starved that surplus and weakened the urban wave. 'Cities grew even faster' contradicts iron's enabling role. 'Harappan civilisation never ended' is irrelevant, since its collapse happened long before and for other reasons. 'Coins invented sooner' is unsupported, since the money economy rested on the trade that iron-fed growth made possible.

**35. (A)** The Second Urbanisation itself disproves the claim, since after about a thousand non-urban years city life reappeared in a new region. 'Never abandoned' is false, since the Harappan cities were indeed lost. 'No gap at all' denies the well-attested gap. 'Cities since the Stone Age' overstates continuity and ignores the documented break.

**36. (B)** A jaati was a community defined by occupation, with skills and customs handed down within families. A water-filled ditch describes a moat. A long-distance highway describes a patha or trade route. A silver coin describes a punch-marked coin, not a social group.

**37. (C)** Kshatriyas were the varna associated with protection and warfare. Brahmins were linked with knowledge and rituals, not battle. Vaishyas were linked with trade and agriculture, not rituals. Shudras were linked with service occupations, not ruling as warriors.

**38. (A)** The chapter stresses that in this early phase movement between occupations was possible, so it was not fixed from the start. 'Never existed' denies a system the chapter describes. 'Only the Harappan cities' is wrong, since it belongs to the later Ganga-plains age. 'Rigid on the very day it began' restates the misconception rather than correcting it.

**39. (D)** 'Uttara' means north and 'dakshina' means south, so the names fix the directions: Uttaraapatha north, Dakshinaapatha south. The first distractor reverses them. 'East to west along the coast' misdescribes routes that ran broadly north and south. 'Inside a single mahaajanapada' shrinks routes that spanned many regions into one state.

**40. (A)** To pass from the northern plains into the peninsula, the southern route crossed the Vindhyas, the dividing range between north and south India. The Himalayas lie far to the north, the wrong direction. The Aravallis run through the northwest, off the southward path. The Western Ghats edge the western coast and are not the range a route from Kaushambi southward would first cross.

**41. (A)** The routes were arteries for people and ideas as well as merchandise: pilgrims, armies, beliefs and coins all travelled along them. 'Only royal letters' is far too narrow. 'Only soldiers' ignores pilgrims, ideas and coins. 'Only iron ore, never people' contradicts the mixing of people and ideas that helped unify the regions.

**42. (B)** The southern trio were the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas. Magadha, Kosala and Avanti were powerful northern mahaajanapadas. Vajji, Malla and Anga lay in the north and east. Kuru, Panchaala and Matsya were northern janapadas/mahaajanapadas, so only the Chola-Chera-Pandya set is southern.

**43. (C)** The south's prized resources, gems, gold and spices, pulled it into the wider trade networks. A cold climate is neither accurate nor a draw for trade. 'No rivers, easier travel' is wrong, since the absence of rivers would hinder movement. 'Far from the sea' is false, since the south's coastal position actually aided maritime trade.

**44. (D)** By this date trade, coins and ideas had linked the regions into a single connected land, with influence spilling beyond India's borders. 'Emptied of people' contradicts the bustling, connected picture. 'Three separate islands' is geographically false. 'Ruled by the Harappans again' is impossible, since the Harappan civilisation had ended roughly a thousand years earlier.

**45. (B)** As the land integrated, the mahaajanapadas gave way to the early empires, the next, larger stage of state formation. 'Just being founded' puts their beginning where their end belongs. 'All became republics' is false, since their era was ending, not transforming wholesale. 'Relocated into the Indus valley' invents a migration the chapter does not describe.

**46. (B)** Magadha's strength flowed from its fertile Ganga location, rich harvests, river routes and access to resources such as iron, the very ingredients the chapter ties to powerful states. 'No rivers' is wrong, since rivers were an asset, not a defensive gap. 'Gana/sangha that elected rulers' misclassifies Magadha, a monarchy. 'Far to the south near the Pandyas' misplaces a northern state.

**47. (C)** Shishupalgarh, in the east with its square fortified plan, was the capital associated with Kalinga. Magadha, in Bihar, had capitals such as Rajagriha, not an eastern square-planned site. Gandhaara lay in the far northwest. Avanti lay in central-western India, none of which matches an eastern, square-planned city.

**48. (B)** The developments are interlinked: agricultural surplus underpinned cities and states, the resulting trade called for coins, and each fed back into the others. 'Three different millennia' separates roughly contemporary events. 'Coins first, then farming' reverses the order, since surplus farming preceded and enabled coins. 'Separate continent' is plainly false, since all unfolded in the same land.

**49. (A)** Fertile soil and iron give surplus, but the chapter ties powerful states to connectivity as well; cut off from routes, a region would lose the exchange of goods, coins and ideas. 'Isolation always protects' treats being cut off as an advantage the chapter does not grant. 'Trade played no role' contradicts the chapter's emphasis on connectivity. 'Guaranteed largest empire' overclaims from soil and iron alone.

**50. (D)** Larger states formed as more powerful janapadas, backed by iron arms and surplus wealth, defeated or absorbed weaker ones. 'Harappans returned' is impossible, since they had long since vanished. 'No one wanted land any more' contradicts clans' strong ties to their territory. 'Foreign empires split states into janapadas' reverses the direction, which ran from small units to large.

**51. (A)** Walls, taxation and armies together require an authority able to gather and direct resources, which is exactly what marks an organised state. Harappan seals, weights and drainage point to the earlier First Urbanisation, not these states. The absence of rulers, assemblies or coins would argue against statehood, not for it. Cowrie exchange with no walls, taxes or army lacks every feature that signals an organised state.

**52. (C)** A coin drifting far from its mint is the signature of trade carrying it along routes, while a capital alive after 2,500 years shows the second wave's unbroken continuity, so each find supports its own claim. The first distractor swaps the two matches. 'Harappans founded the Ganga cities' is false, since those cities were not Harappan. 'Barter never gave way to coins' contradicts the rise of coined money the travelled coin reflects.

**53. (D)** These early republics shared decisions among many leading-family heads, broader than one king's will yet short of universal suffrage, so 'early' not 'modern' is right. 'Every adult voted equally' overstates inclusion. 'Rulers inherited by birth' is false, since the assembly chose them, which is what makes them republics. 'Assemblies took no decisions' contradicts their defining feature.

**54. (D)** The chapter stresses that a good rajaa governed with the counsel of the assemblies and ministers, not by himself, so 'ruled entirely alone' is wrong. 'Monarchies did not exist' is false, since most mahaajanapadas were monarchies. 'Assemblies governed directly' describes a republic, not a monarchy. 'Elected and removed each year' contradicts hereditary kingship.

**55. (D)** The chapter's logic begins with iron, which let people clear land and work heavy soil, producing surpluses that fed cities and supported trade needing coins, so cities and states grew. The first distractor puts trade and coins before the iron that made them possible. The second puts cities before the iron and surplus that built them. The third places surplus before the iron that produced it, breaking the causal order.

**56. (C)** Iron became widespread only in the second wave; the Harappans worked copper and bronze, so iron marks the later phase, not a shared feature. 'Harappans used iron, abandoned later' reverses the truth. 'Both relied on iron equally' is false, since the First Urbanisation did not use iron. 'Iron only for coins' is wrong, since coins were silver, not iron, and were a Second-Urbanisation development.

**57. (B)** The Harappan script stays undeciphered, while the Second Urbanisation is reconstructed from both archaeology and readable ancient texts, the pairing the chapter prizes. 'Both fully readable with equal ease' is false, since the Harappan script is not read. 'Harappan script fully understood, second wave wrote nothing' reverses both facts. 'Neither left any evidence' contradicts the seals, ruins, texts and coins the chapter discusses.

**58. (D)** Decision-making resting with an assembly of leading families rather than one hereditary king is the defining mark of a gana or sangha, the early-republic type such as Vajji or Malla. A hereditary monarchy like Magadha is ruled by an inheriting king, not an assembly. A janapada is a smaller, earlier clan territory, but the iron, routes and assembly here describe a developed mahaajanapada. A Harappan city predates iron and coins entirely, so it cannot fit a route-linked, iron-using state.

**59. (C)** Naming states from the far northwest to the east underlines that the mahaajanapadas spread across much of the Subcontinent, not one valley. 'Squeezed into the Indus valley' contradicts the Gandhaara-to-Anga reach. 'Only the Ganga plains' is wrong, since Gandhaara, Anga and Ashmaka lie outside it. 'One single emperor' is false, since these were many separate states, not one empire, which only came later.

**60. (A)** The clearest support is that Second-Urbanisation cities like Varanasi remain alive and busy after roughly 2,500 years, an unbroken thread. 'Harappan cities still inhabited' is false and concerns the First Urbanisation. 'Iron fallen out of use' is false. 'Barter still the main trade' is false, so none of these supports the claim.
