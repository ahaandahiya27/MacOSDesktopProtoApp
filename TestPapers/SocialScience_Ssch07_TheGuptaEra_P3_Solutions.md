## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** The imperial Gupta line runs Chandragupta I, then his son Samudragupta, then Chandragupta II. The list misplaces Chandragupta I, who actually founded the empire's greatness and ruled earliest; he is the father of Samudragupta. Putting Chandragupta II first reverses the whole descent, and the three are distinct individuals (a grandfather, son and grandson), not one person renamed over time. So only moving Chandragupta I to the front fixes it.

**2. (B)** The Prayaga Prashasti, composed by the court poet Harishena, celebrates the wide conquests of Samudragupta, the warrior king. Chandragupta II reigned after him and is remembered above all as a patron of arts and sciences and for defeating the Shakas, not as the subject of that inscription. The inscription names one ruler, not both, and it belongs to the Gupta age, not to the much earlier Maurya Chandragupta. Hence crediting it to Chandragupta II misreads who the eulogy is about.

**3. (A)** Chandragupta Maurya belonged to the Mauryan empire roughly seven centuries before the Guptas; the Iron Pillar and its 'Chandra' inscription fit the Gupta period and Chandragupta II. The name 'Chandragupta' itself contains 'Chandra', so that element is shared, not unique. The Mauryas certainly worked iron and many metals, so the gold claim is false. And Vikramaditya is a Gupta title, not a shared one, so the deciding factor is the gap of centuries between the two dynasties.

**4. (B)** Iron normally rusts readily, so the blanket claim is false. The Delhi pillar endures because the ancient smiths used phosphorus-rich iron that develops a thin passive film, protecting the metal from oxygen and water for over a thousand years. The pillar is genuinely iron, not bronze, and its protection is built into the metal's chemistry, not a yearly coat of paint. So the marvel is the metallurgy, not any idea that iron cannot corrode.

**5. (C)** Faxian, a Chinese Buddhist pilgrim, travelled through India during the Gupta age and saw mostly the prosperous towns and pious institutions; his own account also reveals the harsh exclusion of the chandalas, showing prosperity was uneven. He was a genuine traveller writing of what he witnessed, not a copyist or a later author, and he was an outside pilgrim, not a paid court flatterer. The lesson is that one eyewitness shows a partial view and should be checked against other evidence such as coins and inscriptions.

**6. (C)** A tax-free grant means that land no longer feeds revenue into the royal treasury, and the grant-holder gains rights over the local people and resources; repeated over generations this erodes both income and central control. It cannot raise revenue, since the whole point is exemption from tax. It does not bear on raising armies or on long-distance maritime trade, which continued independently. So the long-run effect is a quiet weakening of the king's direct grip.

**7. (A)** The Guptas held the north and centre under direct rule but elsewhere leaned on land grants and allied or tributary kings, a looser arrangement than the Mauryan model of close direct control everywhere. There was no yearly elected national assembly choosing the king, no use of Roman officials, and the emperor remained very much the supreme authority, projecting it through grand titles. Hence the distinguishing feature is the reliance on grants and subordinate rulers.

**8. (D)** Defeating the Shakas in the west gave the Guptas the prosperous western territories and access to coastal trade toward the Arabian Sea, strengthening the empire economically. The Guptas did not conquer China, and the Mauryas had fallen centuries before the Shakas, so neither is relevant. Zero was a mathematical achievement of Indian scholars such as Aryabhata, not a treasure seized in war. The real reward was strategic and commercial reach to the west.

**9. (D)** Aryabhata's striking idea was that the daily rising and setting of stars is explained by the Earth rotating on its axis, an explanation that puts the motion in the observer rather than in the whole sky. This is not a claim that the Earth is flat, nor a statement about the Sun's yearly path, nor a claim that the stars physically race across the country. The boldness lay in attributing the apparent motion of the heavens to the Earth's spin.

**10. (B)** Zero as a place-holder within a decimal place-value system lets any quantity be written compactly and calculated efficiently, and the method eventually travelled across the world to become standard arithmetic. It did not replace all other numerals; rather it works with them. It was a general tool for all calculation, not a one-off for architecture, and it was preserved and transmitted, not forgotten. Its lasting, worldwide usefulness is what makes it so significant.

**11. (B)** The prashasti is a real and useful source for Samudragupta, and it was indeed written by his court poet Harishena, so both statements are true. But because the author worked for the king, the eulogy is likely to exaggerate, so its claims should be cross-checked rather than accepted as neutral fact. It is not worthless, and Harishena was the king's poet, not a rival. The careful position is to use it while allowing for bias.

**12. (D)** Scholars travelling great distances to study at Nalanda show that it was a renowned centre of higher learning whose reputation reached far beyond India. This does not mean local students were excluded or that study was banned at home; quite the opposite, learning was thriving. Nalanda was an educational institution, not a fortress. Its pull on distant scholars reflects the era's intellectual prestige.

**13. (B)** Coins were a public, widely circulating medium, so the chosen images broadcast the king's might, varied talents and rightful authority to all who handled them. They were money carrying a political message, not mere ornaments, and they recorded royal claims rather than weather. Showing a king as a musician displayed cultivation alongside martial scenes; it did not signal the absence of an army. The purpose was projecting legitimacy and prestige.

**14. (D)** Ajanta's fame rests on its painted cave walls, largely depicting Buddhist subjects, which display the era's mastery of colour and composition. They are paintings, not coins, not astronomical charts, and not legal copper-plate grants. So they belong squarely to the chapter's theme of artistic achievement.

**15. (D)** A 'king of kings' title asserts paramount status above other rulers, fitting an empire that held subordinate and allied kings beneath the emperor. A plain 'raja' would claim only a single kingship. The grand title is not the mark of a headman, nor of a king who surrendered power, nor of a priest. It is precisely a statement of overlordship.

**16. (B)** The continued performance of Kalidasa's works across many centuries shows that the literature of the Gupta court achieved an enduring quality that outlasted the political empire. This longevity argues for the antiquity and excellence of the works, not that they are modern forgeries, and it shows the era prized literature as well as science. Far from nothing surviving, these very texts are the survivors. The point is the lasting legacy.

**17. (B)** Traders leaving their marks on a distant island shows the long reach of Indian seaborne commerce, not political conquest. There is no evidence the Guptas governed Socotra; their capital was Pataliputra, far inland; and the graffiti points to Indians travelling out, not islanders invading India. So the inference is about trade and travel, not empire.

**18. (C)** Brilliant achievements in art and science coexisted with deep social inequality, including the marginalisation of the chandalas, so 'golden' describes culture, not social fairness. There was neither universal equality nor an absence of conflict, and India was in fact well connected by trade. The corrective is to remember the splendour did not reach everyone.

**19. (C)** The Hunas, coming from Central Asia through the northwest, wore down the later Guptas and hastened the empire's break-up. The Pallavas were a southern kingdom that did not conquer the north, no Roman army invaded India, and Chandragupta Maurya belonged to a far earlier dynasty, so his death is irrelevant here. The proximate cause was the Huna onslaught.

**20. (D)** The simultaneous existence of strong southern and north-eastern kingdoms shows the Guptas did not rule all of India; they dominated the north and centre while other powers held their own regions. India was not a single Gupta realm, was not a Chinese province, and the Pallavas and Kamarupa were full kingdoms, not Gupta villages. The picture is one of a major empire alongside regional states.

**21. (A)** A tax-free or tenure grant was meant to last, so it was engraved on copper, a material that endures and is hard to tamper with, preserving the claim across generations. The point was permanence, not portability for travel, and copper was chosen precisely because it could not be easily altered or erased. A copper plate is a record, not spendable coinage. Durability of the legal right is the answer.

**22. (D)** A Gupta princess ruling as regent and managing inter-kingdom relations shows that elite women could exercise genuine authority. This contradicts the idea that women were wholly barred from governance, and Prabhavati was an Indian princess, not a foreigner. The sources credit her with administration and diplomacy, not with leading armies in battle. Her example illustrates women's occasional real political power.

**23. (B)** The Brihat Samhita is a broad, encyclopedic text associated with the scholar Varahamihira, ranging over astronomy and many fields of knowledge. It is not a royal eulogy like the Prayaga Prashasti, not a dramatic play like Kalidasa's, and not a legal land-grant document. So it belongs to the era's scientific and scholarly output.

**24. (A)** Aryabhata's domain was mathematics and astronomy, captured in his treatise the Aryabhatiya; he had nothing to do with forging the Delhi Iron Pillar, which is a feat of metallurgy connected to a Gupta ruler. So the sentence wrongly fuses a scholar with a metalwork monument. He was not an ironsmith at any date, and he was a scientist, not the pillar's poet. The two belong to entirely different achievements of the age.

**25. (C)** An iron column that has resisted rust for over a millennium is direct physical proof of sophisticated indigenous iron technology in the Gupta age. It does not imply imported Roman metal, and it plainly contradicts any notion that Indians worked only gold or could not work iron. The pillar is evidence of high local metallurgical skill.

**26. (C)** As a Buddhist pilgrim, Faxian came chiefly to see holy sites and gather scriptures, and his travel writings incidentally record what he saw of Gupta society. He was not a Gupta court poet (that was Harishena), not a military commander, and not an administrator of any province. His religious journey is what places his account in the chapter.

**27. (C)** The Prayaga Prashasti praises Samudragupta, so that pairing is correct. The Iron Pillar's 'Chandra' is linked to Chandragupta II, not Chandragupta I; the first to raise the Guptas to imperial status was Chandragupta I, not Chandragupta II; and the touring pilgrim was Faxian, a Chinese Buddhist, not the king Kumaragupta. Only the Samudragupta pairing holds.

**28. (D)** Chakravartin expresses the ideal of a paramount, world-encompassing ruler standing above all other kings, an aspiration suited to imperial claims. It is not a priestly, local, or diplomatic role. The term is about supreme overlordship.

**29. (A)** When independent sources of different types converge, each helps confirm the others, so the overall picture becomes more reliable. This is the opposite of trusting one source alone, and it increases rather than removes the value of inscriptions. It also clearly shows the Guptas did keep abundant records. Corroboration across evidence is the key benefit.

**30. (D)** Codifying means setting knowledge out in an ordered, written, systematic way so it can be taught and preserved. It is not destruction, not secrecy, and not specifically translation into Greek. The medical learning of the age was thus given lasting written form.

**31. (D)** Coins survive as concrete, often dateable artefacts that name rulers and display their claims and the realm's prosperity, so they are rich evidence even though the images are deliberate self-promotion to be weighed carefully. They are not message-free, nor are they worthless simply because a king made them, and they carry far more meaning than their metal value. The balanced view is to use them critically.

**32. (D)** Sarnath and Udayagiri are noted for sculpture and religious architecture, marking them as achievements in art. They are not mathematical centres, not seaports, and not mints. Their carved images place them within the era's artistic flowering.

**33. (B)** Marrying into the established Licchavi line lent the rising Guptas legitimacy and allies, a classic political marriage. It was not a tax-collecting device, nor merely a religious ceremony, and it raised the Guptas rather than surrendering their throne. So it is best described as a matrimonial alliance for power.

**34. (A)** The label honours the period's outstanding cultural and scientific accomplishments, which later generations admired. It does not claim universal equality, nor an absence of war, nor a break with past learning; in fact Gupta scholars built on earlier knowledge. The brilliance of its arts and sciences earns the title.

**35. (C)** A pilgrim such as Faxian writes as an outsider recording what he encounters, whereas a prashasti like Harishena's is purpose-built praise of one king and thus shaped by the wish to flatter. The prashasti is therefore the more partial of the two, not the neutral one, and the two were produced by very different authors for very different reasons. Both are usable written sources when read with their purposes in mind.

**36. (A)** Garuda, the mount of Vishnu, links Gupta kingship to Hindu devotional imagery, lending the rulers divine sanction. The Guptas did not adopt Greek gods, were not an exclusively Buddhist dynasty, and chose Garuda deliberately for its religious meaning, not at random. The emblem ties royal power to Hindu tradition.

**37. (C)** That successive outside groups arrived by the northwest marks it as a recurring overland entry point into India. It was clearly not sealed off, since people kept coming through it, and the description concerns movement of peoples, not farmland fertility or maritime ports. The northwest functioned as a historic gateway.

**38. (B)** Visible urban charity does not establish equality across the whole society, and Faxian's own report records the harsh exclusion of the chandalas, undercutting the conclusion. He did mention charity, so the premise stands, and charity does not by itself guarantee equality. Faxian was a pilgrim, not a king. The reasoning over-generalises from one prosperous slice of society.

**39. (C)** Imposing titles and a structured administration both advertised the emperor's paramount status and provided the machinery to run a wide empire. They did not mask a missing army, did not declare the Guptas foreign, and certainly did not remove the need for officials, who staffed that very administration. The aim was authority and organisation.

**40. (A)** Arriving at a close value of pi demonstrates a sophisticated grasp of geometry and computation among Gupta mathematicians. It is unrelated to forecasting invasions or to coin prices in Rome, and it complements rather than replaces the use of zero. The achievement signals genuine mathematical advancement.

**41. (A)** Gupta direct rule centred on the north and centre, while the rest was managed through grants and subordinate or allied kings, and regions like the south (Pallavas) and northeast (Kamarupa) lay outside Gupta control. They governed far more than one city, did have a strong central emperor, and held real territory, so the other claims are false. The empire was substantial but not subcontinent-wide direct rule.

**42. (A)** A cluster of leading writers and scientists thriving under royal favour points to active patronage, kings funding and encouraging talent. The rulers were full sovereigns, not regents, and they fostered rather than suppressed learning, and they were kings at home, not travelling pilgrims. Patronage explains the concentration of genius.

**43. (B)** The statement does muddle two dynasties, so the assertion is sound, but the reason errs by calling Chandragupta II a Maurya when he was a Gupta ruler centuries after the Mauryas. The dynasties are distinct, not identical, and Chandragupta II was a real historical king. Correctly, he was a Gupta, which is the very distinction students must keep straight.

**44. (C)** Engraved land grants prove the administration kept formal written records of who held which land and on what terms, evidence of an organised bureaucracy. This contradicts the idea of an unrecorded oral system, does not show foreign ownership, and shows that documents existed alongside coins rather than being replaced by them. The grants reveal a documented governing order.

**45. (D)** The Vakatakas were a separate ruling house linked to the Guptas by Prabhavati Gupta's marriage, illustrating how marriage built alliances between dynasties. They were not pilgrims, not the Huna invaders, and not a school of scholars. They were a contemporary allied dynasty.

**46. (C)** The Aryabhatiya compactly sets out major mathematical and astronomical findings of its time, marking a high point of scientific writing. It is not a poem, not the Prayaga Prashasti, and not an architectural drawing. Its scientific content is what makes it a landmark.

**47. (B)** The Guptas emerged long after the Mauryan empire, with the Kushana period falling in between, so a substantial gap separates the two. They did not follow the Mauryas at once, did not precede them, and did not rule alongside them. Recognising this gap is what prevents conflating the two Chandraguptas.

**48. (D)** A balanced reading uses both the positive observations and the disturbing ones to build a fuller, fairer picture. Cherry-picking the pleasant parts distorts the past, while discarding the whole source over one detail wastes good evidence, and the presence of mixed reporting actually argues for authenticity, not forgery. Holding the contrasting details together is the historian's task.

**49. (C)** That the place-value system travelled widely and became the basis of arithmetic across the world testifies to the deep, lasting impact of Gupta-age learning. The system was not abandoned at home, its spread came through transmission of knowledge rather than conquest, and it served all kinds of calculation, not just architecture. Its global reach underscores the era's significance.

**50. (D)** A flourishing economy with long-distance trade required dependable money, so the abundant gold coinage served real commerce while also carrying the ruler's image and titles. Trade was thriving, not banned; coins did not displace farming, the economy's base; and they functioned as money, not mere ornaments. Economic need plus royal messaging explains the coinage.

**51. (A)** Two separate capitals in two regions show that independent kingdoms governed different parts of India simultaneously. The Pallavas were not a Gupta sub-office, the two cities were genuinely different places, and the Guptas kept their capital at Pataliputra in the north. The contrast highlights regional political diversity.

**52. (A)** An independent Kamarupa in the northeast demonstrates that Gupta direct rule had limits and did not cover the entire subcontinent. Gupta power did not reach every corner, their base was Pataliputra rather than the Brahmaputra valley, and they were a land empire, not merely trading posts. Kamarupa marks the edge of Gupta reach.

**53. (C)** Placing the Guptas in the 2nd century BCE, right after the Mauryas, clashes with the accepted 4th-to-6th-century CE dating and reflects the classic confusion with the Mauryan age. The other statements all sit comfortably inside the correct span: Chandragupta I early, Samudragupta mid-period, and the Huna pressure near the end. Only the BCE claim is inconsistent.

**54. (C)** The label fits because the period saw rich and continuing achievements across literature, mathematics, astronomy and art. It was not an age devoid of creativity, the flourishing spanned generations rather than one year, and it covered far more than coinage. The breadth and persistence of creative work justify the phrase.

**55. (A)** The archer image signals martial prowess and the 'king of kings' title signals supremacy, so together they present a powerful, paramount ruler. They do not depict humility, renunciation of the throne, or a foreign merchant. The combined message is one of warlike strength and overlordship.

**56. (B)** The era's surviving works include Hindu royal emblems and temple sculpture as well as Buddhist cave paintings at Ajanta, showing patronage across traditions. There is no sign of an empire of only Greek art, no ban on religious art, and art was certainly not limited to mathematical diagrams. Both Hindu and Buddhist artistic strands flourished.

**57. (B)** The point is best proved by setting the era's brilliant achievements beside its real social injustice, such as the treatment of the chandalas, which is exactly what the Gupta record shows. Claiming universal equality would undercut the argument, denying any science or art is plainly false, and the Guptas in fact had elaborate titles and administration. Greatness alongside unfairness is the supporting evidence.

**58. (A)** Pataliputra was an important capital for the Mauryas and again for the Guptas, even though the two empires were separated by centuries; sharing a capital does not make them the same dynasty. It was not the Pallava capital (that was Kanchipuram), and it was an inland northern city, not a southern port. The shared capital links the two ages geographically while their identities remain distinct.

**59. (A)** The Gupta age is remembered for enduring achievements in mathematics, astronomy, literature and art that shaped later India and beyond, even as social inequalities persisted. It did leave a rich legacy rather than nothing, its fame rests on culture rather than on equality it never achieved, and it belonged to the Gupta dynasty, not to Chandragupta Maurya's Mauryan state. Cultural brilliance amid social inequality is the fair summary.

**60. (D)** Toward its end the Gupta empire faced repeated Huna pressure from the northwest, while generations of tax-free land grants had drained royal revenue and dispersed authority, so the late period was one of decline. The empire did not lose its accumulated knowledge, no Roman army occupied the capital, and the southern Pallavas did not swallow the Gupta heartland. The strain came from invasions and the long-term cost of granting away revenue.
