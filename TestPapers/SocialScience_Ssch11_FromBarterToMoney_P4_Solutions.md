## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** Barter is purely goods-for-goods with nothing acting as money; honey-for-figs (i) and rice-for-cloth (iii) qualify. Deal (ii) brings in a copper coin and deal (iv) uses a cowrie shell as a means of payment, so both contain money and are not barter. Pairing by animal produce, by 'a small payment', or merely by the absence of a written rupee price all miss the real test, which is whether any money-thing took part.

**2. (D)** Barter ends the moment something functioning as money enters; cowrie shells used to balance the deal are commodity money, so that change disqualifies it at once. The venue (fair or riverside), an unequal weight between the goods, and a promise to trade again later all leave the deal as a direct goods-for-goods exchange, so none of them ends barter.

**3. (B)** Each person wants something a different person has, so no two of them want exactly what the other offers at the same time; that missing match is the double coincidence of wants. The goods are not perishing here, no dispute over relative worth is described (so it is not the measure-of-value problem), and inflation belongs to a money economy, not to this swap.

**4. (A)** Universal acceptance lets a trader convert goods into money with any buyer and spend that money with any seller, dissolving the need for a perfect match of wants. Money does not alter anyone's tastes, and although it also helps with spoilage and divisibility, the specific obstacle of mismatched wants is cured by wide acceptance, not by preservation or by dividing a good.

**5. (A)** A pure double-coincidence failure is when each party wants something the other does not offer, exactly the sandals-for-eggs mismatch. Souring milk is a perishability (poor store of value) problem, uncertainty over how many goats equal an ox is the lack of a common measure of value, and an unbreakable cart is an indivisibility problem; each of those is a different barter defect.

**6. (A)** Both farmers do want each other's goods, so the wants already coincide; what fails is agreeing the rate of exchange, which is the missing common measure of value. Perishability and portability are not what blocks the deal; the sole sticking point is the disputed worth of wheat in melons.

**7. (C)** With one common unit, each item carries a single price and any pair can be compared at a glance, removing endless good-for-good bargaining. A measure of value does not equalise weights, does not change what people want, and does not preserve perishables; those describe unrelated barter difficulties, not the role of a measuring unit.

**8. (B)** The block is that one whole goat cannot be cut down to the tiny value of a single basket, which is the indivisibility problem. The goat is not losing value here, the relative worth is actually known (ten baskets), and there clearly is demand for grain, so the store-of-value, measure-of-value, and double-coincidence options do not fit.

**9. (B)** Wealth held as fish is lost as the catch rots, so fish fail as a store of value, unlike durable money. Easy divisibility would be a strength, not a weakness; the spoilage problem is not about pricing (measure of value); and a perishable catch is certainly not legal tender, so the other options are wrong.

**10. (B)** The list shows each good missing at least one of durability, divisibility, or portability; good money holds all three together, which is why money beats each commodity. Money is not prized for being edible, heavy, or rare, does not multiply just by being kept, and never dictates what people want, so the other options are false.

**11. (C)** Historically people bartered first and money came afterwards as a remedy for barter's faults, so the very-first-trade claim is wrong. Digital payment is the most recent stage, money did not begin on the same day as barter, and coins cannot predate the people who made them, so each alternative repeats a false sequence.

**12. (B)** The faults of barter came first and are precisely the problems money was designed to solve, so barter's difficulties are the cause and money the response. Money did not precede or cause those faults, barter's faults were real (not absent), and the link is genuinely causal, so the other options misstate the direction or deny the connection.

**13. (D)** Taking money for goods now is the medium of exchange, locking takings away for later is the store of value, and chalking prices for comparison is the common measure of value, matching the order described. The other lists scramble these roles or wrongly insert deferred payment, which would need a future debt being settled, not present here in that order.

**14. (A)** Fixing a money sum to be paid on a future date for goods already supplied is the standard of deferred payment; because nothing is paid now, it is not a medium of exchange. The deal is not money being stored, nor merely a price comparison, and a date being fixed is exactly what makes it deferred payment rather than ruling it out, so the other pairings are wrong.

**15. (A)** A banknote is money, and any deal involving money is a purchase, not barter, which is strictly goods-for-goods. Barter is not limited to particular occupations, does not require three goods, and is not made illegal by the existence of notes, so the other reasons are false.

**16. (B)** A medium of exchange is money actually changing hands to obtain goods or services; merely writing a price for comparison is the measure-of-value function, so it is the exception. Paying a fare with coins, tapping a card for groceries, and scanning a QR code for a snack all move money to complete a purchase, so each is a medium of exchange.

**17. (D)** Lying unused in a box preserves purchasing power, which is the store of value; being handed over for rice is the medium of exchange, so that is the correct order. The reverse mixes the two roles, while deferred payment (a future debt) and measure of value (pricing for comparison) describe neither act of storing nor of buying.

**18. (B)** Money holds value reliably because it neither perishes nor needs tending, and it can be stored and split at will, unlike cattle. Money cannot be eaten, does not multiply merely by being held, and does no farm labour; those are misplaced cattle-style expectations, so each is false.

**19. (B)** Items that are useful goods in their own right yet also act as money are commodity money, exactly what grain, cattle, salt, and cowries were. Fiat money gets value from law and trust rather than usefulness, legal tender is central-bank-issued modern currency, and digital money is electronic; none describes these tangible early goods.

**20. (D)** The stone discs show that what makes something money is shared agreement to accept it, even when the object is enormously heavy. Stone is not a precious metal, the example deliberately contradicts the idea that money is always light, and uncarved minting by a government is not what the stone discs were, so the other options are false.

**21. (C)** People bartered first, then used commodity money like cowries, then struck metal coins, and later printed paper currency, which is the correct order. The other sequences put commodity money or coins or paper before barter, or reverse the whole line, all of which contradict the chapter's history.

**22. (A)** A minted coin's certified fixed weight and stamp let people read both its amount and its genuineness at a glance, removing the slow weighing and the purity-testing that invited cheating. The coin need not contain more valuable metal, is not food, and is struck from metal rather than printed like paper, so the other options are false.

**23. (A)** Stamping marks guaranteed the metal's content and authenticity, helping people accept the coin readily. The marks did not add weight or value, were not merely ornamental, and stamping metal certainly does not turn it into paper money, so the other options are false.

**24. (D)** The crisp alloy coin surviving a year of handling while the soft one wears and bends shows durability, money's ability to last without degrading. The comparison is about wear over time, not about splitting the coin (divisibility), carrying it (portability), or whether people accept it (wide acceptance), so the other options name the wrong property.

**25. (A)** The obverse is the main face bearing the principal design or emblem (the 'heads'), and the reverse is the other face (the 'tails'); here that makes the emblem the obverse and the value the reverse. Swapping the two, calling both faces obverse, or confusing a face with the edge all misuse the terms.

**26. (C)** 'Rupya' means wrought or stamped silver, which matches the fact that India's earliest rupees were silver coins. The rupee did not begin as paper, as a cowrie shell, or as a digital code, so the other meanings and their justifications are all false.

**27. (C)** Paper money began in China, and its real gain over coins was greater portability, since notes carry large sums lightly. Paper did not begin in India, Rome, or Britain in the chapter's account; notes are not made of precious material, can certainly be lost or torn, and get their value from trust and backing, not costly paper.

**28. (C)** A modern note's value rests on public trust and Reserve Bank of India backing, while the idea that the paper or ink is itself worth 500 rupees is the classic trap. The paper holds no gold, colour is only a design and security feature, and paper cannot be melted for precious metal, so the other options invert the correct explanation and the trap.

**29. (B)** Acceptance flows from the Reserve Bank standing behind the note, shown by the Governor's signature and the bank's name, so the first child's evidence is the right one. Colours and patterns are only design and security features worth nothing on their own, attractiveness does not create value, and the cheap paper is not the source of worth, so the other options are false.

**30. (D)** Many different issuers would destroy the uniformity and shared trust that make notes acceptable everywhere, which is why the Reserve Bank of India alone issues India's paper currency. Notes do not become metal or coins, the United Nations and shopkeepers do not issue Indian currency, and many issuers would harm rather than help, so the other options are false.

**31. (B)** A modern banknote is fiat money: it is valued because it is legal tender and trusted, not for its cheap paper. Cowrie shells, salt, and cattle were commodity money, valued as useful goods in their own right, so none of them is fiat money, making the other options wrong.

**32. (D)** Modern coins circulate at face value because they are fiat money, accepted by law and trust regardless of the metal's worth. The face value is not mere decoration, the coin need not contain matching metal, and a coin is money rather than barter, so the other options are false.

**33. (C)** A general rise in prices over time is inflation, and it means the same 20 rupees buys fewer goods than before, so the saver loses purchasing power. Falling prices would be deflation (the opposite), no goods-for-goods swap makes it barter, and money in a tin does not grow by itself, so the other options are wrong.

**34. (A)** Inflation steadily lowers what each rupee can buy, so cash saved at home buys less over time, which is the real concern. Cash does not multiply on its own, does not become illegal, and does not turn into precious metal, so the other options are false.

**35. (A)** Money buying less than before over time is exactly inflation, which reduces purchasing power. The double coincidence of wants is a barter problem, indivisibility concerns splitting a good, and the note remains legal tender, so none of those explains the shrinking shopping bag.

**36. (B)** A bank safeguards deposits and enables electronic payments and transfers, but it does not fix a shop's prices or pick its customers, so the correct list pairs real bank roles with non-roles accurately. Banks do not print currency (only the Reserve Bank does), do not mint coins, and do not barter takings, and they do let depositors withdraw, so each other list misstates both halves.

**37. (D)** A cheque is a written order directing the bank to transfer rupees from one account to a named payee; it moves money between accounts instead of handing over physical notes. It is not a new currency, not a paper coin, and not barter, since rupees in an account are being moved rather than goods swapped, so the other options are wrong.

**38. (C)** A demand draft is prepaid at the bank, so the funds are assured, which is why a stranger trusts it over a cheque that could be dishonoured. It is not a new currency, not barter, and creates no extra rupees; it simply moves guaranteed money, so the other options are false.

**39. (B)** A debit card spends the holder's own deposited money immediately, matching Riya, while a credit card spends the bank's borrowed money to be repaid later, matching Sahil. Swapping the two reverses the definitions, and calling both the same ignores that one spent own money and the other borrowed, so the other options are wrong.

**40. (A)** Banks underpin safe saving and account-to-account electronic transfers, so losing them would hit exactly those tasks. Gardening, walking about, and speaking a language do not depend on banks, so they would be unaffected; the direct link is to handling and moving money.

**41. (D)** UPI shifts rupees that already exist from one account to another electronically, so it is a method of moving money. It creates no new currency, is not barter because money is plainly involved, and mints no coins; the phone merely instructs the transfer, so the other options are false.

**42. (D)** After the transfer A holds 400 and B holds 300, still summing to 700, because UPI merely moves existing rupees and creates no new money. No rupees are created or destroyed, and UPI does not turn money into gold, so the totals of 800, 600, and the gold option are all false.

**43. (B)** Because the same rupees in one account can be spent as cash, by card, or by UPI, UPI plainly moves the existing rupee rather than being a new currency. UPI is not restricted to certain days, shows amounts in rupees, and money received by UPI can be withdrawn as cash, so the other observations are false.

**44. (D)** Cash, cheque, card, and UPI all transfer the same rupees; they differ only in how the money moves, by hand or electronically through accounts. The non-cash methods are not barter, do not use different currencies, and create no new rupees, so the other statements are false.

**45. (A)** The genuine difference is that barter requires a double coincidence of wants while money's wide acceptance frees a trader from needing the other to want their goods. Barter uses no coins (money does), money makes large trades easier not slower, and it is money, not barter, that provides a common price, so the other rows are false.

**46. (B)** The accurate summary traces barter through commodity money, coins, and paper to digital payments and notes that modern money rests on trust and Reserve Bank backing while UPI and cards only move existing rupees. The others revive the traps that money predates barter or is only precious metal, that barter equals money or that paper itself is valuable, or that the evolution order is reversed.

**47. (C)** Money has steadily moved from direct barter toward ever more convenient and trusted means of exchange, ending in digital payments. The other options reverse this progress, suggesting a return to barter, coins, or shells, which contradicts the actual direction of change.

**48. (D)** Barter is defined by goods meeting goods with no money at all, which is the only decisive fact here. The traders being friends, the items sharing a use, and the venue being a school are all irrelevant to whether money was involved, so none of them is the reason.

**49. (C)** It is true that money lets the potter sell to and buy from anyone, and true that this works because money's wide acceptance removes the need for a matching of wants, so R explains A. Both statements are true and genuinely linked, so the options denying either or severing the connection are wrong.

**50. (C)** A common measure does let every good carry one price, and the reason it helps is precisely that without it traders must negotiate a fresh ratio for each pair, so R explains A. Both statements are true and connected, which rules out the options rejecting either or breaking the link.

**51. (A)** It is true that a modern coin circulates at a face value above its metal worth, and true that this is because it is fiat money backed by law and trust, with the reason directly explaining the assertion. Both statements are true and linked, so the options denying either or breaking the connection are wrong.

**52. (C)** Failing to find a matching buyer is the double-coincidence problem, disagreeing on how many pots equal a blanket is the lack of a common measure of value, and one large pot being unable to pay for a tiny needle is indivisibility, exactly the order listed. The other options pair these complaints with the wrong barter defects.

**53. (C)** Barter does still occur in some places even today, so P is right. A note is valued by trust, not gold weight; UPI is a payment method, not a new currency; and money came after barter, so Q, R, and S each repeat a classic misconception and are wrong.

**54. (A)** Studying coins to uncover history is numismatics, exactly what the scholar is doing. Minting is making coins, bartering is goods-for-goods swapping, and inflation control concerns managing rising prices; none of those describes examining old coins for historical evidence.

**55. (D)** Money's divisibility into small units cures the indivisibility problem, where a large good like an ox cannot be split for a tiny purchase, so that pairing is correct. Durability does not settle disputes over relative worth (that needs a common measure), portability does not fix mismatched wants (that needs wide acceptance), and wide acceptance does not stop goods spoiling (that needs durability), so each of the other pairs links the wrong cure to the wrong problem.

**56. (C)** Both errors must be corrected: barter did have real problems such as the double coincidence of wants, and a note's worth comes from trust and Reserve Bank backing rather than precious paper. The other options keep one or both errors, agreeing barter had no problems or that the paper is costly or golden, so each is false.

**57. (C)** Legal tender must by law be accepted to settle payments inside the country, so a 10-rupee coin cannot lawfully be refused for a small bill. It is the home country's money rather than foreign-only, its legal-tender status has nothing to do with metal content, and it cannot simply be turned away on a whim, so the other options are false.

**58. (B)** Wants match and the rate is agreed, so the only block is that a whole camel cannot be split to the value of one small water-skin, which is indivisibility, cured by money being divisible into small units. The wants already coincide, the rate is already agreed (so not the measure-of-value problem), and nothing is spoiling (so not store of value), making the other options wrong.

**59. (D)** Trade began with direct goods-for-goods barter, then settled with coins and notes, and most recently with electronic transfers like UPI, which is the correct arrow. The other arrows put digital first, or place barter after coins, or put electronic transfers before notes, all of which reverse the real history.

**60. (D)** A cheque tells the bank to transfer rupees between accounts, so the buyer avoids carrying and risking heavy cash, which is its real advantage here. It creates no extra rupees, does not change the value or currency of the money, and is not barter, since rupees in accounts are being moved rather than goods swapped, so the other options are false.
