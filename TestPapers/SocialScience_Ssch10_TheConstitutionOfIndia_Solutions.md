## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** A constitution is the supreme rulebook setting up government, listing rights and duties, and stating a country's goals. It is not a budget, a songbook, or a foreign treaty.

**2. (C)** The Constitution is the supreme 'rulebook' of the nation. It is not a storybook, newspaper, or family tree.

**3. (D)** A constitution establishes the three organs and their checks and balances (plus rights, duties and goals). It does not decide sports, jobs, or song lyrics.

**4. (C)** A constitution sets out the nation's long-term goals and values. Weather, hotels and scores are unrelated.

**5. (A)** A constitution provides agreed basic rules defining powers, rights and limits for government and citizens. It does not end lawmaking, enlarge territory, or abolish government.

**6. (A)** Officials pledge to uphold the Constitution, showing it is the supreme authority. They do not pledge to a foreign ruler, their family, or to ignore courts.

**7. (D)** The pledge shows the Constitution is supreme over everyone, even the powerful. It is not ignorable, citizens-only, or limited to one day.

**8. (D)** The Constituent Assembly, formed in 1946, wrote India's Constitution. It was not written by the British Parliament, the UN, or the Supreme Court.

**9. (B)** The Constituent Assembly was formed in 1946. 1947 is independence, 1950 is when the Constitution took effect, and 1976 is when Fundamental Duties were added.

**10. (D)** Membership fell from 389 to about 299 after Partition. 15 was the number of women members, not the total; 100 and 545 are unrelated.

**11. (A)** The Assembly included 15 women. It was not all-male; 299 was the post-Partition total and 150 is invented.

**12. (D)** Dr. Rajendra Prasad chaired the Constituent Assembly. Dr. Ambedkar headed the Drafting Committee; Gandhi and Nehru held other roles.

**13. (B)** Dr. B.R. Ambedkar headed the Drafting Committee. Dr. Rajendra Prasad chaired the Assembly itself; the others held different roles.

**14. (A)** Prasad chaired the Constituent Assembly; Ambedkar headed the Drafting Committee that wrote the text. The other options swap or deny their roles.

**15. (D)** The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949. 15 August 1947 is independence, 26 January 1950 is when it came into effect, and 1946 is when the Assembly formed.

**16. (C)** It came into effect on 26 January 1950, now Republic Day. The 1949 date is adoption, 1947 is independence, and 1 January is unrelated.

**17. (A)** Republic Day marks the Constitution taking effect in 1950. Independence was 15 August 1947, the Assembly formed in 1946, and Duties were added in 1976.

**18. (B)** Independence Day is 15 August 1947; Republic Day (26 January 1950) marks the Constitution's coming into force. They are different dates, and independence was 1947, not 1950.

**19. (C)** The order is 1946 (Assembly), 1947 (Independence), 1949 (adopted), 1950 (effect). The other options scramble these dates.

**20. (B)** The three influences were the freedom movement, India's heritage, and learnings from other countries. It was not one king's rules, the British Parliament alone, or a UN vote.

**21. (D)** The freedom struggle infused the Constitution with hard-won values such as liberty and equality. It did not copy the monarchy, reject Indian traditions, or ban elections.

**22. (B)** The Assembly studied many constitutions and selected what fit India, blending them with the freedom movement's values and India's heritage. It neither copied one country, invented everything alone, nor refused outside ideas.

**23. (A)** The parliamentary system was drawn from Britain. Directive Principles came from Ireland and the Fundamental Rights / judicial review idea from the USA.

**24. (D)** Fundamental Rights and judicial review drew on the US example. Britain influenced the parliamentary system and Ireland the Directive Principles.

**25. (C)** The Directive Principles were inspired partly by Ireland. Britain shaped the parliamentary system and the USA the Fundamental Rights idea.

**26. (A)** The three organs are the legislature, executive and judiciary. The armed forces, those leaders, and tiers of government are not the three organs.

**27. (A)** Checks and balances let each organ limit the others, preventing any from dominating. They are not merged, isolated, or abolished.

**28. (B)** Separation with checks guards against the misuse of power and protects citizens. It is not slowness for its own sake, judicial control of elections, or removing laws.

**29. (D)** Fundamental Rights are court-enforceable basic rights of citizens. Non-enforceable goals are Directive Principles, citizen responsibilities are Fundamental Duties, and they are not ministers' powers.

**30. (B)** Fundamental Rights are enforceable - a citizen can approach the courts if they are violated. They are not beyond all process, day-limited, or tradeable.

**31. (D)** Enforcing a Fundamental Right means using the courts when a right is violated. Planting trees is a duty; road-building and tax debates are ordinary government work.

**32. (D)** Fundamental Rights are court-enforceable; Directive Principles are non-enforceable guidelines. The other options reverse this or confuse them with the Duties added in 1976.

**33. (C)** Fundamental Duties are citizens' responsibilities, like respecting the flag and protecting the environment. They are not enforceable rights, presidential powers, or government-only goals.

**34. (A)** Protecting the environment is a Fundamental Duty. It is not a court-enforceable right, a court-binding principle, or an army power.

**35. (C)** Fundamental Duties were added by amendment in 1976. 1950 is when the Constitution took effect, 1947 is independence, and 1992 brought Panchayati Raj.

**36. (A)** Directive Principles are non-enforceable goals guiding government policy. Enforceable rights are Fundamental Rights, citizen responsibilities are Duties, and they are not police powers.

**37. (A)** Directive Principles steer the government's policymaking even though courts cannot enforce them. They are not purposeless, ignorable, or superior to Fundamental Rights.

**38. (C)** Fundamental Rights can be enforced in court; Directive Principles are guiding goals that cannot. The other options confuse or reverse this.

**39. (C)** A 'living document' can be amended as needs change. It is not unchangeable, named for its authors, or a daily reading.

**40. (A)** An amendment is a change to the Constitution, carefully debated in Parliament. It is not an election, a treaty, or a court verdict.

**41. (D)** The Constitution is amendable, with changes like Fundamental Duties (1976) and Panchayati Raj (1992). It is not rewritten yearly, king-changed, or never changed.

**42. (C)** Rigorous debate means the Constitution can change but only carefully, not easily or carelessly. It is neither forbidden, secret one-person work, nor automatic.

**43. (C)** The 1992 Panchayati Raj amendment shows the Constitution adapting to new needs. It is not unresponsive, foreign-copied, or rewritten.

**44. (B)** The Preamble is the opening statement of guiding values. It is not a final chapter, a full law-list, or a map.

**45. (D)** The Preamble opens with 'We, the People of India'. The other phrases would imply a king, empire or government as the source, which the Preamble rejects.

**46. (D)** 'We, the People' shows the Constitution's authority flows from the people themselves, not a king or foreign ruler.

**47. (B)** Opening with 'We, the People' marks the Constitution as self-given by India's people, not a British gift, a UN order, or a court decree.

**48. (A)** Sovereign means India decides its own affairs with no external power dictating to it. It is not foreign-controlled, government-less, or UN-ruled.

**49. (C)** Secular means freedom of religion, no state religion, and equal respect for all faiths. It is not anti-religion, religion-banning, or single-religion.

**50. (D)** Secular means equal respect for all religions and freedom to practise any, not state atheism, a single allowed religion, or illegality.

**51. (C)** Democratic means the people govern through free and fair elections. King-rule, religious rule and wealthy-few rule are other forms.

**52. (A)** Republic means the head of state is elected, not a hereditary monarch. Religious or foreign heads describe other systems.

**53. (B)** Socialist (from Latin socius, 'companion') aims to reduce inequality and share wealth and opportunity fairly. It is not about enriching one family, abolishing government, or banning trade.

**54. (B)** Justice means fair treatment - social, economic and political - so no one is wronged. It is not blanket punishment, rich-only freedom, or might-makes-right.

**55. (B)** Liberty (Latin liber, 'free') is the freedom to think, speak, believe and worship. It is not lawlessness, theft, or sole rule.

**56. (A)** Equality means equal status and opportunity with no discrimination. It is broader than identical wages or voting alone, and certainly not wealth-limited.

**57. (C)** Fraternity (Latin frater, 'brother') is brotherhood and unity, with respect for each person's dignity. It is not rivalry, family-only loyalty, or foreign obedience.

**58. (B)** The first five words describe the kind of state India is, and the last four name the goals it secures for citizens. They are not about one person, the three organs, or neighbours.

**59. (B)** The key words together envision a sovereign, democratic republic of free and equal citizens in unity and justice. They reject monarchy, a state religion, and foreign control.

**60. (C)** The Constitution is a living, amendable document authored by 'We, the People of India', not fixed forever or foreign-given. The other options are wrong on both counts.
