1. In 2019 a country held an election and a new party formed the government; in 2024 yet another party won. Through both changes, the same people, the same borders, and the same power to make its own laws stayed in place. Which conclusion is best supported?

Worked Solutions

1. (B) Of a state's four parts (population, territory, government, sovereignty), only the government changes at elections; the other three persisted here, so the state endured while the government changed. Saying the state changed but the government stayed reverses the two. Saying both were replaced ignores that the people, land and sovereignty continued. Saying nothing can ever change is false - governments routinely change at elections.

2. (D) The four parts of a state are population, territory, government and sovereignty. An independent judiciary is an organ within the government, not one of the four parts of the state itself, so it is the imposter. Population, territory and sovereignty are three of the genuine four; the missing fourth is the government as a whole, of which the judiciary is only one organ.

3. (B) The analogy maps the lasting school to the state and the changing teachers to the government. Its warning is precisely against thinking the state dies when a government leaves - the wrong conclusion. The other three are exactly what the analogy DOES support: governments change while the state continues, the state outlasts any one government, and successive governments run the same state.

4. (D) A state is a political organisation governing a defined territory and population, possessing sovereignty - it has four parts and endures across elections. Riya reduces it to mere buildings; Sam confuses it with the current government (one changing part). Both errors mistake a part or a place for the whole enduring state, so neither is right and 'both right' compounds both mistakes.

5. (D) Sovereignty is a state's full power to make and enforce its own laws without outside control. A nation that needs a neighbour's approval for its laws lacks exactly this. It already has population (settled people) and territory (marked borders), and it does have a government making laws - the defect is that those laws are not free of outside control, i.e. sovereignty is missing.

6. (A) Making, enforcing and interpreting laws is the government's job, carried out by the legislature, executive and judiciary respectively. The population votes but does not itself write and judge laws; the territory is land, not an actor; sovereignty is the power that backs the laws, not an organ that drafts and judges them. Only the first option correctly assigns the work to the government's organs.

7. (C) When a party loses, a new government simply takes office; existing laws remain in force until changed, and sovereignty belongs to the enduring state, not to any party. So none of the three is lost. Claiming all three vanish confuses the government with the whole state; the partial-loss options wrongly tie sovereignty or the body of laws to one party's term.

8. (D) The state comprises population, territory, government and sovereignty and persists over time; the government is just one of those four parts and is the part that changes at elections. The first distractor swaps state and government. The second wrongly says both change identically - the state endures. The third inverts the whole-part relationship, making the larger state a piece of the smaller government.

9. (D) The large circle holding four parts is the state (population, territory, government, sovereignty). The inner box whose sub-parts make, enforce and interpret laws is the government, with its legislature, executive and judiciary. The distractors invert the whole-part relation or mislabel the circle and box as territory, sovereignty or population, none of which split into three law-handling organs.

10. (B) Sovereignty is the full power to make and enforce one's own laws without outside control. P keeps the final say even while cooperating, so it stays sovereign; Q must obey a foreign capital, so an outside power controls its laws and its sovereignty is incomplete. Voluntary coordination where the state keeps the final word does not end sovereignty, so 'both lack it' is wrong; and being controlled from abroad (Q) is the opposite of sovereignty, so the last option is reversed.

11. (B) Sovereignty is the power to make and enforce one's own laws free of outside control; a parliament passing and courts enforcing a law no foreign power can overrule is exactly that power in action. A minister ignoring the law is a breach of the rule of law, not sovereignty. Voting expresses democracy. Marking a border concerns territory, another part of the state - not the exercise of sovereignty over law-making.

12. (A) The police and the army are part of the state's machinery, directed by the elected government; they enforce, but they do not make or interpret laws and are not the government. Equating force with government is the misconception the chapter rejects. Calling them merely 'population with no role' ignores their enforcement function, and calling them foreign is absurd - they belong to the state.

13. (C) Elections change the political executive (ministers), while permanent state machinery - the bureaucracy, police and armed forces - continues so the state runs smoothly. So a real change (the government) occurred even though the permanent machinery stayed. Replacing officers with party supporters would politicise neutral machinery; mass dismissals do not happen; and continuity of the police does not make them the government.

14. (C) The elected government is chosen by voters for a fixed term and changes at elections; the armed forces are permanent state machinery that endures across governments - this is the sharp difference. They are not elected, they do not make laws (that is the legislature's work), and they do not turn over with each new party - that continuity is precisely what distinguishes them.

15. (A) Democracy concerns WHO rules - the people, through free and fair elections. Republic concerns the HEAD OF STATE being elected rather than inheriting the position. The first option keeps these distinct and correct. The second reverses both meanings, the third wrongly treats them as identical, and the fourth attaches them to the army and territory, which have nothing to do with these terms.

16. (B) A republic requires an ELECTED (non-hereditary) head of state. The UK, Sweden and Canada elect their parliaments (democratic) but keep hereditary monarchs as heads of state, so they are democracies but not republics - disproving the claim. A parliament does not by itself make a republic; a hereditary monarch is the very opposite of a republic; and these countries clearly are democracies, so 'neither' is wrong.

17. (D) Free elections of lawmakers make it a democracy (the people rule). A hereditary king as head of state means it is NOT a republic, which requires an elected head of state. So it is a democracy but not a republic. 'Republic but not democracy' reverses the facts; 'both' ignores the hereditary king; 'neither' ignores the free elections that make it democratic.

18. (C) Electing the head of state (no hereditary monarch) makes it a republic. But if only one party may stand and people cannot freely choose, it lacks the free and fair elections that define democracy, so it is a republic but not a true democracy. 'Democracy but not republic' reverses this; 'both' ignores the lack of real choice; and it does have an elected head, so 'neither' is wrong.

19. (B) A republic requires an elected, non-hereditary head of state, so a country with a hereditary monarch can NEVER be a republic - 'republic = yes' is impossible for it. It can, however, be a democracy if it elects its lawmakers (like the UK), or fail to be one if elections are not free; and 'republic = no' is exactly the correct classification for a monarchy. Hence the impossible case is republic = yes.

20. (A) In a democracy the majority cannot do whatever it wants: constitutional rights and the rule of law shield minorities from the 'dominance of the majority,' which is why the court could strike the rule down. The minority does not rule over the majority; majority votes are valid for many things but not to violate rights; and the idea that only the majority has rights is exactly the misconception being corrected.

21. (A) The chapter stresses that the majority cannot do anything it likes; constitutional rights and the rule of law bind even a majority. So 51% cannot trample protected rights - that is the strongest objection. Saying 51% can pass anything endorses the very error; saying minorities must decide everything is the opposite extreme; and saying democracies have no limiting laws contradicts the rule of law.

22. (B) Protecting minorities demonstrates that the rule of law - everyone, including a majority, must follow the law - is stronger than the bare wishes of the majority. The other options invert this: that majorities override the law, that minorities have no protection, or that the Constitution can be ignored by the larger group - each contradicts the rule of law the chapter upholds.

23. (A) 'Matsya nyaya,' the law of the fishes, describes a lawless world where the powerful prey on the weak; we need a government and the rule of law to keep order and prevent this. The other options misread the phrase: it does not mean automatic equality, it is not literally about fish legislating, and it is the strong who dominate the weak, not the reverse.

24. (C) Matsya nyaya is the lawless state where the strong prey on the weak with no accountability - exactly the collapse of police and courts letting powerful gangs dominate. The other three are the OPPOSITE: a court punishing the powerful, peaceful voting, and a regulator fining a big company all show the rule of law working to restrain the strong.

25. (A) Matsya nyaya lets the strong dominate the weak; the rule of law is its opposite because it subjects everyone - including the powerful and the government - to the law, shielding the weak. Letting the powerful ignore the law would BE matsya nyaya; applying law only to the weak contradicts equal application; and the rule of law needs a government to enforce it, not its abolition.

26. (C) The rule of law holds that no one is above the law - the government and its ministers included. A minister claiming exemption contradicts this core idea. The distractors each restate a version of the very error: exempting officials, placing the government above the law, or applying law only to certain groups - all of which the rule of law rejects.

27. (D) Lawlessness slides toward matsya nyaya, where the powerful dominate the weak - which is exactly why a government and the rule of law are needed to keep order. The distractors imagine that equality, a Constitution, or the rule of law would appear by themselves without any enforcer, contradicting the chapter's point that order must be actively maintained.

28. (C) Passing a bill into law is the legislature's job; carrying it out is the executive's; deciding whether it was broken is the judiciary's. So the order is legislature, executive, judiciary. The distractors scramble this mapping - for example placing enforcement before law-making, or judging before law-making - none of which matches what each organ actually does.

29. (A) 'Legislature' = lex ('law') + a root meaning to bring or propose, matching exactly its defining task of making and proposing laws. 'To carry out' is the root of executive (exsequi); 'judge' (judex) is the root behind judiciary; the bureaucracy is the permanent executive defined by service, not by an etymology about proposing law. Only the legislature fits.

30. (B) Amending and voting a bill into law is law-making, the legislature's defining function. It is not executive enforcement (no carrying-out of an existing law), not judicial interpretation (no dispute being decided), and not bureaucratic administration (elected representatives, not civil servants, are passing the bill). So it is legislative work.

31. (C) The executive has two parts: the political executive (ministers, who set direction and change with elections) and the permanent executive (the bureaucracy of civil servants, who actually run day-to-day administration across governments). So the daily running is done by the bureaucracy, not ministers alone. Courts interpret law and the legislature makes it - neither runs daily administration.

32. (D) Ministers form the political executive - elected, direction-setting, changing with elections. Civil servants form the permanent executive - exam-selected and continuing across governments to give stable administration. The first distractor swaps the two; the second wrongly makes the bureaucracy elected and temporary; the third wrongly makes ministers permanent exam-selected officials. Only the correct option keeps each in its right role.

33. (D) The permanent executive stays on across governments precisely to provide stable, continuous administration regardless of which party is in power. Civil servants implement, but do not make, laws; they are not the head of state; and their staying on shows the bureaucracy does NOT change completely after each election - the very opposite of the last distractor.

34. (C) The bureaucracy is the permanent executive: non-elected civil servants, usually chosen by competitive exams, who carry out administration across governments. Elected ministers are the political executive; judges form the judiciary; voters are the electorate. Each distractor names a different actor in the system, none of which is the bureaucracy.

35. (C) Carrying out a law by instructing local offices is executive work, and the routine implementation is done by the permanent executive (the bureaucracy). It is not legislation - the law already exists; it is not adjudication - no dispute is being decided; and while ministers set direction, the day-to-day implementation is the bureaucracy's, so 'no role for the bureaucracy' is wrong.

36. (B) Judicial review is the judiciary's power to examine laws and government actions and strike down those that breach the Constitution - exactly the watchdog role. Courts do not write and pass laws (that is the legislature), do not order elections, and are not bound to accept an unconstitutional law - so the other options either give courts legislative power or strip them of their checking role.

37. (A) Independence lets the judiciary check even the government fairly, upholding the rule of law - everyone, including the government, must follow the law. The distractors describe the opposite of independence: courts controlled by the ruling party, obeying ministers, or imposing no checks - each of which would let power escape the law rather than be held to it.

38. (B) The legislature makes laws, the executive enforces them, and the judiciary interprets them and settles disputes - the only fully correct set. Each distractor misassigns at least one verb, such as making the legislature enforce, the executive interpret, or the judiciary make laws, none of which matches the organs' actual functions.

39. (D) Judicial review is the power to EXAMINE and STRIKE DOWN unconstitutional laws and actions - not to write and pass new laws, which is the legislature's job, so that falls outside it. Examining a law, striking down an unconstitutional action, and checking a law against constitutional rights are all squarely within judicial review.

40. (C) Keeping people safe is the protector role, supplying services is the provider role, and setting and enforcing fair rules is the regulator role - the government's triple role. The distractors list the three organs, the three tiers, and the four parts of a state respectively - none of which describes 'keeps safe / supplies services / sets rules.'

41. (D) Defending the country and keeping citizens safe is the protector role. Building hospitals is the provider role (supplying services); setting factory safety standards is the regulator role (making and enforcing rules); and announcing election results is part of running elections, not the protector role. Only border defence is purely protection.

42. (A) Supplying services such as schools, hospitals and roads is the provider role. The regulator role is about setting and enforcing fair rules, not building services, so the critic is wrong. Calling it protection confuses 'providing services' with 'defending the country and keeping people safe,' and building is certainly not law-making by the legislature.

43. (D) Both inspecting factories for safety and fixing fair market rules are about setting and enforcing rules - the regulator role. The other pairs mix protector (border, coastline defence) and provider (hospital, roads, textbooks) actions; none of those pairs is purely regulation, so only the first option has two genuine regulator actions.

44. (C) Guarding the coast is protection, running hospitals is provision, and inspecting shops for hygiene rules is regulation - all three roles at once. Each distractor shows only ONE role (defence only, services only, or rule-setting only), so none demonstrates all three the way the correct option does.

45. (D) RTI lets citizens request information about government actions, increasing transparency and enabling them to hold the government accountable. It has nothing to do with extra voting, appointing judges (a judicial matter), or ignoring laws (which would break the rule of law). Only transparency and accountability is the correct, direct effect.

46. (B) Accountability tools include RTI, a free media exposing failures, civil-society pressure, grievance redressal, and voting a poor government out - all in the first option. The others list the OPPOSITE: refusing to vote, one-minister rule and scrapping the Constitution remove accountability; appointing oneself judge or dismissing soldiers is unlawful; ignoring elections and obeying a gang invites matsya nyaya.

47. (B) Removing a poor government by vote is a core way citizens hold it accountable for its record. It is not sovereignty over a foreign state (sovereignty is the state's power over its own laws), not the government's regulator role (citizens are acting, not the government), and not judicial review (a power of the courts, not of voters).

48. (A) By exposing failures and informing citizens, a free media helps hold the government accountable - exactly what the investigation did. The media does not make laws (the legislature's job), does not judge cases (the judiciary's), and does not run administration (the bureaucracy's); its accountability role is the correct one.

49. (A) Grievance redressal lets citizens complain about and seek fixes for problems with government services, which holds the administration accountable - just as the corrected ration card shows. It is not foreign voting, not appointing ministers (an electoral/political matter), and not striking down laws (that is judicial review by the courts).

50. (B) The three TIERS are union, state and local - levels at which government operates. The distractors mislabel the three ORGANS, the three ROLES, and three PARTS of a state as 'tiers,' a classic mix-up. Only union/state/local are the tiers; the others answer entirely different questions about how government is structured.

51. (B) Decentralisation spreads out power so local matters are settled locally and decisions are made at the appropriate level. The distractors describe its opposite or an unrelated harm: concentrating all power centrally, abolishing local bodies, or surrendering power to a foreign country - none of which is decentralisation.

52. (B) Letting a local body decide a local road repair is decentralisation in action - the matter is handled at the level closest to it. It is the opposite of centralising power, it relies on (not abolishes) the local tier, and it has nothing to do with foreign control, which the other options wrongly describe.

53. (D) Federalism shares power between a central government and the state/local governments - a division of authority rather than concentration in one place. The distractors describe non-federal systems: all power held centrally, no centre at all, or rule by a king - none of which matches the shared-power meaning of federalism.

54. (C) Federalism shares power between the centre and state/local governments; decentralisation lets local matters be decided locally. The correct option shows both - shared tiers plus local decision-making. The distractors are non-federal or non-decentralised: total central control, no centre at all, or a monarch approving everything - none combines shared power with local decision-making.

55. (D) All three claims are misconceptions. The government is just one of the state's four parts. The majority cannot do anything it wants because rights and the rule of law shield minorities. Not every democracy is a republic - the UK is a democracy with a hereditary monarch. So zero are correct, and only the full three-part correction fixes them; the other options leave one or more errors standing.

56. (A) Free elections make it a democracy; an elected head of state makes it a republic; courts striking down unconstitutional laws is judicial review; protecting minorities shows the rule of law over the majority - so all four labels fit. The distractors each contradict the facts: it is not a monarchy, it does have judicial review, the majority is limited by rights, and it clearly is a democracy.

57. (A) Parliament passing the law is legislative; the bureaucracy enforcing it is executive; the court striking it down for breaching a right is judicial review by the judiciary. The correct option maps these accurately. Each distractor scrambles the organs - for instance making courts legislate or Parliament strike down laws - none of which matches who actually does each step.

58. (C) An election replaces the political executive - the ministers - while the territory, sovereignty and the permanent bureaucracy all continue. So the ministers are the part that turns over. The distractors name things that endure across elections: territory and sovereignty are stable parts of the state, and the bureaucracy is the permanent executive that stays on to give continuity.

59. (C) An independent judiciary can check even the elected government, and the rule of law binds the government like everyone else - together these refute the idea that courts must do the majority's bidding. The distractors pair concepts that are real but irrelevant here (tiers, roles, parts of a state) and even draw wrong conclusions, such as courts being made to obey the centre or ministers.

60. (A) The correct synthesis: the enduring state has four parts; the government is one part, working through three organs and three roles, and it changes at elections, with everyone bound by the rule of law. The distractors embed familiar errors - swapping state and government, conflating the two under unlimited majority rule, or confusing the tiers with the organs - each contradicting the chapter.
