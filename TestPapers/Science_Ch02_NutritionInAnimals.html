Worked Solutions

1. (C) The five steps run: ingestion (taking food in) → digestion (breaking it down) → absorption (passing nutrients to the blood) → assimilation (cells using them) → egestion (removing undigested waste). Only option C lists this exact order. The others scramble it — e.g. putting absorption before digestion is impossible, because nothing can be absorbed until it is first broken small enough to cross the gut wall.

2. (B) Swallowed food is large and locked in complex molecules; cells can use it only after it is *digested* into simple molecules and then *absorbed* into the blood. So the missing steps are digestion and absorption. "Ingested only" is what already happened; egestion and assimilation come later, and assimilation can never precede digestion.

3. (D) Absorption is precisely the step in which the simple nutrients pass from the small intestine into the bloodstream. Digestion only breaks food down (no blood transfer yet); assimilation is cells *using* what is already in the blood; egestion removes waste.

4. (A) Assimilation is the use of absorbed nutrients by body cells for energy, growth, and repair. Option B describes digestion, C describes ingestion, and D describes egestion — classic step-mix-up distractors.

5. (B) Egestion removes *undigested food* that never entered the cells. Excretion is different: it removes wastes produced *inside the body's cells* (for example during the release of energy). So they are not the same process under a different name (A), nor merely water (C) or bacteria (D).

6. (B) Starch is a giant molecule; the gut wall only lets through small, simple sugar molecules. Until enzymes (starting with salivary amylase) cut the starch into simple sugars, it is too large to be absorbed. It is not poisonous (A), the blood does reach the gut wall via capillaries (C), and starch is mostly absorbed in the small intestine, not the large (D).

7. (D) A butterfly sucking nectar through a proboscis and a snake swallowing prey whole are both *taking food in* — they differ in their mode of ingestion. Absorption, assimilation, and egestion are later, internal steps that are broadly similar.

8. (D) Amoeba extends temporary cytoplasmic projections called pseudopodia ("false feet") to surround food. Villi line the human intestine; cilia and flagella are hair-like motion structures, not feeding arms in Amoeba.

9. (C) The housefly releases digestive juice onto solid food *outside* its body and then sucks up the liquefied result — so some digestion begins outside the body before ingestion is complete. Digestion clearly does occur (so not A), and absorption/egestion still come in their normal later order.

10. (B) Feeding tools match the food: a mosquito pierces and sucks fluids, a cow grinds tough grass with flat molars. So the kind of food an animal eats shapes its feeding tools. Size (A) is irrelevant, the "no exceptions" rule (C) is false (e.g. a butterfly is an insect that sucks while a grasshopper chews), and cows obviously feed (D).

11. (A) The flat, chisel-edged front teeth, the incisors, do the biting and cutting. Canines tear, premolars and molars grind — a different job for each shape.

12. (C) The long, pointed teeth beside the incisors are the canines, well shaped for tearing flesh — prominent in carnivores like dogs. Incisors cut, premolars/molars grind.

13. (C) Enamel is the hard outer covering of the crown and is the hardest substance in the human body. Dentine lies beneath it, pulp is the soft inner core, and bone is not part of the visible tooth surface.

14. (D) The pulp cavity at the centre of the tooth holds the nerves and blood vessels — which is why a deep cavity hurts. The crown is the visible top, enamel is the outer coat, and the root anchors the tooth in the jaw.

15. (C) Decay starts when mouth bacteria turn left-over sugar into acid that attacks enamel. Brushing removes the sugar before the bacteria can make that acid — that is the real protective action. Brushing does not rebuild lost enamel (B), whiteness alone (A) is cosmetic, and it should not harm taste buds (D).

16. (D) Grinding teeth = premolars + molars = 8 + 12 = 20. (12 counts only molars; 8 counts only premolars; 32 is all the teeth, not just the grinders — three tempting miscounts.)

17. (A) The enzyme in saliva (salivary amylase) begins the digestion of starch. Proteins are first attacked in the stomach by pepsin; fats are handled later with bile and pancreatic enzymes; water needs no digestion.

18. (B) Chewing plain bread tastes sweeter over time because salivary amylase breaks the starch into simpler sugars, which taste sweet. The bread does not absorb sugar from the tongue (A), saliva is not hot or acidic enough to caramelise (C), and bacteria do not add sugar that fast (D).

19. (D) Besides starting starch digestion, saliva moistens the food so it can be swallowed smoothly and rolled into a soft lump. Absorption (A) happens in the intestine, HCl (C) is made in the stomach, and waste storage (B) is the large intestine's job.

20. (A) The wave-like muscular squeezing that pushes food down the food pipe is peristalsis. Rumination is cud-chewing in cows, emulsification is bile's action on fat, and absorption is nutrient uptake.

21. (B) Swallowing works lying down because the food-pipe muscles contract in a coordinated sequence that actively pushes the lump forward — it does not rely on gravity (so A is false, and the pipe is not a straight vertical drop, C). Water being "light" (D) is irrelevant.

22. (A) The oesophagus carries food from the throat region straight to the stomach. The small and large intestines come after the stomach; the liver is a gland off to the side, not on the food's swallowing path.

23. (D) The stomach's muscular walls mainly churn the food and mix it thoroughly with gastric juices. Most absorption happens later in the small intestine (A), bile is made by the liver (C), and water reabsorption is the large intestine's role (B).

24. (B) The protein-digesting enzyme in gastric juice is pepsin. Amylase digests starch (mouth/pancreas), bile is not an enzyme (it emulsifies fat), and trypsin is a pancreatic protein-enzyme, not a stomach one.

25. (C) Besides killing germs in food, the stomach's hydrochloric acid creates the acidic conditions that allow pepsin to work (pepsin needs strong acid). It does not cool food (A), break fats into droplets (B — that is bile), or absorb sugar (D).

26. (B) Even though the stomach wall is made of protein, a layer of mucus lines the inner wall and shields it from the acid and pepsin. The wall is not made of bone (C) and certainly contains proteins (D); bile (A) is not involved in stomach protection.

27. (A) The thick, semi-liquid paste of partly digested food released from the stomach is called chyme. Cud is the regurgitated grass of a ruminant, bile is the liver's fat-fluid, and saliva is the mouth's secretion.

28. (C) Following the food's path: mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine. Option C is the only correct order; the others place the stomach or small intestine before the oesophagus, which never happens.

29. (B) The small intestine is long and coiled chiefly to provide a large surface area and enough time to finish digestion and absorb the nutrients. It does not store waste (A — that is the large intestine), make HCl (C — the stomach), or churn like the stomach (D).

30. (D) Into the first part of the small intestine empties bile (from the liver) and an enzyme-rich juice from the pancreas (pancreatic juice). The gall bladder only *stores* bile; the stomach and salivary glands act earlier in the canal.

31. (B) The finger-like projections exist to greatly increase the surface area for absorption, so more nutrients pass into the blood per second. They do not grind food (A), make the gut acidic (C), or store bile (D).

32. (B) These projections lining the small-intestine wall are the villi. Cilia are tiny beating hairs, pseudopodia belong to Amoeba, and sphincters are ring muscles that open and close passages.

33. (C) Because each villus contains tiny blood vessels (capillaries), the step happening there is absorption of nutrients into the blood. Ingestion is at the mouth, protein digestion happens before this in the stomach, and egestion is waste removal at the far end.

34. (B) Flattening the villi removes most of the absorbing surface, so the most direct effect is poorer absorption — the person stays undernourished even while eating enough. It would not speed up mouth digestion (A), increase bile (C), or strengthen stomach churning (D); those are unrelated organs.

35. (D) The large intestine's chief job is to absorb water from the undigested residue, turning it into solid waste. It does not digest proteins (A) or absorb sugars into the blood (B — the small intestine does that), and pepsin is a stomach enzyme (C).

36. (A) If too much water is pulled out, the leftover becomes hard, dry stools that are difficult to egest — constipation. Too *little* water removal would cause loose stools (B); vomiting acid (C) and faster fat digestion (D) are unrelated to large-intestine water uptake.

37. (C) Removing the stored solid waste through the anus is the final step, egestion. Absorption and assimilation happen earlier and deal with useful nutrients; ingestion is the very first step.

38. (C) The largest gland in the human body, and the maker of bile, is the liver. The pancreas makes enzyme juice, salivary glands make saliva, and the gall bladder only stores bile (it is not a gland that makes it).

39. (C) Between meals, bile is stored in the gall bladder, a small sac, and released when fatty food arrives. The rumen is a cow's stomach chamber, the duodenum is the start of the small intestine, and the pancreas makes a different juice.

40. (D) Bile carries no enzyme, yet it helps fat digestion by breaking large fat globules into tiny droplets (emulsification), giving fat-digesting enzymes far more surface to act on. It does not chemically cut fat into amino acids (A — fats give fatty acids, not amino acids), turn fat to starch (B), or absorb fat directly (C).

41. (A) Without a gall bladder, bile is no longer concentrated and released in a burst, so fatty meals are hardest to handle — confirming that bile mainly assists the digestion of fats. Starch (B), proteins (C), and water (D) do not depend on bile.

42. (D) The pancreatic juice is special because it acts on carbohydrates, proteins, *and* fats together, whereas most other digestive juices act on only one or two food types (e.g. saliva on starch alone, gastric juice mainly on protein). It is full of enzymes (so not B), is not made in the mouth (C), and is for digestion, not water absorption (A).

43. (D) Ruminants are grouped together because they swallow grass quickly and later bring it back to the mouth to chew again (chewing the cud). They are plant-eaters, not meat-eaters (A); they have a four-chambered, not single-chambered, stomach (C); and they digest in organs, not inside one cell (D refers to Amoeba).

44. (A) The partly digested grass a cow returns to its mouth to chew at leisure is called cud. Chyme is the human stomach paste, bile is the liver's fluid, and mucus is a protective slime.

45. (B) The largest of the four chambers, where microbes first attack the grass, is the rumen. The reticulum, omasum, and abomasum are the smaller, later chambers.

46. (C) Cows cannot make a cellulose-digesting enzyme themselves; they manage because microbes living in the rumen break the cellulose down for them. They do not cook grass (A), cellulose certainly needs digestion (B), and their saliva does not contain cellulase (D) — the microbes supply that ability.

47. (A) A human gets little from grass because the human lacks both the rumen microbes and the cud re-chewing system that together break down cellulose. The human does have a stomach (B), can swallow grass (C), and does not digest grass in the mouth (D) — none of those is the real reason.

48. (C) Acid-and-enzyme digestion, like that in a human stomach, happens in the abomasum, the "true stomach" chamber. The rumen and reticulum host microbial breakdown, and the omasum mainly absorbs water.

49. (D) Re-chewing the cud grinds the grass finer, which exposes more surface for microbes and enzymes to act on, speeding cellulose breakdown. It does not change taste (A), turn cellulose into protein (B), or remove the need for a stomach (C).

50. (C) After the pseudopodia wrap around the food, it is trapped inside a food vacuole, where enzymes digest it. A villus and gall bladder are human structures; the rumen is a cow's stomach chamber.

51. (A) In Amoeba the enzymes are released into the food vacuole — a closed pocket where digestion happens. The human parallel is enzymes acting inside the cavity of the stomach and intestine (also enclosed spaces). It is not in the brain (B), on the skin (C), or only in the blood (D).

52. (D) The correct comparison: both Amoeba and humans carry out all five steps of nutrition, but Amoeba does them within a single cell while a human uses many specialised organs. Amoeba does digest (so A is false), it does egest (C false), and humans do assimilate (B false).

53. (A) Pseudopodia flowing around and wrapping a food particle is most like a hand cupping and wrapping around a marble — surrounding and enclosing it. A straw sucking (B) is more like a butterfly's proboscis, a sieve straining (C) is like baleen filtering, and a knife cutting (D) is like incisors.

54. (A) Leftover undigested matter in Amoeba is thrown out when the food vacuole reaches the cell surface and discharges its contents — this is egestion in a single cell. It is not kept forever (B), recycled into a pseudopodium (C), or passed to a neighbour (D).

55. (B) A pseudopodium being temporary tells us that Amoeba makes structures as it needs them rather than having fixed organs — a single cell improvises. So it does *not* have permanent organs (A), it certainly can move (C), and its digestion occurs inside the cell, not outside (D).

56. (B) Glucose is "burnt" to release energy mainly inside the mitochondria, the cell's energy structures. Villi absorb nutrients, food vacuoles are Amoeba's digestion pockets, and salivary glands make saliva — none release the cell's energy.

57. (D) Using absorbed amino acids to build new muscle and repair tissue is the step called assimilation. Ingestion is taking food in, digestion is breaking it down, and egestion is waste removal.

58. (A) Digestion and absorption are working fine, but the *cells cannot use the glucose* — so the failing step is the cellular use of nutrients, i.e. assimilation. It is not ingestion, digestion, or absorption, all of which the question says are working.

59. (A) Starch is first attacked by salivary amylase in the mouth; the resulting simple sugar is mainly absorbed in the small intestine (through the villi). Stomach acid does not start starch digestion (B), the small intestine does not start it (C), and the liver/mouth absorption pairing (D) is nonsense.

60. (C) Protein (paneer) is broken down mainly by pepsin in the stomach and finished by enzymes in the small intestine; its building blocks (amino acids) are then absorbed in the small intestine through the villi. It is not handled in the mouth alone (B), nor in the large intestine (A), and protein is certainly digested rather than absorbed whole (D).
