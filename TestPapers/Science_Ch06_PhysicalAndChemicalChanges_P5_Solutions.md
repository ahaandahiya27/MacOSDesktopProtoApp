## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** Warmth alone is the weakest of the four clues, because dissolving an anhydrous salt or other purely physical events can also warm a beaker without forming any new substance. A colour change here from yellow to orange and an insoluble solid settling from a clear liquid (a precipitate) are genuine signs that something new appeared. Inability to recover the starting liquid by a physical step is in fact strong evidence that a new substance formed, the opposite of leaving doubt. So the temperature rise is the observation that, on its own, decides nothing.

**2. (D)** Burning magnesium combines the metal with oxygen from the air, so the solid oxide is heavier than the metal by the mass of oxygen added; the 2.6 g increase is that captured oxygen, not new matter conjured from energy. Matter is never created, so the gain cannot be invented mass and is not a mere weighing error. The escaping white smoke is oxide carried away in the air, so it was not destroyed but simply lost from the tray; magnesium itself is not vanishing into nothing. Hence the gain is added oxygen and the smoke is displaced oxide.

**3. (B)** Melting wax, ice subliming, and drawing copper thinner are all physical: the substance keeps its identity and only its state or shape changes. Wax vapour burning forms new substances such as carbon dioxide and water, and milk curdling produces new substances responsible for the sour taste and solid curd; both are chemical. That makes exactly two chemical changes. Subliming is a state change, not chemical, and curdling genuinely is chemical, so neither one nor three nor four is correct.

**4. (C)** To isolate the effect of air while keeping water constant, the two tubes compared must both have water and differ only in whether air can reach it. T1 (boiled water under oil) has water but its air is excluded, and T2 (tap water open) has both water and air; their only difference is the air, so this pair isolates air. Comparing T2 with T4 changes two things at once (both water and air), so it cannot isolate air alone. T1 versus T4 compares two non-rusting tubes and differs in water as well, and T3 versus T4 both keep a drying agent so neither truly holds water. Only T1 versus T2 changes air alone.

**5. (C)** Galvanising works because zinc is more reactive than iron, so even where the coating is broken the zinc keeps corroding in preference to the iron and protects the exposed steel. Tin is less reactive than iron, so at a scratch the iron is the one that corrodes, and the local set-up can make that rusting worse than for bare steel. The coatings therefore do not behave identically, neither turns steel into stainless steel, and the difference is about which metal is more reactive, not about which surface holds more water.

**6. (C)** Crystallisation and evaporation both leave the very same alum, so all three portions undergo physical changes regardless of how fast or by what means the water is removed. Dissolved alum and solid alum are the same substance in different states, not different substances, and the rate of cooling or the sun's heat does not create anything new. The genuine difference is only in crystal quality: slow cooling grows big pure crystals, fast cooling gives many small ones, and open sun-drying can trap impurities. Hence all three are physical.

**7. (D)** The milky stage is a genuine chemical change: carbon dioxide in the breath reacts with limewater to make insoluble calcium carbonate, the white cloudiness. Continuing to add excess carbon dioxide does not undo chemistry; it converts that solid into a soluble substance, which is why the liquid clears, so the clearing is also chemical, not just bubbles rising or a solid evaporating away. The student is wrong because both the clouding and the clearing involve new substances forming, not a purely physical mist.

**8. (C)** Iron is more reactive than copper, so it pushes copper out of the solution: copper metal deposits on the nail, making it reddish-brown and heavier, and the iron that goes into solution forms iron sulphate, which is pale green. New substances form, so the change is chemical, not physical mixing. The brown coat is copper, not rust; the nail gains rather than loses mass; and dissolving like sugar would leave the solution unchanged, which is not what happens. So the displacement description is the correct one.

**9. (C)** Recovering the original by a purely physical method shows that no new substance was ever formed, so R is a reliable test. Q is false: breaking a clay pot cannot be undone by simple means yet makes no new substance, so it is physical despite being irreversible. P often holds but is not airtight as a rule, since reversibility is only a rough guide and is not the defining feature. Therefore only R can be trusted, and claims that lean on irreversibility or treat reversibility as decisive in both directions are wrong.

**10. (C)** To isolate moisture while keeping air, Aria must compare two open-air nails that differ only in being dry or moist, which her dry-air-versus-moist-air pair does. To isolate air while keeping water, Dev must compare two wet nails that differ only in whether air reaches them, which the boiled-water-under-oil versus open-water pair achieves. Salty-versus-plain water changes salt not moisture-versus-dry, painted-versus-bare changes the whole sealing not one variable, and salt does not supply oxygen, so the other pairings fail to isolate a single factor.

**11. (D)** The recognised clues of a chemical change include heat being given out, a gas evolving, a new smell, a precipitate forming, a colour change, and the original being unrecoverable by a physical method. All six listed observations match clues on that list, so all six count. It would be wrong to discard warmth or the recovery failure, both of which are listed clues, and a colour change is itself one of the standard clues. Hence the count is six, with the caution that any single clue alone is not decisive.

**12. (C)** Glowing is hot iron emitting light, with the metal staying iron, so it is physical; bending changes only the shape, also physical. The grey-black surface scale, however, is iron that has combined with oxygen during heating to form a new oxide, which is a chemical change at the surface even though the bulk stays iron. So two events are physical and one is chemical. Treating the glow or the hammering as chemical is wrong, and dismissing the scale as mere flaking ignores the new substance formed.

**13. (D)** Dissolving lets the salt and the soluble impurity go into solution while the insoluble clay can be filtered out; slow crystallisation then grows pure salt crystals, leaving the soluble coloured impurity behind in the remaining solution, which is the principle that makes crystallisation purify. Strong heating does not selectively burn clay or a coloured impurity away, hand-picking cannot remove a soluble impurity, and skipping the filtration leaves the clay mixed in while a dissolved impurity does not leave as a gas. So the dissolve-filter-crystallise order is correct.

**14. (B)** Grease and paint form barriers that keep air and moisture off the iron, and intact zinc both seals and, being more reactive, would protect even at a break, so W, X and Y stay well protected; the bare object Z in humid air has free access to both moisture and oxygen and rusts the most. Grease does not attract moisture to speed rusting, zinc's sacrificial corrosion protects rather than ruins the iron, and coatings clearly do not all rust equally, so the only correct ranking places the protected objects ahead of the bare one.

**15. (A)** Heated copper combines with oxygen to form a new black compound on its surface, a chemical change, shown by the fresh copper still lying beneath. Silver darkening is also chemical: the silver combines with substances in the air to make a dark tarnish, a new substance, even though it happens slowly and without a flame. The fact that metal remains underneath does not make either change physical, slowness does not make tarnishing physical, and neither is merely the colour of hot metal since the silver was never heated. Both are chemical surface changes.

**16. (C)** Heating limestone is a chemical change that absorbs heat: it takes in energy and gives off a gas while leaving a new solid, directly showing that a chemical change can take in heat rather than always give it out. Dissolving an anhydrous solid releases heat and is physical, melting ice absorbs heat but is a physical state change, and burning magnesium releases heat, so none of those refutes the claim. Only the heat-absorbing chemical change of limestone breaks the rule that chemical changes must give out heat.

**17. (A)** A precipitate and a new smell are both recognised clues that new substances formed, so the change is chemical. There is no rule that a chemical change must alter the temperature; many reactions proceed with little or no temperature change, so a constant temperature does not rule out chemistry. Mixing two clear liquids can certainly produce a reaction when an insoluble product forms. Hence the lack of a temperature change is not evidence of a physical change here.

**18. (A)** Galvanising is best where scratching is likely because zinc is more reactive than iron and goes on sacrificing itself to protect the iron even where the coating is broken, whereas a scratch through paint or oil leaves bare iron exposed to rust. Paint does not chemically turn iron into a rust-proof metal, oil does not feed controlled oxygen to make 'harmless' rust, and the three options are not equal precisely because only zinc keeps protecting at a scratch. So galvanising gives the most durable protection.

**19. (A)** Rusting needs both oxygen from the air and water, and the brown product is a hydrated iron oxide, so iron + oxygen + water → rust is the complete correct equation. Carbon dioxide is not the gas that rusts iron, nitrogen does not combine with iron to form rust despite being plentiful, and salt only speeds rusting up rather than being a reactant that forms rust. So the oxygen-and-water equation is the right one.

**20. (B)** The white powder is magnesium oxide, a new substance formed when magnesium combined with oxygen; its water mixture behaving like a base (turning red litmus blue) is a property the original metal did not have, which confirms a chemical change. The ribbon was not merely melted, the result is not due to cleaning grit, and the bare metal does not itself turn litmus blue. The new property is the proof that a new substance was made.

**21. (D)** Camphor turning to vapour and then re-forming as solid on the cool funnel is sublimation followed by deposition of the same camphor, a physical change throughout. Naphthalene balls shrinking are also subliming directly to vapour, the same substance leaving as a gas, which is physical too. No new substance forms in either case, so neither is chemical; vanishing completely does not make naphthalene chemical, and vaporising is not a chemical step. Both are physical.

**22. (D)** Giving off a gas does not always mean a chemical change: boiling water releases water vapour and dry ice subliming releases carbon dioxide gas, yet both are physical state changes, so that rule is false. The other three are sound: a chemical change is defined by new substances forming, freezing does release heat to the surroundings while staying physical, and crystallisation gives pure crystals of the same substance and is physical. So the gas rule is the false one.

**23. (C)** Where the paint chipped, air and moisture reached the exposed iron and rust formed; because rust is bulky and flaky it lifts and pushes off the surrounding paint, baring fresh iron that rusts in turn, which is why the spot spreads. The paint does not itself convert to rust, no solvent is corroding the iron, and intact paint protects rather than speeds rusting, so only the chip-exposure explanation is correct.

**24. (C)** A solid melting to a liquid of the same material and re-solidifying unchanged is a state change with no new substance, so it is the weakest evidence of a chemical change — indeed it points the other way. A new-smelling gas, a precipitate from mixing two clear liquids, and bursting into flame leaving a residue are all recognised clues that new substances formed. Therefore the simple melting is the weakest indicator of chemistry.

**25. (D)** Iron in the open solution can combine with oxygen and moisture from the air to form a brown rust-like substance at the surface, a chemical change that produces a new substance. The colour is not merely fading, since a new brown material is appearing; crystallisation would give the same pale solid, not a brown film; and dissolving further does not turn a solution brown. So the air-oxidation explanation is correct.

**26. (D)** A burning candle shows both at once: the solid wax melting and rising is a physical change, while the wax vapour burning to new substances at the flame is chemical. Ice melting and the drink cooling are both physical, salt spreading and the water turning salty are both physical (dissolving makes no new substance), and a wire bending with a little warming is entirely physical. So the candle is the event that genuinely combines a physical and a chemical change.

**27. (C)** Melting butter and letting it re-set is a state change of the same butter, a physical change, which shows that not every cooking step is chemical. Boiling an egg sets new substances, toasting bread browns it into new substances with a roasted smell, and caramelising sugar makes a new brown bitter substance — all chemical. So melting butter is the counter-example that disproves the over-broad claim.

**28. (B)** Stainless steel is an alloy and chrome plating is a surface layer; in each case a chromium-containing surface keeps the iron shielded from air and moisture, which is why both resist rust. The iron is not turned into pure chromium, the protection works even when the items get wet, and speeding the iron's reaction with oxygen would corrode it, not protect it. So the shared protective-surface explanation is correct.

**29. (D)** Rust is heavy only in N1, the one nail that has both oxygen and moisture; every set-up missing either factor barely rusts, which shows both are needed together. If only oxygen mattered, the dry-air oxygen nail would have rusted heavily, and if only moisture mattered, the moist-air no-oxygen nail would have; neither did. So the experiment cleanly shows rusting requires both oxygen and moisture.

**30. (B)** A change is chemical exactly when a new substance forms; the direction of heat flow is irrelevant. The first two changes both form new substances, so both are chemical; the third and fourth form none, so both are physical regardless of the heat. That gives two chemical changes. Heat release is not required for chemistry, not every heat-involving change is chemical, and exchanging heat alone never makes a change chemical.

**31. (B)** Evaporating water from a salt solution recovers the same salt whether the heat is gentle sun or a strong flame, so both halves are physical changes; the quantity of heat used does not by itself create a new substance. Strong heat only drives off the water faster. So it is wrong to call the strongly heated half chemical, wrong to let the amount of heat decide the type, and wrong to claim evaporation makes a fresh compound.

**32. (A)** A chemical change is defined by the formation of a new substance, not by whether a dramatic signal happens to be visible; some reactions form new substances quietly with no obvious colour, gas, or flame. So Bina is right. Visibility is not the definition, the absence of a visible clue does not prove no new substance formed, and a clue-free change is not automatically physical. The deciding question is always whether a new substance was made.

**33. (A)** Both events have iron combining with oxygen to form new substances: rapidly with sparks in the flame, and slowly as rust in the damp salty air, where the salt speeds the corrosion. Speed does not decide the type, so slowness does not make rusting physical, and the sparks are burning iron, not mere hot bits. That iron remains in the bulk does not make either change physical. Hence both are chemical.

**34. (B)** Carbon dioxide from the air reacts slowly with limewater to form insoluble calcium carbonate, which appears as a faint white skin on the surface, a chemical change. The film is not dust left by evaporation, the surface has not frozen at ordinary room conditions, and oxygen does not bleach limewater white. So the reaction with carbon dioxide is the correct explanation.

**35. (D)** Melting solder and letting it set again are state changes of the same metal, which keeps its identity, so they are physical regardless of the substance being a metal. The heat does not create a new substance, the cooled lump is the same solder not a new solid, and simple melting is not rusting. So melting a metal is a physical change.

**36. (A)** Diluting only spreads the same colouring matter through more water, so the colour looks weaker without any new substance forming; evaporating the water would bring the colour back, confirming a physical change. A colour change is not automatic proof of chemistry, water does not react with the dye here, and the change can be classified — it is physical. So no chemical change occurred.

**37. (D)** Magnetising and de-magnetising change only the arrangement that gives magnetism; the needle stays the same steel and no new substance forms, so both steps are physical. Gaining or losing magnetism is not the formation of a new substance, so neither is chemical, and merely heating the needle to de-magnetise it is not rusting. So both are physical changes.

**38. (C)** In both cups the salt simply dissolves, a physical change, and nothing new appears, so no reaction has occurred in either. Two dissolved substances do not automatically react, dissolved salt can be recovered by evaporation so it is not destroyed, and the mere presence of colour does not make one cup chemical. So both are physical dissolving with no reaction.

**39. (B)** Sea air is both humid and salty, and since salt speeds the rusting reaction the beachfront bench meets more moisture plus salt and rusts faster than the dry inland one. Cooler temperature is not what drives rusting, carbon dioxide is not the gas that rusts iron, and the difference is the salty damp environment, not a difference in the grade of iron. So the extra moisture and salt of sea air explain the faster corrosion.

**40. (D)** Failing to recover the original by a physical means is strong evidence that a new substance formed, so Test 1 is the best indicator of a chemical change here. A small temperature rise alone does not prove chemistry, the absence of a colour change proves nothing about being chemical, and seeing no gas certainly does not prove a new substance formed. So the recovery failure is the strongest single piece of evidence.

**41. (D)** Ice melting is only a state change of the same water, so it is physical; bread going mouldy involves mould growth that produces new substances and a new smell, so it is chemical. Being left in the open does not make both physical, warmth from the surroundings does not make both chemical, and absorbing heat does not make the melting chemical. So the melting is physical and the mould is chemical.

**42. (A)** Crystallisation purifies physically: the soluble copper sulphate dissolves so that insoluble impurities can be filtered out, and as crystals grow they exclude soluble impurities, which remain in the leftover solution. The impurities are not converted into copper sulphate, the dissolving heat does not burn impurities away, and the crystals are the same copper sulphate, not a new compound. So the dissolve-filter-crystallise mechanism explains the purification.

**43. (A)** Inability to recover the starting materials by a physical step is the most decisive sign that a new substance formed, and the limewater going milky confirms the gas is carbon dioxide produced by the new chemistry. A gas alone is not proof, since physical changes such as boiling release gas too; limewater milkiness is a specific test for carbon dioxide rather than a general proof of any chemistry; and measuring heat is not required to confirm a chemical change. So the recovery failure, backed by the gas test, is the decisive reason.

**44. (C)** Oil forms a barrier that keeps air and moisture from the iron, so the oiled hinge is protected, not at risk; only the bare bracket, exposed to both, rusts heavily. Oil does not draw in moisture to speed rusting, glossy paint protects rather than accelerates rusting, and the galvanised pail's zinc protects the iron rather than leaving it to rust faster than bare metal. So the student's prediction about the oiled hinge is wrong.

**45. (B)** Fermentation of dough makes new substances (the gas and flavours that raise and sour it), so it is chemical, while sharpening a pencil only shaves off shape, a physical change; this pair is fully correct. In the others, the coating step of galvanising is a physical layering while cooking an egg is chemical (the reverse of what is claimed), naphthalene subliming is physical while rusting is chemical, and dissolving washing soda is physical while digestion is chemical. So only the fermentation-and-sharpening pair classifies both members correctly.

**46. (A)** The reaction makes a new substance, a gas that turns limewater milky and does not support burning, both of which identify carbon dioxide, so a chemical change occurred. The fizzing is not escaping dissolved air, the gas is not oxygen since it fails to support a flame, and it is not hydrogen since the limewater test points to carbon dioxide. So the change is chemical and the gas is carbon dioxide.

**47. (A)** Both kinds of change can take in or give out energy: freezing (physical) releases heat, melting (physical) absorbs it, and chemical changes can do either too, so energy flow alone cannot tell physical from chemical. It is false that only chemical changes release energy, false that chemical changes always release it, and false that the direction of energy flow sorts changes into types. So energy flow alone is not decisive.

**48. (C)** Crystallisation simply brings the same sugar out of solution as crystals, a physical change, while the browning at the overheated edge turns sugar into a new dark substance, a chemical change. Separating from water is not chemical, the crystals are the same sugar not a new substance, so it is wrong to call crystallisation chemical. So crystal-forming is physical and the browning is chemical.

**49. (B)** Both coatings keep air and moisture off the iron, which is their shared protective role, but zinc is more reactive than iron and so keeps protecting even at a scratch, whereas tin does not, so they are not identical. Neither turns iron into a new metal, they do not work in completely opposite ways (both seal the surface), and the coatings, not the food, are what protect the iron. So the reply noting both the shared sealing and zinc's extra sacrificial action is most accurate.

**50. (B)** Rusting, burning, and respiration are all chemical changes involving oxygen: burning is rapid, respiration is a slow controlled use of oxygen inside the body that releases energy, and rusting is slow oxidation at the iron's surface. All three make new substances, so it is wrong to call any of them physical; respiration in particular forms new substances and is chemical, not merely warmth. So all three are chemical.

**51. (C)** Dissolving salt and evaporating to get the same salt back is reversible and keeps the same substance throughout, so it supports the idea that reversibility goes with physical changes. Burning a candle, cooking an egg, and rusting a nail are all chemical and cannot be reversed by simple physical means, so they do not support the claim. So the salt example is the right support.

**52. (A)** Being irreversible does not by itself make a change chemical; the egg is chemical because cooking forms new substances, whereas the smashed tile, though irreversible, is still the same clay and so is physical. So the egg verdict is right but for the right reason, not irreversibility. It is wrong to say irreversibility guarantees chemistry, wrong to call the egg physical, and wrong to say the egg forms no new substance. So the flaw is treating irreversibility as proof.

**53. (B)** The boiled-water nail had water but no air and did not rust, which shows water by itself is not enough; only the nail with both water and air rusted, so both are needed. The dry-air nail had air but no water and did not rust, so air alone is not enough either, and the open-water nail rusted because of the conditions, not because it was disturbed. So rusting needs both water and air.

**54. (A)** Dissolving sugar is physical, toasting bread to brown forms new substances and is chemical, and bending a spoon is physical, so this list is physical-chemical-physical as required. The others fail the pattern: rusting is chemical not physical to start; freezing, crystallising and sharpening are all physical (no chemical middle); and burning a sparkler is chemical not physical to start. So only the sugar-toast-spoon list fits the required order.

**55. (A)** Melting wax and the same liquid wax rising up the wick are both physical, since the wax keeps its identity; only the wax vapour burning at the flame forms new substances and is chemical. So two are physical and one is chemical. The flame's heat does not make melting and rising chemical, the wax does not stay unchanged forever since it burns, and climbing the wick does not change the wax's identity. So the count is two physical and one chemical.

**56. (B)** Heating ice to melt it needs heat yet only changes the state of the same water, which freezes back unchanged, so it is physical and directly refutes the rule that any heat-needing change is chemical. Caramelising sugar, heating limestone, and burning magnesium all need heat and form new substances, so they are chemical and support rather than refute the claim. So melting ice is the refuting example.

**57. (B)** The black layer is a new copper-oxygen compound formed where copper combined with oxygen from the air, a chemical surface change, shown by the fresh copper beneath when scraped. It is not soot merely sitting on top, not just the colour of hot copper, and not melted-and-refrozen copper. So a new copper-oxygen compound is the correct explanation.

**58. (D)** Both halves recover the same salt, which the flame-dried solid is confirmed to be, so evaporating the water is physical regardless of how fierce the heat was; the strength of heating does not by itself create a new substance. So calling the flame-dried half chemical is wrong, the heat strength does not decide the type, and evaporation does not build a fresh compound. Both are physical.

**59. (D)** Crystallisation gives back the same substance as pure crystals with no new substance, so it is physical, while rusting forms a new substance from iron, oxygen, and water, so it is chemical. They are therefore not both chemical and not both physical, and the roles are not reversed — crystallisation is the physical one and rusting the chemical one. So the contrast as stated is correct.

**60. (A)** Rust is iron joined with oxygen and water, so the iron that converts to rust picks up the mass of that oxygen and water; that is why 12 g of iron lost from the sheet ends up as more than 12 g of flakes, here 21 g. The extra is added oxygen and water, not trapped air bubbles and not matter made from nothing, and the steady thinning of the solid sheet is normal rusting rather than a sign of impurity. So the flakes outweigh the iron consumed because rust is heavier than the iron it formed from.
