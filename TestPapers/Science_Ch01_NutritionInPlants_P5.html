Classic Paper-5 traps: forgetting respiration runs in the light too, so a net gas figure is read as one process; treating the compensation point as 'both stopped' rather than 'equal rates'; assuming soil nitrogen or air nitrogen is a same-day photosynthesis raw material; reading a positive starch test as proof of chlorophyll, or a negative one as proof food was never made when it was just locked behind an unsoftened waxy leaf; confusing xylem (water, up) with phloem (food, out) and confusing which controlled set-up isolates light versus chlorophyll versus carbon dioxide; calling a host-dependent parasite a saprotroph or a mutualist; crediting most new dry mass to soil rather than to carbon dioxide; and assuming an insectivorous or nodulated plant is not a true autotroph. 1. In one bright hour a sealed-chamber leaf fixes carbon dioxide at 24 units per hour by photosynthesis while respiration releases it at 5 units per hour. Cloud then halves the light, and photosynthetic fixing falls in proportion to the light while respiration is unchanged. What does a meter reading only the NET carbon-dioxide change of the surrounding air now show per hour?
(A) A net fall of 19 units, because respiration switches off whenever any light still reaches the leaf.(B) A net fall of 12 units, because only the photosynthetic uptake affects the air.(C) A net rise of 5 units, because halving the light makes respiration the only process that still runs.(D) A net fall of 7 units, because photosynthesis now fixes 12 while respiration still releases 5.
2. A leaf in a sealed chamber sits at its exact compensation point, where the meter shows zero net carbon-dioxide change. The experimenter slowly RAISES the light from this point. Before any other change, what does the meter begin to show, and why?
(A) Still zero, because the compensation point is fixed and does not shift when light changes.(B) A net RISE in carbon dioxide, because brighter light makes the leaf respire faster to cope.(C) A net FALL in carbon dioxide, because more light speeds photosynthesis above the steady respiration rate so uptake now exceeds release.(D) A net RISE in carbon dioxide, because at high light the leaf stops photosynthesising to protect its chlorophyll.
3. A young tree adds 30 kg of dry mass in a year. A student's accounting: 18 kg from carbon dioxide, 6 kg traced to water supplying hydrogen, 1 kg of soil minerals, and the rest 'lifted as solid soil through the roots.' Using only this chapter's ideas, which single correction makes the accounting sound?
(A) Roots do not lift solid soil; the remaining mass also traces to carbon dioxide fixed in photosynthesis, not to whole soil carried up.(B) The carbon-dioxide share is far too high; almost all dry mass actually comes from minerals dissolved in soil water.(C) Water cannot supply any mass at all, so the 6 kg should be reassigned to oxygen the tree absorbed.(D) The minerals figure is too low; soil minerals normally make up most of a tree's dry weight.

7. Two well-watered destarched plants on one sunny day differ only in their air: plant A breathes normal air, plant B breathes air enriched with extra carbon dioxide. 8. A variegated leaf has a green ring around a white centre. Before a sunny day, a foil disc is pinned over PART of the green ring AND part of the white centre. After the day, the leaf is destarched-checked and given the iodine test. Which single region turns blue-black?

12. A child destarches a plant, then on a sunny day seals one leaf in carbon-dioxide-free air (a dish of potassium hydroxide inside) and leaves a second leaf in normal air. Only the normal-air leaf later turns blue-black. The child says 'the destarching step was pointless busywork.' The best response is:
(A) The child is right; destarching changes nothing because potassium hydroxide alone decides the outcome.(B) Without destarching, leftover old starch could turn the carbon-dioxide-free leaf blue-black too, falsely suggesting it had made food — so destarching is what makes the result trustworthy.(C) Destarching mattered only because it re-greens the leaves so the iodine colour shows up.(D) Destarching mattered only because it opens the stomata so carbon dioxide can enter faster.
13. A pondweed in water releases 64, 16 and 4 oxygen bubbles per minute as a lamp sits at 10 cm, 20 cm and 40 cm, with water, temperature and the plant held constant. A student says 'the rate quarters each time the distance doubles because the plant is losing chlorophyll as the lamp retreats.' The best correction is:
(A) The chlorophyll is intact and works again when the lamp returns; the rate falls because the light reaching the plant weakens as the lamp moves away, and rate tracks the available light.(B) The chlorophyll really is being destroyed, which is the only reason the bubble rate drops with distance.(C) The bubbles are carbon dioxide, and less of that gas forms as the lamp retreats.(D) The plant runs short of water as the lamp moves off, which slows the bubbling.
14. A leaf on a hot, dry, windy afternoon closes its stomata to conserve water. A student lists four effects: (i) carbon-dioxide intake drops, (ii) photosynthesis slows, (iii) sunlight can no longer reach the chlorophyll, (iv) water loss by transpiration falls. Exactly which of these actually follow from the stomata closing?
(A) All four follow, because closed stomata seal the leaf against both gas and light.(B) Only (iii) and (iv) follow, since closing pores blocks light and water but never affects the carbon-dioxide supply.(C) Effects (i), (ii) and (iv) follow; (iii) does not, because light enters through the leaf surface, not the tiny pores.(D) Only (iv) follows, because transpiration is the single thing the stomata control.
15. Three leaves on one well-watered plant get a sunny day: leaf 1 greased thickly on its UPPER surface only, leaf 2 greased thickly on its LOWER surface only, leaf 3 untouched. Knowing most stomata sit on the lower surface, which leaf is expected to test the LEAST blue-black, and why?
(A) Leaf 2, because greasing the lower surface clogs most of the stomata, cutting off the carbon dioxide needed to make starch.(B) Leaf 1, because the upper surface faces the sun and greasing it blocks the light that the chlorophyll needs.(C) Leaf 3, because an untouched leaf has no protection and loses its starch fastest.(D) All three equally, because grease anywhere simply stops iodine from reacting later.
16. A clear bag is tied over a living leaf on a sunny plant and droplets collect inside. An identical bag is tied over a freshly killed leaf on the same plant and far fewer droplets form. A student says 'the dead leaf makes less water inside the bag.' The correct account is:
(A) Correct as stated: the living leaf manufactures water from its sugar, which is why more droplets form.(B) Neither leaf 'makes' water; the living leaf loses water vapour through its working stomata by transpiration, which the dead leaf's stomata can no longer drive.(C) The droplets are simply rain that leaked into the living leaf's bag but not the dead one's.(D) The living leaf's xylem squirts liquid water straight out of the leaf surface into the bag.
17. A scientist injects a harmless dye into the water at a stem's base and, the same hour, feeds a labelled sugar to a brightly lit upper leaf. 18. A ringed branch has a complete band of OUTER phloem stripped all the way round, with the inner xylem left whole; its leaves stay in full sun. A gardener predicts the swelling will appear BELOW the ring as sugars drain down to it. What actually happens, and why?

19. A student claims a leaf's broad, flat, thin shape with green cells packed near the upper surface evolved 'mainly to lose water fast and keep the plant cool.' Within this chapter, the best correction is:

20. Four sealed transparent jars stand in bright light for a day, each with one organism: jar 1 a green moss, jar 2 a leafless Cuscuta vine with no host, jar 3 a bracket fungus, jar 4 a green alga. In how many jars is the carbon-dioxide level expected to have FALLEN by the end?

(B) Even a pure carnivore depends on plants indirectly: its prey (and its prey's prey) ultimately ate plants, so the energy in every meal traces to plant food, and the oxygen the snake breathes is released by plant photosynthesis — the dependence runs through the whole chain. A snake does not need to eat plants directly for this to hold. The claim that only herbivores depend on plants ignores this indirect chain. Snakes get no minerals 'straight from the soil'; the key dependence is plant-made food and plant-released oxygen.

46. (C) Rhizobium fixes nitrogen and takes food from the legume; it does not photosynthesise, so it is the one non-autotroph. The green pitcher plant photosynthesises and only uses insects for nitrogen, so it remains an autotroph. The green cactus stem photosynthesises in place of leaves, so it is an autotroph. The mango tree, green alga and photosynthetic microbes all make their own food. So exactly one is not an autotroph. Insect-trapping does not strip the pitcher plant of autotrophy, and lacking ordinary leaves does not stop the cactus stem from photosynthesising — so two and three overcount. Rhizobium does not photosynthesise, so 'all six' is wrong.

47. (C) A leaf's veins are its transport tissues: the xylem carries water up to all the food-making cells, and the phloem carries the food those cells make away to the rest of the plant. Veins do not snap shut on insects — that is a special trap, not what ordinary veins do. Veins are tubes for water and food, not light-pipes; light reaches the cells through the leaf surface, so the 'carry sunlight inward' option is invented. Veins transport food rather than storing the leaf's starch overnight, so that option is wrong.

48. (B) A green plant makes its own food by photosynthesis; from the soil it takes only water and minerals (like nitrogen), which keep it healthy but are not its food. So fertiliser is a mineral supply, not food — the gardener's analogy is the food-from-soil misconception the chapter overturns. Fertiliser is not absorbed ready-made as food, so 'exactly the plant's food' is wrong. The plant's energy comes from sunlight, not fertiliser, so 'replacing sunlight' is wrong. Roots do not turn fertiliser into glucose; glucose is made in the leaves by photosynthesis, so that option is invented.

49. (B) Starch forms only where chlorophyll AND light coincide. The white centre has no chlorophyll, so it never makes starch; the foil blocks light wherever it lies, including over part of the green border. The only region with both chlorophyll and light is the uncovered green border, so only it turns blue-black. 50. (D) Both interventions cut off carbon dioxide. Potassium hydroxide absorbs it from Leaf X's air; grease clogs the lower-surface stomata through which carbon dioxide enters Leaf Y. Starved of this raw material, neither makes starch, so both stay brown, while the untouched Leaf Z turns blue-black. 51. (D) Xylem carries water (and dissolved minerals) UP from the roots; phloem carries the FOOD made in the leaves outward and downward. The student swapped both the substances and the directions, so NEITHER claim is correct. Xylem carries water, not food, so the 'xylem carries food up' half is wrong. Phloem carries food, not water, so the 'phloem carries water down' half is wrong. The two tubes have distinct jobs and do not simply share the work, so 'both correct' is false.

52. (C) The shaded leaf transpired little but still tested positive for starch, so it clearly made food despite low water loss — disproving the student. The two are distinct processes: transpiration is water vapour leaving the stomata, photosynthesis is food being built, and one can be low while the other proceeds. Since the shaded leaf made food with low transpiration, transpiration is not required for photosynthesis and they need not rise and fall together. They are not one process under two names — the observation of low loss with successful food-making separates them.

53. (C) A seed carries a food store that fuels early growth even without light, so the shoot does NOT come from dark photosynthesis. Photosynthesis needs light, so in the dark the seedling cannot make more food once the seed's store is used up, and it dies — not from lack of air. So both halves of the child's reasoning are wrong. The death is from exhausted stored food, not thirst. Chlorophyll cannot make starch in the dark, so 'dark photosynthesis' is impossible. A damp drawer supplies moisture, not ready-made food, so it did not feed the seedling.

54. (C) Autotrophs make their own food but not from nothing: they need carbon dioxide and water as raw materials and light as the energy source, all from their surroundings — so 'needs nothing external' is the independence misconception. They do not take ready-made food from the soil; that is the food-from-soil misconception the chapter overturns. Ordinary green plants do not trap insects for energy; they photosynthesise using light. Only the raw-materials correction is right.

55. (D) The dependence runs both ways: animals get their food (directly or through a food chain) and the oxygen they breathe from plant photosynthesis, while plants use the carbon dioxide released by animals' respiration (and their own). Saying plants depend on animals 'for nothing' misses the carbon dioxide return, so that is incomplete. Plants make their own food rather than depending on animals for it, so 'plants depend on animals for food' is wrong. The two are clearly linked through food and gases, so 'neither depends on the other' is false.

56. (A) Tracing backward: stored starch came from glucose carried as food in the PHLOEM, which was built in a lit leaf from carbon dioxide that entered through a stomatal pore. Water travels in the xylem, not food, and carbon dioxide enters the leaf through stomata, not the roots — so the xylem/roots path is wrong. Food moves in the phloem, not the xylem, and the carbon comes from carbon dioxide, not oxygen, which is a product released. Minerals are not the food carried in phloem, and carbon dioxide is drawn from the air through stomata, not from soil water.

57. (A) Removing carbon dioxide (set-up 1) tests the carbon-dioxide requirement; foil over half a uniformly green leaf (set-up 2) varies only light between the halves, isolating light; a variegated leaf (set-up 3) varies only chlorophyll between green and white parts, isolating chlorophyll. The three do not all isolate light — each targets a different factor, which only the correct matching gets right.

58. (C) Carbon dioxide is an essential raw material; while the potassium hydroxide keeps absorbing it, leaf Q cannot build glucose no matter how much light, water or warmth it gets, so it stays brown — none of those changes helps. A leaf does not manufacture its own carbon dioxide from warmth; the gas must come from the air, which the potassium hydroxide is stripping away.

59. (B) In light, the plant's photosynthesis recycled the carbon dioxide and supplied oxygen for both organisms. In the dark photosynthesis stops, yet the plant and the snail both respire — using oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide — so oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises. Neither organism 'stores and releases' oxygen at night; respiration consumes oxygen. Respiration never pauses, so 'nothing changes' is wrong. Without light there is no photosynthesis to absorb carbon dioxide, so it rises rather than falls.

60. (D) Light is the energy source for photosynthesis; as it fades the plant makes less and less food despite ample carbon dioxide, water and warmth, so it weakens and dies — light is the limiting factor. Carbon dioxide is a raw material the plant uses, not a poison, so the student's diagnosis is wrong. Keeping carbon dioxide topped up does not consume chlorophyll; the two are unrelated. A green plant does not turn into a saprotroph feeding on chamber walls — that is not a real mode by which a green plant lives.
