## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** All three outcrops are the same granite, so original hardness cannot explain the gradient. The decisive variable is climate: warmth and plentiful water at the foot accelerate both chemical dissolution and physical (freeze-thaw, root) breakdown of rock, while the cold dry summit weathers very slowly. Hence depth tracks environment, not a pre-existing softness. The claim that the summit rock was hardest is contradicted by 'identical' rock. Altitude does not deepen soil at the top; here the high site has the least. Groundwater does not lift finished soil upward; soil forms in place as rock weathers and organisms add humus.

**2. (A)** The dated series shows depth increasing with age over millennia — soil deepens as rock weathers and humus accumulates slowly. One season cannot replicate thousands of years of weathering and litter decay. The 50-year flow has only a dusting, not finished soil, so the 'one monsoon' claim is refuted by its own data. Floods do not deposit ready-made deep soil. The youngest flow has the least soil, so depth does not start high and thin with age — it is the reverse.

**3. (D)** Weathering breaks rock in place into smaller pieces; erosion is the carrying-away of loosened material. Frost-splitting (i) and wind-scouring (iii) break rock where it sits, so both are weathering even though (iii) uses wind. Runoff sweeping topsoil (ii) and a river transporting gravel (iv) both remove and transport material, so both are erosion. 'All erosion' wrongly reclassifies the in-place break-up. Sorting by 'wind involved' is a false rule — wind can do either job. 'All weathering' ignores that (ii) and (iv) are transport, not break-up.

**4. (B)** Forest litter kept plot 1's A horizon thick and dark while erosion was held in check. Bare cropping on plot 2 let the unprotected A horizon erode away; the layer now at the surface is firm and stained with clay washed down from above — the signature of the B (accumulation) horizon, now exposed. A 'paler A horizon' contradicts the clay-staining and loss of humus. Bedrock does not rise and weather to clay in 120 years. A fresh O horizon would be spongy half-rotted leaves, not a firm clay-stained layer.

**5. (A)** The dark crumbly band is the A horizon (zone of loss); the stained firm band is the B horizon (zone of gain). Rainwater seeps down through the A horizon, washing fine clay and dissolved iron out of it and depositing them in the B horizon below — so the staining is imported from above, not self-made. Capillary rise from bedrock does not supply the bulk clay-iron of the B horizon. Sideways wind delivery to a single buried band is not how leaching works. Earthworms move material but the dominant clay-iron transfer here is downward leaching, not upward worm carriage.

**6. (B)** Gain of leached clay and iron is greatest in the B horizon (the zone of accumulation). The A horizon is a zone of loss, but it still passes and holds a little before it migrates, so it ranks above the deep layers. The C horizon is barely-weathered parent rock with little imported fine material, and solid bedrock receives essentially none. Ordering A first reverses loss and gain. Putting bedrock or C first ignores that the deep unweathered layers are not where leached material collects.

**7. (A)** The imported pale, clay-stained, firm layer is B-horizon subsoil — low in humus and nutrients — and it now caps the original humus-rich A horizon, which is buried out of root reach, so vegetables struggle. It is not a re-laid A horizon; the A horizon is dark and humus-rich, not pale subsoil. It is loose subsoil, not solid bedrock. And clay-stained subsoil is not an O horizon of leaf litter, which would be spongy and organic.

**8. (A)** Humus forms from decaying plant and animal litter at the surface, so the A horizon is humus-richest; the C horizon is broken, partly-weathered parent rock with little organic matter. Rock does not pump humus upward into deep layers. The C horizon is not the surface topsoil — that is the A horizon. And C is mostly mineral rock fragments, not entirely living organisms, so that 'wrong' reasoning is itself incorrect.

**9. (B)** Top to bottom a profile runs A (dark, crumbly, root-filled topsoil = jar X), B (pale, firm, clay-stained accumulation = jar Y), then C (loose parent-rock chips = jar Z). So the fill order is X, Y, Z. Rock fragments lie deep, not at the top, so Z is not first. The clay-stained layer is the buried B horizon, not the surface, so Y is not first. The C horizon (Z) lies below the B horizon, not directly under the topsoil, so X-Z-Y is wrong.

**10. (C)** A dark surface band is humus-rich topsoil, and humus needs a steady supply of decaying plant and animal litter. Deserts have sparse vegetation, so little litter and little humus form — hence the thin or absent dark band. Deserts are dry, not heavily rained on, so 'rain washing it down' is false. The profiles share bedrock, so 'no bedrock' is contradicted. Humus does not melt and evaporate; it simply fails to accumulate without litter.

**11. (A)** Rate = volume / time. Column 1: 480 / 8 = 60 mL/min. Column 2: 360 / 9 = 40 mL/min. So column 1 drains faster. Longer time does not mean faster — it is the rate that matters, not the clock. Comparing raw volumes (480 vs 360) ignores the differing times, so '480 mL/min' is a misuse of the figures. The two rates differ (60 vs 40), so they are not equal.

**12. (B)** Rate = volume / time. Bed A: 540 / 9 = 60 mL/min. Bed B: 720 / 18 = 40 mL/min. 60 / 40 = 1.5, so bed A drains 1.5 times faster — the gardener's 'more water means faster' is wrong, since bed B simply ran longer. The larger volume does not cancel the longer time to make them match. Dividing the times (18/9 = 2) ignores the unequal volumes. Comparing the raw volumes (540 vs 720) is meaningless once rates are found.

**13. (A)** The columns are stated to be the same loam, so texture is constant. Drain time rises in step with depth (5, 10, 15 min for 10, 20, 30 cm) — exactly what greater path length predicts at one rate. So the longest time reflects depth, not clay. 'Longer time proves finer particles' ignores the depth confound. Depth clearly does affect drain time here, so claiming it has no effect is false. Finishing fastest reflects the least depth, not the smallest pores.

**14. (C)** Comparing raw drain times is only valid when the same volume is used; here 400 mL versus 200 mL breaks the fair test. The proper fix is to compute each rate (volume / time) and compare those, since rate normalises for volume. Clay's percolation is perfectly measurable, just slow. Mixing the soils would destroy the comparison entirely. The timer must run while water drains, not start after — starting late would measure nothing.

**15. (D)** Compute rates: P = 600 / 12 = 50 mL/min; Q = 450 / 9 = 50 mL/min; R = 800 / 20 = 40 mL/min. So P and Q tie at 50 mL/min and R is slowest at 40. Largest total volume (R) does not mean fastest — R is actually the slowest once normalised. Ranking by poured volume or by longest time both ignore that rate is volume per minute, the only fair measure.

**16. (A)** Time = volume / rate. 600 / 24 = 25 min; 900 / 24 = 37.5 min. Reading 24 as the time ignores that 24 is a rate (mL per minute), not minutes. Multiplying rate by volume (24 x 600) gives a meaningless 14400. Dividing rate by volume inverts the formula. Only volume divided by rate yields the correct times.

**17. (B)** Rate = 150 / 3 = 50 mL/min. In 10 minutes total, drained = 50 x 10 = 500 mL, so 1000 - 500 = 500 mL remain. The column does not stop after 150 mL — the rate is steady. There is no rule forcing all 1000 mL through in 10 minutes; at 50 mL/min that would need 20 minutes. And 50 is the per-minute rate, not the volume drained, so 950 remaining is wrong.

**18. (A)** Rates: pot 1 = 720 / 12 = 60 mL/min; pot 2 = 720 / 16 = 45 mL/min; gap = 60 - 45 = 15 mL/min. Subtracting times (16 - 12 = 4) gives a time difference in minutes, not a rate gap in mL/min — that is the lab's error. Equal depth and volume with unequal times still give unequal rates, so 'no gap' is wrong. The faster pot (1) is the sandier; pot 2's longer drain means it is the slower, finer soil, not the sandier one.

**19. (D)** Managed: 540 / 6 = 90 mL/min. Compacted: 540 / 18 = 30 mL/min. The looser, humus-improved structure opens pore spaces, raising percolation, so 90 > 30. Humus and good structure open pores rather than clog them, so the rate rises, not falls. Equal volume with very different times means unequal rates. The faster rate comes from open pore structure, not from the soil suddenly being coarse sand.

**20. (C)** X: 480 / 8 = 60 mL/min. Y: 480 / 32 = 15 mL/min. 60 / 15 = 4, so X drains four times faster — consistent with X = loam, Y = clay. Y is the slower drainer, so Y is not the loam. Equal volumes over very different times give unequal rates. A higher percolation rate means water passes quickly and is NOT retained, so X (fast) is the loam, not the clay.

**21. (A)** Paddy needs water to stand, so it belongs in the slow-draining plot A (6 mL/min). Groundnut needs loose, well-drained, airy soil, so it belongs in the fast-draining plot B (54 mL/min). Putting paddy in the fast plot would let its needed water drain away. Groundnut in the slow plot would water-log its roots. Percolation rate is central to crop choice, so 'either crop anywhere' is wrong.

**22. (D)** With unequal volumes you must compute rates. W = 200/5 = 40; X = 360/4 = 90; Y = 150/10 = 15; Z = 480/8 = 60 mL/min. The lowest rate, 15 mL/min (Y), means slowest drainage = clay. Shortest time (X, 4 min) is the fastest, not the slowest. Largest volume (Z) does not mean slowest — its rate is high. Smallest volume (W) does not by itself mean clay; its rate of 40 is middling. Only the computed rate identifies the clay.

**23. (B)** Gentle drying removed the water (100 to 90 g). The further loss on strong heating (90 to 82 g), with the soil turning pale, is the dark humus burning off — paling confirms organic matter is gone. The water was already removed in the drying step, so it cannot account for the second drop. Mineral sand and silt do not burn away. Adding oxygen would not lower weight, and that is not what strong heating does to humus — it burns it off.

**24. (C)** Loss = 100 minus final mass: A lost 8 g, C lost 15 g, B lost 22 g. The largest water loss (B, 22 g) points to the soil that had retained the most moisture — clay, with its high water-holding capacity. Sample A lost the least, so it held the least water (more like sand), not clay. The middle loss (C) is loam-like, not the clay extreme. Heating to dryness and weighing the loss is precisely how retained-water content is compared, so 'cannot reveal' is wrong.

**25. (C)** Bubbles come from air trapped in the soil's pore spaces escaping when water displaces it. Pressing compacts the clod, closing pores, so less air is trapped and fewer bubbles form. The bubbles are air, not water, so 'destroyed the water' is irrelevant. The clod releases air to the water, it does not absorb air from it. And it is the presence of trapped air — not its absence — that produces bubbles.

**26. (D)** Bubbles rising from a clod dropped in water are trapped air escaping (experiment 1 = air); mist condensing on cool glass after heating is the soil's own water turning to vapour and back to liquid (experiment 2 = water). Mist is condensed water, not gas, so 'both prove air' is wrong. The conclusions are not reversed — bubbles mean air, mist means water. And soil naturally contains both air and water, so 'neither proves anything' is false.

**27. (C)** Drying evaporates the water; the pores it leaves are then occupied by air, so the air fraction rises as the water fraction falls. Mineral grains are solid and do not evaporate. Humus does not boil off merely on sun-drying, and air does not disappear — it expands to fill the vacated pores. Air does not turn into water; that is the reverse of what drying does.

**28. (B)** Sand (largest grains) drained fastest at 4 min, loam at 8 min, clay (finest) slowest at 20 min. A 6-min time sits between sand and loam, so the fourth soil is coarser than the loam but a touch finer than pure sand — a sandy-loam. 6 min is NOT the longest time (20 min is), so it is not the clay. The finest soil took 20 min, not 6, so they are not the same. Drain time at equal volume directly indicates texture, so it is judgeable.

**29. (A)** Worm burrows are open channels that admit air and let water percolate; remove the worms and those channels are lost, so percolation falls and aeration worsens in the worm-free field. Worms do not clog channels — they create them. Worms affect structure and water movement, not merely colour. And killing worms removes, not adds, loosening, so percolation and aeration drop rather than improve.

**30. (D)** Fast (instant) drainage requires wide pores between coarse particles; maximal wetness requires fine, tightly-packed pores that retain water. These structures are opposites, so a soil cannot maximise both — percolation rate and water-holding capacity are inversely related. They are not the same property. Humus improves balance but cannot make a soil simultaneously the fastest-draining AND the most retentive. Clay's slow drainage is the opposite of 'drains instantly,' so the clay claim is self-contradictory.

**31. (C)** Sand drains fastest and holds least, so it firms up first (bed 1); clay drains slowest and holds most, so it stays soggy longest (bed 3); loam is intermediate (bed 2). Clay does not shed water fastest — it is the slowest. Sand does not stay soggiest — it holds the least water. Different textures dry at different rates, so they could not all firm up together.

**32. (C)** Aeration is worst where pores stay full of water. Pot 1's clay, standing in water, keeps its fine pores saturated, starving roots of air — the worst. Pot 2's sand drains freely, so air re-enters its wide pores once the tray is emptied; being flooded once does not permanently exclude air. Pot 4's dry sand is full of air, not devoid of it. Pot 3's moist loam still holds plenty of air in its open pores. Only standing water in fine clay pores chokes off air.

**33. (A)** Well-rotted compost (humus) improves structure in both directions: in pure sand it boosts water-holding capacity (and nutrients), and in heavy clay it opens the structure so it drains and aerates better. So it helps both beds. It does improve sandy soil's retention, so 'only the clay' is wrong. Compost opens pores and feeds soil rather than sealing it. And it cannot transmute sand into clay or clay into sand — it moderates each toward a better loam-like balance.

**34. (B)** Only a direct count of micro-organisms, mites and worms shows living things. Bubbling in water shows trapped air, not life. Misting on a heated tube shows moisture (condensed water vapour), not breathing. Mass loss on strong heating is mostly evaporated water plus burnt humus — humus is dead organic matter, so the loss does not by itself prove living organisms. Each non-life option misreads a physical property as evidence of life.

**35. (D)** Soil organisms need moisture to live, humus as food, and air for respiration — all abundant in loam, scarce in salty sand. Organisms do not favour the largest particles; loam's value is its balanced structure with humus, not coarse grains. Fast drainage is not what supports life — water and food do. Cold does not maximise life; many organisms need moderate warmth.

**36. (B)** Clay's fine particles pack so tightly that its pores are tiny and resist water flow — that is why a clay bowl holds water and why clay soil drains slowly. Tight packing slows flow, it does not force water straight through fast. Clay actually holds a lot of water (high water-holding capacity), not none. And clay's percolation rate is far lower than sand's despite both being mineral — particle size and packing, not just composition, set the rate.

**37. (A)** Standing water saturates clay's tiny pores, shutting out air, so gram roots are starved of oxygen and rot — 'more water' harms an aeration-loving crop. Clay drains slowly, so plot B did not dry out from fast drainage. Flooding leaches nutrients downward, not upward to the surface. Gram is not a water crop; plot A, which thrived, was the correctly managed one, so the standing-water plot is the failure, not the reverse.

**38. (D)** Paddy needs standing water, so it suits the water-logging clay; gram favours moisture-holding clayey-loam; cotton prefers a light, well-drained sandy-loam; groundnut wants loose, fast-draining sand for its pods. The other matchings put paddy in fast-draining soil (it would dry out), groundnut in water-logging clay (its pods rot), or cram all crops into clay despite their differing water needs.

**39. (D)** Soil K's intermediate drainage holds steady moisture yet sheds the surplus, matching tomatoes' need to stay moist but not water-logged. Soil J drains so fast it dries out by noon — too dry. Soil L stays soggy for days, water-logging the roots; 'wettest is best' ignores that standing water starves roots of air. Slow drainage does not guarantee air — it causes the opposite by keeping pores water-filled.

**40. (C)** Field 1's coarse sand drains so fast that watered moisture sinks past the roots, causing drought wilting; field 2's heavy clay holds water and excludes air, water-logging and rotting the roots — two opposite causes. Humus is not the culprit in either, and field 1 is not water-logged. Sand drains (it does not water-log) and clay retains water (it does not dry out), so swapping their behaviours is wrong. High water-holding capacity keeps soil moist, the opposite of drying crops out.

**41. (D)** No single soil is best for all crops — match the soil to the crop's water needs. Fast-draining sand suits groundnut and other drought-tolerant crops; water-holding clay suits paddy and other water-lovers; loam suits most general crops. So both sweeping claims fail. The third option is also self-contradictory, since fastest drainage and highest retention cannot coexist in one soil.

**42. (C)** Cotton favours lighter, well-drained sandy-loam, so it belongs in field G; paddy (needs standing water) and gram suit the moisture-holding clayey-loam of field H. Paddy in the fast-draining field G would lose its needed water. Soil type matters, so 'all crops in G equally' is false. Cotton prefers light, drained soil, not water-logged clay, so placing it in field H is wrong.

**43. (B)** Soil pollution is the addition of harmful substances; toxic effluent (i) and plastic waste (ii) both fit, poisoning or choking the soil. Runoff stripping topsoil (iii) is erosion — removal, not contamination. Leaves rotting into humus (iv) is natural enrichment that improves soil. So only (i) and (ii) are pollution. Calling erosion 'pollution' confuses removal with contamination; calling beneficial humus 'pollution' is wrong; and not every change is pollution — only harmful additions are.

**44. (A)** Up-and-down furrows act as channels that speed runoff and cut gullies (farmer 1). Level terraces and contour ploughing interrupt and slow the downhill flow so water soaks in, holding soil in place (farmer 2). Farmer 2's method slows water, not speeds it. It does not rely on evaporation. And terracing protects the soil in place rather than removing it beforehand.

**45. (C)** Wind erosion on a flat plain is best slowed by shelter belts (tree rows that break the wind); water erosion on a steep slope is best fought by terraces and contour ploughing that slow runoff. Swapping them is wrong — terraces suit slopes, not flat ground, and shelter belts suit wind, not gullies. Deep ploughing loosens soil and can worsen erosion, not stop it. Leaving sites bare maximises both wind and water erosion.

**46. (D)** Gap (pore) size set by particle size governs flow: large marbles leave wide gaps (fast, like sand); fine grains leave narrow gaps (slow, like clay). Marbles do not dissolve to open channels. Fine grains do not push water upward against gravity. And the marbles clearly have gaps between them — wide ones — which is exactly why water runs straight through, so 'no gaps' is the opposite of the truth.

**47. (B)** Weathering is the in-place break-up of rock — here the frost cracking the boulder over decades. Erosion is the carrying-away of loosened material — here the flood sweeping the rubble to the valley. So only the flood stage is erosion; labelling the whole event erosion ignores the in-place break-up. Calling it all weathering ignores the transport. Reversing the two mislabels both. And weathering and erosion are not restricted to wind — frost and water do both jobs.

**48. (B)** Identical granite means climate decides: the hot humid valley weathers faster and its lush vegetation drops litter that becomes deep humus, so its soil is thicker. Cold dry air slows, not speeds, weathering, so the ridge prediction is backward. A high windy position favours erosion and bare rock, not deep soil. Same rock does not force same soil — environment makes the depths differ markedly.

**49. (A)** Freeze-thaw is a powerful physical weathering agent: water seeps into cracks, freezes, expands and pries the rock apart, repeated each winter — so the riverbed chunk (1) crumbles most. The sealed dry case lacks the water needed for freeze-thaw, so little happens. Dry buried desert sand provides no freezing water, and burial alone does not crush sandstone to bits. Steady dry indoor air offers neither moisture nor freezing, so weathering is minimal. Water plus freezing drives the break-up, present only in the riverbed.

**50. (D)** Soil = weathered mineral grains + humus + living organisms; bedrock = solid unweathered rock with essentially none of that life. The first distractor reverses which layer holds life. Bedrock is not 'equally full' of humus and organisms — soil holds them, bedrock does not. And soil is not pure organic matter; it is mostly weathered mineral material, so 'no mineral content' is false.

**51. (B)** The paired change — A horizon paling and thinning, B horizon gaining clay and iron — is the signature of leaching: rainwater seeps down, strips fine clay and dissolved iron from the A horizon (zone of loss), and deposits them in the B horizon (zone of gain). Capillary rise from bedrock moves upward and would not pale the A horizon. Wind does not selectively load a buried horizon. Soil cannot manufacture clay and iron from trapped air; the material was transferred from above.

**52. (C)** Rates: S = 360 / 4 = 90; U = 360 / 6 = 60; T = 360 / 12 = 30 mL/min. Sandiest = fastest = S, then U, then T (clayiest). U is not sandiest — S drains faster. The longest time (T) is the clayiest, not the loosest. Equal volume with different times means different rates, so the textures differ.

**53. (B)** Rate = 240 / 6 = 40 mL/min. Time for 600 mL = 600 / 40 = 15 min. The 24000 figure wrongly multiplies 40 x 600 instead of dividing. The 1440 mL/min figure multiplies 240 x 6 instead of dividing. The 6 mL/min figure mistakes the time (6 min) for the rate. Only rate = volume/time and time = volume/rate give the right pair.

**54. (D)** The soil is the SAME in both; halving the depth halves the path and roughly halves the time (5 vs 10 min), so the shorter time reflects depth, not texture. Longer time does not prove coarser grains. Depth clearly affects drain time here, so saying it has 'no effect' is false. A faster time does not always mean larger particles when depth differs — that is exactly the confound being exposed.

**55. (D)** A-horizon thickness tracks organic input minus loss. Forest (plot 1) adds the most litter and is never disturbed, so it is thickest; mulched, rotated cropland (plot 2) keeps some cover and organic matter, so it is intermediate; bare cropping with burned stubble (plot 3) strips organic matter and exposes soil to erosion, so it is thinnest. Cropping and burning destroy, not build, topsoil. Same climate and rock do not force equal A horizons — management differs. Mulched cropland helps but does not out-build undisturbed forest.

**56. (D)** Mulch absorbs the impact of falling rain and shelters the surface from wind, and it conserves moisture beneath — so plot 2 kept its topsoil and stayed damper. Mulch does not make soil heavier. It does not seal off percolation — water still soaks through. And it cannot turn topsoil into bedrock; it simply shields and conserves the surface.

**57. (C)** Trampling compacts the soil, collapsing its pore spaces, so water cannot seep down — hence puddles and a LOWER percolation rate on the path than the loose lawn. Compaction slows, not speeds, drainage, so a higher rate is wrong. The same soil can differ in drainage once compacted, so 'identical' is wrong. Compaction shrinks pores, the opposite of enlarging them, so that reasoning contradicts the puddling.

**58. (B)** Weathering that makes soil parent material is slow: lichens and roots prising grains (i), freeze-thaw wedging cracks (ii), and wind-blown sand abrading the face (iv) all fragment rock over long times. A one-second lightning char (iii) is a momentary surface event, not a soil-building weathering agent. Weathering is not confined to sudden violent events — slow steady processes dominate. And rock does not turn to soil by melting; it weathers gradually.

**59. (A)** Top to bottom: O (leaf litter), A (dark crumbly humus topsoil), B (pale clay-stained accumulation band), C (loose rock chips = parent rock), bedrock (solid rock). So the pale stained band is correctly the B horizon. The dark crumbly band is the A horizon, not C. The loose rock chips are the C horizon, not the humus-rich A. The half-rotted leaves are the O horizon, not bedrock.

**60. (C)** Rate = volume / time. Soil X: 720 / 9 = 80 mL/min. Soil Y: 720 / 15 = 48 mL/min. So X is the faster drainer, and Y's longer time means it is slower, not faster — a longer clock for the same volume signals a lower rate. Equal volume with different times gives unequal rates, so they are not equal. Multiplying 720 x 9 (= 6480) misuses the formula; rate is volume divided by time, not multiplied.
