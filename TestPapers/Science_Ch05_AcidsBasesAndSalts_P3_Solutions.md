## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** Sourness is the hallmark taste of acids, and acids turn blue litmus red, so that is the safe prediction. Turning red litmus blue is a base behaviour, and bases taste bitter, not sour. Saying taste reveals nothing ignores the reliable link between sour taste and acid character. Turmeric turning red is a base reaction, not an acid one; acids leave turmeric yellow.

**2. (A)** Bottle 1 is a base (red litmus to blue) and bottle 2 is an acid (blue litmus to red). Mixing roughly equal amounts neutralises them, giving a near-neutral mixture that leaves litmus unchanged. There is no rule that the acid or the base is always stronger, so neither one-sided result is justified. Litmus only goes red or blue; green is not a litmus colour.

**3. (A)** Milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) is a mild base; an antacid relieves acidity because a base neutralises the excess stomach acid. Calling it an acid is the misconception, since adding more acid would worsen acidity. A neutral substance would only dilute, not neutralise, and would give little relief. A salt does not neutralise an acid the way a base does.

**4. (D)** Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a base. Of the four, only lime water is basic, so exactly one turns pink. Lemon juice is acidic and sugar water and distilled water are neutral, all of which leave phenolphthalein colourless. So the answer is one, not two, three, or zero.

**5. (B)** Vinegar contains acetic acid, so it is acidic, and china rose turns dark pink (magenta) in acids. Green is the colour china rose shows in bases, not acids, so green is wrong here. Vinegar is not basic and not neutral, so those reasons fail too.

**6. (C)** Neutralisation gives salt and water; here HCl plus NaOH gives sodium chloride and water, and evaporating the water leaves the salt. If the acid and base were exactly neutralised, none of the base is left over, so leftover NaOH is wrong. Likewise no unreacted acid remains. Neutralisation produces a salt, not a new stronger acid.

**7. (D)** The sting is acidic, so relief needs a mild base, and baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) paste neutralises it. Lemon juice is itself acidic, so it cannot neutralise the acid and would not help. Two acids do not cancel each other. Plain water only dilutes, while the proper remedy is a base, so saying only water works is wrong.

**8. (A)** No litmus change means the liquid is neutral, but many neutral liquids exist besides water, such as salt water and sugar water, so the conclusion is unsafe. Water does not turn blue litmus red, since it is neutral. Neutral liquids do not turn red litmus blue either; that is a base reaction.

**9. (B)** Blue litmus turning red shows the waste is acidic, so it must be neutralised with a base; slaked lime is the standard cheap base used. Adding more acid makes it more acidic, not safer. Common salt does not neutralise an acid. Sugar has no neutralising action and only dissolves.

**10. (B)** Turmeric turns red only in bases; staying yellow tells you the sample is not basic, but it cannot distinguish between an acid and a neutral substance, both of which leave turmeric yellow. So you cannot say it is definitely an acid or definitely neutral. It is certainly not a base, which rules out that option.

**11. (B)** Tamarind water is acidic (tartaric acid), lime water is basic, and sugar solution is neutral, matching acid then base then neutral. In the soap, lemon, NaOH list the order is base, acid, base, which is wrong. Distilled water, vinegar, milk of magnesia is neutral, acid, base, the wrong order. Baking soda, curd, salt water is base, acid, neutral, which has the acid and base swapped.

**12. (C)** Slaked lime is a base, so it neutralises the acidic soil, but added to already-basic pond water it would make it more basic, which is the contrast asked for. Vinegar is acidic, not neutral, so the claim about it is wrong. Lemon juice is an acid, not a base. Sugar is neutral and does not neutralise either.

**13. (A)** Red litmus to blue means the solution is basic; bases turn turmeric red, so that result must follow. A base does not turn blue litmus red, since that is an acid reaction. A base turns phenolphthalein pink, not colourless. China rose turns green in bases, not dark pink, so that option is wrong.

**14. (B)** While dry, the acid and the base in baking powder sit side by side without reacting; only when water is added to the batter can they meet, react and release the gas that makes the cake rise. The acid does not lose its sourness in storage. Water is neutral, not a base that "switches on" the powder. The gas comes from the reaction, not from air beaten into the dry powder.

**15. (C)** Phenolphthalein is pink in base and colourless when not basic, so the pink vanishing means the added acid has neutralised the base, leaving the solution roughly neutral. It is not now strongly basic, since the indicator would still be pink. Lemon juice stays an acid. The soap solution is being neutralised, not made more basic.

**16. (D)** Phenolphthalein is a synthetic, lab-made indicator, so it is the one not from a living source. Litmus comes from lichens, turmeric from the turmeric plant, and china rose from hibiscus flowers, so all three are natural indicators.

**17. (D)** Vinegar is an acid and baking soda is a base; mixed in the sealed bag they neutralise and release a gas, and that trapped gas inflates the bag until it is taut. The bag does not swell from vinegar vapour, since there is no strong heating. No air enters a sealed bag from outside. Plain dissolving would not generate the gas needed to inflate the bag.

**18. (C)** Marble is calcium carbonate, which behaves as a base, so it reacts with the acidic rain and is gradually corroded. Marble is not acidic, so the first wrong option reverses the chemistry. Acid rain is acidic, not neutral, and it does more than wash dust off. Heating and cracking is not the cause of acid-rain damage.

**19. (B)** A wasp sting is basic, so it needs a mild acid like vinegar to neutralise it, which is the reverse of the bee or ant sting remedy. Baking soda is a base and would not neutralise a basic sting. Plain water only dilutes. Sodium hydroxide is a strong base and would worsen the sting, besides being dangerous on skin.

**20. (A)** Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a base. X turns turmeric red, the mark of a base, so X turns phenolphthalein pink. Y turns blue litmus red, so Y is an acid and gives no pink. Z changes neither litmus, so it is neutral and gives no pink. Phenolphthalein needs a base, not an acid, so the option claiming an acid turns it pink is wrong.

**21. (A)** An antacid is a base that neutralises stomach acid, producing salt and water, exactly fitting acid plus base gives salt plus water. Ice melting is a physical change of state. An apple browning is a reaction with air, not an acid-base neutralisation. Salt dissolving is mixing, not a chemical reaction between an acid and a base.

**22. (C)** Blue litmus to red means acidic, and phenolphthalein is colourless in acids, so the two results agree and the sample is an acid. Phenolphthalein being involved does not make it basic; it is colourless here precisely because there is no base. Phenolphthalein is colourless in both acids and neutrals, but blue litmus turning red rules out neutral. The results are consistent, not contradictory.

**23. (D)** A neutral substance changes neither litmus paper, and water does exactly that, which is the evidence of neutrality. Turning blue litmus red is acid behaviour, which water does not show. Turning red litmus blue is base behaviour, also not shown by water. Phenolphthalein turning pink would mean a base, so that too would contradict neutrality.

**24. (A)** Calcium carbonate behaves as a base, so the eggshell reacts with the acid in vinegar, releasing a gas (the bubbles) while the shell is gradually eaten away. The vinegar does not dry the shell out — it is a liquid that reacts with it. The shell does not dissolve unchanged like sugar; a chemical reaction consumes it. There is no warming source to make the egg heat and flake the shell.

**25. (C)** Soap solution is basic, and methyl orange is yellow in bases, so the colour is yellow. Red would mean acid, but soap is not acidic. Saying red for a basic solution mismatches the rule, since red goes with acids. Soap is not neutral, and methyl orange is not colourless, so that option fails too.

**26. (D)** Different acid-base pairs give different salts; HCl with NaOH gives sodium chloride, while sulphuric acid with NaOH gives sodium sulphate, so the salt depends on the reactants. It is not always common salt. The product is a new salt, not the starting acid. Sodium hydroxide is the base used up, not the salt produced.

**27. (B)** Carbon dioxide acts as an acidic gas; once it just neutralises the basic lime water, the result is roughly neutral and leaves litmus unchanged. It is no longer strongly basic, so red litmus would not turn blue. Just neutralising does not make it strongly acidic, so blue litmus to red is wrong. Green is not a litmus colour.

**28. (C)** Turmeric stays yellow in acids and turns red in bases, which is correct. Phenolphthalein is the opposite of the stated wrong claim: colourless in acids and pink in bases. Blue litmus turns red in an acid, not a base. China rose is dark pink in acids and green in bases, the reverse of the wrong option.

**29. (D)** China rose turns green in a base; a base turns red litmus blue and feels soapy, so that is the matching set. Turning blue litmus red and tasting sour are acid properties, the opposite of a base. A base does change litmus, so leaving both unchanged is wrong. A base turns turmeric red, not staying yellow.

**30. (A)** Neutralisation between an acid and a base releases heat, so the mixture warms up. Evaporation would cool, not warm, and is not what is happening. Bases do not freeze a mixture. The warmth comes from the reaction itself, not from external sunlight.

**31. (C)** Lime water and ammonia solution are bases, so the count is two. Curd is acidic (lactic acid) and tamarind water is acidic (tartaric acid), so they are not bases. Thus one is too few and three or four is too many.

**32. (B)** Feeling soapy and turning turmeric red are both base properties, so the liquid is a base. An acid would feel sour-tasting (not soapy) and leave turmeric yellow. A neutral substance changes no indicator. Common salt solution is neutral and would not turn turmeric red.

**33. (D)** Slaked lime is a cheap, widely used base, so it neutralises the acidic water. Vinegar and lemon juice are acids, so they would worsen the acidity, not fix it. Adding more acidic runoff obviously makes the problem worse.

**34. (D)** Sour taste is the signature of acids, however weak or strong, so a stronger sour taste still means an acid, not a base. Strength of taste does not flip an acid into a base. Sourness never becomes basic. And sour does not mean neutral; neutral substances have no acid or base taste.

**35. (D)** Turmeric turns red only in a base. Q and S turn red, so two are basic. P and R stay yellow, so they are not basic (acid or neutral). Hence one is too few and three or four too many.

**36. (C)** Milk of magnesia is a base (magnesium hydroxide), so calling it an acid is the wrong pairing. Vinegar is correctly an acid, lime water is correctly a base, and curd is correctly an acid, so those three pairings are right.

**37. (C)** An antacid is a mild base, and it neutralises the excess HCl, forming salt and water and relieving the burning. Adding more acid would worsen the problem. Sugar coating does not neutralise the acid. Making the acid stronger is the opposite of relief.

**38. (A)** Phenolphthalein pink means solution 1 is a base. Solution 2 is colourless in phenolphthalein and turns blue litmus red, which marks it as an acid. So the order is base then acid. The reverse order is wrong. Solution 2 cannot be neutral because it turns blue litmus red. They are not both bases, since solution 2 is an acid.

**39. (D)** Baking soda solution is a base, so it turns red litmus blue and phenolphthalein pink. Lemon juice is an acid, not a base, so it does neither. Acids do not turn red litmus blue; that is a base reaction. Both are not acids, since baking soda is a base.

**40. (B)** Carbon dioxide dissolving makes carbonic acid, so a fizzy drink is mildly acidic. It is not basic, since an acid forms, not a base. It is not strongly basic for the same reason. And it is not neutral, because the dissolved acid makes it acidic.

**41. (D)** A base must turn phenolphthalein pink, turmeric red, and china rose green, and it must turn red litmus blue. If it leaves litmus unchanged and phenolphthalein stays colourless, both point to it not being a base, so that disproves the claim. Phenolphthalein turning pink, turmeric turning red, or china rose turning green would each support, not disprove, the base claim.

**42. (B)** Lime improves acidic soil by neutralising it and worsens basic soil by adding more base, so lime is a base. It is not an acid; an acid would fix the basic soil, not the acidic one. It is not neutral, since it clearly changes the soil. It is not an inert salt, because it does change the soil character.

**43. (C)** At the endpoint the acid and base have neutralised, so the mixture is neutral and leaves both litmus papers unchanged, which is the confirmation. Blue litmus turning red would mean leftover acid, meaning the endpoint was overshot with too much acid. Red litmus turning blue would mean leftover base. A neutral mixture is not a fresh acid.

**44. (B)** Turmeric turns red only in a base, and soap solution is basic, so it turns turmeric red. Lemon juice and vinegar are acids, which leave turmeric yellow. Sugar water is neutral and also leaves turmeric yellow.

**45. (B)** China rose is natural (from hibiscus) and phenolphthalein is synthetic, so that pair is one of each. Litmus and turmeric are both natural. Turmeric and china rose are both natural. Litmus and china rose are both natural, so only the first pair mixes natural with synthetic.

**46. (D)** A bee sting is acidic, so it needs a mild base such as baking soda to neutralise it, and honey is not a base. Honey is not basic, so it does not neutralise. Sweetness has nothing to do with neutralising an acid. Sweet substances do not neutralise acids, so that general claim is false.

**47. (C)** The first test shows an acid; after adding the base milk of magnesia, red litmus turning blue shows the mixture is now basic, meaning so much base was added that it overshot neutral. Milk of magnesia is a base, not an acid. The original solution turned blue litmus red, so it was an acid, not a base. A clear change from acid to base proves a reaction did happen.

**48. (A)** Distilled water is neutral, and phenolphthalein stays colourless except in a base, so there is no colour. Distilled water is not acidic, and phenolphthalein does not turn pink in acids anyway. It is not basic, so the weak-base reason is wrong. Phenolphthalein responds to bases, not specifically to salt water.

**49. (B)** Blue litmus to red, phenolphthalein staying colourless, and china rose turning dark pink are all acid behaviours, so the contents are an acid and the results agree. China rose changing to dark pink is the acid colour, not a base sign. Phenolphthalein colourless alone could be neutral, but the litmus change rules that out. The results are consistent, not contradictory.

**50. (B)** Common salt solution is neutral, so no indicator shows an acid or base colour: litmus unchanged, turmeric yellow, china rose unchanged, phenolphthalein colourless. The other sets mix acid signs (blue litmus red, turmeric staying yellow with acid) and base signs (turmeric red, phenolphthalein pink), none of which a neutral salt solution produces.

**51. (C)** Marble and limestone are calcium carbonate, a base that reacts with acid rain and is corroded. Glass is not acidic and is not the classic acid-rain casualty. Iron is a metal, not a base in this sense, though it rusts by a different process. Plastic does not neutralise acid and is not the example meant.

**52. (A)** Blue litmus turning red marks an acid. Vinegar and lemon juice turn it red, so two acids. Lime water is basic and milk is roughly neutral, so they do not turn blue litmus red. Hence two, not one, three, or four.

**53. (D)** Phenolphthalein is colourless in acids and in neutrals alike, so a colourless reading cannot distinguish them and a second indicator like litmus is needed. Litmus does not give the same colour in acids and bases; it clearly differs. Turmeric turns red only in bases, not in acids. Indicators do not all give one colour everywhere; that is why they are useful.

**54. (A)** Souring of milk is due to lactic acid forming, so spoiled milk is acidic. A base would taste bitter and not sour, so the base option is wrong. Becoming sour is moving away from neutral, not toward it. Sourness comes from an acid, not from a salt.

**55. (D)** After exact neutralisation the salt is dissolved in the water formed, so evaporating the water leaves the solid salt. Adding more acid or base would unbalance the neutral mixture and leave excess reactant, not cleanly recover the salt. Freezing would not separate out an acid, since no acid is left over.

**56. (C)** Red litmus turning blue means a base, and soap solution is basic, so it fits. Orange juice is acidic (citric acid). Vinegar is acidic (acetic acid). Soda water is acidic (carbonic acid). Only soap solution among these is a base.

**57. (A)** Fizzing stops and litmus is unchanged when the acidic lemon juice and basic baking soda have neutralised, giving a neutral mixture. It is not still strongly acidic, since litmus would then change. It is not strongly basic for the same reason. Neutralisation gives salt and water, not a stronger pure acid.

**58. (B)** Bitter taste, soapy feel, red litmus turning blue, and phenolphthalein turning pink are all base properties, so the liquid is clearly a base. A soapy feel is a base trait, not an acid one. A neutral substance would not change any indicator, so many positive base results rule out neutral. The four results agree rather than conflict.

**59. (A)** Lime is a base, useful only for acidic soil; basic soil needs a mild acid such as decaying compost to neutralise it. Lime is not neutral, so it cannot fix any soil. Lime is a base, not an acid, so it would worsen basic soil. Sodium hydroxide is a strong base and would make basic soil even more basic, so it is the wrong fix.

**60. (A)** China rose turns green in a base, so cup B is basic, and phenolphthalein turns pink in a base, so cup B turns pink. Cup A is dark pink in china rose, which is the acid colour, so cup A is an acid and stays colourless with phenolphthalein. The two indicators agree rather than disagree, since both identify cup B as the base.
