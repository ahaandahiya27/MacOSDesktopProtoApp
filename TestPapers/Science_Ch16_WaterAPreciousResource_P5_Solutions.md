## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** Track the states: ocean liquid to atmospheric vapour is one change; vapour freezing into glacier snow is a second; glacier ice melting into river liquid is a third. The river-to-sea step is liquid staying liquid, so it adds no state change, giving three in total. Crucially, every step only rearranged the same molecule into a new form and place; nothing was manufactured by the snowfall, nothing was annihilated, and there is no need to invoke loss to space. That conservation across rearrangement is precisely the chapter's reason the planet's water total barely changes year to year.

**2. (B)** A sealed terrarium is a tiny model of the global cycle: water leaves the soil as vapour (evaporation), the vapour meets the cool glass and condenses to mist, and that liquid drips back to the soil — a precipitation-like return. Because the jar is sealed, the same water simply goes round and round with no net gain or loss, mirroring why Earth's total stays constant. Plants release water they first drew up, they do not manufacture it; sealed glass loses nothing; and the glass droplets are the very same water that evaporated, not a new sunlight-made substance.

**3. (B)** Begin with the frost present at dawn: it is solid ice. By midday it has become liquid beads, so the first change is melting (solid to liquid). By dusk the rail is dry, meaning the liquid has gone to vapour — evaporation (liquid to vapour) is second. Overnight new frost grows, so vapour has gone directly back to ice, the solid-forming return that closes the day. The order is therefore melt, evaporate, then re-form as ice. Putting evaporation or condensation first contradicts the fact that frost (ice) was the starting state, and freezing-first ignores that the frost already existed at dawn.

**4. (D)** The white plume you can see above a pot is not vapour at all — true water vapour is invisible. The plume is vapour that has already cooled enough to condense into a mist of tiny liquid droplets. The genuinely gaseous water is the invisible dampness near the spout, which only reveals itself when it later condenses on a cold tile. So both are one substance, water, in different states, and the child has it backwards: the invisible part is the gas, the visible plume is liquid. The dampness is not air, and neither is a separate chemical.

**5. (B)** The skater rests on solid ice, fish move through liquid water beneath, and breath-fog is condensed vapour above — three states of the identical substance coexisting because temperature and exposure differ from the surface down into the water and up into the air. This shows water can occupy all three states simultaneously when local conditions differ. It is not forced into one state per body of water, the three forms are all just water rather than separate chemicals, and ice plainly forms far from the poles, here directly over liquid water.

**6. (B)** In the open sky, air rises, expands and cools, and that cooling is what condenses vapour into cloud droplets. But rising is only the means of cooling, not the cause of condensation itself — the cause is the vapour reaching a low enough temperature. The flask achieves the same cooling directly by chilling the walls, so droplets form without any rising air, which is fully consistent with the cloud rule rather than contradicting it. Heating would not condense vapour, and ordinary chilled-wall droplets are liquid condensation, not ice.

**7. (B)** The orchard's advantage is transpiration: many trees pull water up from the soil and release it as vapour through their leaves, so a living canopy adds far more moisture to the air than bare gravel. Condensation would remove vapour from the air (turning it to liquid), not add it, so the pupil's reasoning is the wrong direction. Infiltration moves water downward into the soil, not upward into the air, and orchards do not conjure their own private rainfall. Only transpiration explains the moisture surplus.

**8. (D)** A loop starting at the sea must begin with evaporation: the parcel rises off the sea as vapour. Aloft it cools and condenses into a cloud. The cloud then releases the water as rain that lands on the hill. Finally the rain on the ground seeps downward through the soil toward the groundwater (infiltration). The only self-consistent order is rise, condense, rain, seep. Every other option places condensation or seeping before evaporation, which is impossible because the parcel cannot condense or infiltrate before it has first left the sea as vapour.

**9. (A)** Sleet, drizzle, hail and snow all share one thing: each delivers water from the atmosphere down to the ground, which is the definition of precipitation. Their differing states (solid hail and snow, liquid drizzle) and speeds are irrelevant to the classification. They are not evaporation, which moves water upward off the ground; they are not condensation, which is the cloud-forming step that precedes the fall, not the fall itself; and they are not transpiration, which is moisture released by plant leaves.

**10. (C)** The 71% figure is a surface-area statistic — how much of the planet's face is covered by water — and says nothing about quality. The 97% figure tells the real story of the total volume: almost all of it is salty ocean, leaving only a small fresh fraction, most of which is frozen or deep underground. The student's error is treating surface coverage as if it equalled usable supply. Surface coverage does not imply freshness, oceans are salty not drinkable, and the drinkable fraction is far below 71% — it is tiny.

**11. (A)** Scaling the real proportions to 1000 jugs: roughly 970 are salty ocean and only about 30 are fresh. Within those 30, the bulk is frozen in ice-caps and glaciers, so the readily usable groundwater and surface water is a small slice of an already small fraction. The 710 answer wrongly imports the surface-coverage percentage as if it described volume and freshness. A 500-500 split grossly overstates fresh water. And '970 fresh, 30 salty' simply inverts the true figures, since the oceans hold the overwhelming majority.

**12. (C)** The guide's first claim is correct: most of Earth's fresh water really is stored in ice-caps and glaciers. But the conclusion does not follow, because that ice is frozen and largely inaccessible, so it cannot be the everyday tap source. Day-to-day usable water is drawn mostly from groundwater and surface sources. Hence one half is true and one false. The options that call both halves true, both false, or reverse which half is true all misjudge either the storage fact or the accessibility fact.

**13. (B)** The oceans dominate every other store by far, so they top the list. Among the fresh stores, the chapter teaches that most fresh water is frozen in ice-caps and glaciers, placing them next, ahead of groundwater. River water flowing at any instant is a very small store and comes last. The correct descending order is oceans, ice/glaciers, groundwater, rivers. Putting rivers first inverts the scale entirely; the other wrong orders either rank groundwater above the ice or place the oceans below the ice, both of which contradict the chapter's proportions.

**14. (A)** Equal rainfall does not guarantee equal usable water, because availability also turns on how much water each district stores, how heavily it pumps, and how much rain infiltrates to recharge rather than runs off. District B may over-extract or pave its ground, draining its table despite the same rain. So the lesson is uneven distribution shaped by local management, not rainfall alone. The claim that equal rain forces equal water ignores withdrawal and recharge; population alone does not fix supply; and water is demonstrably not spread evenly everywhere.

**15. (A)** Compare shares: 17% of people but only 4% of the fresh water means each person's slice is well below the global average — many people dividing a much smaller resource. That mismatch is the textbook signature of water stress. A larger-than-average share would require more water per person, the opposite of these numbers. The shares are not equal, so they do not cancel. And population growth does not summon more water; it only divides the fixed share among still more people, worsening the stress.

**16. (B)** The water table is the upper level at which the pore spaces first become completely filled with water — exactly the 7 m mark here. The aquifer is the porous, water-bearing layer of rock or gravel that stores and yields that groundwater — the 7-to-40 m zone. So the level is the table and the layer is the aquifer. Swapping the two names confuses a single surface with a thick layer; 'infiltration depth' and 'spring line' are not the chapter's terms for these; and the water table is a level, not the whole layer.

**17. (B)** An aquifer must do two things: store water and let it move freely to a well, which requires connected pore space or cracks. The porous sandstone and the fractured limestone both qualify — they hold water and yield it readily. Solid granite is non-porous and stays dry, so it is not an aquifer; dense clay may hold water but barely lets it move, so it yields only a trickle and acts more as a barrier. Not every layer is an aquifer — only those that both store and transmit water do.

**18. (A)** Rain reaching the groundwater does so by infiltration: it seeps slowly downward through the soil pores until it joins the saturated zone and recharges the wells, which is why they rose. Condensation would form liquid from vapour on a surface, not move rain downward, so the pupil's idea is wrong. Evaporation moves water upward off the soil into the air, the reverse of recharge. Transpiration sends water out through leaves into the atmosphere, also upward and away, not down to the groundwater.

**19. (A)** Whether a well or spring holds water depends on its position relative to the water table. The spring discharges below the table all year, so it keeps flowing; the well's base lies above the lowered summer table, so it goes dry until the table rises again. Depth alone does not decide this — a deeper well would actually reach water longer, contradicting the bystander. Springs do not create water independently of the table, and the same rain that recharges one feeds the whole local table, not one spot selectively.

**20. (A)** The water table is not a dead-flat plane; it broadly mimics the land above, sitting closer to the surface under low spots and dropping deeper under high ground — which is exactly why hollows reach water shallowly and rises require deep wells. A perfectly flat table would make the depths equal, contradicting the observation. The pattern is the reverse of 'deepest under hollows', and the table is certainly not absent under the low areas — that is where it is shallowest.

**21. (B)** Depth changes where a well taps water, not how much water the aquifer holds. Reaching deeper does nothing to raise the table; if anything, enabling heavier pumping draws the table down faster. A deeper well does not push water upward across the area, it does not create or refill stored water, and depth has no power to desalinate groundwater. So by itself the switch reaches lower water and may worsen, never raise, the table.

**22. (D)** Balance is recharge minus withdrawal. At 60 in and 60 out the table held. Tripling withdrawal to 180 against an unchanged 60 recharge creates a 120-unit annual deficit, so the store loses 120 each year and the table falls steadily, the loss compounding as the aquifer empties. Pumping does not summon matching recharge — recharge depends on rain and infiltration, not on how hard you pump. Harder pumping does not pull extra rain down, and a system in permanent deficit does not self-correct back to the old level.

**23. (C)** Each year the store drops, so withdrawal must exceed the fixed recharge — outflow beats inflow. The losses grow (9, 12, 15), meaning the deficit itself is widening year on year, perhaps from more wells or harder pumping. If withdrawal were below recharge the store would rise; if it equalled recharge the store would stay flat; and zero withdrawal would let recharge build the store up, not draw it down. Only a widening withdrawal-over-recharge deficit produces an accelerating fall.

**24. (A)** Net change is recharge minus withdrawal. Town P: 80 minus 110 is a 30-unit deficit each year, so its table falls. Town Q: 110 minus 80 is a 30-unit surplus, so its table rises. The numbers being mirror images means the two tables move in opposite directions, not that they stay identical. Larger recharge fills, it does not 'disturb' and lower, a table, so Q does not fall. And pumping more does not raise a table — withdrawal lowers it, which is why P, the heavier pumper, declines.

**25. (C)** Recharge is fixed, but each new well adds to the total withdrawal, so the outflow rises year after year while the inflow does not. The withdrawal-minus-recharge gap therefore widens annually, driving the table steadily lower. It cannot narrow while withdrawal only grows; it cannot stay constant when wells keep being added; and adding wells does nothing to increase recharge, which depends on rainfall and infiltration, not on how many wells draw from the ground.

**26. (A)** If withdrawal is genuinely unchanged, a newly falling table must mean inflow has dropped. Paving over open ground blocks rain from infiltrating, slashing recharge, so the same steady pumping now exceeds the smaller inflow and the table declines — a textbook cause. There is no need to assume hidden over-pumping when reduced recharge fully explains it. Salinity does not lower a table by 'sinking', and inventing a simultaneous rainfall collapse is an unsupported extra assumption when paving alone accounts for the deficit.

**27. (B)** A long-full well going dry after years of nearby tube-well expansion points to chronic over-extraction lowering the whole area's water table until it sank beneath the well's base, so the well now reaches only dry ground. A single normal monsoon does not wreck an aquifer; if anything it recharges it. Water does not evaporate up through solid rock to empty a well, and turning salty would change the water's quality, not cause the well to hold no water at all.

**28. (A)** Drying shallow wells, deeper drilling and costlier pumping all flow from one underlying fact: the water table is falling. They are downstream symptoms, not the root cause, which is over-extraction outrunning recharge. Treating symptoms as causes leads to chasing the water deeper rather than fixing the imbalance. They are not the true cause, so deeper drilling only postpones the problem; they are tightly linked to the table, not coincidental; and they signal a falling, not a rising, table.

**29. (D)** Near a coast, fresh groundwater normally sits above or beside salty water. Heavy pumping lowers the fresh supply so much that the adjacent salty water encroaches toward the wells, turning them brackish. The cause is the lowered fresh table letting saline water move in. Pumping is a physical withdrawal, not a chemical reaction that creates salt; rain is fresh, not naturally briny; and concrete does not manufacture salt in soil. Only the inward movement of nearby salt water fits the chapter's reasoning.

**30. (B)** The root cause is withdrawal exceeding recharge, so the only true fix is to close that gap — pump less and recharge more — restoring balance to the table. Stronger pumps, deeper wells and imported bottled water all leave the imbalance untouched: the first two simply chase the falling water (a symptom) and accelerate depletion, while trucking water sidesteps the aquifer problem entirely without healing it. Only reducing extraction and raising recharge addresses the cause.

**31. (B)** The fastest fall needs minimal inflow and maximal outflow at once: scanty rain starves recharge, a booming population and new factories raise withdrawal, and concreting blocks what little rain falls from soaking in — every factor pushing the table down together. The other three all favour the table: heavy rain on pitted open soil boosts recharge; drip plus harvesting cuts withdrawal and lifts recharge; and trees, leak repair and open ground all help recharge and reduce waste, raising rather than lowering the table.

**32. (D)** Recharge depends on rain soaking into the ground. Plot M's open soil lets water infiltrate to the aquifer, so its table rises. Plot N's tiles seal the surface and its rain is piped to a storm drain that whisks it away, so almost none recharges — its table stays lower. Tiles block, not trap, infiltration; a storm drain removes water from the site rather than returning it to the aquifer; and sealed and open ground plainly do not recharge equally, which is the whole point.

**33. (D)** What matters for groundwater is not how much rain falls but how much infiltrates. Paving seals the surface, so rain that once soaked in now sheets off into drains and leaves the area, cutting recharge even though the rainfall total is unchanged — that is the planner's blind spot. Paving reduces, not increases, infiltration; it does not chemically salt the rain; and it certainly does affect recharge, since sealing the ground is precisely what stops the soaking-in that feeds the aquifer.

**34. (A)** Forests slow runoff and let rain soak in. Remove the trees and rain rushes off the bare slope instead of infiltrating, so less water recharges the valley aquifer and its table falls, starving the wells — all consistent with unchanged rainfall. Trees do not control whether soil water freezes, and clearing them does not make wells ice over. Deforestation does not stop rain from falling, only from soaking in. And it does not make rain salty; the issue is reduced infiltration, not contaminated water.

**35. (D)** Living trees and their litter slow runoff and encourage rain to infiltrate, which recharges the groundwater that feeds the valley well; removing them lets rain run off the tarmac instead, so recharge falls and the well struggles. Trees do not pump groundwater up to refill a well, nor do they manufacture new water in their roots — water is conserved, not created. And trees do not block rain from the soil; they help it soak in, so losing them hurts rather than helps the well.

**36. (C)** A recharge pit works by giving rooftop rain a place to seep slowly into the soil and reach the aquifer, adding to the groundwater — exactly the recharge a sealed drain prevents by sweeping the water off-site. The pit does not chemically convert rain; rain is already water, it just needs to infiltrate. It does not pump water upward; it sends water downward. And it does not rely on heating water to sink — infiltration through gravel and sand, not temperature, drives the recharge.

**37. (A)** These traditional structures share a single purpose: capturing and conserving water — storing rain and recharging groundwater — so communities had water during dry months. They were not built to generate hydroelectric power, which is unrelated to their design and scale; they were not desalination plants, since inland villages dealt with rain and groundwater, not sea water; and they were storage and recharge works, not rain gauges for measuring rainfall.

**38. (D)** A johad is a check-dam: by detaining the stream's flow it gives the water time to infiltrate the streambed and surrounding soil, recharging the aquifer and raising the table. It is not a pump and lifts no water to the surface; sealing the bed would do the opposite of recharge by preventing infiltration; and spreading water thin to evaporate it would lose the water to the air rather than send it underground. Detention for infiltration is the mechanism.

**39. (C)** Drip irrigation targets water precisely at each plant's root zone, so almost none is lost to runoff or evaporation from bare ground, and the field uses far less water than flooding — relieving pressure on the groundwater. It does not use more water than flooding; that is backwards, since flooding is the wasteful method. Crops still need some water, so 'none at all' is wrong. And dripping water onto crop roots does not salinate the surrounding groundwater.

**40. (C)** Drip delivers water only to the crop's roots, leaving the inter-row strips dry, so weeds there get little water and grow poorly — fewer weeds as a free bonus of targeted watering. The drip water is plain water, not a chemical weedkiller; drip releases small drops slowly and does not flood or wash anything away (that would be the wasteful method it replaces); and drip does not freeze the soil. The weed suppression is simply a consequence of keeping the gaps dry.

**41. (D)** Drip delivers water precisely to each pot's roots with minimal loss, so it wastes the least and best meets the save-water goal. Flooding the bench soaks the whole surface, wasting water on everything but the roots; an all-afternoon sprinkler loses huge amounts to evaporation and to gaps between pots; and hosing the leaves wets foliage and surroundings rather than roots, again wasting water. Only the drip line concentrates the scarce water exactly where the plants need it.

**42. (B)** Cities are covered in roofs and paving, so rain that lands on them cannot infiltrate and instead pours into drains and is lost; harvesting captures that runoff and routes it to recharge or storage, which is why it is promoted so strongly there. Cities do not inherently get more rain than villages; their groundwater is finite, not endless, which is exactly why harvesting matters; and harvesting is not limited to soil-less places — it is valuable precisely where sealed surfaces would otherwise waste the rain.

**43. (C)** To slow a falling table you must raise recharge or cut withdrawal; mandating harvesting recharge structures directly increases the rain returning to the aquifer, making it the reliable choice. More bore wells only add to withdrawal and pull the table down faster. Tiling open ground seals the very surfaces through which rain recharges, worsening the fall. And rushing storm water out of the city throws away the rain that could have soaked in. Only the recharge requirement helps.

**44. (C)** Targeting the biggest user yields the biggest savings: irrigation at 65% dwarfs every other category, and switching from flood to drip can cut that use sharply, easing the stress most. Drinking and cooking at 5% is the smallest slice, so squeezing it saves little. Industry at 8% is far from the largest, so calling it the biggest is simply misreading the table. And claiming nothing can be reduced ignores the well-known water savings drip irrigation offers on the dominant irrigation use.

**45. (C)** A natural floodplain absorbs monsoon water, both easing floods and recharging groundwater. Concreting it seals the surface, so during the rains water cannot soak in and instead pools and floods, while afterwards little has infiltrated, so recharge drops — a damaging twin effect. The reverse pairing (less flooding, more recharge) describes the unpaved floodplain. Sealing the surface cannot leave flooding and recharge unchanged, and it cannot increase recharge while also flooding, since blocked infiltration is the common cause of both.

**46. (B)** The two measures push the balance in the same favourable direction: harvesting raises recharge and drip lowers withdrawal, so inflow now exceeds outflow and the surplus lifts the table. The skeptic has it backwards — these measures add water and save water, they do not drain the aquifer. Recharge and withdrawal are not forced to match; here they are deliberately unbalanced toward surplus. And the rise comes from keeping the measures running, not from removing them, which would simply undo the surplus.

**47. (A)** With soil and rainfall held equal, the only difference is the irrigation method, so the longer-lasting well on Farm B must come from drip's lower water use, which drew down the shared groundwater more slowly. Flood irrigation is the wasteful method, so it conserves less, not more, than drip. The method clearly does affect well life here, contradicting the 'no bearing' option. And the rainfall was stipulated identical, so Farm B cannot have received more rain — the method is the deciding factor.

**48. (D)** Individually a drip seems trivial, but dozens of taps summing to 30 litres a day is a serious cumulative loss of scarce fresh water, so the right move is to repair the leaks promptly. Ignoring them lets the daily waste continue; opening taps fully wastes far more water, not less; and parking a bucket under each tap merely collects the loss without stopping it and still squanders the running water. Fixing the leaks is the only response that conserves.

**49. (D)** Each action in the correct set saves water: a bucket-and-mug bath uses far less than a shower, closing the tap while brushing stops clean water running away, and mending leaks ends a steady loss. The other sets are all the opposite — long showers, running taps and full bathtubs waste water; paving, flood-irrigation and dripping taps waste water and harm recharge; and draining roof rain away, over-watering and running hoses all squander it. Only the first set conserves throughout.

**50. (C)** Normal rainfall rules out a recharge failure, so the steady fall must come from rising withdrawal — exactly what the growing number of tube wells delivers — outpacing the recharge and depleting storage year on year. If recharge had exceeded withdrawal the level would rise, contradicting the data. Rainfall was normal, not absent, so a total rain failure is false. And reservoir water does not simply vanish into vapour at a rate that explains a multi-year decline driven by heavy pumping.

**51. (C)** A working recharge programme raises the water table, so the direct evidence is dried-up wells reviving and water being reached at shallower depths — the table has risen. A less salty ocean is unrelated to local groundwater recharge. Doubled monsoon rainfall would be a change in weather, not proof that recharge structures are working, and it is outside human control anyway. Rivers ceasing to flow would suggest worsening water stress, the opposite of success. Only the reviving, shallower wells show recharge succeeding.

**52. (A)** Tube wells do not make water; they only extract it from a finite aquifer recharged at a limited rate. Doubling the wells doubles the rate of withdrawal, and if that exceeds recharge the table falls and the wells eventually run dry — so the supply is not doubled permanently but undermined. The aquifer is limited, not unlimited, which is the whole reason for the flaw. And wells certainly do not add rain to the sky to refill themselves; that reverses cause and effect entirely.

**53. (C)** All four measures push the balance toward surplus: harvesting and trees raise recharge, while drip and leak repair cut withdrawal and waste, so inflow exceeds outflow and the table rises. More structures here means more water saved and recharged, not more used, so the table does not fall. None of these measures introduces salt, so the water does not turn salty. And measures that genuinely change recharge and withdrawal must move the table, so it cannot stay forever unchanged.

**54. (C)** Identical rainfall is only the inflow potential; the resulting table depends on how much each village pumps out and how much rain it actually lets soak in rather than run off — differences that can produce very different tables. Equal rain does not force equal tables, because withdrawal and infiltration vary. Concrete lowers rather than raises a table by blocking infiltration, so 'more concrete raises it' is wrong. And digging deeper merely reaches lower water; it never raises the table.

**55. (A)** Plants depend on water for their life processes, so depriving them stunts growth and cuts the harvest — a direct loss of yield. Less water does not speed growth or boost yield; that is the opposite of how plants respond to drought. Plants cannot turn absent water into vapour to thrive; you cannot transpire water you do not have. And a shortage reduces the size and health of the crop rather than altering its saltiness, so the yield falls.

**56. (D)** Sum the losses: X drops 10+10+10 = 30 units with a constant yearly deficit, while Y drops 5+10+20 = 35 units with a deficit that grows each year. So Y has fallen further and is accelerating, signalling a widening withdrawal-over-recharge gap, whereas X's gap is steady. A steady loss does not exceed Y's accelerating one here — 30 is less than 35. The totals are not equal. And Y's growing yearly figures are losses, not additions, so Y is falling, not rising.

**57. (D)** Rising vapour is just one half of a loop: it cools, condenses into clouds, and returns to the surface as rain or snow, so it is not lost but recirculated, and Earth's total water holds roughly steady as the same water changes form and location. The vapour does not escape forever, the planet is not drying out, vapour is not destroyed in the air, and it does not transmute into some new material — it remains water and comes back down, which is the essence of the water cycle.

**58. (C)** Within the water cycle the global total is essentially fixed; no rooftop device creates water. At best such a unit is rainwater harvesting — it captures rain that already fell and channels it to storage or recharge, redistributing existing water rather than making more. It cannot manufacture new water against the cycle's conservation, it is not a desalinator turning sea breeze fresh, and it certainly does not destroy rain. Its only honest function is collecting and redirecting what is already there.

**59. (D)** World Water Day is observed on 22 March, the date set aside to focus attention on valuing and conserving fresh water. The 5 June date is World Environment Day, a broader environmental observance; 22 April is Earth Day; and 14 November is celebrated in India as Children's Day. None of these is the water-themed day, so 22 March is the correct date for World Water Day.

**60. (B)** The chapter's core message is that although Earth holds a great deal of water, the fresh water we can actually use is a tiny, scarce fraction, so it must be used wisely and managed with care. Treating water as unlimited contradicts that scarcity. Conservation is everyone's responsibility, not just farmers', since all living things depend on the shared supply. And the sea is salty and unfit for drinking or most crops, so it cannot be relied on to supply everyone's needs.
