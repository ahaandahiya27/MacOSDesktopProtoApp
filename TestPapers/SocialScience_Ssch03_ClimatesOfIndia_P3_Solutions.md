## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** Weather is the atmospheric condition of a place over a short stretch such as an afternoon, while climate is the average pattern built up over many years. The headline describes one afternoon's showers, so weather is the right word there; the textbook describes a steady long-term average, so climate is right there. The lure that swaps the two ignores the time-scale that separates them, and the idea that a single rainy afternoon equals climate confuses one event with a thirty-year average.

**2. (C)** Climate is the average atmospheric behaviour of a place taken over a long period, typically decades, so no single day's weather can alter it. Riya has mistaken a one-day event (weather) for the long-run pattern (climate). The claims that one rainy day or a heavier downpour could shift the climate keep the same time-scale error, and saying the two words are identical ignores that the only real difference between them is the length of time observed.

**3. (D)** Latitude controls how directly sunlight strikes a place and how much that directness changes through the year. Near the Equator the Sun stays nearly overhead all year, giving an even climate; farther north the angle swings widely between summer and winter, giving Delhi its big seasonal range. Altitude is not the driver here, the Equator is not meaningfully nearer the Sun (the difference is trivial against 150 million km), and Delhi certainly has an atmosphere.

**4. (D)** Earth is so far from the Sun that the tiny difference in distance between Equator and poles is negligible; what matters is the angle of the rays. At the Equator rays strike nearly straight down, concentrating heat on a small patch, while near the poles they slant and spread thinly over a wider area (and travel through more air). The "closer to the Sun" and "no air" answers are the classic misconceptions, and Earth's internal heat plays no role in surface climate here.

**5. (C)** As you climb, the air thins and holds heat less well, and you move away from the ground, which is the air's main heat source; so temperature drops with altitude. Town X, much higher, is therefore cooler despite the shared latitude. "Higher is nearer the Sun" repeats the equatorial-heat fallacy, sharing latitude does not erase the height difference, and altitude not latitude is what separates these two towns.

**6. (D)** Since both share a latitude, that factor is ruled out; the deciding factor is altitude. The peaks rise kilometres higher, where thin air and distance from the ground keep temperatures freezing year-round, while the low plains stay hot. The other options misattribute the difference to latitude, to the sea (the plains are not nearer the moderating sea than the peaks in any decisive way), or to imaginary equatorial winds.

**7. (D)** Water heats and cools far more slowly than land, so coastal places have their extremes softened, giving a small temperature range; this is continentality's mirror image. Chennai is coastal and Hyderabad is inland, so Chennai's range is smaller. Altitude is not the key (Chennai is low, not high), the two share a latitude so nearness to the Equator is not the cause, and Chennai obviously gets summer sunshine.

**8. (A)** Distance from the sea removes the ocean's smoothing effect, so land heats and cools sharply, widening the range; this is the heart of continentality. The first wrong option describes the opposite (sea + low land = small range), the equatorial option would give an even climate not a wide swing, and constant moist sea winds would moderate rather than widen the range.

**9. (D)** The monsoon is driven by the unequal heating of land and sea: land warms quickly in summer to form low pressure, drawing in moist sea air. The Moon's pull governs tides, not monsoon winds; rivers do not power the system; and a fixed equatorial temperature has nothing to do with the seasonal land-sea pressure flip that creates the monsoon.

**10. (C)** Air rushes from high pressure toward low pressure. The hot summer land forms a low-pressure zone, so the relatively cooler, high-pressure sea air flows inland and brings moisture; this is the south-west summer monsoon. High pressure over the land would push air out (the winter case), and the option pairing low pressure with outward dry winds reverses the direction air actually moves.

**11. (A)** Winds are named for the direction they come from; the summer rains are carried by winds arriving from the south-west, hence "south-west monsoon." It does not name a region the rain avoids, river flow direction, or the Sun's setting point. Naming by source direction is the same convention used for the north-east winter monsoon.

**12. (D)** The summer monsoon blows from sea to land, soaking up moisture over warm oceans and releasing it as rain; the winter monsoon blows from land to sea, so it is dry. The wrong options reverse the source of each wind, wrongly tie rain to coldness, or imagine rain dropping vertically from space rather than being carried in by moist sea air.

**13. (A)** The "burst" marks the arrival of the rain-bearing south-west winds, which switch a parched, scorching landscape to days of heavy downpour quite abruptly. The Sun does not stop shining, rivers do not evaporate upward to make the rain, and the burst is a summer (south-west) event, not the north-east winter monsoon arriving.

**14. (A)** The south-west monsoon enters at the southern tip in early June and progresses northward, blanketing the country by roughly mid-July. It does not march from north to south, it does not move from the land out to the seas, and it does not arrive everywhere simultaneously because it takes weeks to advance across India's length.

**15. (B)** The monsoon exists only because land heats and cools faster than the sea, creating the pressure differences that drive the winds. Erase that difference and there is no pressure gradient, so no monsoon. It cannot strengthen, shift to winter, or merely reverse, because all of those still require the very land-sea contrast that has been removed.

**16. (B)** Wind is air flowing down the pressure gradient, from high pressure to low pressure. The hot summer interior is low pressure, so the higher-pressure sea air flows in. Air does not move from low to high pressure, is not fixed to a north-south path, and does not simply chase the overhead Sun independent of pressure.

**17. (D)** In winter the land cools faster than the sea, becoming a high-pressure zone, while the relatively warmer sea is lower pressure; air therefore flows from land to sea, giving the dry north-east monsoon. The other options reverse the land-sea pressure roles or deny the pressure difference entirely, none of which would produce the observed offshore winter winds.

**18. (B)** Although offshore winds are mostly dry, those that travel over the Bay of Bengal absorb moisture and then shed it on the south-east coast as winter rain. A desert cannot supply moisture, the Himalayas lie to the north not in this wind's path to the south-east coast, and the Arabian Sea is on the west, not the source for the north-east winter flow reaching Tamil Nadu.

**19. (C)** In summer the land is hotter (low pressure) and pulls sea air in; in winter the land is colder (high pressure) and pushes air out. This seasonal flip of the land-sea temperature and pressure pattern reverses the winds. Earth's tilt is fixed, mountains do not move, and the Moon's orbit is irrelevant to the monsoon.

**20. (B)** The retreating (autumn) season is when the south-west monsoon weakens and pulls back from the country, bridging the rainy season and the dry winter. It is not the burst (the arrival), it has nothing to do with rivers moving, and it follows rather than precedes the rains, so it is not the pre-monsoon heat build-up.

**21. (A)** The Indian year runs from the cool dry winter, into the hot dry pre-monsoon summer, into the rainy south-west monsoon when the burst arrives, and finally the retreating monsoon (autumn) as the rains withdraw before winter returns. The other orders break this cycle by placing the rains or the retreat out of their natural sequence.

**22. (A)** The monsoon so thoroughly governs Indian weather, farming and life that the rainy season stands out as its own distinct season, giving the familiar "fifth season." It was not borrowed from Europe (the reverse, India recognises the monsoon more finely), India does have a winter, and festivals follow the seasons rather than creating them.

**23. (D)** The six-ṛitu scheme reads the monsoon-dominated Indian year in finer detail, recognising transitions the four-season model lumps together. It is ancient rather than recent, it is built around the monsoon rather than ignoring it, and six categories cannot be a mere renaming of four.

**24. (D)** Seasons recur because Earth completes one revolution around the Sun each year while tilted on its axis, so each part of the year repeats. The Sun's output does not rise and fall seasonally, festival dates follow the seasons rather than commanding the winds, and river floods are a result of the seasons, not their cause.

**25. (C)** Winds carry the qualities of the land they cross; the loo passes over hot, dry deserts and arrives blistering and parched. Had it crossed the sea it would be moist, not dry; coming off the Himalayas it would be cold, not hot; and it is a surface wind, not heated by underground springs.

**26. (C)** Winds bring the character of wherever they have been; cold air off the snowy mountains arrives cold and chills the plains. Winds cannot change a place's latitude, raise its altitude, or relocate it nearer the sea; they simply transport the heat or cold and moisture they picked up en route.

**27. (B)** A microclimate is a small area whose conditions differ from the surroundings, like a shady, moist forest patch. A rain shadow is the dry leeward side of a mountain (a different, larger phenomenon), a ṛitu is a season, and a monsoon is a seasonal wind system, none of which describes this small local pocket.

**28. (A)** Buildings and pavements store and release heat, creating a warm "urban heat island," a microclimate produced by human activity. It is not a rain shadow (which is a wind-and-relief effect on the dry side of mountains), not a season, and not a cyclone; it is simply a small, locally altered climate.

**29. (C)** Relief, the land's physical shape, both exposes the flat Thar to hot dry winds and lets the Himalayas block cold northern winds; this is topography at work. Latitude, sea distance and the Sun's position do not explain why a mountain wall shields one region while an open plain is exposed; the deciding factor here is the lay of the land.

**30. (B)** Ladakh-type areas sit in the rain shadow of the Himalayas, so they receive little rain (a desert), and their high altitude makes them bitterly cold; hence "cold desert." It is not a hot desert, its cold comes mainly from altitude rather than nearness to the pole, and far from being near the sea, its dryness comes from the mountain barrier cutting off moist winds.

**31. (D)** When moisture-laden monsoon winds are pushed up against the north-eastern hills, they cool, condense and unleash extraordinary rainfall, classic orographic rain. The Thar supplies dryness not rain, descending leeward air warms and dries (it does not rain), and these are warm moist summer winds, not cold Himalayan winter winds.

**32. (A)** The Western Ghats lift the incoming moist winds; the air cools and rains on the seaward (windward) slopes, so by the time it crosses to the Deccan it is dry, a rain shadow. The Deccan is not nearer the Equator, the coast is not higher than the Ghats, and rivers do not generate rainfall, so relief is the true cause.

**33. (A)** Rising air expands and cools; cool air holds less water vapour, so the excess condenses into cloud and rain on the windward slope. Rising air does not warm or hold more vapour, it does not dry without cooling, and air that has risen moves over the crest and sinks (warming and drying) on the far side, not back down the windward face.

**34. (C)** By the time air crosses the crest it has shed most of its moisture as windward rain, so the leeward side lies in a "shadow" of rain, dry. It has nothing to do with literal sunlight shadows or shadiness, and the leeward side is the one facing away from the moist wind, not into it.

**35. (B)** The heavy west-coast rain comes from moist winds being forced up the Ghats, cooling and condensing; remove the barrier and the winds would sweep inland without rising sharply, so the coast would get less rain. It would not get more rain (the lifting is what wrings out the water), would not freeze, and certainly would not stay unchanged.

**36. (A)** Srinagar is both far north (high latitude, weaker, more slanting sunlight) and high in the mountains (low temperatures with altitude), and these stack up to make it cold. It is not at a lower latitude, it is nowhere near the Equator, and it is not bathed in moderating sea winds, so the correct pair is latitude plus altitude.

**37. (B)** Mumbai is coastal and Nagpur is inland, so the slow-changing sea evens out Mumbai's temperatures, giving milder extremes; both statements are true and the reason is exactly why. The other choices wrongly sever the link or deny one statement, but the sea's slow heating is precisely the mechanism behind Mumbai's gentler climate.

**38. (D)** Near the Equator the Sun's overhead position barely changes through the year, so heating stays steady and the seasonal temperature change is small; the reason genuinely explains the assertion. The distractors either break the true causal link or wrongly deny one of the statements, but here both are true and connected.

**39. (B)** It is true that the winter monsoon is mostly dry, but the stated reason is backwards: winter winds blow from the cold high-pressure land toward the sea, which is exactly why they are dry. So the assertion is true while the reason is false, and the options that accept the reason or deny the dry-winter fact are wrong.

**40. (A)** With latitude held equal, the remaining controlling factors are altitude and exposure to the sea's moderating influence, so those are the sensible things to check. Distance to the Sun and the Moon's path do not vary meaningfully between two nearby cities, and festivals have no effect on temperature range.

**41. (C)** The pattern, drenched windward coasts and hills with drier leeward interiors, is explained by moist monsoon winds being forced up over relief and raining there, then arriving drier beyond. Rainfall does not simply track latitude, height alone does not guarantee rain (it depends on windward exposure), and inland is generally drier, not wetter, so relief plus wind direction is the unifying idea.

**42. (B)** Most Indian farming depends on monsoon rain, so a failed monsoon dries the soil, shrinks harvests and pushes food prices up, sometimes forcing migration. A failed (weak) monsoon means less water, so floods and bumper harvests are the opposite of what happens, and it neither warms winters nor erases the seasons.

**43. (C)** When the periodic Pacific warming (El Niño) shifts winds and weakens the monsoon, India tends to get less rain, hurting crops and water supplies. A weakened monsoon brings shortfall, not record floods; it does not alter mountain heights; and it has nothing to do with whether the sea moderates the coast.

**44. (C)** Indian agriculture relies heavily on the monsoon, so when the rains fail, crops fail, food becomes scarce and dear, and rural incomes fall, all of which strain the economy. Festivals are not legally cancelled, the sea's moderating role does not switch off, and the Himalayas do not block trade because of a dry year.

**45. (A)** El Niño is the periodic warming of Pacific waters that can shift winds and weaken the Indian monsoon. The loo is a hot summer land wind, a rain shadow is the dry leeward side of mountains, and the burst is the sudden onset of monsoon rain, none of which is the Pacific warming described.

**46. (B)** A sea breeze forms when sun-warmed land creates low pressure and pulls in cooler sea air, exactly the land-sea heating contrast that powers the monsoon, just on a country-sized scale. A rolling ball, the Moon's tides and a flowing river involve different forces and have nothing to do with land-sea pressure differences.

**47. (B)** These harvest and seasonal festivals fall when crops are sown or gathered, showing how the monsoon-driven climate steers farming and hence cultural life. Festivals do not command the rains, climate clearly does shape culture (that is the very point), and the festivals follow the farming calendar rather than mere latitude.

**48. (A)** Mausim means "season," and "monsoon" properly names the seasonally reversing winds; the rain is a consequence of those winds, not the word's meaning. It does not name the rain by itself, nor the ocean, nor the six ṛitus, so the seasonally shifting winds are the correct sense.

**49. (B)** A cyclone is built around an intense low-pressure centre into which surrounding air and moisture spiral, feeding the storm. High pressure would push air out (the opposite of a cyclone), equal pressure produces no winds at all, and cyclones form over warm seas, not from winter cold over land.

**50. (D)** The calm, clear "eye" at a cyclone's centre is deceptive; once it passes, the violent winds of the far eyewall strike from the opposite side, so people must stay sheltered. The lull does not mean the storm is over or harmless, and although the calm eye feels safe, the dangerous part is the surrounding eyewall, not the eye itself.

**51. (C)** The fiercest winds whirl in the eyewall, the thick cloud band ringing the calm eye. The eye itself is deceptively still, the violent winds are within the storm rather than far outside it, and they certainly do not appear after the cyclone has gone.

**52. (A)** Greenhouse gases trap heat; adding more from burning fuels thickens the blanket and traps even more, warming the Earth. They do not let heat escape (that would cool it), they do not mainly block incoming sunlight, and they do not remove the atmosphere, so added greenhouse gas means more trapped heat and warming.

**53. (B)** Burning coal releases carbon dioxide, which traps extra heat, warming and shifting the climate, which can disrupt the monsoon and cause crops to fail; that is the correct chain. The alternatives go wrong by cooling the sea, freezing rivers or growing forests, none of which follows from burning coal, which adds greenhouse gas and warms.

**54. (D)** Mitigation tackles the cause by reducing greenhouse-gas emissions and slowing warming, while resilience prepares communities to cope with impacts like floods or droughts. The first wrong option swaps the two definitions, the second over-narrows both to tree-planting, and the third mischaracterises each as ignoring half the problem.

**55. (B)** Mitigation reduces the greenhouse gases that drive warming, so switching to renewable energy and planting trees (which absorb carbon dioxide) qualify. Clearing forests, burning more coal and wasteful consumption all add greenhouse gases, making the problem worse rather than mitigating it.

**56. (D)** The rapid warming since the industrial era is driven mainly by human activity, above all burning fossil fuels, which adds greenhouse gases. Natural processes and occasional volcanoes have not caused this fast rise, and the Earth is not drifting away from the Sun, so human emissions are the principal cause.

**57. (C)** Without the natural greenhouse effect, Earth would be far too cold; greenhouse gases trap part of the Sun's heat and keep the planet comfortably warm. They do not block all sunlight or freeze the planet (the opposite of warming), and they do not directly create the monsoon, which arises from land-sea heating.

**58. (D)** Air forced up the windward slope cools and rains there, then descends dry on the leeward side, so a short crossing can take you from wet to dry, the rain shadow. A few kilometres cannot change latitude or nearness to the Equator meaningfully, and soil type does not control rainfall, so relief and wind direction are the cause.

**59. (A)** Because India stretches across many degrees of latitude, from sea level to the high Himalayas, and from coast to deep interior, its controlling factors vary widely, producing many climates at once. Calendars do not cause climate, the Sun does not single out states, and weather is not controlled by political boundaries; geographic variety is the real reason.

**60. (C)** Ladakh is a cold desert because of high altitude plus the Himalayan rain shadow; Mumbai's mild range comes from the moderating sea; Cherrapunji's deluge comes from relief forcing moist winds to rise; and Kanniyakumari stays warm because of its low (near-equatorial) latitude. The other matchings misassign these, for instance crediting Ladakh's cold to low latitude or Mumbai's mildness to altitude, which contradicts each region's true controlling factor.
