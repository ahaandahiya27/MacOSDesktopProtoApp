Instructions: Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Worked Solutions

1. (D) Barter is the direct swap of goods or services for other goods or services, with no money. Using coins, cards or bank accounts all involve money, so none is barter.

2. (A) Swapping a pencil for an eraser, with no money, is barter. Paying rupees, using UPI, or saving money all involve money, so they are not barter.

3. (A) Barter means no money is used - goods for goods. The moment money is the medium it is no longer barter, so shopping with money, modern shops and net banking are not barter.

4. (B) Commodities like cowrie shells, salt, cattle and cloth acted as money before coins. Cards, notes and UPI are far later inventions.

5. (D) Giant stone discs served as money in one society, showing how varied early 'money' was. They were not law tablets, cart wheels, or weapons.

6. (A) Commodities are tradable goods. Gold coins are one kind of money, digital payments are a transfer method, and the study of coins is numismatics.

7. (B) The Junbeel Mela shows barter surviving in pockets even now. It does not prove barter is gone, that money was never invented, or that only digital money exists.

8. (C) Barter needs a double coincidence of wants - each must have what the other wants and want what the other has. It is not about two coins, kinship, or double-counting.

9. (B) The trade fails because there is no double coincidence of wants - the shoemaker doesn't want wheat. Inflation, portability and legal tender are different issues.

10. (D) Since everyone accepts money, you can sell to anyone and buy from anyone without matching exact wants. It does not magically divide goods, force wants, or end trade.

11. (D) Without a common measure, comparing the worth of an ox and bags of wheat was hard. Carrying, spoiling and splitting are the separate portability, durability and divisibility problems.

12. (A) A common measure lets people compare values on one agreed scale. It does not equalise weights, force shared wants, or change goods into shells.

13. (A) Divisibility is being splittable into parts. Easy carrying is portability, lasting is durability, and being legally accepted is legal tender.

14. (B) Portability is being easy to carry. Lasting is durability, splitting is divisibility, and government backing is fiat or legal tender.

15. (A) Durability is lasting and being storable. Splitting is divisibility, easy carrying is portability, and money need not be gold.

16. (B) An ox can't be split for small purchases, is hard to carry, and may sicken or die - failing divisibility, portability and durability. It is not easy to split, weightless, or legal tender.

17. (C) Money is the commonly accepted tool for payments. It need not be metal, is not only paper notes, and direct goods-for-goods swapping is barter, not money.

18. (D) Money was invented to overcome barter's problems (double coincidence, no common measure, poor divisibility, portability and durability). It replaced barter rather than merely improving it, and did not end trade or goods.

19. (A) Barter, swapping goods for goods, came first; money was invented afterwards. So money is not older than barter, did not predate people, and did not begin alongside barter.

20. (B) Spending money to buy goods is its medium-of-exchange function. Saving it is store of value, paying later is deferred payment, and pricing goods is the measure function.

21. (C) Keeping money for future use is the store-of-value function. Spending it is medium of exchange, pricing is measure of value, and double coincidence is a barter problem.

22. (D) Expressing worth in rupees so goods can be compared is the measure-of-value function. Saving is store of value, paying later is deferred payment, and buying is medium of exchange.

23. (D) Settling future debts in money is the standard-of-deferred-payment function. Buying now is medium of exchange, pricing is measure of value, and saving is store of value.

24. (C) Money's four functions are medium of exchange, store of value, measure of value and standard of deferred payment. The other lists name forms of money, commodities, or barter problems.

25. (A) Unlike a living ox, money does not die or rot and is easy to store and split, making it a better store of value. It cannot be eaten, does not grow, and cannot plough.

26. (C) Karshapanas bore stamped 'rupa' symbols. QR codes, bank logos and watermarks are features of much later money.

27. (B) Rupee derives from rupya, 'wrought (stamped) silver'. It is not from paper, shells, or 'quick response' (which is QR).

28. (D) Early Indian coins were stamped metal like silver. Paper, plastic cards and shells alone came at other points in the story.

29. (B) Minting is making coins (a mint is the facility). Spending is not minting, rising prices are inflation, and carrying is portability.

30. (A) An alloy mixes metals to give coins strength and durability. It does not guarantee gold value, make coins edible, or turn them to paper.

31. (A) A coin's faces are the obverse ('heads') and reverse ('tails'). The other pairs name money functions, exchange types, or ancient coin terms.

32. (D) The obverse is the main 'heads' side; the reverse is the 'tails' back. The edge and any central hole are not the obverse.

33. (C) Numismatics (Greek nomisma, 'coin') is the study of coins and currency and their history. Printing notes, barter and inflation are different things.

34. (D) Studying coins for history is numismatics. Minting is making coins, bartering is swapping goods, and inflation control is economic policy.

35. (D) Paper currency was first used in China. It reached India only in the late 18th century; Rome and Britain were not its origin.

36. (C) Paper money came to India in the late 18th century. It did not exist in Vedic or Gupta times, and 1950 is the year the Constitution took effect.

37. (D) Only the Reserve Bank of India issues India's paper currency. Shopkeepers, individual states and the UN do not.

38. (C) A note's value rests on universal acceptance and RBI backing - trust, not the material. The paper is not precious, the ink is not worth 500 rupees, and notes are not food.

39. (B) Modern money is valuable because people trust it and the government backs it, not because of the material. Paper has no gold, and weight and colour do not set value.

40. (A) Fiat money (Latin fiat, 'let it be done') is valuable because the government makes it legal tender and people trust it, not because the material is precious.

41. (C) Legal tender must legally be accepted for payments in a country. It cannot be freely refused, is not abroad-only, and need not be gold.

42. (B) Modern money is fiat - its value comes from trust and legal-tender status, not precious metal. Coins are not solid gold, money needs no precious metal, and barter is not the only 'real' exchange.

43. (B) Early coins held value through their precious metal; modern money is fiat, resting on trust and legal-tender status. The reversal, the digital claim and the 'never accepted' claim are all wrong.

44. (C) Fiat money depends on continued trust and legal-tender status. It does not rely on gold content, a barter ban, or unchanging prices.

45. (A) Inflation (Latin inflare, 'to inflate') is a general rise in prices, reducing each rupee's buying power. Falling prices, minting and numismatics are different.

46. (D) A general rise in price over time is inflation. It is not barter, a fall to zero, or a barter-matching problem.

47. (A) Under inflation each rupee buys less, so the same money buys fewer goods. It does not buy more, become gold, or lose legal-tender status.

48. (D) Digital money is intangible, electronic money moved between accounts. It is not a new metal coin, a paper note, or shells.

49. (C) A QR ('quick-response') code is a scannable square pattern carrying the receiver's account details. It is not a coin, a metal disc, or a cheque.

50. (D) UPI, cards and QR payments move the same rupees electronically - only the transfer method changes, not the money. No new currency is created, no gold replaces rupees, and it is not barter.

51. (A) A QR payment moves rupees electronically - digital money. It is not barter (money is used), nor minting or printing.

52. (C) Digital payments change how money moves, not what the money is - the rupees are the same. They are not a new currency, barter, or commodity money.

53. (C) Money evolved from barter to commodity money to metal coins to paper currency to digital money. The other orders scramble this timeline.

54. (B) Commodity money preceded metal coins in the evolution of money. It did not come after, alongside, or permanently replace coins.

55. (C) Coins gave a portable, divisible, durable, accepted measure - fixing barter's flaws. They did not provide bigger animals, end transactions, or revive barter.

56. (C) Money evolved from direct barter toward ever more convenient, trusted means of exchange (coins, paper, digital). The other options reverse the direction of this evolution.

57. (B) A transaction is an act of business such as buying or selling. The study of coins is numismatics, rising prices is inflation, and an alloy is a metal mixture.

58. (B) Currency is a country's money system; a denomination is a specific unit such as 10 or 100 rupees. The other options reverse or confuse these.

59. (B) Paper money began in China and 'rupee' comes from rupya. Paper money did not start in India, 'rupee' is not Greek, digital money is recent, and coins came after barter.

60. (A) Modern money is fiat - valued by trust and legal-tender status, not gold - and UPI merely transfers the same rupees electronically. The other options repeat or compound the misconceptions.
