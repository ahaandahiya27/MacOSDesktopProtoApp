## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** The three terms answer three separate questions and all hold together. Effort per unit of land is high (much labour and water on one hectare), so she farms intensively; her purpose is to feed the family rather than to sell, so it is subsistence; and she waters artificially from a tube well, so it is irrigated. The option limiting her to one label is wrong because the axes are independent and stack. Treating purpose as the only valid axis ignores the effort and water descriptions, which are equally real. Saying no label applies because she sells nothing confuses subsistence (a recognised category) with being outside farming.

**2. (A)** A applies thin effort over much land (extensive) and sells the grain (commercial); B concentrates effort on a small plot (intensive) and eats the harvest (subsistence). The option swapping the effort labels reverses high-versus-low effort per area. The option swapping the purpose labels gives the seller subsistence and the eater commercial, which inverts who sells. Calling both commercial ignores that B sells nothing, and merely producing a crop never settles purpose.

**3. (B)** Subsistence versus commercial is decided by purpose, and purpose can change year to year while the land stays identical, which fits a sequence of mixed labels. Invoking a changing soil type contradicts the statement that soil never changed and confuses soil with purpose. The season axis (kharif/rabi) is separate from purpose and would not flip the subsistence/commercial label. Claiming the two terms are identical is false, since one means growing to eat and the other to sell.

**4. (B)** The day-labourer supplies the hired labour Indian agriculture leans on, and the flower-and-vegetable seller grows horticultural crops for the market, which is commercial horticulture. Calling the labourer a subsistence farmer is wrong because she keeps no crop and feeds no household from it, and the relative sells, so she is not subsistence either. Calling the labourer a commercial farmer is wrong because she owns no crop to sell, and the relative grows no cereals. Treating both as outside agriculture ignores that labour and horticulture are squarely inside farming.

**5. (A)** Commercial farming brings income but also market risk and input costs, so guaranteed family food can be safer than cash crops that fail or fetch a collapsed price. Claiming subsistence always out-feeds commercial per hectare is an unsupported blanket reversal. Saying commercial farming is impossible on small farms contradicts the many small commercial growers in the chapter. Treating the two as the same activity erases the purpose distinction the argument turns on.

**6. (B)** Sowing with June rains and harvesting as the monsoon ends marks the kharif (monsoon) season, and getting no canal or well water means it lived on the southwest monsoon. Rabi is the cool-season winter crop, sown later and often irrigated, which the June timing rules out. Zaid is the brief summer crop between rabi and kharif, not one sown with the monsoon's onset. The final option contradicts the stated fact that no canal water was used.

**7. (D)** The timing of each crop relative to the rains explains it: kharif rice sits in the rainy monsoon and drinks rain, while rabi wheat sits in the dry winter and must be irrigated. The idea that wheat dislikes water is false, since wheat needs water and simply has little rain in winter. Claiming the belt's rain falls in winter contradicts a dry-winter belt where the monsoon comes in summer. Saying rice needs canal water reverses which crop is rain-fed.

**8. (D)** The southeastern coast is watered chiefly by the retreating northeast monsoon of October to December, which is exactly the late rain described. June to September is the southwest monsoon that soaks most of India but largely misses this belt's main season. March to May is the dry hot season before any monsoon. Rainfall in India is strongly seasonal, never spread evenly through the year, so timing is decisive.

**9. (A)** Starting just after spring, the short hot gap (zaid, e.g. watermelon) comes first, then the monsoon crop (kharif rice) sown in June, then the cool-season crop (rabi wheat) sown in winter. Putting kharif before zaid skips the early-summer gap that opens right after spring. Leading with rabi places the winter crop before the summer and monsoon crops, which is out of sequence for a year beginning after spring. Putting rabi before kharif reverses winter and monsoon sowing.

**10. (A)** Alluvial silt is nutrient-rich and moisture-retentive, so identical plants do better on it than on thin red soil, which fits Field 1 out-yielding Field 2. Saying nutrients are irrelevant is self-contradictory, since it is the soil's nutrients that drive the difference. Calling fertile silt desert sand confuses a rich river deposit with barren wind-blown sand. Claiming silt clogs roots predicts the opposite of the observed higher yield.

**11. (C)** Humus enriches the soil with nutrients and improves moisture retention, which explains both the better crop and the reduced need to water. Saying humus removes water contradicts the observed lower watering need. Turning soil to rock would harm, not help, root growth and yield. Crediting the gain to killing soil organisms misstates humus, which supports rather than destroys the living soil that crops rely on.

**12. (D)** Different crops draw on and return different nutrients, so a pulse rotated in repairs the imbalance left by a single repeated cereal, and the next cereal grows better. 'Spinning' the soil literally is a misreading of crop rotation, which is about alternating crops, not stirring earth. Removing every nutrient at once would weaken, not strengthen, the next crop. The field was not left empty; a pulse was grown, so 'resting empty' is not the mechanism.

**13. (A)** Terracing creates flat steps that retain rainwater and check erosion, making steep ground farmable. Removing the need for soil and water describes nothing terracing does, since crops still need both. Turning a hillside into desert sand is a loss of fertility, the opposite of the goal. Crops never grow without seeds, so the wind-blown-plant option is nonsense.

**14. (B)** Irrigation's chief value is supplying water when the monsoon fails, so its edge is greatest in a weak year and narrows when rain is ample. Claiming it always wins every year overstates the case, since in a good monsoon a rain-fed farm may do nearly as well. Saying it helps only in good years inverts the logic, as pumps do not need rain to run and matter most in shortfalls. The claim that rain-fed always wins ignores that free rain is useless when it does not fall.

**15. (D)** The honest trade-off is cheap, locally adapted saved seed versus costlier high-yield seed that raises output but needs more water and chemicals, so the answer turns on her means. Claiming saved seed always wins ignores the real yield gains modern seed can bring. Saying bought seed is free and input-free is factually wrong. Treating the two as identical erases the cost-and-input differences the choice hinges on.

**16. (B)** Tanks and bunds are old indigenous techniques, so their presence shows water management predates and now coexists with modern pumps. Saying it began with the Green Revolution ignores the antiquity of those very structures. Claiming the structures prove farmers ignored water is self-contradictory, since the structures exist to manage water. Restricting their use to rainless places misses that they store and direct seasonal rain.

**17. (C)** The output and the damage flow from one source: the intensive input-heavy methods raised yields while degrading soil, depleting groundwater and piling up costs, so they are causally linked, not coincidental. Blaming abandonment is wrong, since the harms came from continued use, not from stopping. Pinning debt on low yields ignores that yields rose; the debt came from high input costs. Denying any link between farming method and soil-water harm contradicts the whole cause-and-effect chain.

**18. (A)** Output did rise, so the assertion is true, but the claim of no lasting effect is false, since the same chemicals and pumping degraded soil and lowered water tables. The option calling both true and linked endorses the false 'no effect' claim. Calling both false denies the real rise in output. Calling the assertion false ignores the documented yield gains.

**19. (C)** Reducing chemicals lessens the pollutants leaching into groundwater, and organic matter rebuilds soil, so both effects follow the change in practice over years. Saying organic farming uses no water or soil is false, as crops still need both. The improvement is gradual, not an overnight magical refill. Calling it chance ignores the clear cause-and-effect from the changed inputs.

**20. (D)** The chapter frames organic and natural farming as a conscious modern reaction to chemical harm, even as it draws on older natural inputs, which is why it is a 'response.' Saying organic methods were invented in the 1960s alongside the chemicals confuses the response with the cause. Claiming pre-Green-Revolution farming used heavy chemicals is false, since those chemicals came with the Green Revolution. Saying manure and neem were unknown until recently contradicts their long traditional use.

**21. (D)** A gradual blend of natural methods with careful modern inputs cushions income while improving soil, which matches the chapter's balanced advice. Doubling chemicals worsens the very harms she is trying to escape. Stopping farming surrenders both food and income and ignores the gradual path. Ignoring soil health and chasing maximum yield reproduces the Green Revolution's long-term damage.

**22. (B)** Growing plants in nutrient water without soil is hydroponics, cutting a slope into steps is terrace farming, and raising fruits, vegetables and flowers is horticulture. The first wrong option scrambles each term onto the wrong scene. Calling all three merely commercial ignores their distinct techniques and crops. The last option again misassigns every label.

**23. (C)** The hands-on survey shows women doing most field work, while the wage register misses them because their labour is often unpaid family work, so they are undercounted. Concluding women do little work flips the survey evidence. Saying only paid male labour counts arbitrarily defines away the women's real work. Claiming farming needs almost no labour contradicts the very tasks the survey records.

**24. (A)** Targeting only male landowners overlooks the women and landless labourers who do much of the work, so the support reaches the wrong hands. Saying women contribute nothing contradicts the chapter's central point. Assuming all farmers are wealthy male landowners is false, since many are women and landless. Claiming farm labour is no longer needed denies the ongoing human work the scheme should support.

**25. (D)** Mechanisation favours larger, consolidated land; on tiny scattered patches a big tractor is often too costly and awkward to justify. Saying it is equally easy on any farm ignores how size and fragmentation matter. Claiming tractors cannot be used anywhere is false. Guaranteeing wealth from one purchase overstates what a tractor can do.

**26. (B)** A good harvest fetching little is the market-price problem, erratic heat and rain is climate change, and splitting land too small to mechanise is fragmentation, matching the order given. The first wrong set mislabels the price crash as a weak monsoon and adds an impossible 'too fertile' harm. The second misorders price and climate and invents a total lack of buyers. The third mislabels the climate year as monsoon and ends with 'perfect prices,' which is not a challenge at all.

**27. (C)** Ancient rice and barley sites, old farming texts, and farms worked today together show a long, continuous tradition. Calling it only decades old ignores the millennia-old evidence. Saying it was all borrowed contradicts the home-grown ancient sites and texts. Claiming it was abandoned and revived is wrong, since the chain runs unbroken to the present.

**28. (C)** Crops are matched to soils and climates, so the variety of Indian soils is a reason to grow different crops in different places, not to standardise one. Saying India has one soil type contradicts its several major soils. Claiming soil makes no difference denies the link between soil and yield the chapter stresses. Saying only one crop can grow anywhere is false and the opposite of the chapter's point.

**29. (D)** Monsoon paddy rice is kharif, cool-winter wheat is rabi, and the short hot-gap watermelon is zaid, in that order. The first wrong option swaps rice and wheat between kharif and rabi. The second swaps wheat and watermelon. The third scrambles all three.

**30. (B)** Each of the student's three errors is reversed in the correct option: mixed Green-Revolution record, women doing most field work, blended methods, and wide regional variation. The first wrong option simply repeats the student's mistakes. The second over-corrects into the opposite falsehoods. The third invents new errors about machines and soil that the chapter rejects.

**31. (C)** Drip water is most valuable where natural rain is scarce, so the dry-season wheat and the hot-gap melon gain most, while the monsoon rice already drinks the rains. Putting it on the rice wastes the grant where rain is plentiful. Saying all need equal water ignores that seasons differ sharply in rainfall. Saying drip cannot help any field crop contradicts the chapter's whole point about irrigation.

**32. (B)** '-cide' means killer, so a pesticide kills pests, not waters crops. The water meaning is invented and contradicts the ending. There is no seed meaning in '-cide.' There is no garden meaning either, so that option is also wrong.

**33. (B)** Hydroponics proves soil is not strictly necessary when nutrients reach the roots in water, yet most ordinary field crops still depend on fertile soil, so soil is far from useless. Saying plants never benefit from soil is false. Claiming plants need no nutrients misreads hydroponics, which supplies nutrients in the water. Saying plants can grow only in soil is exactly the misconception hydroponics disproves.

**34. (A)** High-yield seed demands water and chemicals she lacks, so on a dry, cash-poor, rain-fed plot it is likely to fail or push her into debt, a clear mismatch. Saying high-yield seed always wins ignores the conditions it requires. Claiming modern seed needs no water or money is the very misconception the chapter corrects. Guaranteeing a harvest regardless of water or money ignores that the seed's needs are unmet.

**35. (D)** The record shows genuine early gains followed by long-term harm, so yields and damage are weighed together. Insisting on only benefits forces an invented separate cause for the decline. Insisting on only costs denies the documented doubling of output. Denying any link between chemical methods and soil, water and income contradicts the cause-and-effect the district shows.

**36. (D)** Organic farming controls pests with natural means such as neem, so Grower 2 fits. Grower 1 uses synthetic chemicals, the very thing organic farming avoids. Grower 3 abandons control, but organic farming still controls pests, just naturally. The method clearly does affect the label, so 'all equally' is wrong.

**37. (A)** Meeting present needs without harming the future ability to farm is exactly sustainable farming. Extensive farming refers to thin effort over large land, not to long-term resource care. Commercial farming is about selling crops, a purpose, not sustainability. Intensive farming concerns high effort per area, again unrelated to protecting future resources.

**38. (B)** Soil conservation bundles crop rotation, adding humus, and preventing erosion, which the first option names exactly. Repeating one hungry crop and over-pumping degrades soil and water rather than conserving them. Maxing chemical fertiliser harms long-term fertility. Exporting topsoil literally removes the resource conservation aims to protect.

**39. (B)** Two facts correct the picture: farming is not fully mechanised, and women do most field work, so a woman is at least as typical. Saying tractors are never used and only men farm contradicts both reality and the women's role. Claiming machines do all the work denies the human labour the chapter stresses. Saying women own most farmland and drive all tractors misstates the basis of their role, which is hands-on field work, not land ownership.

**40. (A)** A winter sowing is rabi, a canal supply is irrigated, and river silt is alluvial soil, so all three fit. The kharif/rain-fed option is wrong because a winter crop is not kharif and a canal-served field is not rain-fed. The zaid/desert-sand option mislabels the winter season and calls fertile silt desert sand. The rabi/rain-fed/black-soil option correctly says rabi but wrongly calls a canal-fed field rain-fed and silt soil black soil.

**41. (C)** Subsistence versus commercial turns on purpose, to eat or to sell, not on plot size or effort, so both halves of his reasoning are confused. The first wrong option just inverts his errors rather than correcting them. Tying the labels to soil type substitutes the wrong axis. Tying them to season likewise uses the wrong axis, since kharif and rabi describe timing, not purpose.

**42. (C)** Since this belt's main rain is the October-December northeast monsoon, a June sowing misses the water and the crop suffers. Saying the June southwest monsoon soaks everywhere equally ignores that this region largely misses it. Claiming all regions peak in June is false for northeast-monsoon belts. Saying rainfall is even all year contradicts India's sharply seasonal rains.

**43. (A)** June sowing on rain, harvested as the monsoon ends, is a rain-fed kharif crop; November sowing watered by canal, harvested in spring, is an irrigated rabi crop. The first wrong option reverses the order of the two. Calling the first a zaid crop ignores its monsoon timing. Calling both kharif ignores that the second is a canal-watered winter crop.

**44. (C)** The chapter defines agriculture broadly to take in horticulture, forestry, fisheries and beekeeping alongside crops, so orchards, ponds and hives are part of it. Limiting it to cereals repeats the owner's error. Banking and money-printing belong to other sectors entirely. Road and bridge building is construction, not agriculture.

**45. (C)** The headline gain of the Green Revolution was national food self-sufficiency in grains, ending heavy reliance on imports. Saying crops no longer needed water is false, since the package relied heavily on irrigation. Claiming every farmer became permanently wealthy ignores the debt and harm many faced. Saying the country abandoned farming contradicts the very rise in grain output.

**46. (D)** Rabi is the cool-season winter crop, not the monsoon crop sown in June, so that statement is the false one. Kharif being sown with the southwest monsoon in the rainy season is true. Rabi growing in winter and harvested in spring is true. Zaid being a short hot-gap crop between rabi and kharif is true.

**47. (C)** Irrigation supplies water when the monsoon fails, so expanding it most directly reduces weak-monsoon crop failures. Pushing more dependence on rainfall worsens the problem. Banning kharif crops removes the main monsoon harvest without solving water shortage. Forcing one crop everywhere ignores soil and season variation and does nothing for water security.

**48. (B)** Her ongoing purpose is to feed the family, so she is subsistence; she works the plot intensively and waters it, so intensive and irrigated also fit, and one tiny barter does not make her commercial. A single exchange does not flip the dominant purpose to commercial. Having one hectare does not make her extensive, since she works it intensively. Growing food is not horticulture, which means fruits, vegetables and flowers.

**49. (D)** The chapter's whole case is that farming rightly varies with soil, season and need, that heavy chemical monocropping degrades soil and water and creates debt, and that women and small farmers matter, so forced uniformity is the deep flaw. Saying there is no objection endorses the very approach the chapter critiques. Reducing the problem to machine cost ignores the soil, water, debt and labour issues. Saying India lacks commercial farms is false and misses the point.

**50. (C)** Sustained heavy pumping can outpace recharge and lower the water table, threatening future supply, while a rain-fed plot does not pump. Saying pumping raises the table inverts the cause. Treating groundwater as endless ignores that wells do run low. Blaming the rain-fed neighbour, who pumps nothing, misassigns the cause.

**51. (A)** The Green Revolution clearly raised grain output and aided self-sufficiency, so the claim that traditional methods always produce more is false, even granting the real harms. Saying saved seed always out-produces high-yield seed ignores the documented gains. Claiming the Green Revolution lowered output reverses the record. Saying neither approach affects output is plainly false.

**52. (A)** Blending saved seed and compost with careful modern inputs balances yield, soil health and cost, which the chapter favours. Maximum chemicals raise cost and harm soil, the opposite of her goal. Abandoning all inputs risks losing the yield she wants to keep. Buying costly seed and maximising irrigation drives up cost and water use, against her aims.

**53. (C)** Since women do most of the work, directing support to them and to landless labourers matches aid to the people who use it. Calling the budget well aimed contradicts the survey showing women do most work. Withdrawing support from farms with women punishes the main workers. Replacing women with imported labour ignores both the survey and the real labour force.

**54. (B)** Terracing turns a steep slope into level steps that retain water and reduce erosion while allowing crops, exactly the need described. Hydroponics addresses growing without soil, not stabilising a slope. The Green Revolution package concerns yield-boosting inputs, not erosion control. Export horticulture is about what and why to grow, not about holding soil on a slope.

**55. (B)** Organic farming's defining feature is replacing synthetic fertilisers and pesticides with natural inputs, the clear true difference. Saying it produces no wheat is false, since organic farms do grow food. Growing without soil describes hydroponics, not organic field farming. Saying the organic field uses more chemical pesticide reverses its whole definition.

**56. (A)** Saved seeds are prized for being cheap and locally adapted, though not necessarily the highest yielding. Claiming they always give the highest yield overstates them, since high-yield modern seed can out-produce them. Saying they can be used only once contradicts their being saved and replanted yearly. Saying they need heavy chemicals and irrigation describes high-yield seed, not adapted saved seed.

**57. (D)** Repeated division shrinks and fragments holdings into small scattered patches, which hampers efficiency and machinery, a key present-day challenge. Saying farms grow larger reverses what division does. Dividing land does not improve soil fertility. Smaller plots do not automatically raise crop prices.

**58. (D)** Fragmented holdings, climate change, uncertain prices, water stress and debt are the real pressures the chapter lists. Abundant land and guaranteed perfect prices are not problems and are false. Soil being too fertile and irrigation everywhere are not the chapter's challenges. Claiming no difficulties exist contradicts the whole final section on challenges.

**59. (D)** Women are the backbone because they do most of the hands-on field tasks that keep farms running. Owning most farmland is not the basis of the claim, which is about labour, not ownership. Saying they alone run all machinery misstates their role and ignores the chapter's point about limited mechanisation. Saying they manage only from offices contradicts their hands-on field work.

**60. (A)** The correct sentence weaves the chapter's key threads: ancient and varied tradition, women doing most work, mixed Green-Revolution record, and blended methods. The first wrong option restates every misconception the chapter dismantles. The second denies change, overstates machines and dismisses soil and season. The third falsely makes farming uniformly commercial, erases women and dismisses saved seed.
