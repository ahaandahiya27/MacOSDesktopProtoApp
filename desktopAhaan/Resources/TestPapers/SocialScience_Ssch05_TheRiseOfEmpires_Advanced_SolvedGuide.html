Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

An *empire* is best defined as:

  • Aa single small village
  • Ba trade guild
  • Ca collection of smaller kingdoms or territories under one ruler's supreme power✓
  • Da republic with no ruler

Working

✓

An empire is a collection of smaller kingdoms or territories brought under one ruler's supreme power. Trap: a single village or a trade guild is far too small, and a republic has no single supreme ruler — the defining feature of an empire.

Q2

The word "empire" comes from the Latin *imperium*, meaning:

  • Asupreme power✓
  • Bsmall farm
  • Criver crossing
  • Dmarket

Working

✓

"Empire" descends from the Latin *imperium*, "supreme power" or "command." The root makes the meaning concrete: an empire is the reach of one supreme authority over many lands — not a farm, a river crossing or a market.

Q3

The chief difference between a kingdom and an empire is that an empire:

  • Ais always smaller
  • Bhas no army
  • Cnever collects taxes
  • Dis larger and brings many kingdoms under one supreme ruler✓

Working

✓

A kingdom is one realm; an empire is larger and binds *many* kingdoms under one supreme ruler. An empire need not be smaller, and both kingdoms and empires keep armies and collect taxes, so the "no army" and "never taxes" choices are false.

Q4

Which of these is one of the six features that hold an empire together?

  • Athe absence of any laws
  • Ba strong army and an organised administration✓
  • Cbanning all trade
  • Dhaving no currency

Working

✓

A strong army plus an organised administration is one of the six binding features. The other choices describe the *absence* of binding features — no laws, no trade, no currency — which would pull an empire apart, not hold it together.

Q5

A *tributary* (or vassal) was a ruler who:

  • Aruled with no overlord at all
  • Bhad submitted to an emperor and paid tribute to keep his throne✓
  • Cwas a travelling merchant
  • Dwrote the edicts

Working

✓

A tributary (vassal) had submitted to an emperor and paid tribute to keep his own throne — local rule under an overlord. That is the opposite of ruling with no overlord; a travelling merchant or an edict-writer is a different role entirely.

Q6

A *śhreni* in this period was a:

  • Aroyal crown
  • Btype of coin
  • Cwar elephant
  • Dpowerful association/guild of traders, craftsmen or farmers✓

Working

✓

A *śhreni* was a powerful association or guild of traders, craftsmen or farmers — an economic body, not a crown, a coin or a war elephant. Guilds strengthened Magadha's economy and so its rise.

Q7

The empire of Magadha rose to dominance out of the:

  • Asixteen mahājanapadas✓
  • BHarappan cities
  • CMughal provinces
  • DGupta kingdoms

Working

✓

Magadha rose to dominance out of the sixteen *mahājanapadas*, the large states of the period. The Harappan cities long predate this era; the Mughals and Guptas come much later.

Q8

Magadha's rise was helped by a powerful combination of:

  • Aonly its distance from rivers
  • Bthe absence of any army
  • Crich farmland, iron, trade routes and guilds✓
  • Dhaving no farmland

Working

✓

Magadha's edge was a *combination*: rich farmland, iron ore, control of trade routes, and active guilds. Distance from rivers, no army or no farmland would each be a *weakness*, so they cannot explain its rise.

Q9

The dynasty founded by Mahāpadma Nanda just before the Mauryas was the:

  • ANanda dynasty✓
  • BGupta dynasty
  • CChola dynasty
  • DMaurya dynasty

Working

✓

Mahāpadma Nanda founded the Nanda dynasty, which ruled Magadha just before the Mauryas overthrew it. The Guptas, Cholas and Mauryas are different dynasties on different timelines.

Q10

Alexander of Macedonia reached the north-west of India around:

  • A1500 CE
  • B268 BCE
  • C326 BCE✓
  • D1000 BCE

Working

✓

Alexander of Macedonia reached the north-west of the subcontinent around 326 BCE. The CE dates and the later BCE dates are off by centuries — anchor him to the late 4th century BCE.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

In the north-west, Alexander fought a famous battle against the Indian king:

  • AAshoka
  • BChandragupta
  • CBindusara
  • DPorus✓

Working

✓

In the north-west Alexander fought the hard-won battle of the Hydaspes against King Porus. Ashoka, Chandragupta and Bindusara are all Mauryan rulers who come *after* Alexander's incursion.

Q12

A *satrap* was a:

  • Akind of coin
  • Bgovernor of a province in the Persian and Greek empires✓
  • CBuddhist monk
  • Dtrade route

Working

✓

A *satrap* was the governor of a province in the Persian and later Greek administrative systems — a high official, not a coin, a monk or a road. Chandragupta later cleared these satraps from the north-west.

Q13

Chandragupta Maurya strengthened his new empire partly by defeating the:

  • AHarappan cities
  • BMughal armies
  • CBritish traders
  • DGreek satraps left behind in the north-west✓

Working

✓

Chandragupta strengthened his new empire partly by defeating the Greek satraps left behind after Alexander. The Harappan cities were long gone; the Mughals and the British are centuries away.

Q14

Chandragupta Maurya is remembered as:

  • Aa Greek general
  • Bthe first emperor to unite most of the subcontinent✓
  • Cthe author of the Vedas
  • Da Harappan king

Working

✓

Chandragupta Maurya is remembered as the first emperor to unite most of the subcontinent. He was an Indian ruler, not a Greek general, a Vedic author or a Harappan king.

Q15

Chandragupta's brilliant mentor and chief adviser was:

  • AAshoka
  • BAlexander
  • CKauṭilya (Chāṇakya)✓
  • DPorus

Working

✓

His mentor and chief adviser was Kauṭilya, also called Chāṇakya. Ashoka was Chandragupta's grandson; Alexander and Porus were adversaries, not advisers.

Q16

The *Arthaśhāstra*, written by Kauṭilya, is literally a treatise on:

  • Athe science of governance and economics✓
  • Bcooking
  • Castronomy
  • Dpoetry only

Working

✓

The *Arthaśhāstra* is literally a treatise on the science of governance and economics (*artha* = wealth/statecraft). It is not about cooking, astronomy or poetry.

Q17

Kauṭilya's *saptānga* theory describes a state as made up of:

  • Atwo kingdoms
  • Bfour castes only
  • Cseven essential parts or "limbs"✓
  • Dsixteen guilds

Working

✓

Kauṭilya's *saptānga* ("seven limbs") theory describes a state as made of seven essential parts — not two kingdoms, the four varnas or sixteen guilds. The number to remember is *seven*.

Q18

Which set best matches the saptānga "limbs" of a state?

  • Aking, ministers, territory & people, forts, treasury, army & justice, allies✓
  • Brain, rivers, hills, forests, deserts, coasts, plains
  • Cgold, silver, copper, iron, bronze, tin, lead
  • Dsowing, weeding, watering, harvesting, storing, selling, eating

Working

✓

The seven limbs are the king, the ministers, the territory & people, the forts, the treasury, the army & justice, and the allies. The other lists describe landforms, metals or farm steps — distractors built to look like a "seven-item" set.

Q19

The saptānga idea warns that:

  • Aonly the king matters; the rest can be ignored
  • Ba weakness in any one of the seven limbs harms the whole state✓
  • Ca state needs no treasury
  • Dallies are useless

Working

✓

The saptānga warns that a weakness in *any one* of the seven limbs harms the whole state — like a body losing a limb. So it is false that only the king matters, that a treasury is dispensable or that allies are useless.

Q20

The emperor Ashoka ruled roughly during:

  • A1526–1530 CE
  • B326–300 CE
  • C600–550 BCE
  • D268–232 BCE✓

Working

✓

Ashoka ruled roughly 268–232 BCE. The CE option and the wrong-century BCE options miss him; place him in the mid-3rd century BCE, after Chandragupta and Bindusara.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

The event that filled Ashoka with horror and turned him toward *dhamma* was the:

  • AKalinga war✓
  • Bbattle against Porus
  • Cfall of Harappa
  • DGreek invasion of Magadha

Working

✓

The Kalinga war — with its appalling death toll — filled Ashoka with horror and turned him toward *dhamma*. The other choices are battles or events he was not part of, or belong to other periods.

Q22

*Annexation* means:

  • Agiving land away freely
  • Babandoning a city
  • Cminting coins
  • Dadding a conquered territory directly into one's own empire✓

Working

✓

*Annexation* is adding a conquered territory directly into one's own empire. It is the opposite of giving land away or abandoning a city; minting coins is unrelated.

Q23

Ashoka's annexation of Kalinga is remembered above all for its:

  • Apeaceful, bloodless nature
  • Bterrible cost in human lives, which he came to regret✓
  • Cfailure to capture the region
  • Dlack of any record

Working

✓

Kalinga is remembered above all for its terrible cost in human lives, which Ashoka came to regret — the turning point of his reign. So it was neither bloodless nor a failed nor an unrecorded campaign.

Q24

An *edict* is:

  • Aa kind of war elephant
  • Ba coin
  • Can official declaration issued by an authority such as a king✓
  • Da guild meeting

Working

✓

An *edict* is an official declaration issued by an authority such as a king. It is not a war elephant, a coin or a guild meeting.

Q25

Ashoka had his edicts engraved on:

  • Apaper scrolls only
  • Brocks and pillars across his empire✓
  • Ccloth banners
  • Dclay tablets buried underground

Working

✓

Ashoka had his edicts engraved on rocks and pillars across the empire so they would last and be seen widely — not on perishable paper or cloth, nor hidden underground.

Q26

Ashoka's edicts were composed mostly in:

  • ALatin
  • BEnglish
  • CPrakrit, the everyday language✓
  • Donly refined Sanskrit

Working

✓

The edicts were composed mostly in Prakrit, the everyday spoken language. Latin and English are foreign; refined Sanskrit alone would have shut out ordinary readers — exactly what Ashoka wanted to avoid.

Q27

Ashoka chose Prakrit rather than Sanskrit chiefly so that:

  • Aordinary people could understand his messages✓
  • Bonly priests could read them
  • Cforeigners alone could read them
  • Dthe words would rhyme

Working

✓

Ashoka chose Prakrit precisely so that ordinary people could understand his messages. The point was the *opposite* of restricting them to priests or foreigners; rhyme was never the goal.

Q28

The script in which Ashoka's edicts were carved, and the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts, is:

  • ARoman
  • BArabic
  • CChinese
  • DBrahmi✓

Working

✓

The script was Brahmi, the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts. Roman, Arabic and Chinese scripts are unrelated to the Mauryan edicts.

Q29

*Dhamma* (dharma), which Ashoka promoted, broadly means:

  • Aa tax on traders
  • Ba kind of fort
  • Ca war strategy
  • Dmoral law and one's ethical duties✓

Working

✓

*Dhamma* (dharma) broadly means moral law and one's ethical duties — compassion, honesty, respect. It is not a tax, a fort or a war strategy.

Q30

The *dharmachakra*, the "wheel of dharma," appears today at the centre of:

  • Athe Indian national flag✓
  • Bevery rupee coin's edge
  • Cthe Constitution's cover only
  • Dthe Red Fort gate

Working

✓

The *dharmachakra*, the wheel of dharma, sits at the centre of the Indian national flag — a direct link to the Mauryan heritage. It is not on the rupee's edge, the Constitution's cover alone or the Red Fort gate.

Q31

India's national emblem is taken from the lion capital that Ashoka set up at:

✓

India's national emblem is the lion capital Ashoka set up at Sarnath. Kalinga, Taxila and Ujjain are important places, but the lion capital is from Sarnath.

Q32

  • Athe country's main city
  • Bthe carved top or "head" of the column✓
  • Cmoney invested in trade
  • Dthe base of the column

Working

✓

The "capital" of a pillar (Latin *caput*, "head") is the carved top of the column. Don't confuse it with a country's main city or money invested in trade; it is certainly not the base.

Q33

The four lions of the Ashokan capital are familiar today because they appear on:

  • Acricket bats
  • Bpostage from Greece
  • CIndian currency notes and official seals✓
  • DMughal coins only

Working

✓

The four lions of the Ashokan capital appear today on Indian currency notes and official seals — not on cricket bats, Greek postage or Mughal coins.

Q34

The great Mauryan city of Pāṭaliputra stands at the site of the modern city of:

  • ADelhi
  • BPatna✓
  • CMumbai
  • DChennai

Working

✓

Mauryan Pāṭaliputra stands where modern Patna is today. Delhi, Mumbai and Chennai are unrelated to the Mauryan capital.

Q35

The word *emperor* comes from the Latin *imperator*, meaning:

  • Afarmer
  • Bmerchant
  • Cpriest
  • Dcommander✓

Working

✓

*Emperor* comes from the Latin *imperator*, "commander" — fitting for the supreme military and political authority over an empire. Not a farmer, a merchant or a priest.

Q36

A *dynasty* is:

  • Aa line of rulers from the same family passing power down✓
  • Ba single coin
  • Ca kind of guild
  • Da province

Working

✓

A *dynasty* is a line of rulers from the same family passing power down the generations. It is not a coin, a guild or a province.

Q37

The Nanda dynasty was overthrown and replaced by the:

  • AMauryan dynasty✓
  • BGupta dynasty
  • CGreek satraps
  • DChola dynasty

Working

✓

The Nanda dynasty was overthrown and replaced by the Mauryan dynasty (Chandragupta, guided by Kauṭilya). Not the Guptas, the Greek satraps or the Cholas.

Q38

A *bureaucracy* (from *bureau* + *-kratia*) is:

  • Athe royal family alone
  • Bthe body of appointed officials who run an empire's day-to-day administration✓
  • Cthe army's cavalry
  • Da group of poets

Working

✓

A *bureaucracy* is the body of appointed officials who run an empire's day-to-day administration. It is broader than the royal family alone, and it is not the cavalry or a group of poets.

Q39

A common currency across an empire mattered because it:

  • Astopped all trade
  • Bwas used only for decoration
  • Creplaced the army
  • Dmade trade and tax collection smoother over a vast area✓

Working

✓

A common currency made trade and tax collection smoother across a vast area — a genuine binding feature. It did not stop trade, serve as decoration or replace the army.

Q40

A strong army was a binding feature of empires because it:

  • Awas only for parades
  • Bcollected the edicts
  • Ccould defend the borders and hold conquered territory together✓
  • Dwrote the Arthaśhāstra

Working

✓

A strong army could defend the borders and hold conquered territory together — its real political purpose, not mere parades, carrying edicts or authorship.

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

Good communication networks (roads, messengers) mattered to an empire because they:

  • Awere used only by traders, never the state
  • Breplaced the treasury
  • Cmade coins unnecessary
  • Dlet the centre govern and supply distant provinces✓

Working

✓

Good roads and messengers let the centre govern and supply distant provinces — essential for holding an empire together. They were used by the state too, not only traders; they did not replace the treasury or make coins unnecessary.

Q42

Why did emperors support art and learning?

  • Ait weakened the state
  • Bit was forbidden by the Arthaśhāstra
  • Cit added to the empire's prestige and helped bind its culture together✓
  • Dit had no effect at all

Working

✓

Emperors supported art and learning because it added to the empire's prestige and helped bind its culture together. It did not weaken the state, and the Arthaśhāstra encouraged rather than forbade such support.

Q43

"Control over resources" as an imperial feature meant command of things like:

  • Airon, fertile farmland and the taxes they yielded✓
  • Bonly seashells
  • Cnothing of value
  • Dforeign languages

Working

✓

"Control over resources" meant command of things like iron, fertile farmland and the taxes they yielded — real economic power. Seashells, nothing of value and foreign languages miss the point.

Q44

The *Arthaśhāstra* can be described as:

  • Aa collection of folk songs
  • Ban ancient handbook for running a state — defence, administration, justice, welfare✓
  • Ca map of trade routes only
  • Da book of Ashoka's edicts

Working

✓

The *Arthaśhāstra* is best described as an ancient handbook for running a state — defence, administration, justice and welfare. It is not a collection of folk songs, a trade-route map or a record of Ashoka's edicts.

Q45

Ashoka's edicts were historically remarkable because, perhaps for the first time, a king:

  • Arefused to speak to anyone
  • Bspoke directly to his people, explaining his ideals✓
  • Cwrote only in a secret code
  • Dbanned all writing

Working

✓

Ashoka's edicts were remarkable because, perhaps for the first time, a king spoke directly to his people, explaining his ideals. The opposite choices — silence, secret code, banning writing — describe the reverse.

Q46

Place these in correct historical order: (i) the Mauryan empire under Chandragupta; (ii) the sixteen mahājanapadas; (iii) the Nanda dynasty.

  • A(i) → (ii) → (iii)
  • B(iii) → (ii) → (i)
  • C(ii) → (i) → (iii)
  • D(ii) → (iii) → (i)✓

✓

Correct order: the sixteen mahājanapadas first, then the Nanda dynasty that ruled Magadha, and last the Mauryan empire under Chandragupta that overthrew the Nandas. That is sequence (ii) → (iii) → (i).

Q47

The *Arthaśhāstra* and the *saptānga* theory are both credited to:

  • AAshoka
  • BAlexander
  • CKauṭilya (Chāṇakya)✓
  • DMahāpadma Nanda

✓

Both the *Arthaśhāstra* and the *saptānga* theory are credited to Kauṭilya (Chāṇakya). Ashoka, Alexander and Mahāpadma Nanda wrote neither.

Q48

Tributary kings typically paid the emperor in the form of:

  • Agold, grain or elephants✓
  • Bnothing at all
  • Conly seashells
  • Dedicts

Working

✓

Tributary kings typically paid the emperor in gold, grain or elephants — real wealth and military assets. "Nothing at all," seashells and edicts are distractors.

Q49

Modern trade and industry associations are often compared to ancient:

  • Aguilds (śhrenis)✓
  • Bsatraps
  • Cedicts
  • Dpillars

Working

✓

Modern trade and industry associations are often compared to the ancient guilds (*śhrenis*). They are nothing like satraps (governors), edicts (declarations) or pillars.

Q50

Why do even the greatest empires eventually decline?

  • Athey are forbidden by law to last
  • Bemperors choose to dissolve them for fun
  • Cthey never actually decline
  • Dthey are fragile — over-stretch, weak successors, revolts and money troubles all strain the binding features✓

Working

✓

Even great empires decline because they are fragile: over-stretch, weak successors, revolts and money troubles all strain the binding features. There is no law against lasting, emperors do not dissolve them for fun, and they certainly do decline.

  • Aendless further conquest
  • Babolishing all officials

✓

After Kalinga, Ashoka's *dhamma* policy emphasised compassion, non-violence and the moral welfare of his people — a deliberate turn away from further conquest. He did not abolish officials or ban trade.

Q54

  • Basked the Mauryas directly
  • Cguessed at random
  • Ddeciphered the Brahmi script✓

Working

✓

We can read Ashoka's messages today because scholars in the 1830s deciphered the Brahmi script. There was no printed translation; one cannot ask the Mauryas directly; and decipherment was scholarship, not random guessing.

Q55

An *emperor* differs from an ordinary *king* in that the emperor:

  • Bstands above other kings, ruling an empire of many territories✓
  • Chas no army
  • Drules only one village

Working

✓

An emperor stands above other kings, ruling an empire of many territories. That is greater authority, not weakness; emperors command armies and rule far more than a single village.

Q56

*Annexation* comes from the Latin *annectere*, meaning:

  • Bto give away
  • Cto destroy
  • Dto forget

Working

✓

*Annexation* comes from the Latin *annectere*, "to bind to" — fitting, since annexation binds a new territory into the empire. Not "to give away," "to destroy" or "to forget."

Q57

Magadha's iron and fertile farmland gave it an edge because they:

Magadha's iron and fertile farmland supplied better weapons and tools plus the food surplus to feed a large army and bureaucracy — a double advantage. They were valuable on their own, aided trade rather than preventing it, and did not make Magadha abandon farming.
