## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** *Imperium* is Latin for "supreme power", and the chapter uses the word's origin to stress that an empire is built on one ruler holding supreme power over many lands. (A) and (C) over-read it into Rome; (D) confuses an empire with mere wealth — size/wealth alone is not the defining trait.

**2. (C)** A kingdom is one ruler over one people/territory; an empire layers many kingdoms under one emperor, with smaller kings kept on as tributaries who pay tribute. (A)/(B) are just "more of the same" and not the defining difference. (D) is false — tributaries usually keep their thrones.

**3. (D)** A defeated ruler who keeps his throne but sends tribute (gold, grain, elephants) is a tributary, also called a vassal. A satrap (A) is an *appointed* provincial governor, not a king who pays tribute; (B) a bureaucrat is an official; (C) a guild-head leads traders.

**4. (A)** The six features are: army, administration, laws and currency, control over resources, support for art and learning, and communication. Empires were NOT democracies, so "holding regular elections to choose the emperor" (A) is not among them. (B), (C), (D) are all genuine features.

**5. (B)** A common currency, weights and measures let distant regions trade and be taxed under one shared system — a unifying administrative feature. (A) misreads the purpose; (C) confuses coins with art; (D) is false — currency does not replace the need for an army.

**6. (D)** The administration (officials managing land, taxes and order) plus communication networks (to move messages and goods) are exactly the two features that let an emperor rule far-off villages he cannot visit. (B) names communication but pairs it with resources rather than administration; (A) and (C) miss administration entirely.

**7. (A)** Armies are hugely expensive (feeding, arming and paying soldiers, horses and elephants), and the chapter says the wealth came mainly from a thriving economy, especially trade. (B), (C) and (D) are not how empires actually funded their forces.

**8. (C)** A wise ruler built and controlled trade routes because more trade meant more goods, richer producers and more tax income for the treasury. (A) is false — trade does not remove the need for an army; (C)'s rivals (B)/(D) confuse routes with edicts or guilds.

**9. (A)** India's two great routes were the Uttarapatha (northern) and the Dakṣhiṇapatha (southern). The Silk Road/Spice Route (C) and the invented "Royal Road" (D) are not the chapter's terms; the Ganga and Son (B) are rivers that aided Magadha, not the two named trade routes.

**10. (A)** A guild (śhreni) that fixes prices, shares market news, elects an honest head and settles disputes by its own rules is organising and governing itself — which helped trade flourish. (B) is the opposite (kings let guilds be); (C) confuses guilds with tribute; (D) ignores their written rules and elected leaders.

**11. (B)** An enlightened king let guilds self-govern because self-regulating guilds kept trade — and thus tax revenue — flowing smoothly. (A) wrongly says he was powerless; (C) is false (trade was taxed); (D) invents a law.

**12. (C)** Of the sixteen mahājanapadas, Magadha (in south Bihar) rose to become India's first great power. Gandhāra and Avanti were rivals that faded; Kalinga was the region Ashoka later conquered, not the empire's core.

**13. (A)** Magadha's strength came from fertile Ganga-plains land, iron ore, forests for timber and elephants, and Ganga/Son river trade. (B) wrongly stresses a navy and sea ports; (C) describes the opposite terrain; (D) invents gold mines and the Silk Road.

**14. (D)** Iron helped in two ways at once: iron ploughs grew more food, and iron weapons made the army strong. (A), (B), (C) describe uses (ships, temples, export tribute) that the chapter does not give for Magadhan iron.

**15. (C)** The Ganga and Son rivers made it cheap and easy to move goods and troops — a transport advantage. (A) wrongly makes them a border; (B) invents worship; (D) confuses the rivers with the iron hills.

**16. (A)** Mahāpadma Nanda founded the Nanda dynasty around the 5th century BCE. Dhana Nanda (B) was its last king; Ajātaśhatru (C) was an earlier Magadhan king; Chandragupta Maurya (D) overthrew the Nandas later.

**17. (C)** Mahāpadma Nanda showed imperial power by unifying many kingdoms, issuing coins and keeping a huge army. The Arthaśhāstra (A) was Kauṭilya's; edicts (B) were Ashoka's; defeating Alexander (D) never happened — Alexander was in the northwest, not fighting the Nandas.

**18. (B)** Dhana Nanda, though very rich, was hated for oppressing and over-taxing his subjects, costing him their loyalty. (A), (C), (D) are inventions; his fault was oppression, not generosity or abdication.

**19. (A)** Alexander first conquered the mighty Persian Empire (to avenge earlier Persian attacks on Greece) before reaching northwest India around 326 BCE. The Maurya (B) and Nanda (C) empires were Indian and not conquered by him; the Roman Empire (D) came much later.

**20. (D)** Alexander reached only the northwest, defeated Porus, and turned back when his tired soldiers refused to advance toward the Ganga — so he did not conquer all of India. (A) and (B) are false; (C) reverses it — he never reached the far south.

**21. (B)** After defeating Porus, Alexander turned homeward chiefly because his exhausted soldiers refused to go further. (A) is false (Chandragupta rose only later); (C)/(D) are inventions.

**22. (D)** Alexander's impact was *indirect*: he left weak Greek satraps in the northwest and opened contacts, rather than holding Indian territory himself — which later helped Chandragupta. (A)/(B) are false; (C) confuses him with Ashoka and uses the wrong language.

**23. (A)** Around 321 BCE Chandragupta Maurya founded the empire by overthrowing the unpopular Nandas with Kauṭilya's help, making Pāṭaliputra his capital. (B) reverses the generations (Ashoka was his grandson); (C)/(D) are inventions.

**24. (C)** After Alexander's death the northwest was held by weak Greek satraps Chandragupta could defeat or absorb. (A) is the opposite; (B) is anachronistic (Ashoka came later); (D) is invented.

**25. (B)** Kauṭilya is also called Chāṇakya (and Viṣhnugupta). Megasthenes (A) was a Greek visitor to the court; Porus (C) was the king Alexander fought; Pāṇini (D) was a grammarian, not Chandragupta's mentor.

**26. (C)** The *Arthaśhāstra* means "the science of governance and economics", covering defence, administration, justice and welfare. It is not poetry (A), Ashoka's edicts (B), or a religious text (D).

**27. (D)** Kauṭilya's saptānga (seven limbs) are: king, ministers, territory and people, forts, treasury, army and justice, and allies. (A), (B), (C) are plausible-looking but invented lists that do not match the seven limbs.

**28. (A)** Kauṭilya treated the state like a body whose limbs must all work together — if any one of the seven is weak, the whole state suffers. (B), (C), (D) are inventions; the reasoning is the body/limbs analogy.

**29. (C)** The treasury funds the state's needs while the army defends and enforces — two different essential jobs, so each is a separate limb. (A), (B), (D) are false claims about how they were used or owned.

**30. (B)** Chandragupta Maurya → founder of the empire (c. 321 BCE); Kauṭilya → mentor and author of the Arthaśhāstra; Megasthenes → Greek visitor to the Mauryan court. The other options scramble these roles.

**31. (D)** Ashoka (ruled 268–232 BCE) was Chandragupta Maurya's grandson. He was not his father, brother or mentor — Kauṭilya was the mentor, and Bindusāra (Chandragupta's son) was the generation between them.

**32. (D)** Ashoka's empire covered almost the whole subcontinent EXCEPT the far south. It DID include the northwest/Afghanistan, the Ganga plains and modern Bangladesh, so those are not the exception.

**33. (A)** The turning-point war was the bloody Kalinga war (modern Odisha). The northwest Persians (B) were Alexander's foe; Porus in Punjab (C) was Alexander's foe; the Nandas (D) were overthrown by Chandragupta, not Ashoka.

**34. (C)** The claim reverses cause and effect: Ashoka attacked Kalinga to *expand* his empire by conquest; the horror of the bloodshed afterwards led him toward dhamma and peace. (A)/(B) are false; (D) overstates a rule.

**35. (B)** We know Ashoka was genuinely shaken because he openly admitted his remorse in his own edicts — a rare, first-hand confession. Megasthenes (A) predates Kalinga's aftermath in this context; the Arthaśhāstra (C) is Kauṭilya's; (D) is invented.

**36. (C)** Dhamma is moral and ethical duty — honesty, non-violence and doing one's duties truthfully, living in harmony with the order of the universe. It is not a replacement religion (A), a tax (B), or warfare (D).

**37. (D)** Ashoka is called a "great communicator" because he engraved edicts on rocks and pillars across the empire. (A), (B), (C) are inventions — soldiers, printing and a single temple were not his method.

**38. (C)** Ashoka's edicts were mostly in the Prakrit language using the Brahmi script. Not Sanskrit/Devanagari (A), Greek/Latin (B), or Tamil (D) — Prakrit was the common spoken language, Brahmi the widely understood script.

**39. (B)** The correction to the classic mix-up: Prakrit is a *language* (what people spoke); Brahmi is a *script* (a way of writing). (A) and (D) deny one is a script; (C) reverses the two.

**40. (D)** Ashoka likely chose everyday Prakrit so ordinary people could understand his messages directly, rather than refined Sanskrit. (A) and (B) are false; (C) reverses the purpose (he wanted the message public, not secret).

**41. (A)** Carving on rock and stone pillars made the messages permanent and public — stone survives weather and centuries. (B) is not the chapter's point; (C) is false; (D) confuses material with script.

**42. (B)** Brahmi is historically important as the ancestor of most of today's Indian scripts. It is not the Roman alphabet (A); it WAS deciphered (by James Prinsep) so (C) is false; it is a script, not a court language (D).

**43. (C)** The Lion Capital of the Sarnath pillar was adopted as India's national emblem. The Great Stūpa at Sanchi (A), the Dhauli rock elephant (B) and a terracotta figure (D) are other ancient artworks, not the emblem.

**44. (B)** Here "capital" means the carved top, or head, of the stone column (from Latin *caput*, "head") — a different word from a capital city (A). It is not the treasury (C) or an edict (D).

**45. (D)** Ashoka set up the pillar at Sarnath because that is where the Buddha gave his first teaching. (A), (B), (C) are inventions — Sarnath's significance is its link to the Buddha.

**46. (A)** The dharmachakra is the "wheel of dharma", symbolising the Buddha's teachings; it sits at the centre of India's national flag. It is not a chariot wheel (B), a sun-god (C), or trade routes (D).

**47. (B)** The Maurya empire ended around 185 BCE, roughly half a century after Ashoka's death (232 BCE) — successors could not hold it together. (A), (C), (D) misjudge the span.

**48. (C)** Empires are "fragile" because they rest mainly on force and the emperor's strength, so distant regions break away when the centre weakens. (A) is false (they were large); (B) is false (they had armies); (D) is false (tribute was collected).

**49. (D)** A weak emperor following a strong one, combined with heavier tribute demands, most tempts a distant province to break away. (A), (B), (C) all describe *stabilising* conditions that would make breaking away less likely.

**50. (A)** The paradox: an empire can bring unity and prosperity, yet because it rests on war and force and distant regions drift away, it is fragile and tends to decline. (B), (C), (D) misstate what an empire is.

**51. (C)** Comparing the two: the Nandas first unified Magadha's lands (showing early imperial features), and the Mauryas then took over and expanded much further. (A) reverses who carved edicts; (B) reverses the order; (D) is false — they ruled in succession, not as simultaneous rivals.

**52. (B)** Chronology: (i) Mahāpadma Nanda (5th century BCE) → (iv) Alexander reaches the northwest (c. 326 BCE) → (iii) Chandragupta founds the Maurya empire (c. 321 BCE) → (ii) Ashoka's Kalinga war (during 268–232 BCE). So the order is (i), (iv), (iii), (ii).

**53. (D)** A satrap was a governor of a province, left by the overlord to manage a far-off territory (in the Persian and Greek empires). He was not a bodyguard (B), a guild (C), or a payment (A — that is tribute).

**54. (B)** Both let an emperor rule distant lands indirectly, but the key difference is that a tributary keeps his *own* throne while paying tribute, whereas a satrap is an *appointed* provincial governor with no kingdom of his own. (A) reverses this; (C) and (D) are false.

**55. (A)** Well-planned, prosperous, food-secure cities show strong administration and control over resources at work — two of the six features. (B), (C), (D) describe the *absence* of features, which is the opposite of what prosperous cities show.

**56. (A)** If Dhana Nanda had ruled justly and kept the people's loyalty, the Nanda empire might have lasted longer and resisted Chandragupta's challenge (which depended on Nanda unpopularity). (B), (C), (D) are unrelated leaps the chapter does not support.

**57. (B)** Had Ashoka felt no remorse after Kalinga, he might have kept waging aggressive wars — and the dhamma edicts, which sprang from that remorse, would not exist. (A), (C), (D) are unconnected consequences.

**58. (D)** An empire with army, officials, coins and roads but no support for art and learning lacks the unifying culture that helps bind its many peoples together — one of the six features. (A), (B), (C) are false; the missing feature is cultural unity, not soldiers, tribute or forts.

**59. (C)** The spread of Ashokan edicts, especially far-flung ones, is most useful for telling us roughly how far his empire and influence reached. (A), (B), (D) are things an edict's location cannot reveal.

**60. (D)** The chapter's deepest lesson is that building an empire is easier than holding one together, so even the greatest empires eventually decline. (A), (B), (C) all contradict the chapter's point about fragility and the six features.
