## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** The four parts of a state are population, territory, government and sovereignty. This community plainly has a settled population, fixed borders (territory) and the unoverruled power to make its own laws (sovereignty); what it lacks is a body actually running affairs, which is the government. Population is wrong because the people are already settled there. Territory is wrong because the borders are fixed. Sovereignty is wrong because no outside power overrules its law-making, which is exactly what sovereignty means.

**2. (B)** A state's four parts are population, territory, government and sovereignty; of the six listed, population, territory and sovereignty are three genuine parts (the missing fourth is the government as a whole). The judiciary is one ORGAN inside the government, the provider role is one of the government's three ROLES, and the union tier is one of the three TIERS - none of these is a part of the state. So the option naming the judiciary, provider role and union tier lists three non-parts; the two mixed options each smuggle in an organ, a role or a tier alongside genuine parts, so they are wrong.

**3. (D)** The analogy maps the enduring institution (the school) to the state, which is a political organisation of population, territory and sovereignty - not a set of buildings. Saying the building is the whole school repeats the very 'state = buildings' error the chapter rejects. Saying the teachers are the lasting institution reverses the analogy, since teachers (the government) are the changing part. Saying the school ends when teachers leave contradicts the analogy's whole point, that the institution endures while staff come and go.

**4. (B)** Sovereignty is the full power to make and enforce one's own laws without outside control; once X's people live entirely under a neighbour's laws with no power to make their own, sovereignty is gone and X is no longer a state - this is the decisive loss. A flag is a symbol, not one of the four parts (population, territory, government, sovereignty). An independent judiciary is an organ within the government, not itself a part of the state. A standing army is state machinery, not a defining part - a state can exist without one, so its absence does not end statehood.

**5. (D)** The state is the larger whole composed of population, territory, government and sovereignty; the government is only one of those four parts. Making the government the whole and the state a part inverts the true whole-part relation. Treating territory as the whole that contains the other parts mislabels one part as the whole - territory is land, one of four parts, not the container of all. Treating sovereignty as the whole likewise promotes one part above the state itself, which the chapter does not do.

**6. (C)** Population (citizens within the borders), territory (agreed borders) and government (an elected parliament and cabinet) are all present; the defect is that a neighbour can veto its laws, so it lacks full sovereignty - the power to make laws free of outside control. Electing a parliament does not by itself create sovereignty, so 'complete state' is wrong. Agreed borders are still real borders, so it does not lack territory. It does have a functioning parliament and cabinet, so it does not lack a government - the vetoes limit its sovereignty, not its possession of a government.

**7. (A)** Only the government (one of the state's four parts) changes when the ruling party changes; the people, borders and self-rule (population, territory, sovereignty) are the enduring state, which persisted. Saying the state was rebuilt while the government stayed identical reverses which part changes. Saying both dissolved each time ignores that the people, land and sovereignty continued unbroken. Saying nothing changed is wrong because the government genuinely did change three times.

**8. (D)** Making, enforcing and interpreting laws is the government's job, done through the legislature (makes), executive (enforces) and judiciary (interprets). The population votes and is governed but does not itself draft, police and judge every law. Territory is land, not an actor that performs tasks. Sovereignty is the backing power behind the laws, not an organ that drafts and judges - that work requires the government's organs.

**9. (C)** When a party loses, a new government simply forms; the body of laws continues in force until changed, and sovereignty (independence) belongs to the state, not to a party - so neither claim is true. Saying both are true ties the laws and sovereignty to one party's term, confusing the government with the whole state. The two partial options each keep one of these errors, wrongly attaching either the laws or sovereignty to the defeated party rather than to the enduring state.

**10. (A)** The big oval holding four shapes is the state (population, territory, government, sovereignty); the inner shape split into 'makes / enforces / interprets' is the government with its legislature, executive and judiciary. Labelling P the government and Q the state inverts the whole-part relation, since the state is the larger whole. Sovereignty and territory are single parts that do not split into three law-handling organs, so 'P sovereignty, Q territory' is wrong. The population and union tier likewise do not split into three law-handling organs, so the last option is wrong too.

**11. (C)** The government is one part of the state and changes at elections; the state - people, territory and sovereignty - endures across many governments, so the country can be far older than the current government. There is a real flaw, so 'no flaw' is wrong. Governments certainly can change in five years (that is the normal election cycle), so that is not the flaw. The state changes more SLOWLY than the government, not faster, so the last option reverses the relationship.

**12. (B)** Sovereignty is the power to make and enforce one's own laws free of outside control; a tax passed, upheld at home, and beyond any foreign power's reach is exactly that power in action. Voting expresses democracy (who rules), not sovereignty over law-making. Marking a boundary concerns territory, a different part of the state. A minister breaking a rule unpunished is a failure of the rule of law, not an exercise of sovereignty.

**13. (A)** Sovereignty is the unoverruled power to make and enforce one's own laws. M keeps the final say even while cooperating, so it remains sovereign; N must obey a foreign capital, so an outside power controls its laws and its sovereignty is incomplete. Voluntary cooperation where the state keeps the last word does not end sovereignty, so 'both lack it' is wrong. Merely signing an agreement does not by itself prove sovereignty, so 'both fully sovereign' is wrong. Being controlled from abroad (N) is the opposite of sovereignty, so saying N is sovereign and M is not reverses the truth.

**14. (D)** Sovereignty is a state's recognised authority to make and enforce its own laws free of outside control; it is a matter of lawful authority, not the size of an army. Equating it with military strength is the error, so 'correct' is wrong. Sovereignty does not require having no army; a peaceful state can be fully sovereign, so that option is wrong. Sovereignty means NO outside power controls or approves one's laws, so saying it means a foreign power approves the laws describes the opposite of sovereignty.

**15. (C)** Elections change the political executive (the ministers), while the permanent machinery - armed forces, police and civil servants - continues so the state runs smoothly; a real change occurred even though the permanent staff stayed. Sacking neutral staff for the winning party would politicise the state machinery and does not happen, so the complaint is wrong. The ministers and the permanent staff are different things, so 'one and the same' is false. Permanent staff continuing does not make them the government; they remain state machinery the government directs.

**16. (B)** The bureaucracy (permanent executive) is non-elected, exam-selected and continues across governments; the political executive is the elected ministers who set direction and change at elections - that is the sharp contrast. Saying the bureaucracy is elected and ministers are exam-selected swaps the two. Neither the bureaucracy nor the ministers makes laws - that is the legislature's job. Saying the bureaucracy changes at every election while ministers are permanent reverses which one actually turns over.

**17. (A)** The police are state machinery the elected government directs; they enforce laws but do not make or interpret them and are not the government, so holding weapons or making arrests does not make a police chief the head of government. Equating force with governing is the very misconception the chapter rejects, so 'it agrees' is wrong. The police do have a role (enforcement), so calling them mere population with no role is wrong. The police belong to the state, not a foreign power, so the last option is absurd.

**18. (C)** The permanent executive serves the state, not a party, and continues across governments to provide stable, experienced administration - so the demand to resign at a loss misunderstands the role. The bureaucracy is NOT replaced when a party loses; that continuity is its purpose, so 'should resign' is wrong. Civil servants implement the lawful decisions of ministers and can be lawfully instructed, so 'outranks the minister' is wrong. The bureaucracy enforces, it does not make laws (the legislature does), so 'makes the laws' is wrong.

**19. (C)** The army is state machinery that the elected government directs - claim 3 is correct. Claim 1 wrongly lists the army among the four parts (which are population, territory, government and sovereignty), and claim 2 wrongly equates the army with the government; both confuse machinery the government uses with either a part of the state or the government itself. So claim 1 is not correct, claim 2 is not correct, and 'all three correct' cannot hold because claims 1 and 2 contradict the correct claim 3.

**20. (A)** Democracy answers WHO rules - the people, via free elections; republic answers whether the HEAD OF STATE is elected rather than inheriting the post. The first option keeps these distinct and accurate. The second reverses both meanings. The third attaches them to territory and army, which are unrelated to these terms. The fourth wrongly treats them as identical, when each adds a different idea.

**21. (A)** Free elections for the lawmakers make Spain a democracy (the people rule); a hereditary king as head of state means it is not a republic, which requires an elected head of state - so it is a democracy but not a republic. 'Republic but not democracy' reverses the facts. 'Both' ignores the hereditary king that blocks republic status. 'Neither' ignores the free elections that clearly make it a democracy.

**22. (A)** Electing the head of state instead of inheriting it makes the country a republic; but with only one permitted party and no real choice, it lacks the free and fair elections that define democracy - so it is a republic but not a true democracy. 'Democracy but not republic' reverses this, since the head is now elected. 'Both' ignores the absence of genuine choice. It does have an elected head, so 'neither' and 'no elected head' are wrong.

**23. (D)** A republic requires an elected, non-hereditary head of state, so a country with a hereditary monarch can NEVER be a republic - 'republic = yes' is impossible. It CAN be a democracy if it elects its lawmakers (like the UK), so 'democracy = yes' is possible. It can also fail to be a democracy if elections are not free, so 'democracy = no' is possible. 'Republic = no' is simply the correct, possible classification for a monarchy, not an impossible case.

**24. (A)** Democracy (the people rule) and republic (the head of state is elected, not hereditary) are distinct; a country can be one without the other, as the UK is a democracy but not a republic, so they do not always arrive together. The claim that every democracy is automatically a republic is the error itself, so 'it is sound' is wrong. There is no rule that a republic must precede democracy, so that ordering claim is wrong. Many countries are both a democracy and a republic, so 'no democracy can be a republic' is also false.

**25. (B)** G holds free elections (democracy) and elects its head of state (republic), so it is both. H holds free elections (democracy) but keeps a hereditary monarch, so it is a democracy but not a republic. Saying G is a republic but not a democracy ignores its free elections that make it democratic. Free elections for lawmakers do not make a country a republic - that depends on the head of state - so 'both are republics' is wrong. Having a head of state does not rule out democracy, so 'neither is a democracy' is false.

**26. (D)** Constitutional rights and the rule of law protect minorities from the 'dominance of the majority,' which is why the court could strike the vote down - the majority cannot trample protected rights. The minority does not rule over the majority; rights protect it, they do not give it rule. Majority votes are valid for many ordinary decisions, just not for violating rights, so 'never valid' is wrong. The claim that only the majority has rights is the very misconception being corrected.

**27. (D)** The chapter stresses that constitutional rights and the rule of law bind even a large majority, so a 60% vote cannot strip protected rights - that is the strongest objection. Saying a 60% majority can pass anything endorses the very error. Saying only minorities may decide swings to the opposite extreme and is not what the chapter holds. Saying democracies have no limiting laws contradicts the rule of law, which is precisely the limit at work here.

**28. (C)** Protecting minorities demonstrates that the rule of law - which binds everyone, including a majority - is stronger than the mere wishes of the numerically larger group. Saying a large majority always overrides the law inverts this. Saying minorities have no protection contradicts the guaranteed rights at work. Saying the Constitution can be set aside by more votes denies the rule of law that the example upholds.

**29. (A)** The rule of law holds that everyone, including the government, its ministers and the police, must follow the law - so the citizen is right that officials are bound just like ordinary people. Saying it binds only ordinary citizens contradicts the core idea. Saying the government writes its own exceptions places the government above the law, which the rule of law rejects. Saying the law applies only to minorities and the weak is the opposite of equal application under the rule of law.

**30. (C)** Matsya nyaya, the law of the fishes, describes a lawless world where the powerful prey on the weak; a government under the rule of law exists to keep order and prevent exactly that. It does not mean automatic equality - lawlessness produces domination, not equality. It is a metaphor, not a claim that fish legislate. And it is the STRONG who dominate the weak, not the reverse, so the last option misreads the saying.

**31. (B)** Matsya nyaya is the lawless state where the strong prey on the weak with no accountability - exactly what happens when courts and police collapse and gangs dominate. The other three are the OPPOSITE, showing the rule of law working to restrain the powerful: a court convicting a rich owner, citizens voting peacefully, and an inspector fining a strong factory all demonstrate accountability, not its absence.

**32. (D)** Matsya nyaya lets the strong dominate the weak; the rule of law is its opposite because it subjects everyone, including the powerful and the government, to the law and so shields the weak. Letting the powerful ignore the law would BE matsya nyaya, not its opposite. Applying the law only to the weak denies equal application. The rule of law needs a government to enforce it, so abolishing the government would undermine it, not define it.

**33. (A)** Lawlessness slides toward matsya nyaya, the domination of the weak by the strong, which is exactly why a government and the rule of law are needed to keep order and protect the weak. The other options imagine that equality, a Constitution, or the rule of law would appear by themselves with no enforcer, contradicting the chapter's point that order must be actively maintained by a government.

**34. (D)** The rule of law means everyone, including the leader, must follow the law and answer to independent courts; a leader who ignores courts and silences critics breaks this and risks the strong-over-weak domination of matsya nyaya. Saying there is no danger redefines the rule of law as the leader's will, which is the opposite of the principle. The deeper problem is the breach of principle, not merely whether crime falls. Courts checking a leader does not make courts the law-makers; the legislature still makes laws, so the last option misstates the issue.

**35. (A)** Debating, amending and voting a bill into law is law-making, the legislature's defining function. It is not executive work, since no existing law is being carried out. It is not judicial work, since no dispute over an existing law is being decided. And it is not bureaucratic work, since elected representatives - not civil servants - are passing the bill, so administration is not what is happening here.

**36. (A)** 'Legislature' (law + proposing) matches the organ that makes laws; 'executive' (to carry out) matches the organ that enforces laws. Saying legislature fits enforcing and executive fits making swaps the two roots. Neither root describes the judiciary, whose task is to judge and interpret, so 'both fit the judiciary' is wrong. The last option misassigns both roots to the wrong organs.

**37. (B)** Passing a bill into law is the legislature's work; issuing instructions to carry it out is the executive's; deciding whether someone broke the law is the judiciary's - so the order is legislature, executive, judiciary. The other options scramble this mapping, for example placing enforcement or judging before law-making, or swapping the executive and judiciary, none of which matches what each organ actually does.

**38. (B)** The executive has two parts: ministers (political executive) set direction and change at elections, while the bureaucracy (permanent executive) actually runs daily administration across governments - so ministers do not personally process every file. Saying ministers do everything alone ignores the bureaucracy. Courts interpret law and do not run administration. The legislature makes laws and does not manage offices day to day, so those options are wrong.

**39. (C)** Ministers form the political executive - elected, direction-setting and changing with elections; civil servants form the permanent executive - exam-selected and continuing across governments for stable administration. The first distractor swaps the two. The second wrongly makes both elected and temporary, whereas the bureaucracy is permanent. The third wrongly makes both exam-selected and permanent, ignoring that ministers are elected and do set policy direction.

**40. (D)** The permanent executive stays on across governments precisely to provide stable, continuous administration regardless of who is in power - that is the value of the continuity. Civil servants implement, but do not make, laws (the legislature does), so the first distractor is wrong. Staying on does not make her the head of state. And her staying on shows the bureaucracy does NOT change completely after each election - the opposite of the last distractor.

**41. (B)** Judicial review lets the judiciary examine a law and strike it down for breaching the Constitution - the watchdog role - but writing and passing a replacement law is the legislature's job, not the court's. So the court cannot rewrite and pass a new law. It is not powerless against an unconstitutional law, so 'do nothing' is wrong. And ordering a fresh election is not within the judiciary's role of checking laws, so that option is wrong too.

**42. (C)** Independence lets the judiciary check even the government fairly, upholding the rule of law that everyone, including the government, must follow. The distractors all describe the OPPOSITE of independence - courts controlled by the ruling party, courts obeying ministers, or no checks at all - each of which would let those in power escape the law instead of being held to it.

**43. (B)** The legislature makes laws, the executive enforces them, and the judiciary interprets them and settles disputes - the only fully correct set. Each distractor misassigns at least one verb, for example making the legislature enforce, the executive interpret or make, or the judiciary make or enforce - none of which matches the organs' actual functions.

**44. (B)** Judicial review is the power to examine and strike down unconstitutional laws and actions - it does NOT include writing and passing new laws, which is the legislature's job, so that falls outside it. Examining whether a law violates the Constitution, striking down an unconstitutional government action, and checking a law against constitutional rights are all squarely within judicial review.

**45. (A)** Parliament passing the law is legislative; police enforcing it is executive; the court striking it down for breaching a right is judicial review by the judiciary - the first option maps these accurately. Each distractor scrambles the organs, for instance making the judiciary legislate, the police make laws, or Parliament strike down laws - none of which matches who actually performs each step.

**46. (D)** Judicial review lets independent courts strike down laws that violate the Constitution, upholding the rule of law that binds even an elected government - so it is not anti-democratic. Saying an elected government's laws may never be reviewed denies judicial review and the rule of law. Review does not depend on which party won; it depends on whether a law breaches the Constitution. And judicial review covers both laws and government actions, so 'only actions, never laws' is wrong.

**47. (A)** Keeping students safe is the protector role, running the library and meals is the provider role (supplying services), and setting and enforcing fair rules is the regulator role - the government's triple role. The distractors instead list the three organs, the three tiers, and the four parts of a state, none of which describes 'keeps safe / supplies services / sets and enforces rules.'

**48. (B)** Supplying services such as public hospitals is the provider role. Guarding the coast with the navy is the protector role (keeping people safe and defending the country). Inspecting restaurants to enforce hygiene standards is the regulator role (setting and enforcing rules). Counting election results is part of running elections, not any of the three roles - so only building and staffing hospitals is purely provision.

**49. (D)** Both inspecting factories for safety and fixing fair market rules are about setting and enforcing rules - the regulator role. The other pairs mix protector actions (border defence, coastline defence) with provider actions (building a hospital, building roads, supplying textbooks), and one even includes counting votes, which is election administration; none of those pairs is purely regulation.

**50. (C)** Guarding the border is protection, running clinics is provision, and inspecting shops for pricing rules is regulation - all three roles together. Each distractor shows only ONE role: defence only, services only, or rule-setting only - so none demonstrates all three the way the correct option does.

**51. (B)** RTI brings transparency by revealing the government's actions, and a free media spreads that information so citizens can question the government - together they hold the government accountable. Judicial review is the courts' power to strike down unconstitutional laws, which RTI and the media do not do. Setting business rules is the government's regulator role, not a citizen's accountability tool. And making laws is the legislature's job, not what RTI and the media do, so 'exercising sovereignty' is wrong.

**52. (D)** RTI, a free media, civil-society organising, and voting a poor government out are all genuine, lawful accountability tools. The other lists describe the OPPOSITE or the unlawful: refusing to vote, one-minister rule and scrapping the Constitution remove accountability; appointing oneself a judge, dismissing soldiers or writing laws personally are not lawful citizen powers; and ignoring elections, attacking courts and obeying a gang invite matsya nyaya rather than accountability.

**53. (A)** Removing a poor government by vote holds it answerable for its record, which is accountability. Sovereignty is the state's power over its OWN laws, not power over a foreign state. The regulator role is the GOVERNMENT setting rules, not citizens acting. And judicial review is a power of the COURTS, not of voters - so none of those names what the citizens are doing.

**54. (C)** Grievance redressal lets citizens complain about and resolve problems with government services, holding the administration accountable - distinct from judicial review, which is a court's power to strike down unconstitutional laws. Complaining about a service is not the same as a court striking down a law, so calling it judicial review is wrong. Sovereignty is the state's power over its own laws, not a citizen's service complaint. And decentralisation is about WHERE decisions are made (which tier), not about a citizen fixing a stopped pension.

**55. (C)** The three TIERS - levels at which government operates - are union, state and local. The wrong options mislabel the three ORGANS (legislature, executive, judiciary), the three ROLES (protector, provider, regulator), and three PARTS of a state (population, territory, sovereignty) as 'tiers,' a classic mix-up. Only union, state and local are the tiers.

**56. (D)** Letting a local body decide a local matter is decentralisation, and it fits federalism, which shares power between the centre and state and local governments. It is the OPPOSITE of concentrating power at the centre, so that option (and its false definition of federalism) is wrong. It relies on the local tier rather than abolishing it. And decentralisation has nothing to do with foreign control, so that definition is also false.

**57. (B)** Federalism shares power between the centre and state and local governments; decentralisation lets local matters be decided locally - the correct option shows both, with shared tiers and local bodies handling local matters. The distractors are non-federal or non-decentralised: total central control over everything, no centre at all, or one ruler approving every decision - none combines shared power with local decision-making.

**58. (B)** The three organs of government are defined by their law-related functions: the legislature makes laws, the executive enforces laws, and the judiciary interprets laws and settles disputes. 'Is elected by the people every five years' describes how a government is chosen, not the law-handling function of an organ, so it is the odd one out. 'Makes the laws' is exactly the legislature's function, 'enforces the laws' is the executive's, and 'interprets the laws and settles disputes' is the judiciary's - so each of those genuinely names an organ's function and is not the odd one out.

**59. (D)** All three claims are misconceptions. The government is just one of the state's four parts (population, territory, government, sovereignty). The majority cannot do anything it wants, because rights and the rule of law protect minorities. Not every democracy is a republic - the UK is a democracy with a hereditary monarch. So zero are correct, and only the full three-part correction fixes them; the other options leave one or more of these errors standing.

**60. (C)** The correct synthesis is that the enduring state has four parts; the government is one part, working through three organs and three roles and across three tiers, and it changes at elections, with everyone bound by the rule of law. The distractors embed familiar errors: swapping state and government and invoking unlimited majority rule; conflating state and government under the majority's will; and confusing the three tiers with the three organs - each contradicts the chapter.
