## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** The Arabs had already reached Sindh, so the Turkic wave was not the 'first' arrival; what set it apart was reaching deep into the north and finally giving lasting rule (the Ghurids, then the Delhi Sultanate). It was a military invasion, not peaceful trade, and no Gupta empire was restored, since the Guptas were long gone.

**2. (C)** Both waves were invasions, so the difference lay in how far they reached: the Turks went deep while the Arabs stayed near Sindh. Neither wave conquered the whole subcontinent (the south stayed free), both did cross into India, and the Cholas were a southern Indian sea power, not invaders.

**3. (A)** Resilience means continuing to flourish, shown by new scholarship, philosophy and temple art DURING the invasions. Kingdoms were not all levelled, the invaders returned repeatedly, and a pause in learning would be the opposite of resilience.

**4. (D)** The Khyber and similar passes were established overland routes, making the north-west the recurring entry point. India had a long coast (the Cholas were a sea power), the passes were mountain routes rather than Chola bases, and the south had wealthy kingdoms it simply was not reached by these overland invaders.

**5. (B)** Since the passes were the gateway for overland invaders, closing them would have made repeated entry much harder. Mahmud raided rather than ruled regardless of the passes, the Chola navy was a southern sea matter unaffected by northern passes, and al-Biruni's Sanskrit study did not depend on the passes.

**6. (D)** A gateway invaders keep using is a weak point, not a safe one. The north-west was a land frontier rather than sea-sealed, it lay in the north rather than being a southern port, and far from protecting India best it was the very route invaders exploited.

**7. (B)** The Hindu Shahis defended the north-western frontier the passes opened up. The Tamil south and Brihadishvara belonged to the Cholas, the Sultanate was founded later by Aibak, and the Srivijaya voyage was Rajendra Chola's.

**8. (D)** Ruling means staying to govern; Mahmud instead seized loot and returned to Ghazni after each raid. He did reach India repeatedly and was not always defeated, he ruled neither north nor south, and the Delhi Sultanate was founded later by Aibak, a Ghurid general.

**9. (B)** Mahmud's notorious sack was of Somnath, while Brihadishvara was Rajaraja Chola's temple in the south, untouched by him. Angkor Wat is a Cambodian temple, the original claim is false, and Nalanda was a Buddhist university (later destroyed by Bakhtiyar Khilji), not Brihadishvara.

**10. (C)** Plunder is loot grabbed by force, with the raider then leaving, which matches Mahmud exactly, unlike conquest, which means keeping and governing land. A treaty is an agreement, and the tower over a sanctum is a vimana, an unrelated term.

**11. (A)** Great loot can tempt one to assume rule, but raiders gather wealth and leave without holding land, so the treasure alone does not prove rule, and he did not rule. He clearly entered India many times, he sacked rather than built temples, and he had no link to the Chola navy.

**12. (B)** The core difference is leaving versus staying-to-rule: raiders carry off loot and withdraw, conquerors keep and govern. A raider does not build an empire (that is the very distinction), the words are not synonyms, and the last option simply scrambles the roles.

**13. (B)** Mahmud ruled from Ghazni and returned there with his loot, which is exactly why he is called 'of Ghazni' and never ruled India. Delhi's sultanate came later under Aibak, Kannauj was an Indian city he did not rule from, and Srivijaya was the distant destination of Rajendra Chola's fleet.

**14. (D)** The dividing line is leaving versus staying: Mahmud raided and withdrew, the Ghurids stayed to rule (Aibak's Sultanate followed). Both reached India, Mahmud did not govern for centuries, and the Ghurids came through the north-west and did not absorb the southern Cholas.

**15. (A)** 'From plunder to rule' marks the move from Mahmud's loot-and-leave raiding to the Ghurids' staying-to-govern. It is not a shift from ruling to raiding, the Cholas remained a sea power, and the Hindu Shahis defended the frontier rather than plundering Ghazni.

**16. (C)** Lasting rule began with the Ghurids because they stayed and governed, with Aibak's Sultanate following, unlike Mahmud who repeatedly withdrew. Both reached India, Mahmud did not govern, and the Ghurids came through the north-west, not from the south, and did not absorb the Cholas.

**17. (D)** Mahmud's raids came first (early 11th century), the Ghurids then won and stayed to rule (after 1192), and Aibak, a Ghurid general, then founded the Delhi Sultanate. The other orders put a Tarain-era event before Mahmud or place Aibak before the Ghurid victory that made his Sultanate possible.

**18. (C)** Force was common to both, but only the Ghurids stayed to govern, giving the Delhi Sultanate, while Mahmud left no government behind. Mahmud's raids did not create the sultanate, the Ghurids did not merely loot, and their conquest plainly did have a lasting result.

**19. (A)** Aibak, a Ghurid general, founded the Delhi Sultanate after the Ghurid victory, long after Mahmud's raids, so Mahmud cannot be among its founders. Mahmud did reach India (many times), the Hindu Shahis defended the frontier rather than founding the Sultanate, and the Cholas were a southern sea power, not its founders.

**20. (C)** Al-Biruni mastering Sanskrit to study Indian sciences, religion and customs shows learning continued and was two-way. This directly contradicts the claims that foreigners learned nothing, that Indian learning stopped, or that no books were written.

**21. (D)** Al-Biruni did the genuine scholarly work of learning Sanskrit and studying India carefully, the mark of real inquiry rather than propaganda. He certainly did write a study, did not refuse to study India, and the universities were destroyed by Bakhtiyar Khilji, not al-Biruni.

**22. (A)** Two-way learning needs flow in both directions: a foreigner studying India and Indian knowledge spreading abroad, exactly the given pairing. The other options describe destruction, one-way borrowing, or no exchange, none of which is two-way.

**23. (A)** Sine tables travelling out proves India exported knowledge as well as receiving it. India clearly had mathematics (Bhaskaracharya, the sine tables themselves), it did contribute rather than only borrow, and its knowledge plainly did leave the subcontinent.

**24. (A)** 'Al-jabr' means 'reunion of broken parts,' from al-Khwarizmi's book, giving the word algebra. The cosmic mountain is Mount Meru, a fleet of ships is a navy, and a short Kannada devotional poem is a vachana, all unrelated terms.

**25. (C)** Trigonometry deals with triangles and angles, the tools astronomers needed to chart the heavens. Gateways (toranas), warships (a navy) and Kannada poems (vachanas) are unrelated terms from other parts of the chapter.

**26. (B)** An Arabic-named technique and an Indian export side by side show mathematics crossing cultures. It was not purely Indian and isolated, was not the work of generals, and was not abandoned, since Bhaskaracharya was advancing it.

**27. (D)** A great Indian mathematician-astronomer working during the invasions shows Indian science endured and advanced, the chapter's resilience theme. His work was Indian rather than foreign, mathematics did not vanish, and science thrived in the free south too, not only in raided areas.

**28. (D)** A torana is an ornamental gateway, like the one the Kakatiyas built at Warangal. The tower over a shrine is a vimana, a village assembly is a sabha, and a subordinate ruler is a samanta, distinct terms students may confuse.

**29. (C)** Active southern dynasties building monuments show the south stayed free and creative even as the north was invaded. This refutes the ideas that all India fell, that the Deccan was Ghazni-ruled (Mahmud only raided and withdrew), or that the south lacked kingdoms, since the Kakatiyas and Hoysalas were exactly such kingdoms.

**30. (D)** The Kakatiyas, Hoysalas and Cholas were all Indian powers of the south and Deccan that thrived in the period. None were Turkic invaders, none founded the Sultanate (Aibak did), and none were Central Asian allies of Mahmud.

**31. (B)** The label 'scholar-king' marks Bhoja's pairing of ruling with a genuine love of learning. He did rule (so 'never held a throne' is wrong), he was an Indian Paramara king rather than a foreign invader, and he had no link to the Chola navy.

**32. (A)** Rajaraja built Chola sea power and the Brihadishvara temple, and Rajendra extended Chola reach by sea, so together they show a flourishing southern dynasty. Neither raided Somnath (Mahmud did), neither fought at Tarain (Prithviraj and Ghuri did), and neither founded Nalanda.

**33. (A)** Sailing all the way to Srivijaya in Southeast Asia shows the Cholas were a powerful maritime force reaching far overseas. They were not landlocked, not Ghurid vassals, and clearly aware of distant lands they could reach by sea.

**34. (D)** A towering Chola temple shows grand architecture thriving in the independent south. The Cholas built it well before the Sultanate (which came later in the north), Mahmud raided temples rather than sponsoring them, and the south was producing some of its greatest temples, not abandoning them.

**35. (C)** A navy is the war-fleet (the Cholas had a strong one), and a vimana is the tall tower over a South Indian temple's main shrine, as at Brihadishvara. The other options swap the two, conflate them with a gateway (torana), or mislabel them as a poem (vachana) and an assembly (sabha).

**36. (C)** The contrast shows two theatres: a strong maritime south and a north hit by overland raiders, so power was split. It was not concentrated in Ghazni (Mahmud withdrew there but did not rule India), not wholly maritime (many land kingdoms existed), and not limited to the Hindu Shahis.

**37. (D)** Mahmud's raids fall in the early 11th century, the First Battle of Tarain was 1191, and the Second was 1192. So the order is raids, then First, then Second. The other sequences put a Tarain battle before Mahmud or reverse the two battles (1192 cannot precede 1191).

**38. (C)** Ghuri lost the First Battle of Tarain (1191) to Prithviraj, then came back to win the Second (1192). He did not win or conquer all India in 1191, was not killed (he returned the next year), and the distant southern Cholas had no part in these northern land battles.

**39. (B)** Ghuri's win in 1192 broke Prithviraj's resistance and led to lasting Ghurid rule, soon the Delhi Sultanate under Aibak. He won rather than lost in 1192, the Chola navy was untouched by a northern land battle, and Mahmud's raids came long before 1192, not after.

**40. (B)** Reversing them would suggest Prithviraj failed first and then beat back the Ghurids, obscuring how the 1192 defeat opened the way to Ghurid rule. The reversal has nothing to do with Mahmud (a Ghaznavid, not a Tarain combatant), the Cholas, or the much earlier Arab arrival in Sindh.

**41. (B)** Aibak, Muhammad Ghuri's general, established the Delhi Sultanate, turning conquest into lasting rule. The Srivijaya voyage was Rajendra Chola's, the careful study of India was al-Biruni's, and the Lingayat movement was Basavanna's.

**42. (C)** Aibak founding the Sultanate and Bakhtiyar Khilji destroying the universities together show the conquest both created new rule and destroyed old learning. It did not leave things unchanged, it went beyond mere raiding to governance (unlike Mahmud), and it had nothing to do with serving the Cholas.

**43. (A)** Nalanda and Vikramashila were renowned Buddhist universities, so destroying them was a heavy blow to learning, the chapter's point about the cost of the invasions. They were not naval bases, frontier forts or trading ports.

**44. (A)** Bakhtiyar Khilji destroyed Nalanda and Vikramashila; Mahmud's famous target was instead Somnath. Rajaraja Chola built temples in the south rather than destroying universities, al-Biruni was a scholar of India, and the Hindu Shahis defended the frontier.

**45. (A)** Vishishtadvaita holds that the world and souls are real and that loving devotion (bhakti) and surrender lead to liberation. It does not call the world unreal, does not make ritual sacrifice the sole path, and does not deny the gods.

**46. (D)** Basavanna's Anubhava mandapa was a hall where people of every background could meet and share ideas, embodying his rejection of caste. The Delhi Sultanate was Aibak's political creation, a torana is a gateway, and the Chola navy is a war-fleet.

**47. (B)** A vachana is a short Kannada poem, used by Basavanna to share his message of devotion and equality. A temple's tower is a vimana, a fleet is a navy, and a gateway is a torana.

**48. (A)** Welcoming people of every background to share ideas, while rejecting caste, marks equality and open dialogue as the movement's core values. It did not uphold caste hierarchy, did not promote rule by a sultan, and was a spiritual-social movement rather than a campaign of conquest.

**49. (D)** Ramanuja and Basava demonstrate vibrant new religious thought during the period, part of the resilience theme. This refutes the claims that there was no religious thought, that the age was only war, or that spiritual life was simply replaced by foreign rule.

**50. (D)** Two great temple complexes from different regions show the period's flourishing temple art and architecture. This contradicts the ideas that there was only warfare and no building, that India was Ghazni-ruled (Mahmud only raided), or that art had ceased.

**51. (B)** Angkor Wat's towers evoking Mount Meru show Indian ideas reaching Cambodia through cultural influence, not conquest. India did not conquer Cambodia, Cambodia did not rule India, and India had many great temples of its own, such as Khajuraho and Brihadishvara.

**52. (C)** Mount Meru is the cosmic mountain of Indian cosmology, symbolised by Angkor Wat's five towers, evidence of India's cultural reach. It is not a Ghaznavid fortress, a Chola warship, or a university like Nalanda.

**53. (B)** Magnificent home temples plus influence abroad show India as a flourishing, outward-reaching cultural centre. It was neither barren nor isolated, was not ruled everywhere by invaders (the south stayed free), and certainly did not forbid temple-building.

**54. (C)** A vimana is the tall tower above a temple's main shrine (as at Brihadishvara), and a torana is an ornamental gateway (as at Warangal). The others swap them, call them warships, or mislabel them as a vachana (poem) and a sultanate (a sultan's realm).

**55. (B)** Mahmud raided and withdrew, the Ghurids (Muhammad Ghuri) came to govern, and Aibak founded the Delhi Sultanate, the only fully correct chain. The other options scramble deeds: Mahmud did not found the Sultanate or rule the south, Aibak did not sail to Srivijaya (Rajendra Chola did) or write on algebra, Ghuri did not build Angkor Wat or destroy Nalanda (Bakhtiyar Khilji did), and the Khyber was guarded by the Hindu Shahis.

**56. (A)** The Cholas, Kakatiyas and Hoysalas were all free southern or Deccan powers, while the Ghurids were the north-western invaders who came to rule the north, so they are the odd one out and do not belong in this group.

**57. (D)** A foreign scholar studying India (al-Biruni) and an Indian advancing mathematics (Bhaskaracharya) together prove scholarship continued, refuting the claim that learning had ended. They do not bear on whether India had mathematics (it clearly did), nor on the south's freedom or the Chola navy, which are separate, true facts the pairing does not deny.

**58. (C)** The age's scholars, thinker-saints and temples, all flourishing during the invasions, directly disprove 'nothing but ruin.' Invasions did occur, the south was not conquered but flourished, and learning did not cease, since al-Biruni, Bhaskaracharya and others were active throughout.

**59. (A)** All three claims are false: Mahmud merely raided (lasting rule came later under the Ghurids), much of the north and all the south stayed free, and the period was rich in scholarship, devotion and temple art. The other options accept one or more of these misconceptions, including the false notion that Mahmud ruled India, and so are wrong.

**60. (C)** The 'turning tide' is the move from raiding to lasting rule in the north, set against a free, flourishing south and a vigorous culture, the chapter's balanced picture. The subcontinent did not all fall and culture did not die, the period was a clear change from before, and India was the one invaded overland, not the conqueror of the north-west or overseas.
