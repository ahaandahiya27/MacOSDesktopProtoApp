Classic traps: confusing weather with climate, blaming equatorial heat on nearness to the Sun, ignoring altitude and the moderating sea, reversing which monsoon is wet, mistaking the calm eye for the safe part of a cyclone, and assuming the wet windward and dry leeward sides of a range rain equally. 1. Three reports describe a single hill town: (i) 'It snowed here this morning,' (ii) 'Winters here are reliably cold and snowy every year,' (iii) 'The thermometer reads 2 degrees right now.' Which report alone describes climate rather than weather?
   (A) Only (i), because snow is always a sign of a place's climate
   (B) Only (iii), because a thermometer reading is the most scientific measure of climate
   (C) All three, because anything to do with cold and snow counts as climate
   (D) Only (ii), because it states the long-term average pattern repeated across many years

2. A pupil argues: 'Two cities both averaged exactly 600 mm of rain last year, so they must have the same climate.' What is the strongest flaw in this reasoning?
   (A) There is no flaw, since equal yearly rainfall guarantees identical climate
   (B) The flaw is that rainfall has nothing whatever to do with climate
   (C) The flaw is only that 600 mm is too small a figure to compare cities
   (D) One year's total cannot define climate, which is judged over many years, and rainfall alone ignores temperature and seasonal spread

3. Which single sentence below uses BOTH 'weather' and 'climate' correctly?

   (C) Yesterday's climate in Mumbai was humid, while its weather over thirty years is mild and even.
   (D) A place's weather never changes, whereas its climate shifts from hour to hour.
