Worked Solutions

1. (A) Blue litmus turns red only in an acid. Of the four, only lemon juice (citric acid) is an acid, so it is the single liquid that turns blue litmus red. Lime water and soap solution are both bases, which leave blue litmus unchanged (they turn red litmus blue instead). Sugar water is neutral and leaves blue litmus unchanged. Since exactly one strip turned red, that liquid is the acid, i.e. lemon juice.

2. (C) Turning blue litmus red is an acid property, so removing every such substance removes all the acids. Curd (lactic acid) and vinegar (acetic acid) are acids, so they are removed. Milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) and lime water are bases, which do not turn blue litmus red, so both remain. Keeping only milk of magnesia would wrongly discard the equally basic lime water; keeping vinegar would keep an acid.

3. (A) Tomato juice is an acid and milk of magnesia is a base. Mixed in exactly neutralising amounts they cancel, leaving the mixture neutral, so red litmus shows no change (it is already red, and only a base would turn it blue). Adding one extra drop of tomato juice tips the mixture slightly acidic, so it now turns blue litmus red. It would not turn red litmus blue (that needs excess base), and the neutral state is not permanent once more acid is added.

4. (A) Leaving both litmus papers unchanged is exactly the result of any neutral liquid. Distilled water, common salt water and sugar water are all neutral, so all three leave red and blue litmus unchanged and give identical results. The test therefore establishes neutrality, not identity; it cannot single out the distilled water. Salt water does not turn blue litmus red and sugar water does not turn red litmus blue (both are neutral, not acidic or basic), and dissolved solids do not make these solutions acidic.

5. (C) Turmeric turns red only in a base and stays yellow in acids and in neutral liquids. Soap solution is basic, so the paper turns red. Lemon juice is acidic, so the paper stays yellow. Plain water is neutral, so the paper stays yellow. The order is therefore red, yellow, yellow. Reversing red to the acid (lemon) misremembers turmeric's direction; turmeric does not turn red in acids or in water.

6. (C) China rose shows dark pink (magenta) in acids and green in bases. Vinegar is acidic (acetic acid), so it turns china rose magenta. Soap solution is basic, so it turns china rose green. Swapping the two reverses china rose's direction. China rose is not a single fixed colour, and it is not colourless like phenolphthalein in acid; it goes magenta there.

7. (C) Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a base. Lime water and milk of magnesia are bases, so both turn it pink, giving a count of 2. Tamarind water is acidic (tartaric acid) and sugar solution is neutral, so both leave phenolphthalein colourless. Counting 1 forgets that both lime water and milk of magnesia are bases; counting 3 wrongly includes an acid or neutral.

8. (A) Methyl orange is yellow in bases and red in acids. Lime water is a base, so methyl orange is yellow in it; dilute hydrochloric acid is an acid, so methyl orange is red in it. The order is yellow then red. Reversing them confuses the directions; methyl orange shows neither pink nor colourless (those are phenolphthalein behaviours), and the acid would not also be yellow.

9. (D) Bitter taste is a signature of a base no matter how strong or weak it is, and milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) is a base. A mild bitterness simply means a weakly basic taste, not an acid; the intensity of a taste does not switch the chemical family. An acid would taste sour, not bitter, so mild bitterness cannot point to an acid, and a neutral liquid has no bitter taste at all.

10. (D) Neutralisation gives a salt plus water, and the salt depends on which acid and base reacted. Here the acid (hydrochloric acid) is the same both times, but the bases differ: sodium hydroxide gives sodium chloride, while potassium hydroxide gives potassium chloride. So the two solids are different salts. They are not both common salt or both potassium chloride, none of the base remains after exact neutralisation, and matter is not destroyed, so a solid salt does form each time.

11. (D) Phenolphthalein is pink only in a base and colourless otherwise. While the washing soda solution was basic the indicator was pink; when the pink vanished the base had been neutralised and the solution was about neutral. Adding more vinegar (acid) makes the solution acidic, which is also colourless for phenolphthalein, so it stays colourless. Adding more liquid does not bring back pink, excess acid never turns phenolphthalein pink, and phenolphthalein has no deep-red colour.

12. (B) Turmeric turns red only in a base and stays yellow in acids and neutral liquids. P turns red litmus blue, so P is a base and turns turmeric red. Q turns blue litmus red, so Q is an acid and leaves turmeric yellow. R changes neither litmus, so R is neutral and leaves turmeric yellow. S turns blue litmus red, so S is an acid and leaves turmeric yellow. Hence three of the four (Q, R and S) stay yellow, and only P (the base) turns red.

13. (C) A salt is named from the metal of the base combined with the acid part of the acid, exactly as the worked example shows (sodium from the base + sulphate from the acid → sodium sulphate). Applying the same rule, hydrochloric acid (acid part: chloride) with potassium hydroxide (metal: potassium) gives potassium chloride. It is not sodium chloride, since no sodium is present — chloride does not 'always' pair with sodium — and the acid is not regenerated by neutralisation.

14. (C) Baking soda is a base; lemon juice is an acid, so they undergo a neutralisation that releases carbon dioxide gas, seen as fizzing. Sugar water is neutral, so there is no acid for the baking soda to react with and little gas is produced. Lemon juice is the acid (not a base) and sugar water is neutral (not an acid). The bubbles are a gas produced by a real reaction, not trapped air, which is why they appear strongly only with the acidic lemon juice.

15. (C) Each sting is relieved by neutralising it with the opposite family. Stinging nettle injects an acid, so a mild base — baking soda paste — neutralises it.
