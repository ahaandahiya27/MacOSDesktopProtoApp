**Instructions:** Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. 1. You must construct the perpendicular bisector of a segment PQ that is 8 cm long, but your compass can open to a maximum radius of only 3 cm. Using arcs of equal radius from P and from Q, can the perpendicular bisector be constructed?
   (A) No, because the arcs need a radius greater than half of PQ (more than 4 cm) so that the two arcs cross on each side; 3 cm is too small and the arcs never meet.
   (B) Yes, any compass radius works as long as it is the same from both ends.
   (C) Yes, because 3 cm is less than the full 8 cm length, which is the only requirement.
   (D) No, because the radius must be exactly half of PQ, namely 4 cm.

2. A perpendicular to a line m is to be erected at a point A lying on m. From A you mark two points equidistant from A on m, call them L and R, then swing equal arcs from L and R that cross above the line at T. The ray AT is perpendicular to m because T is:
   (A) equidistant from L and R, so AT is the perpendicular bisector of segment LR, and that bisector passes through A and is perpendicular to m.
   (B) the midpoint of segment LR, which automatically makes any line through it perpendicular.
   (C) 60° away from the line, which is then doubled to a right angle.
   (D) the centre of a circle through L, R and A, so AT is a radius.

3. Every point on the perpendicular bisector of segment AB is equidistant from A and B. Using only this fact, where must the centre of a circle that passes through BOTH A and B lie?
   (A) Somewhere on the perpendicular bisector of AB, since the centre must be equally far from A and from B.
   (B) Exactly at the midpoint of AB and nowhere else.
   (C) Anywhere inside segment AB.
   (D) On the line AB extended, beyond A or beyond B.

4. The perpendicular bisector of XY meets XY at exactly one angle. A student insists it could be 80° if the compass radius is chosen carefully. What is the truth?
   (A) It is 80° only if the radius is less than the length of XY.
   (B) It can be any angle depending on the compass setting.
   (C) It is 60°, the natural angle of compass arcs.
   (D) It is always exactly 90°; the equal-radius arcs force a right angle no matter what (valid) radius is chosen.

5. You are asked to construct a triangle with sides 4 cm, 5 cm and 10 cm using SSS. Before drawing, you check feasibility. The triangle:
   (A) can be constructed, since all three lengths are different.
   (B) can be constructed, since 10 is less than 4 + 5 + anything.
   (C) cannot be constructed, because 4 + 5 = 9 is less than 10, violating the triangle inequality (two sides must sum to more than the third).
   (D) cannot be constructed, because no side may exceed 5 cm.

6. To construct a triangle by SAS you are given two sides and the angle BETWEEN them. Which of these data sets is a valid SAS set that determines a unique triangle?
   (A) Sides 6 cm and 5 cm with the 50° angle lying between those two sides.
   (B) Sides 6 cm and 5 cm with a 50° angle that is opposite the 6 cm side.
   (C) Three angles 50°, 60° and 70°, with no side given.
   (D) Sides 6 cm, 5 cm and 12 cm with no angle given.

7. A triangle is to be built by ASA with the two given angles measuring 110° and 80°, on a base of 6 cm. Is this constructible?
   (A) No, because 110° + 80° = 190° already exceeds 180°, leaving no room for a third angle, so no triangle exists.
   (B) Yes, the third angle is 180 − 190 = −10°, which is allowed.
   (C) Yes, because the base length 6 cm is positive.
   (D) No, because ASA requires the two angles to be equal.

8. Using RHS you construct a right triangle given the hypotenuse 13 cm and one leg 5 cm. The construction succeeds and the other leg comes out as:
   (A) 18 cm, since 13 + 5 = 18.
   (B) 12 cm, since the right angle and Pythagoras give leg = √(13² − 5²) = √144 = 12.
   (C) 8 cm, since 13 − 5 = 8.
   (D) It cannot be built, since the hypotenuse must equal the leg.

9. Which of the following data sets does NOT determine a unique triangle (i.e. more than one non-congruent triangle can fit)?
   (A) Three sides satisfying the triangle inequality (SSS).
   (B) Two sides and an angle that is NOT between them (the SSA / 'ambiguous' case).
   (C) Two angles and the side between them (ASA).
   (D) Two sides and the angle between them (SAS).

10. A regular polygon has interior angle 156°. Using the relation that the interior angle of a regular n-gon is 180° − (360°/n), how many sides does it have?
   (A) 12 sides, because 156 ÷ 13 ≈ 12.
   (B) 15 sides, because 360°/n = 180° − 156° = 24°, so n = 360 ÷ 24 = 15.
   (C) 10 sides, because 156° is between a decagon and a dodecagon.
   (D) 24 sides, because the exterior angle equals the interior angle minus 132°.

11. Bisecting a given 60° angle is easy with compass and straightedge, but a classmate claims you can TRISECT any angle the same way, by 'just swinging three equal arcs'. The correct response is:
   (A) Trisection is just as easy as bisection; three equal arcs always split the angle into thirds.
   (B) Trisection is possible only for angles larger than 90°.
   (C) Trisecting a general angle is impossible with only compass and straightedge; bisection works, but three equal arcs along an arm do not divide the ANGLE into three equal parts.
   (D) Neither bisection nor trisection is possible without a protractor.

12. To copy an angle ABC onto a new ray, you draw an arc across both arms of ABC cutting them at P and Q, then transfer that same arc onto the new ray and STEP OFF the chord PQ. The construction reproduces the angle exactly because:
   (A) the protractor reading is carried over invisibly by the compass.
   (B) equal radii give congruent arcs, and copying the chord PQ forces the same arc length, hence the same angle, at the new vertex.
   (C) any two arcs of the same colour subtend the same angle.
   (D) stepping off PQ doubles the angle, which is then halved.

13. You are asked to construct a triangle with angles 90°, 90° and 0°. Is this possible?
   (A) No, because the three angles of a triangle must sum to 180° and each must be positive; 90 + 90 + 0 = 180 but a 0° angle means two sides coincide, so it is not a genuine triangle.
   (B) Yes, because the angles add to exactly 180°.
   (C) Yes, it forms a thin right triangle.
   (D) No, because the sum 180° is too small for any triangle.

14. A perpendicular is dropped from an external point F to a line m. You swing an arc from F crossing m at two points G and H, then construct the perpendicular bisector of GH. The foot of the perpendicular from F is:
   (A) the point G, the first crossing of the arc.
   (B) any point on m, since all perpendiculars look the same.
   (C) the point on m closest to G, not to F.
   (D) the midpoint of GH, where the perpendicular bisector of GH meets m; the segment from F to that midpoint is perpendicular to m.

15. The interior angles of a regular polygon are each 162°. How many of these polygons would meet at a single point if they tiled, and does the polygon tile by itself?
   (A) 360 ÷ 162 ≈ 2.22, round to 2, so two tile at a vertex.
   (B) 360 ÷ 162 ≈ 2.22, which is not a whole number, so it does NOT tile by itself.
   (C) It tiles, with about 2.22 polygons at a vertex.
   (D) It tiles, because 162° is close to 180°.

16. For a regular polygon to tile the plane by itself, its interior angle A must satisfy which exact condition?
   (A) 360° must be an exact whole-number multiple of A (i.e. 360 ÷ A is a positive integer).
   (B) A must be less than 120°.
   (C) A must be a multiple of 45°.
   (D) 360 ÷ A must be at least 2, whether or not it is a whole number.

17. A regular dodecagon has interior angle 150°. At a tiling vertex, ONE dodecagon corner is placed; the remaining angle is filled by ONE more regular polygon. That polygon's interior angle must be:
   (A) 150°, another dodecagon.
   (B) 60°, an equilateral triangle.
   (C) 90°, a square.
   (D) 210°, which is impossible for a regular polygon (interior angles are always under 180°), so a single extra polygon cannot complete the vertex.

18. At a vertex of a semi-regular tiling, the corner angles must total exactly 360°. A proposed vertex uses one square (90°), one regular hexagon (120°) and one regular dodecagon (150°). Does this vertex close up exactly?
   (A) Yes, because 90 + 120 + 150 = 360 exactly, so the three polygons fit around the point with no gap or overlap.
   (B) No, it leaves a 30° gap.
   (C) No, it overlaps by 30°.
   (D) Yes, but only because all three angles are multiples of 30°, regardless of the sum.

19. A floor is tiled with regular octagons (135°) and one other regular polygon. At each vertex two octagons meet plus one more tile. The third tile's interior angle, and its name, are:
   (A) 60° — an equilateral triangle.
   (B) 120° — a regular hexagon.
   (C) 90° — a square, since 360 − 2 × 135 = 360 − 270 = 90.
   (D) 45° — a regular octagon corner cut in half.

20. Three regular pentagons (108° each) are pushed snugly around a point. After studying the leftover, a student wants to fill it with a single regular polygon. What interior angle would that polygon need, and is it possible?
   (A) It would need 36° (since 360 − 3 × 108 = 36), but no regular polygon has a 36° interior angle, so the gap cannot be filled this way.
   (B) It would need 36°, which an equilateral triangle supplies.
   (C) It would need 72°, supplied by a regular pentagon half.
   (D) The leftover is 0°, so three pentagons already tile.

21. While constructing a perpendicular bisector, your compass slipped so that the arc from X used radius 5 cm but the arc from Y used radius 6 cm. The line you draw through the two crossings will:
   (A) still be the exact perpendicular bisector, since both crossings exist.
   (B) be parallel to XY.
   (C) become the angle bisector of angle X instead.
   (D) not be the true perpendicular bisector — it will be tilted and will cross XY off the midpoint, nearer the end you drew with the larger radius having its crossing pushed across.

22. You are given a base BC = 7 cm, and told the angle B = 40° and the angle C = 30°. The triangle is constructed by ASA. The third angle A and the constructibility are:
   (A) A = 70°, and it is constructible.
   (B) A = 110°, and the triangle is constructible, since 40 + 30 = 70 < 180 leaves a valid positive third angle.
   (C) A = 110°, but it is not constructible because two angles are unequal.
   (D) It is not constructible, since the base must be the longest side.

23. Among regular polygons, the self-tiling ability stops after the hexagon. The precise reason is that, beyond six sides:
   (A) the interior angle exceeds 180°, which cannot occur in a tiling.
   (B) the polygons become too large to fit on a floor.
   (C) the exterior angle becomes negative.

24. A 60° angle is constructed, then bisected to 30°, then that 30° is bisected, and that result is bisected once more. The smallest angle now present measures:
   (A) 15°, after two halvings.
   (B) 7.5°, since 60 → 30 → 15 → 7.5 after three successive halvings.

25. A regular polygon has interior angle 165°. A student computes 360 ÷ 165 ≈ 2.18 and concludes 'round it to 2, so two tile at a vertex'. The flaw is:
   (A) the division is correct and two really do tile with a tiny harmless gap.
   (B) two such corners give 2 × 165 = 330°, which is 30° short of 360°, leaving a gap; rounding the divisor does not produce a real tiling.
   (C) they should have rounded up to 3, giving a perfect tiling.
   (D) the polygon does tile, because 165° is under 180°.

26. A regular hexagon's interior angle is 120°. In a mixed tiling, at one vertex you place one hexagon (120°) and want to fill the rest with equilateral triangles (60° each). How many triangles complete the vertex exactly?
   (A) Two triangles, since 360 − 120 = 240 and 240 ÷ 120 = 2.
   (B) Three triangles, since 3 × 60 = 180 fills the rest.
   (C) Four triangles, since 360 − 120 = 240 and 240 ÷ 60 = 4.
   (D) Six triangles, since six triangles always meet at a point.

27. To construct a 105° angle with compass and straightedge, which combination of constructible angles works?

28. Two squares (90° each) and three equilateral triangles (60° each) are arranged around a point. Do they close the vertex exactly?

   (B) No, it overlaps by 30°.

29. A rhombus tile is made and you ask whether ANY triangle (not just equilateral) can tile the plane. The correct general fact is:

30. At a tiling vertex you find squares and triangles arranged so the angles total 350°. This means:

31. You bisect a given angle ABC by drawing an arc from B cutting the arms at P and Q, then equal arcs from P and Q meeting at R. The ray BR bisects the angle because R is:

60. A semi-regular tiling has the same arrangement of polygons at every vertex. One proposed vertex is: triangle (60°), square (90°), triangle (60°), and a regular twelve-gon (150°). Does this single vertex close to exactly 360°?
   (A) No, it leaves a 30° gap.
   (B) No, it overlaps by 30°.
   (C) Yes, since 60 + 90 + 60 + 150 = 360 exactly.
   (D) Yes, but only because four tiles are used.
