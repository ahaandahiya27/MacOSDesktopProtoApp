1. Three towns A, B and C are not on one line. A water tank must be built equidistant from all three towns. Using only the perpendicular-bisector construction, where is the unique spot?
   (A) At the crossing of the perpendicular bisectors of AB and of BC; that point is equally far from A, B and C.
   (B) At the crossing of the three angle bisectors of triangle ABC, which is equally far from the three towns.
   (C) At the midpoint of the longest of the three connecting segments.
   (D) Anywhere on the perpendicular bisector of AB, since that line is equidistant from A and B.

2. A segment PQ is 10 cm long. A point R is built so that RP = RQ = 6 cm by swinging equal arcs from P and Q. How far is R from the midpoint M of PQ?
   (A) 1 cm, since 6 − 5 = 1.
   (B) 6 cm, since R is built with radius 6 cm.
   (C) √11 cm, since RM is perpendicular to PQ and RM² = 6² − 5² = 11.
   (D) √61 cm, since RM² = 6² + 5² = 61.

3. You drop a perpendicular from an external point F to a line m, getting foot N. You then mark any other point K on m (K ≠ N). Comparing FN and FK:
   (A) FN = FK, because both are distances from the same point F to the same line.
   (B) FK < FN, because the perpendicular is the longest such segment.
   (C) FN is the shortest of all distances from F to points of m, so FN < FK.
   (D) They cannot be compared without knowing the length of segment NK.

4. A circle is drawn through the two endpoints of a 12 cm chord AB, and its centre O lies 8 cm from the chord (measured perpendicularly). The radius of the circle is:
   (A) 8 cm, the perpendicular distance from O to AB.
   (B) 14 cm, since 6 + 8 = 14.
   (C) √28 cm, since radius² = 8² − 6² = 28.
   (D) 10 cm, since O is on the perpendicular bisector of AB and radius² = 6² + 8² = 100.

5. Two perpendicular bisectors of the three sides of a triangle are constructed and meet at point W. A classmate adds the perpendicular bisector of the third side. It will:
   (A) pass through W as well, because W is already equidistant from all three vertices.
   (B) miss W, since three lines almost never meet at one point.
   (C) pass through W only if the triangle is equilateral.
   (D) meet the other two at the midpoint of the third side.

6. A perpendicular bisector of AB is drawn. A point T is chosen on it with TA = 13 cm, and AB = 24 cm. The distance from T to the line AB (along the bisector) is:
   (A) 13 cm, since TA = TB = 13.
   (B) 1 cm, since 13 − 12 = 1.
   (C) 5 cm, since the bisector meets AB at the midpoint, giving a right triangle with hypotenuse 13 and leg 12.
   (D) √313 cm, since the distance² = 13² + 12².

7. On line m, two points L and R are marked with A as their midpoint (AL = AR). Equal arcs from L and R meet at T above m. A student then claims line AT is the perpendicular bisector of LR AND makes a 90° angle with m. Which is correct?
   (A) Both claims are correct: T is equidistant from L and R, so AT is LR's perpendicular bisector through its midpoint A, hence perpendicular to m.
   (B) Only the first is correct; AT need not be perpendicular to m.
   (C) Only the second is correct; AT is perpendicular but not a bisector of LR.
   (D) Neither is correct unless the arcs use radius equal to AL.

8. A 60° angle is constructed, bisected, and the resulting half is bisected again. This smallest angle is then placed beside the original 60°. The combined angle measures:
   (A) 75°, since the smallest piece is 60 ÷ 4 = 15° and 60° + 15° = 75°.
   (B) 67.5°, since the smallest piece is 7.5° and 60 + 7.5 = 67.5.
   (C) 90°, since 60° + 30° = 90°.
   (D) 80°, since the smallest piece is 20° and 60 + 20 = 80.

9. Which angle below CANNOT be constructed with only a compass and straightedge?
   (A) 75°, as 60° + half of a 30° angle (15°).
   (B) 50°, since it would require trisecting a constructible 150° angle, and general trisection is impossible.
   (C) 105°, as 60° + 45° (45° being half of 90°).
   (D) 7.5°, by bisecting 60° three times.

10. To construct a 67.5° angle with compass and straightedge, a valid route is:
   (A) trisect a 202.5° angle to get 67.5° directly.
   (B) construct a right angle (90°), bisect to 45°, bisect to 22.5°, then add 45° + 22.5°.
   (C) bisect a 135° angle, since 135 ÷ 2 = 67.5, where 135° is got by trisecting 405°.
   (D) construct 60° and add 7.5°, where 7.5° comes from trisecting 22.5°.

11. A right angle is bisected, the 45° is bisected, and the 22.5° is bisected. The smallest angle obtained is:
   (A) 7.5°, since 90 ÷ 12 = 7.5.
   (B) 15°, since two halvings give 22.5 and then 22.5 − 7.5 = 15.
   (C) 11.25°, since 90 → 45 → 22.5 → 11.25 over three halvings.
   (D) 5.625°, after four halvings of 90°.

12. You construct a 30° angle (half of 60°) and lay three of them side by side sharing arms, then bisect the whole result once. The final angle is:
   (A) 45°, since three 30° angles make 90° and half of 90° is 45°.
   (B) 30°, since bisecting returns to one 30° piece.
   (C) 60°, since three 30° make 90° and 90 − 30 = 60.
   (D) 135°, since three 30° make 90° and you add a 45°.

13. An angle of measure 96° is given. A student wants a 24° angle from it using only bisections. Is this possible, and how?
   (A) No, because 24° is not a multiple of 15° and so is never constructible.
   (B) Yes, by trisecting 96° once, since 96 ÷ 3 = 32, then bisecting to ... no, it is impossible.
   (C) Yes: bisect 96° to 48°, then bisect 48° to 24° (two halvings, 96 ÷ 4 = 24).
   (D) Yes, by bisecting 96° once, since 96 ÷ 2 = 48 and 48 is close enough to 24.

14. If you accidentally use a SMALLER radius r' at the new vertex but the SAME chord length PQ, the copied angle will be:

   (B) larger than the original, because the same chord across a smaller arc subtends a wider angle.

15. A segment of length 7 cm is to be copied. Your compass can open to at most 5 cm. Can the segment be copied exactly with compass and straightedge?
   (A) No directly in one step, but yes by stepping off in parts (e.g. 4 cm then 3 cm along a line), since the compass carries each part exactly.
   (B) No, a segment longer than the compass span can never be copied.
   (C) Yes, in a single arc, since the compass span does not limit copying.
   (D) No, because copying a length requires a marked ruler when the compass is too small.

16. A triangle has two sides 9 cm and 4 cm. For how many INTEGER values of the third side (in whole cm) does a genuine triangle exist?
   (A) 7, since the third side x satisfies 5 < x < 13, giving x = 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12.
   (B) 9, since x can be 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13.
   (C) 5, since x ranges over 9 − 4 = 5 values.
   (D) 8, since x can be any whole number from 5 to 12.

17. A triangle is to be built by ASA with angles 95° and 86° on a 6 cm base. Before drawing you check feasibility. The triangle:
   (A) cannot be built, since 95° + 86° = 181° exceeds 180°, leaving no room for a third positive angle.
   (B) can be built; the third angle is 180 − 181 = −1°, which is allowed for an obtuse triangle.
   (C) can be built, since the base 6 cm is positive and both angles are under 90° together count as acute.
   (D) cannot be built, because ASA requires the two base angles to be equal.

18. Using SAS you are given sides 6 cm and 8 cm with included angle 90°. The third side (opposite the right angle) comes out as:

20. A right triangle is built by RHS with hypotenuse 17 cm and one leg 8 cm. The perimeter of the triangle is:

21. A triangle ABC is built by SAS with AB = 6 cm, AC = 6 cm and included angle A = 60°. The triangle turns out to be:

22. Two sides of a triangle measure 5 cm and 12 cm and the angle between them is increased steadily from 30° to 150°. The third side (opposite that angle):

23. You are told a triangle has sides 3 cm, 4 cm, and an angle of 90° opposite the longest side. The longest side must be:
   (A) 7 cm, the sum 3 + 4.
   (B) 5 cm, since with the right angle opposite it, it is the hypotenuse √(3² + 4²).

24. A regular polygon has an interior angle of 168°. How many sides does it have?
   (A) 12, since 168 ÷ 14 = 12.
   (B) 15, since the exterior angle 12° and 180 ÷ 12 = 15.
   (C) 30, since the exterior angle is 180 − 168 = 12° and 360 ÷ 12 = 30.

25. Two regular polygons have interior angles differing by 30°. One is a regular hexagon (120°). The OTHER, if it has MORE sides than the hexagon, has interior angle 150° and is a:
   (A) regular dodecagon (12 sides), since exterior angle = 180 − 150 = 30° and 360 ÷ 30 = 12.
   (B) regular octagon, since 150° is the octagon's interior angle.
   (C) regular decagon, since a 30° difference means 10 sides.
   (D) regular nonagon, since 150° gives 360 ÷ 150 = 2.4 ≈ 9 sides.

26. A regular polygon's exterior angle is exactly one-fifth of its interior angle. The polygon is a:
   (A) regular hexagon, since one-fifth points to 6 sides.
   (B) regular pentagon, since the word 'fifth' means 5 sides.
   (C) regular dodecagon (12 sides), since interior = 5 × exterior and interior + exterior = 180 give exterior 30°, so n = 360 ÷ 30 = 12.
   (D) regular decagon, since exterior 36° and 36 = interior ÷ 5.

27. The interior angle of a regular n-gon is 180° − 360°/n. For which n is the interior angle EXACTLY twice the exterior angle?

   (C) n = 12, since 12 is double 6.
   (D) n = 6, since interior = 2 × exterior gives exterior = 60° and 360 ÷ 60 = 6.

31. A regular polygon tiles the plane by itself with exactly k copies at each vertex. The only possible values of k are:
   (A) 3, 4, 5 and 6, covering pentagons too.

   (C) 3, 4 and 6 (hexagon, square, triangle).

32. A regular polygon has interior angle 150°. A student says 'since 150° is under 180° and 360 ÷ 150 = 2.4 rounds to 2, two of them tile.' The correct verdict is:
   (A) It does not self-tile; two give 300° (a 60° gap) and three give 450° (overlap), and rounding the divisor never produces a real tiling.
   (B) It tiles with two corners, since 2.4 rounds down to 2.
   (C) It tiles with three corners, since 2.4 rounds up to 3.

33. Of the regular pentagon (108°), octagon (135°) and decagon (144°), how many self-tile the plane?

34. A regular polygon has interior angle A with 90° < A < 120°. Can such a polygon EVER self-tile?
   (A) Yes, if A is a multiple of 30°, such as the pentagon's 108°.

   (D) No, because A in that range exceeds 180°.

47. Equilateral triangles tile the plane. Does EVERY triangle (scalene, obtuse, any shape) tile the plane?

60. The single rule that governs every interior vertex of any tiling — regular, semi-regular, or made of triangles and quadrilaterals — is that the corner angles meeting there sum to:
   (A) exactly 360°, the full turn — less leaves a gap, more forces an overlap.
   (B) exactly 180°, a straight angle.
