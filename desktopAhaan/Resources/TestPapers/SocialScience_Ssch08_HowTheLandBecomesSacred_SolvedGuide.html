Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

In this chapter, something 'sacred' is best described as:

  • Asimply very old and made of stone
  • Bowned and run by the government
  • Cthe largest building in a town
  • Dholy or divine, worthy of deep reverence and spiritual significance✓

Working

✓

Sacredness means deep religious or spiritual significance - holy, divine and worthy of reverence. Age, ownership and size do not by themselves make something sacred.

Q2

The chapter's central new idea about sacredness in India is that it:

  • Aapplies only to temples and mosques
  • Bbelongs only to the oldest religion
  • Ccan exist only inside cities
  • Dextends beyond buildings to journeys, rivers, mountains, forests, trees and even the whole land✓

Working

✓

In India sacredness reaches far beyond buildings - a pilgrimage, a river, a mountain, a grove or the land itself can be holy, creating a 'sacred geography'. It is not confined to buildings, one religion, or cities.

Q3

To say India has a 'sacred geography' means that:

  • Aonly one mountain in India is holy
  • Bholy places are spread across the land so that the territory itself feels sacred✓
  • Cgeography lessons are taught inside temples
  • DIndia has no maps of its holy sites

Working

✓

'Sacred geography' means sacred sites are scattered across the whole land, making the territory itself sacred. It is not about a single mountain, temple schooling, or missing maps.

Q4

A 'pilgrimage' is:

  • Aa tax paid to a temple
  • Ba market held beside a river
  • Ca royal coronation ceremony
  • Da journey to a sacred place significant within a religion✓

Working

✓

A pilgrimage is a journey to a sacred site. It is not a tax, a market, or a coronation.

Q5

The Sanskrit word 'tirtha' literally means a:

  • Amountain peak that touches the sky
  • Bpot that contains nectar
  • Cplace to cross a river - symbolically, a crossing from ordinary to higher life✓
  • Dgrove protected by a village

Working

✓

Tirtha literally means a river-crossing place; symbolically it is where one crosses from ordinary life to a higher, spiritual one. The peak, pot and grove are other sacred things in the chapter.

Q6

The fact that 'tirtha' (a sacred site) comes from a word for a river-crossing best suggests that:

  • Asacred places were often imagined as points of crossing to a higher life✓
  • Ball sacred places must lie deep underwater
  • Crivers were never considered holy
  • Dpilgrims were forbidden to cross rivers

Working

✓

The river-crossing root captures the idea of a tirtha as a crossing-point from the ordinary to the spiritual. It does not mean sites are underwater, that rivers were unholy (they were deeply revered), or that crossing was banned.

Q7

A 'shrine' is a place regarded as holy because of its association with:

  • Aa famous battle and nothing else
  • Ba market town and nothing else
  • Ca royal palace and nothing else
  • Dthe divine, a sacred relic, or a spiritual figure✓

Working

✓

A shrine is holy through links to the divine, a relic, or a spiritual figure. A battlefield, market or palace is not, by itself, a shrine.

Q8

A 'relic', as the chapter uses it, is:

  • Aa counted calendar era
  • Ba tax-free village grant
  • Ca confluence of two rivers
  • Da part of a holy figure's body or one of their belongings, kept as an object of reverence✓

Working

✓

A relic is a venerated remain or belonging of a saint or spiritual figure. The calendar era is a samvat, the land grant an agrahara, and the river-meeting a sangam.

Q9

Which statement about religions and sacred places in India is correct?

  • Aonly Hinduism has sacred places in India
  • Bonly religions born in India have sacred sites here
  • Calmost every religion in India, whether born here or from outside, marks its own sacred places✓
  • Dsacred places exist only for religions that came from outside

Working

✓

Faiths born in India (Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism) and those from outside (Islam, Christianity, Judaism, Zoroastrianism) all mark sacred places here. It is not limited to one religion or to either group alone.

Q10

Which group of religions ORIGINATED in India?

  • AIslam, Christianity, Judaism and Zoroastrianism
  • BChristianity, Sikhism, Judaism and Islam
  • CZoroastrianism, Hinduism, Islam and Buddhism
  • DHinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism✓

Working

✓

Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism arose in India. Islam, Christianity, Judaism and Zoroastrianism came from outside; the other lists mix the two groups.

Many of India's holy places - dargahs, rivers, mountains, groves - are shared across communities, so the 'one religion only' idea is wrong. Sharing is common, and the sites genuinely are sacred.

Natural places such as rivers, mountains and groves are described as sacred:

  • Aonly to people living abroad

✓

India's natural sacred places are revered across many faiths and communities. Their sacredness rests on shared belief, not foreign residence, government declaration, or a yearly limit.

Q14

Q15

Many important shrines sit on hilltops, making the journey hard. The chapter reads this difficulty as:

  • Ca way to keep all pilgrims away
  • Dmirroring an inner, spiritual path the pilgrim travels✓

✓

The hard climb to a hilltop shrine is seen as mirroring the pilgrim's inner spiritual journey. It is not meaningless, a sign of divine dislike, or meant to bar pilgrims.

Q16

A difficult hilltop pilgrimage is meaningful mainly because:

  • Bit is the fastest possible way to reach a shrine
  • Cthe effort of the outer journey reflects the effort of inner spiritual growth✓
  • Dit avoids any contact with Nature

✓

The outer hardship mirrors inner growth, giving the journey its meaning. It is not a strength contest, a shortcut (it is the opposite), and it brings the pilgrim closer to Nature, not away from it.

Q17

In Jainism, a 'Tirthankara' is:

  • Aa river that two streams form
  • Ba supreme preacher of dharma - literally 'one who makes a tirtha'✓
  • Ca king who grants tax-free land
  • Da giant fig tree

Working

✓

A Tirthankara is a supreme Jain teacher of dharma, literally 'one who makes a tirtha', guiding the crossing to a higher life. The river-meeting is a sangam, the grant an agrahara, and the fig tree a peepal or banyan.

Q18

Jain tirthas are especially linked to the:

  • A12 jyotirlingas of Shiva
  • BTirthankaras, their supreme teachers✓
  • C51 Shakti pithas of the goddess
  • DKumbh Mela cycle

Working

✓

Jain sacred tirthas are tied to the Tirthankaras. The jyotirlingas are Shaiva, the Shakti pithas relate to the goddess, and the Kumbh is a riverbank fair - none of them Jain teacher-sites.

Q19

The shared root of 'tirtha' and 'Tirthankara' shows that, across Indian faiths, sacredness is often tied to:

  • Athe idea of crossing from ordinary life to a higher, spiritual one✓
  • Bpaying money to a king
  • Cwinning a war
  • Dbuilding the tallest tower

Working

✓

Both words share the 'crossing' idea, central to Indian notions of the sacred. It is not about payment, war, or tower-building.

Q20

The 'char dham' are the four holy abodes that:

  • Aall stand in a single northern valley
  • Bring India at its north, west, east and south✓
  • Clie entirely outside India
  • Dare four shrines in one city

Working

✓

The char dham - Badrinath (north), Dwarka (west), Puri (east) and Rameswaram (south) - ring the whole country. They are not in one valley, abroad, or in a single city.

There are 12 jyotirlingas, each a shrine to Shiva. The 51 sites are the Shakti pithas (the goddess), and the other options are invented.

Q23

✓

The 51 Shakti pithas mark where, by tradition, parts of the divine mother Sati fell, covering the subcontinent. They are not Buddhist sermon-sites, the char dham, or Kumbh grounds.

✓

Networks of sites stretching across the land knit distant regions into a single sacred geography. They did not isolate regions, confine worship to one city, or end pilgrimage - they encouraged it.

Q25

  • Alink the whole subcontinent through shared sacred journeys✓

Networks like the char dham, jyotirlingas and Shakti pithas connected the whole land through pilgrimage. They were not kingdom-border markers, royal-only sites, or river replacements.

Shared sacred sites and pilgrimage routes helped bind India's diverse languages and customs into one civilisation over millennia. They did not cause permanent war, erase local customs, or block travel - they promoted it.

Q28

Q29

The chapter corrects the idea that pilgrimage and trade were separate by showing that:

  • Bpilgrimage routes and trade routes often overlapped, carrying both devotion and goods✓
  • Cpilgrims never bought anything on the way
  • Dthe two never used the same roads

Working

✓

The same roads often carried pilgrims and traders together; pilgrims needed goods and some traders were pilgrims too. Trade was not banned at sites, pilgrims did buy goods, and the routes overlapped.

Q30

Why would busy pilgrimage routes naturally attract traders?

  • Bsacred sites paid traders to stay away
  • Ctrade was illegal everywhere in India
  • Dlarge numbers of travelling pilgrims needed food, supplies and lodging✓

Working

✓

Crowds of pilgrims created demand for food, supplies and lodging, drawing traders to the same routes. Pilgrims were not barred from money, sites did not pay traders off, and trade was not illegal.

The Kumbh moves among four cities on a roughly 12-year cycle set by the sky, so 'same place every year' is false. It is not in a new country yearly, a once-a-millennium event, or non-existent.

Q40
