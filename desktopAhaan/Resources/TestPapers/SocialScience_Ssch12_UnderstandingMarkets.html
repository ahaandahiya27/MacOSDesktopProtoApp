Instructions: Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Watch the classic traps: a market is ANY arrangement with buyers, sellers and a price - online counts, not just a physical shop; the cheapest price is NOT always best (too low and the seller stops selling) - the 'just right' price balances both sides; more demand against limited supply pushes price UP, not down; and middlemen (wholesalers, distributors, retailers) ADD value rather than being useless. Match the certification marks (FSSAI food, ISI/BIS goods, AGMARK farm produce, BEE Star energy) correctly. Do not write on this paper — mark answers on your answer sheet.

Worked Solutions

1. (D) A market is any arrangement where buying and selling happen, physical or online. It is not limited to a building, an app, or a government office.

2. (C) A market is defined by buying and selling at a price, which can happen online. It is not mall-only, haat-only, or money-free.

3. (C) Hampi Bazaar illustrates a glorious historical market. It is not an app, a tax office, or a certification mark.

4. (D) Markets exist so people can obtain the goods and services they need and want but cannot all make themselves. They are not about a home-making ban, government orders, or avoiding trade.

5. (D) Needs are essentials like food and water; wants are extras like a video game. The terms are not identical, not reversed, and not government-decided.

6. (C) A market needs buyers, sellers and an agreed price. A king/army/temple, gold coins alone, or a permanent inspector are not the defining features.

7. (C) Price is the amount at which buyer and seller agree to trade. It is not buyer-only, the shop's building cost, or a tax.

8. (A) Without willing buyers the market is missing one of its three essentials. Sellers and goods are present, and a building is not essential.

9. (D) Negotiation is bargaining between buyer and seller to agree a price. It is not government price-fixing, a tax, or numismatics.

10. (B) The 'just right' price balances the seller's profit and the buyer's willingness. It is not rock-bottom (loss-making), sky-high, or buyer-set.

11. (B) A price too low leaves the seller with no profit or a loss, so they may quit selling - bad for everyone. Low prices are not illegal, disliked by buyers, or banned.

12. (B) Bargaining from 40 and 60 to 50 rupees is negotiation arriving at a 'just right' price. It is not a subsidy, an export, or a mark.

13. (C) Demand is what consumers will and can buy at a price. What sellers will sell is supply; making-cost and tax are different.

14. (B) Supply is what sellers will and can sell at a price. What consumers want is demand; the printed price is MRP; government help is a subsidy.

15. (D) Strong demand against limited supply tends to push price up, not down. Demand does affect price, and prices are usually not all government-set.

16. (C) Demand pushes price up while plentiful supply pushes it down; the 'just right' price is where they meet. The other pairings are wrong.

17. (A) The settling price is where demand and supply balance. Zero demand or supply, or a sales ban, would not yield a working price.

18. (B) Less supply against steady demand pushes the price up. It does not fall to zero, stay fixed, or become illegal.

19. (D) A market is wherever buyers and sellers meet at a price, including online. Online stores do have prices and real sellers, and trade needs no building.

20. (A) A shopping app is an online market. Hampi Bazaar, a haat and a mall are physical markets.

21. (D) A haat is a periodic rural market held on fixed days. It is not an app, a daily market, or a tax office.

22. (B) A domestic market is trade within one country; an international market crosses borders. It is not internet-only or ancient-only.

23. (D) Export is selling a country's goods to a buyer abroad. Buying from abroad is import; village selling and storage are neither.

24. (B) Import is bringing in goods from abroad. Selling abroad is export; making goods is production; a periodic rural market is a haat.

25. (A) Bringing oil in from abroad is an import. Selling abroad is export, trade within India is domestic, and a subsidy is government support.

26. (A) A wholesaler buys in bulk from producers and sells to retailers. Selling small amounts to consumers is a retailer's job; the final buyer is a consumer; an inspector is a regulator.

27. (C) A retailer sells small quantities to consumers. Bulk-buying for resale to shops is a wholesaler, making goods is a producer, and selling abroad is exporting.

28. (A) Wholesalers handle bulk and sell to retailers; retailers sell small quantities to consumers. The other options reverse or confuse these roles.

29. (A) A neighbourhood shop selling you one notebook is a retailer. A wholesaler sells in bulk, a producer makes goods, and an exporter sells abroad.

30. (C) A distributor moves goods from makers and wholesalers to retailers, often over distances. The final user is a consumer; MRP and tax-setting are different.

31. (B) An aggregator is an app or website organising many sellers' offers together. It is not a factory, a single shop, or an inspector.

32. (B) Combining many sellers' offers in one app is an aggregator's role. A producer makes goods, a wholesaler sells bulk, and an exporter sells abroad.

33. (C) Goods flow producer to wholesaler to distributor to retailer to consumer. The other orders scramble the chain.

34. (C) The consumer is the final buyer and user. The bulk reseller is a wholesaler, the maker is a producer, and the listing app is an aggregator.

35. (D) Surat's textile market shows a living supply chain for cloth. It is not an app, a tax office, or a food mark.

36. (A) Each link adds value by moving and dividing the cloth toward the buyer. There is no five-owner law, per-shop tax rule, or ban on direct buying as the reason.

37. (D) Middlemen add genuine value by bulk-buying, storing, distributing far and selling small near you, making goods cheaper to move and easier to buy. They are not useless or mere price-raisers, and they do not replace producers.

38. (B) The wholesaler-retailer split lets you buy a single shirt conveniently nearby - clear value. Middlemen are not purposeless, do not force bulk buying, and do help goods reach consumers.

39. (D) Without retailers you'd buy bulk directly from producers - costly and inconvenient, showing why retailers add value. It would not become free, government-given, or impossible.

40. (D) Markets build trust and mix cultures, connecting people, traditions and ideas. They do not separate communities, ban conversation, or serve only the government.

41. (A) The Ima Keithal of Imphal is a famous women-run market. It is not an app, a tax office, or a certification mark.

42. (A) A women-run cultural market shows markets as social and cultural centres. They are not government-only, womenless, or online-only.

43. (B) A 'melting pot' market mixes people, traditions and ideas. It is not literal metal-melting, culture-separating, or single-product.

44. (C) Governments help control prices and ensure quality and safety, among other roles. They do not buy everything, ban private trade, or stay entirely out of pricing.

45. (C) A subsidy (Latin subsidium, 'help') is government money to lower prices or support producers. A purchase tax, the printed MRP, and a haat are different.

46. (A) A seed subsidy lowers costs and supports producers. It does not aim to raise prices, ban farming, or tax farmers more.

47. (B) Public goods are available to all and not used up by one person's use, usually government-provided. They are not rich-only, single-use, or export-only.

48. (D) A street light serves everyone without being used up - a public good. A chocolate, a sold shirt and a private bicycle are private goods used by one owner.

49. (A) Curbing pollution protects society and the environment. It is not done for profit, to encourage pollution, or to ban all manufacturing.

50. (A) FSSAI marks food safety. Energy ratings are BEE Star, graded farm produce is AGMARK, and industrial standards are ISI/BIS.

51. (B) ISI/BIS marks industrial and manufactured goods to Indian standards. Food is FSSAI, farm produce AGMARK, and energy efficiency BEE Star.

52. (B) AGMARK grades agricultural produce. Energy use is BEE Star, and machine parts fall under ISI/BIS.

53. (B) The BEE Star label rates energy efficiency. Food safety is FSSAI, farm grading is AGMARK, and 'Persian market' refers to a bazaar.

54. (C) FSSAI signals food safety and BEE Star signals energy efficiency. The other pairings mismatch the marks.

55. (A) MRP is the highest price, including taxes, a consumer can be charged. It is not a minimum, a factory cost, or a wholesaler-only price.

56. (A) Charging above MRP is not allowed, as MRP is the legal maximum consumer price. It is not a mere suggestion, a requirement, or a subsidy.

57. (D) Marks, labels and genuine reviews help judge quality. Flashy ads, seller promises and price alone are unreliable guides.

58. (D) A bazaar (Persian) is a permanent market of many shops. A fixed-day market is a haat, an app is an aggregator, and a subsidy is government support.

59. (C) Commerce (Latin com- + merx, 'goods together') is large-scale buying and selling, the root of 'merchant' and 'e-commerce'. Note-printing, numismatics and a haat are different.

60. (C) Too-cheap selling can drive sellers out, so the 'just right' price matters; and a market is any arrangement with buyers, sellers and a price, including online. The other options repeat the misconceptions.
