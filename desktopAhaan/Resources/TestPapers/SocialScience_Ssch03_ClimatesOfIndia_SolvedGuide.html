Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

Ahaan says, "It is drizzling outside right now." His teacher says, "Cherrapunji is one of the wettest places on Earth." Which pair correctly labels the two statements?

  • AAhaan = weather; teacher = climate✓
  • BAhaan = climate; teacher = weather
  • CBoth describe weather
  • DBoth describe climate

Working

✓

"Drizzling right now" is what is happening this moment — that is weather. "Cherrapunji is one of the wettest places on Earth" describes a long-term pattern over decades — that is climate. So Ahaan = weather, teacher = climate. The trap (B) reverses the two; the catch is that a single moment is always weather, never climate.

Q2

A scientist studies thirty years of daily readings for a town and reports the steady, average pattern that emerges. She has described the town's:

  • Aweather
  • Bclimate✓
  • Cseason
  • Dmicroclimate

Working

✓

Averaging weather over thirty years to find the steady pattern is the very definition of climate. Weather (A) is hour-to-hour; a season (C) is a yearly recurrence; a microclimate (D) is a small local area — none fits a 30-year average for a whole town.

Q3

The single most important difference that separates "weather" from "climate" is the:

  • Atemperature being measured
  • Bplace being described
  • Clength of time you look at✓
  • Damount of rainfall

Working

✓

Weather and climate describe the *same* atmosphere and can describe the same place and rainfall — the ONLY thing that separates them is the length of time you look at: a moment is weather, decades give climate. Hence (C), not (A), (B) or (D).

Q4

Most of the world divides the year into four seasons, but India is usually described as having a fifth. That extra season is the:

  • Aharvest season
  • Bdry season
  • Cwindy season
  • Drainy season (monsoon)✓

Working

✓

Most of the world uses four seasons; India is famous for adding the rainy season, the monsoon, as a fifth. "Harvest", "dry" and "windy" seasons (A, B, C) are not the chapter's named extra season.

Q5

Which of these is NOT one of the six traditional Indian ṛitus?

  • AVasanta (spring)
  • BVarṣhā (rainy)
  • CMaru (desert)✓
  • DŚhiśhir (winter)

Working

✓

The six ṛitus are vasanta, grīṣhma, varṣhā, śharad, hemanta and śhiśhir. "Maru" (desert) is not a ṛitu — it is a distractor built from the Thar. Vasanta, Varṣhā and Śhiśhir are all genuine ṛitus, so the odd one out is (C).

Q6

Seasons return every single year mainly because:

  • Athe Earth revolves around the Sun✓
  • Bthe monsoon winds reverse
  • Cthe Sun grows hotter each summer
  • Dhuman festivals are repeated yearly

Working

✓

Seasons recur every year because the Earth revolves around the Sun, so the same conditions return annually. Reversing monsoon winds (B) explain the two monsoons, not the yearly return of all seasons; the Sun does not grow hotter each summer (C); festivals (D) follow the seasons, they don't cause them.

Q7

India recognises six ṛitus rather than four seasons chiefly because the six-season scheme:

  • Ais older than the four-season idea
  • Bwas borrowed from European calendars
  • Creads the monsoon-ruled year more finely✓
  • Dgives every festival its own season

Working

✓

India uses six ṛitus because that finer scheme reads a monsoon-ruled year better than four seasons — a four-season model has no slot for the all-important rains. Age (A), borrowing from Europe (B) and festival-matching (D) are not the chapter's reason.

Q8

Match the regions to climate types: high Himalayas, Northern Plains, Thar, west coast. The correct order of climate types is:

  • Aalpine, subtropical, arid, tropical wet✓
  • Barid, alpine, tropical wet, subtropical
  • Ctropical wet, arid, subtropical, alpine
  • Dsubtropical, alpine, tropical wet, arid

Working

✓

High Himalayas = alpine, Northern Plains = subtropical, Thar = arid, west coast = tropical wet. Only (A) lists them in that order; the other options scramble the pairing.

Q9

India has many different climates at the same time fundamentally because:

  • Aits people follow many different calendars
  • Bthe Sun shines differently on different states
  • Cevery state controls its own weather
  • Dit is large and varied in latitude, height and nearness to the sea✓

Working

✓

A country stretching from snowy mountains to a hot desert to rainy coasts cannot have one climate: its range of latitude, altitude and nearness to the sea produces many climates side by side. Calendars (A), a "different Sun" (B) or states "controlling weather" (C) are not real causes.

Q10

Hill stations like Shimla and Ooty stay cool even in summer. The climate type that best fits these mild lower hills is:

  • Aarid
  • Balpine
  • Ctemperate✓
  • Dtropical wet

Working

✓

Lower hills with hill stations that stay mild are temperate. Arid (A) is the dry Thar; alpine (B) is the freezing high Himalayas; tropical wet (D) is the rainy west coast — none describes mild lower hills.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

The Northern Plains have very hot summers and cold winters, ideal for wheat. This points to which climate type?

  • Atropical wet
  • Bsubtropical✓
  • Calpine
  • Dmaritime

Working

✓

Very hot summers, cold winters and good wheat country describe the subtropical Northern Plains. Tropical wet (A) is the rainy coast; alpine (C) is freezing mountains; maritime (D) means a mild coastal climate — the opposite of "hot summers and cold winters".

Q12

Places nearer the Equator are hotter than places near the poles MAINLY because:

  • Athe Equator is far closer to the Sun
  • Bthe poles receive no sunlight at all
  • Cthere is no atmosphere over the Equator
  • Dthe Sun's rays strike the Equator almost straight down, concentrating heat✓

Working

✓

The Equator is hotter mainly because the Sun's rays strike it almost straight down, concentrating their heat on a small area. The "closer to the Sun" idea (A) is the classic misconception — the distance difference is tiny. There is air over the Equator (C) and the poles do get some sunlight (B); both are false.

Q13

Near the poles the Sun's rays arrive slanting. Two things weaken that sunlight: the energy is spread over a wider area, AND the rays:

  • Areflect off the ice immediately
  • Blose heat to passing winds
  • Cpass through more air on a longer journey✓
  • Dare blocked by mountains

Working

✓

Slanting polar rays are weakened two ways: spread over a wider area AND passing through more air on a longer journey. Reflecting off ice (A), losing heat to winds (B) and being blocked by mountains (D) are not the chapter's two reasons.

Q14

Kanniyakumari (near the Equator) is warm all year while Kashmir in the far north is much colder. The climate factor chiefly responsible is:

  • Aaltitude
  • Bdistance from the sea
  • Cwinds
  • Dlatitude✓

Working

✓

Kanniyakumari is warm and Kashmir cold because of how far each lies from the Equator — that is latitude. Altitude (A) would matter for a mountain-vs-plain pair, distance from the sea (B) for a coast-vs-inland pair, winds (C) for a seasonal contrast — but the equator-to-far-north comparison is a latitude effect.

Q15

The two reasons the chapter gives for temperature falling as you climb a mountain are: the air becomes thinner and less able to hold heat, and:

  • Ayou move closer to the cold Sun
  • Byou move farther from the ground, the air's main heater✓
  • Cwinds always blow downhill
  • Dsnow reflects all the sunlight

Working

✓

The two reasons given are: the air higher up is thinner and holds less heat, AND you are farther from the ground, which is the air's main heater. You do not move "closer to the cold Sun" (A — the Sun isn't cold and you barely move toward it); (C) and (D) are invented.

Q16

Two towns sit at exactly the same latitude. Town A is on a high plateau; Town B is in a low valley nearby. Town A is cooler chiefly because of its greater:

  • Alatitude
  • Baltitude✓
  • Cdistance from the sea
  • Drainfall

Working

✓

Same latitude rules out latitude; the high plateau town is cooler because of its greater altitude — temperature falls as you climb. Distance from the sea (C) and rainfall (D) are not what sets these two apart.

Q17

The Himalayan peaks stay snow-covered all year while the plains below them swelter. The single factor that best explains this contrast is:

  • Alatitude
  • Bdistance from the sea
  • Caltitude✓
  • Dwinds

Working

✓

Peaks are snowbound while the plains below swelter — the towering height of the peaks (altitude) is the deciding factor, since temperature drops with elevation. Latitude (A) is similar for peak and plain; sea distance (B) and winds (D) don't explain the snow line.

Q18

Mumbai (on the coast) has cooler summers and milder winters than Nagpur (far inland), even at nearly the same latitude. This is because:

  • AMumbai is at a much higher altitude
  • BNagpur lies much nearer the Equator
  • CNagpur gets no sunshine
  • Dthe sea moderates Mumbai's temperature✓

Working

✓

Same latitude, so latitude is ruled out; Mumbai is milder because the sea moderates its temperature (water warms and cools slowly). "Higher altitude" (A) is false for coastal Mumbai; Nagpur is not nearer the Equator (B); it certainly gets sunshine (C).

Q19

The sea keeps coastal places mild because water, compared with land:

  • Aheats and cools much more slowly✓
  • Bheats and cools much faster
  • Creflects all sunlight away
  • Dnever changes temperature at all

Working

✓

The sea moderates because water heats and cools much more slowly than land, storing summer heat and releasing it gently in winter. (B) is the reverse and would make coasts extreme; (C) and (D) are false — the sea does change temperature, just slowly.

Q20

"Temperature range" means the gap between a place's highest and lowest temperatures. A place with a SMALL temperature range most likely lies:

  • Anear the sea✓
  • Bfar inland on a high plateau
  • Cdeep inside a desert
  • Dat a very high latitude

Working

✓

A small temperature range means an even, moderate climate — typical of places near the sea, which the ocean keeps mild. A high inland plateau (B), a desert (C) and a very high latitude (D) all give large ranges (big extremes), the opposite of small.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

Mumbai's yearly temperature range is about 14°C while Nagpur's is much larger. The smaller range at Mumbai is best explained by its:

  • Ahigher altitude
  • Blower latitude
  • Cnearness to the moderating sea✓
  • Dheavier rainfall

Working

✓

Mumbai's small ~14°C range is explained by its nearness to the moderating sea, which evens out summer and winter. Altitude (A) is low at the coast; lower latitude (B) would affect overall warmth, not the smallness of the range; rainfall (D) does not set the temperature range.

Q22

The hot, dry "loo" winds that cause summer heat waves in Punjab and Rajasthan get their heat by crossing:

  • Athe Bay of Bengal
  • Bthe snowy Himalayas
  • Cthe Western Ghats
  • Dthe deserts of Arabia and Afghanistan✓

Working

✓

The loo gets its heat by crossing the hot deserts of Arabia and Afghanistan before reaching north India. The Bay of Bengal (A) and the Western Ghats (C) would add moisture, not dry heat; the snowy Himalayas (B) would chill, not heat, the wind.

Q23

In winter, cold winds slipping over the Himalayas chill the foothills and cause cold waves. This shows that winds affect a region by:

  • Achanging its latitude
  • Bcarrying the qualities of the land they crossed✓
  • Craising its altitude
  • Dmoving it nearer the sea

Working

✓

Cold Himalayan winds chilling the foothills show that winds carry the qualities (here, cold) of the land they have crossed. Winds do not change a place's latitude (A) or altitude (C), nor move it nearer the sea (D).

Q24

The same plain can swelter under a desert wind in one season and shiver under a mountain wind in another. The best explanation is that air:

  • Apicks up the character of wherever it has travelled✓
  • Bis created fresh at each place
  • Calways warms as it moves
  • Dcarries no heat or moisture at all

Working

✓

The same plain can be hot under a desert wind and cold under a mountain wind because air picks up the character of wherever it has travelled — heat from deserts, cold from snowy mountains. Air is not "created fresh" (B), does not always warm as it moves (C), and certainly carries heat and moisture (D).

Q25

The flat Thar suffers hot, dry winds while the lands behind the Himalayas are partly shielded from cold Central-Asian winds. This contrast shows the climate role of:

  • Alatitude
  • Btopography (relief)✓
  • Cdistance from the sea
  • Dthe monsoon

Working

✓

Flat Thar lying open to hot winds while ranges shield the land behind them shows the climate role of topography (relief) — the shape of the land blocking or admitting winds. It is not about latitude (A), distance from the sea (C) or the monsoon (D).

Q26

A small shady forest patch stays cooler and moister than the open fields around it. Such a small area with its own special climate is called a:

  • Aseason
  • Brain shadow
  • Cmicroclimate✓
  • Dṛitu

Working

✓

A small patch with its own special climate is a microclimate. A season (A) and a ṛitu (D) are time periods, not places; a rain shadow (B) is the dry leeward side of a mountain, not a shady forest patch.

Q27

"Topography" in this chapter refers to a region's:

  • Apopulation and cities
  • Bphysical surface features such as mountains, valleys and coasts✓
  • Cyearly festivals
  • Dtotal rainfall only

Working

✓

Topography means a region's physical surface features — mountains, hills, valleys, slopes and coasts. It is not population/cities (A), festivals (C) or rainfall alone (D).

Q28

A concrete city centre is several degrees warmer than the green countryside nearby — an "urban heat island". This is essentially an example of a:

  • Aseason
  • Bcyclone
  • Crain shadow
  • Dhuman-made microclimate✓

Working

✓

A concrete city warmer than the green countryside is a human-made microclimate (an urban heat island). It is not a season (A), a cyclone (B) or a rain shadow (C).

Q29

The word "monsoon" comes from the Arabic *mausim*. What does *mausim* mean?

  • Arain
  • Bwind
  • Cseason✓
  • Dflood

Working

✓

*Mausim* (Arabic) means "season" — which is why "monsoon" really refers to seasonal winds. Rain (A), wind (B) and flood (D) are tempting because we link the monsoon to rains and winds, but the literal meaning is "season".

Q30

Strictly speaking, the word "monsoon" really names the:

  • Arains alone
  • BIndian Ocean itself
  • Csix ṛitus
  • Dseasonal winds that reverse with the seasons✓

Working

✓

Strictly, "monsoon" names the seasonal winds that reverse direction with the seasons; people only loosely use it to mean the rains (A) those winds bring. It is not the six ṛitus (C) or the ocean itself (B).

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

The whole monsoon system is driven by one basic fact:

  • Athe Moon pulls the clouds toward India
  • Bthe Equator stays at a fixed temperature
  • Crivers push the air upward
  • Dland heats up and cools down faster than the ocean✓

Working

✓

The whole monsoon turns on one fact: land heats and cools faster than the ocean, creating the pressure differences that move the winds. The Moon (A), a "fixed" Equator temperature (B) and rivers pushing air (C) are all false.

Q32

In summer, moist sea winds rush inland because the hot Asian land has become a zone of:

  • Ahigh pressure, which pushes air out
  • Blow pressure, into which surrounding air flows✓
  • Ccold air sinking down
  • Dcalm with no pressure difference

Working

✓

In summer the hot land becomes a low-pressure zone; since air flows from high to low pressure, moist sea air rushes inland. If the land were high pressure (A) the air would flow out (that is winter); (C) and (D) describe no inflow.

Q33

Air in the monsoon always flows:

  • Afrom high pressure to low pressure✓
  • Bfrom low pressure to high pressure
  • Conly along the Equator
  • Dstraight downward from space

Working

✓

Air always flows from high pressure to low pressure — that is what drives the monsoon winds. Option (B) is backwards; (C) and (D) are false.

Q34

The summer monsoon and the winter monsoon are called, in that order, the:

  • Asouthwest and northeast monsoons✓
  • Bnortheast and southwest monsoons
  • Ctrade-wind and western-disturbance monsoons
  • Dmaritime and continental monsoons

Working

✓

The summer monsoon is the southwest monsoon and the winter monsoon is the northeast monsoon — in that order, (A). Option (B) reverses them; (C) and (D) invent names.

Q35

The summer (southwest) monsoon brings India's main rains because its winds blow:

  • Afrom the dry land out toward the sea
  • Bstraight down from the upper atmosphere
  • Cfrom the moist sea onto the land✓
  • Donly along the Himalayan crest

Working

✓

The summer monsoon brings the main rains because its winds blow from the moist sea onto the land, carrying water vapour that falls as rain. Land-to-sea winds (A) are the dry winter monsoon; (B) and (D) are false.

Q36

The winter (northeast) monsoon winds are mostly dry because they blow:

  • Afrom the moist sea onto the land
  • Bfrom the land out toward the ocean✓
  • Cup the Western Ghats
  • Dfrom the Equator northward

Working

✓

The winter (northeast) monsoon is mostly dry because its winds blow from the land out toward the ocean, carrying little moisture. Sea-to-land winds (A) are the wet summer monsoon; (C) and (D) are false.

Q37

The winter monsoon, though mostly dry, still brings some rain to parts of south-east India. It picks up that moisture by crossing the:

  • AArabian Sea
  • BThar Desert
  • CHimalayas
  • DBay of Bengal✓

Working

✓

Part of the winter monsoon crosses the Bay of Bengal, picking up moisture and bringing some rain to south-east India. The Arabian Sea (A) feeds the summer monsoon's west coast; the Thar (B) and Himalayas (C) would not add moisture to these winds.

Q38

The monsoon winds reverse between summer and winter because the:

  • AEarth changes its tilt twice a year
  • Bland–sea temperature difference flips between the seasons✓
  • CMoon changes its orbit
  • DWestern Ghats move with the seasons

Working

✓

The winds reverse because the land–sea temperature difference flips between summer and winter, flipping which side is high pressure. The Earth's tilt (A) and the Moon's orbit (C) don't reverse twice a year in this way, and the Ghats (D) do not move.

Q39

The monsoon rains usually reach the southern tip of India in early June and cover the whole country by:

  • Amid-July✓
  • Bthe end of June
  • Cearly September
  • DOctober

Working

✓

The rains reach the southern tip in early June and cover the whole country by mid-July. End of June (B) is too soon; September (C) and October (D) are too late.

Q40

The summer monsoon has been compared to "a sea breeze the size of a country" because both work on the same principle:

  • Athe Moon's pull on water
  • Brivers heating the air above them
  • Cwarm land creating low pressure that draws in cooler sea air✓
  • Dsnow melting on the mountains

Working

✓

Both a sea breeze and the summer monsoon work by warm land creating low pressure that draws in cooler sea air — the monsoon is just that breeze on a country-sized scale. The Moon (A), rivers (B) and melting snow (D) are not the principle.

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

When moist winds are forced up the windward slopes of the Western Ghats, they cool and drop heavy rain. The Deccan Plateau just behind gets far less rain. This dry leeward region is called a:

  • Amicroclimate
  • Bcold desert
  • Crain shadow✓
  • Ddelta

Working

✓

The dry leeward region behind the Western Ghats, left in the "shadow" of the rain, is a rain shadow. A microclimate (A) is a small local area, a cold desert (B) is a high-altitude dry zone, and a delta (D) is a river mouth — none names this dry plateau.

Q42

The west coast of India is drenched while the Deccan stays drier mainly because:

  • Athe Deccan is much nearer the Equator
  • Bthe west coast is at a higher altitude
  • Cthe Deccan has no rivers
  • Dthe Western Ghats force the monsoon winds up, raining on the western slopes✓

Working

✓

The west coast is wet because the Western Ghats force the moist monsoon winds to rise and rain on the western slopes, leaving the Deccan drier. The Deccan is not nearer the Equator (A); the west coast is not higher (B); river count (C) does not decide rainfall.

Q43

Orographic (relief) rainfall happens because, as air is forced to rise over hills, it:

  • Awarms up and holds more water vapour
  • Bexpands, cools, and can hold less water vapour, so the vapour condenses✓
  • Cspeeds up and dries out instantly
  • Dsinks back down the windward side

Working

✓

Orographic rain forms because rising air expands, cools, and can hold less water vapour, so the vapour condenses into rain. Rising air does not warm or hold more vapour (A); it does not dry instantly (C) or sink on the windward side (D).

Q44

If the Western Ghats suddenly vanished, the most likely change to the west coast would be that it:

  • Areceived much less rain✓
  • Breceived much more rain
  • Cbecame a desert overnight
  • Dhad no change at all

Working

✓

Without the Ghats there would be nothing to force the moist winds up, so the west coast would receive much less rain. "Much more" (B) and "more rain → flood" reasoning are backwards; "desert overnight" (C) overstates it; "no change" (D) ignores the Ghats' role.

Q45

Suppose land and ocean somehow heated up at exactly the same speed. The monsoon would:

  • Agrow twice as strong
  • Breverse only once a year
  • Cnot exist at all✓
  • Dshift to the winter only

Working

✓

The monsoon exists only because land heats and cools faster than the ocean. If both heated at the same speed, there would be no pressure difference and no monsoon at all. It would not strengthen (A), reverse once (B) or shift to winter (D).

Q46

The place with the highest average annual rainfall in the world, lying in India, is:

  • ACherrapunji, Assam
  • BMunnar, Kerala
  • CMumbai, Maharashtra
  • DMawsynram, Meghalaya✓

Working

✓

Mawsynram in Meghalaya receives the highest average annual rainfall in the world. Cherrapunji (A) is nearby and very wet but not the highest; Mumbai (C) and Munnar (B) are wet but far below Mawsynram.

Q47

The "windward" side of a mountain is the side that:

  • Ais sheltered from the wind and stays dry
  • Balways faces north
  • Creceives no sunlight
  • Dfaces the wind, where moist air rises and rains✓

Working

✓

The windward side faces the wind, where moist air is forced up, cools and drops heavy rain. The sheltered, dry side (A) is the leeward side; (B) and (C) are unrelated to the term.

Q48

India's festivals such as Bihu, Pongal, Onam and Baisakhi are described as closely tied to:

  • Athe price of fuel
  • Bthe seasons and farming cycle✓
  • Cthe number of cities
  • Dthe latitude of each state

Working

✓

Festivals like Bihu, Pongal, Onam and Baisakhi are tied to the seasons and the farming cycle. They are not tied to fuel prices (A), the number of cities (C) or each state's latitude (D).

Q49

"Monsoon failure" means poor or insufficient monsoon rainfall. A likely chain of effects is:

  • Acrops suffer, water grows scarce, and food prices rise✓
  • Brivers overflow and floods spread everywhere
  • Cwinters become much warmer
  • Dthe six ṛitus disappear

Working

✓

Monsoon failure means poor rains, so crops suffer, water grows scarce and food prices rise. Overflowing rivers and floods (B) come from too much rain, not failure; warmer winters (C) and vanishing ṛitus (D) are not its effects.

Q50

Most Indian farming depends on the monsoon, so when the rains fail the economy is hit because:

  • Afestivals are cancelled by law
  • Bthe sea stops moderating the coast
  • Charvests shrink, pushing up food prices and forcing some workers to migrate✓
  • Dthe Himalayas grow taller

Working

✓

Because most farming depends on the monsoon, failed rains shrink harvests, push up food prices and force some workers to migrate to cities. Festivals are not "cancelled by law" (A); the sea keeps moderating the coast (B); the Himalayas do not grow (D).

Q51

Every few years the monsoon weakens because warm Pacific water shifts eastward, rearranging tropical winds. This periodic Pacific warming is called:

  • Athe loo
  • BEl Niño✓
  • Ca glacial burst
  • Dthe rain shadow

Working

✓

The periodic eastward shift of warm Pacific water that weakens India's monsoon is El Niño. The loo (A) is a hot land wind, a glacial burst (C) is a Himalayan flood, and a rain shadow (D) is a dry mountain region.

Q52

Since the 19th century, climate change has been driven MAINLY by:

  • Ahuman activities, especially burning fossil fuels✓
  • Bnatural processes alone
  • Cthe Earth drifting farther from the Sun
  • Dvolcanic eruptions alone

Working

✓

Since the 1800s, climate change has been driven mainly by human activities, especially burning fossil fuels (plus deforestation). "Natural processes alone" (B) was true long ago, not since the 19th century; (C) and (D) are not the main driver.

Q53

The natural greenhouse effect is important for life because greenhouse gases:

  • Atrap some of the Sun's heat, keeping Earth warm enough to live on✓
  • Bblock all of the Sun's rays
  • Ccool the Earth down to freezing
  • Dcreate the monsoon winds

Working

✓

The natural greenhouse effect matters because greenhouse gases trap some of the Sun's heat, keeping Earth warm enough for life. They do not block all rays (B) or cool Earth to freezing (C), and they do not create the monsoon winds (D).

Q54

Human-caused global warming happens when burning fossil fuels and clearing forests:

  • Athin the greenhouse "blanket", letting heat escape
  • Badd extra greenhouse gases, thickening the blanket and trapping more heat✓
  • Cremove all the air from the atmosphere
  • Dcool the planet by adding smoke

Working

✓

Human warming happens when burning fossil fuels and clearing forests add extra greenhouse gases, thickening the heat-trapping "blanket" so more heat is trapped. Thinning the blanket (A) would cool, not warm; (C) and (D) are false.

Q55

Trace the chain "factory burns coal → farmer's monsoon crop fails". The correct order of links is:

  • Acoal burnt → CO₂ released → extra heat trapped → climate shifts → monsoon disrupted → crop fails✓
  • Bcoal burnt → sea cools → monsoon strengthens → crop thrives
  • Ccoal burnt → rivers freeze → no irrigation → crop fails
  • Dcoal burnt → forests grow → more rain → crop floods

Working

✓

The cause-effect chain runs: coal burnt → CO₂ released → extra heat trapped (greenhouse effect) → climate shifts → monsoon disrupted → crop fails. Burning coal does not cool the sea (B), freeze rivers (C) or grow forests (D), so only (A) is a valid chain.

Q56

Which of these is an example of climate "mitigation"?

  • Acutting down more forests
  • Bburning more coal for cheap power
  • Cincreasing wasteful consumption
  • Dboosting renewable energy and planting trees✓
