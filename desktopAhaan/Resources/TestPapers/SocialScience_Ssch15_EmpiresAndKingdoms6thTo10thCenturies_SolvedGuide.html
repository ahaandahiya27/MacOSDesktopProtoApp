Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

From about 600 CE to 1200 CE, India:

  • Ahad no single empire but many strong regional kingdoms✓
  • Bwas ruled by one vast empire across the whole subcontinent
  • Chad no kingdoms at all
  • Dwas ruled entirely by the Arabs

Working

✓

After the Guptas faded, India had many strong regional kingdoms rather than one empire. It was not one empire, kingdom-less, or Arab-ruled.

Q2

The chapter argues that the period after the Guptas was:

  • Aa 'dark age' with nothing happening
  • Ba time with no art or learning
  • Can age ruled by a single emperor
  • Da creative age of temples, universities and new ideas, not a decline✓

Working

✓

It was a vibrant, creative age (Ellora, Mamallapuram, universities, the rules of zero, Bhakti), not a dark age or single-emperor era.

Q3

The claim 'the period after the Guptas was a dark age with nothing happening' is wrong because it saw:

  • Ano building, learning or religion of any kind
  • Bonly constant defeat by foreigners
  • Cthe complete end of Indian culture
  • Dgreat temples, universities, the rules of zero and the Bhakti tradition✓

Working

✓

The age produced Ellora, Mamallapuram, Nalanda and Vikramashila, the rules of zero and Bhakti - creativity, not a void.

Q4

'No single empire but many kingdoms' best describes a period of:

  • Atotal political unity
  • Bpolitical fragmentation alongside cultural flourishing✓
  • Ccomplete cultural collapse
  • Dforeign rule over all of India

Working

✓

Many regional kingdoms meant political fragmentation, yet culture flourished. It was not unity, collapse, or all-India foreign rule.

Q5

Harshavardhana, the north's last great king of this age, ruled from:

  • AVatapi
  • BKanchi
  • CEllora
  • DKannauj✓

Working

✓

Harshavardhana ruled from Kannauj (from 606 CE). Vatapi was the Chalukya capital, Kanchi the Pallava capital, and Ellora a Rashtrakuta temple site.

Q6

Harshavardhana came to power around:

  • A1206 CE
  • B326 BCE
  • C606 CE✓
  • D1947 CE

Working

✓

Harshavardhana came to power around 606 CE. 1206 is far later, 326 BCE is Alexander's time, and 1947 is independence.

Q7

After Harshavardhana, the north had:

  • Ano single dominant emperor, leading to a contest among regional powers✓
  • Bone unchallenged emperor for centuries
  • Cno rulers at all
  • Dpermanent Arab rule

Working

✓

After Harsha, no single emperor dominated the north, sparking the Tripartite Struggle among regional powers. It was not one emperor, ruler-less, or Arab-ruled.

Q8

Xuanzang, who described Harsha's India, was a:

  • AChinese pilgrim who travelled across India✓
  • BPala king of Bengal
  • CArab general
  • DTamil Bhakti saint

Working

✓

Xuanzang was a Chinese Buddhist pilgrim who toured India. He was not a Pala king, an Arab general, or a Bhakti saint.

Q9

The chapter warns that a traveller's account like Xuanzang's should be treated as:

  • Aa perfect and complete record needing no checking
  • Bcompletely worthless
  • Cthe only source historians may use
  • Done valuable source, to be cross-checked against inscriptions, coins and local records✓

Working

✓

A traveller writes from his own viewpoint and may exaggerate, so his account is one source to cross-check. It is neither perfect, worthless, nor the only source.

Q10

'Epigraphy' is the study of:

  • Acoins and currency
  • Bthe stars and planets
  • Cinscriptions carved on stone or metal✓
  • Dold travelogues only

Working

✓

Epigraphy (Greek epi- 'on' + graphein 'to write') studies inscriptions on stone or metal. Coins are numismatics, stars are astronomy, and travelogues are one source among many.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

A 'prashasti' inscription must be read carefully by historians because it:

  • Ais always completely neutral and exact
  • Bwas written by the king's enemies
  • Cpraises a ruler and can exaggerate his achievements✓
  • Drecords only ordinary village life

Working

✓

A prashasti is a eulogy praising a ruler and may exaggerate, so it needs careful handling. It is not neutral, enemy-written, or about ordinary village life.

Q12

The 'Tripartite Struggle' was a long contest among three powers for control of:

  • AEllora
  • Bthe Tamil south
  • CSindh
  • DKannauj✓

Working

✓

The Tripartite Struggle was for Kannauj, the symbolic key to the north. Ellora was a temple site, the Tamil south had its own kingdoms, and Sindh was taken by the Arabs.

Q13

The three powers in the Tripartite Struggle were the:

  • ACholas, Pandyas and Cheras
  • BChalukyas, Pallavas and Guptas
  • CMauryas, Shungas and Satavahanas
  • DPalas, Gurjara-Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas✓

Working

✓

The Palas, Gurjara-Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas fought the Tripartite Struggle. The southern trio, the Chalukya/Pallava/Gupta set, and the ancient dynasties are different.

Q14

The Tripartite Struggle shows that, in this age, Kannauj was seen as:

  • Aa prized symbol of supremacy over the north✓
  • Ba worthless, ignored town
  • Ca southern port city
  • Dan Arab capital

Working

✓

Three great powers fighting for Kannauj shows it was a prized symbol of northern supremacy. It was not worthless, southern, or Arab.

Q15

That three powers fought for so long without one winning permanently shows the age was one of:

  • Aa single unbeatable empire
  • Btotal peace with no rivalry
  • Cforeign rule over all the north
  • Dbalanced rivalry among strong regional kingdoms✓

Working

✓

A long, indecisive three-way struggle reflects balanced rivalry among strong kingdoms. It was not one empire, peaceful, or foreign-ruled.

Q16

The Palas ruled mainly in:

  • Athe Tamil south
  • Bthe Deccan plateau
  • CKashmir
  • DBengal✓

Working

✓

The Palas ruled Bengal (and the east). The Tamil south, Deccan and Kashmir belonged to other powers.

Q17

The Palas are especially remembered for supporting:

  • Athe stone temples of Mamallapuram
  • Bgreat Buddhist universities like Nalanda and Vikramashila✓
  • Cthe Kailasa temple at Ellora
  • Dthe Arab conquest of Sindh

Working

✓

The Palas patronised Buddhist universities such as Nalanda and Vikramashila. Mamallapuram was Pallava, Ellora Rashtrakuta, and the Sindh conquest Arab.

Q18

A 'dvarapandita' at Vikramashila was a:

  • A'scholar gatekeeper' who tested students before admission✓
  • Bsubordinate ruler paying tribute
  • Cchariot-shaped temple
  • Dvillage assembly

Working

✓

A dvarapandita was a scholar-gatekeeper testing students for admission at Vikramashila. A tribute-paying ruler is a samanta, a chariot-temple a ratha, and a village assembly a sabha.

Q19

That Vikramashila tested students through a dvarapandita shows its universities:

  • Aadmitted no students at all
  • Bmaintained high standards of learning✓
  • Ctaught nothing useful
  • Dwere really army camps

Working

✓

Testing entrants through a scholar-gatekeeper shows high academic standards. The universities did admit students, taught valuable subjects, and were centres of learning, not army camps.

Q20

The Gurjara-Pratiharas are described as the:

  • Arulers of the Tamil far south
  • Bdefenders of the north-west✓
  • Cbuilders of Ellora
  • Dkings of Bengal

Working

✓

The Gurjara-Pratiharas defended the north-west (notably against the Arabs). The Tamil south, Ellora and Bengal belonged to other powers.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

By holding the north-west, the Gurjara-Pratiharas helped:

  • Ainvite the Arabs to conquer all India
  • Blimit the Arab advance beyond Sindh✓
  • Cend the Tripartite Struggle peacefully
  • Dbuild the temples of Mamallapuram

Working

✓

The Gurjara-Pratiharas helped confine the Arabs to a small foothold. They did not invite conquest, peacefully end the struggle, or build Mamallapuram.

Q22

The Rashtrakutas are linked to the wonder of:

  • AEllora✓
  • BMamallapuram
  • CNalanda
  • DKannauj alone

Working

✓

The Rashtrakutas are linked to Ellora's great rock-cut temple. Mamallapuram is Pallava, Nalanda Pala, and Kannauj the prize of the Tripartite Struggle.

Q23

The Kailasa temple at Ellora is remarkable because it is:

  • Abuilt of paper
  • Bmade of digital screens
  • Cmonolithic - carved from a single block of rock✓
  • Dassembled from imported bricks

Working

✓

The Kailasa temple is monolithic, carved from one mass of rock. It is not paper, digital, or imported brick.

Q24

'Monolithic' means:

  • Abuilt from thousands of small bricks
  • Bmade of wood and cloth
  • Cmade from a single block of stone or rock✓
  • Dconstructed entirely underwater

Working

✓

Monolithic means from a single block of stone. Brick, wood-and-cloth and underwater construction are different.

Q25

Carving an entire temple from one rock, as at Ellora, shows the age's:

  • Alack of any artistic ability
  • Bcomplete cultural decline
  • Cdependence on foreign builders
  • Dextraordinary skill in art and engineering✓

Working

✓

A monolithic temple shows extraordinary artistic and engineering skill - evidence the age was creative. It is not a sign of no ability, decline, or foreign dependence.

Q26

The Chalukyas of this age had their base at:

  • AVatapi✓
  • BKannauj
  • CBengal
  • DSindh

Working

✓

The Chalukyas ruled from Vatapi in the Deccan. Kannauj, Bengal and Sindh belonged to other powers.

Q27

The Chalukyas, Pallavas and reviving Cholas built mainly:

  • ABuddhist universities in Bengal
  • Bgreat temples and trade networks in the south and Deccan✓
  • Cthe Kailasa temple at Ellora
  • Dan Arab state in Sindh

Working

✓

The southern and Deccan powers built great temples and trade networks. Bengal's universities were Pala, Ellora Rashtrakuta, and Sindh's state Arab.

Q28

The Pallavas ruled from Kanchi and are famous for the stone temples of:

  • AEllora
  • BNalanda
  • CVatapi
  • DMamallapuram✓

Working

✓

The Pallavas of Kanchi built the stone temples of Mamallapuram. Ellora is Rashtrakuta, Nalanda Pala, and Vatapi Chalukya.

Q29

A 'ratha', as carved at Mamallapuram, is:

  • Aa village assembly
  • Ba subordinate ruler
  • Ca man-made reservoir
  • Da temple or shrine carved to look like a chariot✓

Working

✓

A ratha (Sanskrit 'chariot') is a chariot-shaped shrine, as at Mamallapuram. A village assembly is a sabha, a subordinate ruler a samanta, and a reservoir a tank.

Q30

The rock-cut rathas of Mamallapuram are evidence of the Pallavas':

  • Askill in paper-making
  • Bcontrol of the Silk Route
  • Crule over Bengal
  • Dmastery of stone temple art✓

Working

✓

The rathas show Pallava mastery of stone temple art. They are not about paper, the Silk Route, or Bengal.

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

Both Ellora's Kailasa temple and Mamallapuram's rathas show that this age excelled at:

  • Aprinting books on paper
  • Bcarving temples directly from rock✓
  • Cminting paper money
  • Dbuilding with steel

Working

✓

Both are rock-cut wonders, showing the age's skill in carving temples from stone. Paper printing, paper money and steel building are unrelated.

Q32

In the south, alongside the Chalukyas and Pallavas, which ancient dynasties revived in this age?

  • Athe Palas, Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas
  • Bthe Mauryas, Shungas and Guptas
  • Cthe Hunas, Shakas and Kushanas
  • Dthe Cholas, Pandyas and Cheras✓

Working

✓

The Cholas, Pandyas and Cheras revived in the south. The Tripartite trio ruled the north, and the others are earlier dynasties or invaders.

Q33

The revival of the Cholas, Pandyas and Cheras shows that the Tamil south was:

  • Aagain a centre of strong kingdoms, temples and trade✓
  • Bempty of any kingdoms
  • Cruled entirely by the Arabs
  • Dpart of the Pala empire of Bengal

Working

✓

Their revival made the Tamil south a hub of kingdoms, temples and trade once more. It was not empty, Arab-ruled, or Pala-ruled.

Q34

A 'samanta' was a:

  • Asubordinate ruler through whom a king governed outer regions, in return for tribute and soldiers✓
  • BTamil poet-saint
  • Cchariot-shaped temple
  • DChinese pilgrim

Working

✓

A samanta was a subordinate ruler (vassal) governing outer regions for a king, giving tribute and soldiers. A poet-saint is an Alvar or Nayanar, a chariot-temple a ratha, and a pilgrim like Xuanzang.

Q35

The claim 'a king of this age personally ran every part of his kingdom' is wrong because he ruled through:

  • Ano officials at all
  • Blayers of samantas and largely self-governing villages✓
  • Conly foreign Arab governors
  • Da single nationwide election

Working

✓

Kings ruled through subordinate samantas and self-governing villages, not directly over every village. There were officials, not Arab governors or national elections.

Q36

A 'sabha' was a:

  • Asingle all-powerful emperor
  • Bchariot-shaped temple
  • Cvillage assembly✓
  • DBuddhist university

Working

✓

A sabha was a village assembly through which villages governed themselves. It was not an emperor, a ratha, or a university.

Q37

The Chola Uttaramerur inscriptions are valuable because they describe:

  • Ahow to carve a monolithic temple
  • Bthe rules of zero
  • Chow members of the village sabha were selected✓
  • Dthe Arab conquest of Sindh

Working

✓

The Uttaramerur inscriptions record how village sabha members were chosen - evidence of local self-rule. They are not about temple-carving, zero, or Sindh.

Q38

That villages governed themselves through a sabha shows this age had:

  • Ano government below the king
  • Brule only by foreign powers
  • Ca single national parliament
  • Dreal local self-government beneath the kings✓

Working

✓

Self-governing village sabhas show genuine local self-rule under the kings. There was government below the king, it was not foreign-only, and there was no national parliament.

Q39

Royal power in this age is best described as flowing:

  • Adirectly from the king to each individual villager
  • Bonly from foreign Arab rulers
  • Cdown through many hands - samantas and village assemblies - not straight from king to every villager✓
  • Dfrom a single elected president

Working

✓

Power passed down through samantas and sabhas rather than directly from king to villager. It was not direct, Arab-only, or presidential.

Q40

A 'land grant' in this age:

  • Agave land to a person, community or temple, creating a class of landholders✓
  • Btook all land away from everyone
  • Cwas a chariot-shaped temple
  • Dwas a Tamil devotional poem

Working

✓

Land grants gave land to people, communities or temples, creating landholders. They did not seize land, and they were not rathas or poems.

  • Awar chariot
  • Blarge man-made reservoir storing rainwater for irrigation and daily use✓
  • CBuddhist monastery
  • Dsubordinate ruler

Working

✓

A tank (eri) was a man-made reservoir for water storage and irrigation. It was not a chariot, a monastery, or a samanta.

Q42

Merchant guilds and busy towns in this age show that the economy was:

  • Acompletely destroyed
  • Bthriving, not collapsing✓
  • Cbased only on barter with no trade
  • Dcontrolled entirely by the Arabs

Working

✓

Merchant guilds and flourishing towns show a thriving economy. It was not destroyed, barter-only, or Arab-controlled.

Q43

Land grants, guilds and tanks together show that this age combined:

  • Atotal economic ruin
  • Ba complete lack of trade
  • Ca productive economy with careful management of land and water✓
  • Drule with no landholders at all

Working

✓

Land grants, guilds and water tanks reflect a productive, well-managed economy. It was not ruined, trade-free, or landholder-free.

Q44

The claim 'the caste (varna-jati) system was completely rigid and fixed' is wrong because:

  • Acaste never changed for anyone, anywhere
  • Bthere were no jatis at all
  • Cit was flexible and varied by region - for example, the Palas rose from Shudra to Kshatriya status✓
  • Deveryone belonged to one single group

Working

✓

The system was flexible: the Palas rose in status and professions like the Kayasthas became jatis. It was not unchanging, jati-free, or single-group.

Q45

The Kayasthas are given as an example of how:

  • Aa jati could never change
  • Bforeigners ruled all India
  • Ca profession could turn into a jati over time✓
  • Dtemples were carved from rock

Working

✓

The Kayasthas show a profession becoming a jati over time - evidence of flexibility. It is not about unchanging jatis, foreign rule, or temple-carving.

Q46

That jatis multiplied and some families changed status shows the social system was:

  • Acomplex and changing, not frozen✓
  • Bperfectly simple and unchanging
  • Cabolished completely
  • Didentical everywhere in India

Working

✓

Multiplying jatis and status changes show a complex, changing system. It was not frozen, abolished, or identical everywhere.

Q47

The chapter notes that, in this age, some women and queens:

  • Awere entirely absent from public life
  • Bruled the whole subcontinent alone
  • Cheld real influence and power✓
  • Dwere forbidden from any role

Working

✓

Some women and queens held real influence. They were not wholly absent, sole rulers of all India, or barred from every role.

Q48

The 'Bhakti' tradition began in the:

  • ACentral Asian steppe
  • BArab lands of Sindh
  • CHimalayan north alone
  • DTamil south✓

Working

✓

Bhakti began in the Tamil south. It did not start on the steppe, in Arab Sindh, or only in the Himalayan north.

Q49

'Bhakti' means:

  • Apersonal, loving devotion to a deity, open to all✓
  • Ba war between three kingdoms
  • Ca tax paid to a king
  • Dthe study of inscriptions

Working

✓

Bhakti (Sanskrit bhaj, 'to be devoted') is personal, loving devotion to a deity, open to everyone. It is not a war, a tax, or epigraphy.

Q50

Bhakti differed from older Vedic practice by being:

  • Aopen to all, through personal devotion rather than only formal sacrifice✓
  • Bclosed to everyone except kings
  • Cbased only on grand sacrifices
  • Da foreign Arab religion

Working

✓

Bhakti opened devotion to all through personal love of a deity, rather than only formal Vedic sacrifice. It was not king-only, sacrifice-only, or Arab.

✓

Q56

The Arabs first conquered which part of the subcontinent, in 712 CE?

  • Athe whole of India
  • BSindh✓
  • Cthe Tamil south
  • DBengal

Working

✓

The Arabs conquered Sindh in 712 CE. They did not take all of India, the Tamil south, or Bengal.

Q57

The claim 'the Arabs conquered most of India in 712 CE' is wrong because:

  • Athey conquered the entire subcontinent
  • Bthey never reached India at all
  • Cthey took only Sindh, and Arab rule stayed confined to a small foothold for centuries✓

The Arabs took only Sindh, and their rule stayed limited to a small foothold (Sindh and part of Punjab). They did not conquer all India, nor were they absent, nor did they rule to 1947.

Q58

✓

Indian rulers such as the Gurjara-Pratiharas and Lalitaditya resisted, confining the Arabs. It was not for lack of resistance, an Arab choice, or Chinese help.

Q59

That the Arabs were repulsed from 637 CE and then confined after 712 CE shows:

  • Bstrong Indian resistance limited the foreign advance✓
  • Cthe Arabs easily took everything
  • Dthere were no Arab attempts on India

✓

Being repulsed and then confined shows strong Indian resistance limited the Arab advance. There was resistance, the Arabs did not take everything, and they did attempt expansion.
