1. (C) All three outcrops are the same granite, so original hardness cannot explain the gradient. The decisive variable is climate: warmth and plentiful water at the foot accelerate both chemical dissolution and physical (freeze-thaw, root) breakdown of rock, while the cold dry summit weathers very slowly. Hence depth tracks environment, not a pre-existing softness. The claim that the summit rock was hardest is contradicted by 'identical' rock. Altitude does not deepen soil at the top; here the high site has the least. Groundwater does not lift finished soil upward; soil forms in place as rock weathers and organisms add humus.

2. (A) The dated series shows depth increasing with age over millennia — soil deepens as rock weathers and humus accumulates slowly. One season cannot replicate thousands of years of weathering and litter decay. The 50-year flow has only a dusting, not finished soil, so the 'one monsoon' claim is refuted by its own data. Floods do not deposit ready-made deep soil. The youngest flow has the least soil, so depth does not start high and thin with age — it is the reverse.

3. (D) Weathering breaks rock in place into smaller pieces; erosion is the carrying-away of loosened material. Frost-splitting (i) and wind-scouring (iii) break rock where it sits, so both are weathering even though (iii) uses wind. Runoff sweeping topsoil (ii) and a river transporting gravel (iv) both remove and transport material, so both are erosion. 'All erosion' wrongly reclassifies the in-place break-up. Sorting by 'wind involved' is a false rule — wind can do either job. 'All weathering' ignores that (ii) and (iv) are transport, not break-up.

4. (B) Forest litter kept plot 1's A horizon thick and dark while erosion was held in check. Bare cropping on plot 2 let the unprotected A horizon erode away; the layer now at the surface is firm and stained with clay washed down from above — the signature of the B (accumulation) horizon, now exposed. A 'paler A horizon' contradicts the clay-staining and loss of humus. Bedrock does not rise and weather to clay in 120 years. A fresh O horizon would be spongy half-rotted leaves, not a firm clay-stained layer.

5. (A) The dark crumbly band is the A horizon (zone of loss); the stained firm band is the B horizon (zone of gain). Rainwater seeps down through the A horizon, washing fine clay and dissolved iron out of it and depositing them in the B horizon below — so the staining is imported from above, not self-made. Capillary rise from bedrock does not supply the bulk clay-iron of the B horizon. Sideways wind delivery to a single buried band is not how leaching works. Earthworms move material but the dominant clay-iron transfer here is downward leaching, not upward worm carriage.

6. (B) Gain of leached clay and iron is greatest in the B horizon (the zone of accumulation). The A horizon is a zone of loss, but it still passes and holds a little before it migrates, so it ranks above the deep layers. The C horizon is barely-weathered parent rock with little imported fine material, and solid bedrock receives essentially none. Ordering A first reverses loss and gain. Putting bedrock or C first ignores that the deep unweathered layers are not where leached material collects.

7. (A) The imported pale, clay-stained, firm layer is B-horizon subsoil — low in humus and nutrients — and it now caps the original humus-rich A horizon, which is buried out of root reach, so vegetables struggle. It is not a re-laid A horizon; the A horizon is dark and humus-rich, not pale subsoil. It is loose subsoil, not solid bedrock. And clay-stained subsoil is not an O horizon of leaf litter, which would be spongy and organic.

8. (A) Humus forms from decaying plant and animal litter at the surface, so the A horizon is humus-richest; the C horizon is broken, partly-weathered parent rock with little organic matter. Rock does not pump humus upward into deep layers. The C horizon is not the surface topsoil — that is the A horizon. And C is mostly mineral rock fragments, not entirely living organisms, so that 'wrong' reasoning is itself incorrect.

9. (B) Top to bottom a profile runs A (dark, crumbly, root-filled topsoil = jar X), B (pale, firm, clay-stained accumulation = jar Y), then C (loose parent-rock chips = jar Z). So the fill order is X, Y, Z. Rock fragments lie deep, not at the top, so Z is not first. The clay-stained layer is the buried B horizon, not the surface, so Y is not first. The C horizon (Z) lies below the B horizon, not directly under the topsoil, so X-Z-Y is wrong.

10. (C) A dark surface band is humus-rich topsoil, and humus needs a steady supply of decaying plant and animal litter. Deserts have sparse vegetation, so little litter and little humus form — hence the thin or absent dark band. Deserts are dry, not heavily rained on, so 'rain washing it down' is false. The profiles share bedrock, so 'no bedrock' is contradicted. Humus does not melt and evaporate; it simply fails to accumulate without litter.

11. (A) Rate = volume / time. Column 1: 480 / 8 = 60 mL/min. Column 2: 360 / 9 = 40 mL/min. So column 1 drains faster. Longer time does not mean faster — it is the rate that matters, not the clock. Comparing raw volumes (480 vs 360) ignores the differing times, so '480 mL/min' is a misuse of the figures. The two rates differ (60 vs 40), so they are not equal.

12. (B) Rate = volume / time. Bed A: 540 / 9 = 60 mL/min. 13. (A) The columns are stated to be the same loam, so texture is constant. Drain time rises in step with depth (5, 10, 15 min for 10, 20, 30 cm) — exactly what greater path length predicts at one rate. So the longest time reflects depth, not clay. 'Longer time proves finer particles' ignores the depth confound. Depth clearly does affect drain time here, so claiming it has no effect is false. Finishing fastest reflects the least depth, not the smallest pores.

14. (C) Comparing raw drain times is only valid when the same volume is used; here 400 mL versus 200 mL breaks the fair test. The proper fix is to compute each rate (volume / time) and compare those, since rate normalises for volume. Clay's percolation is perfectly measurable, just slow. Mixing the soils would destroy the comparison entirely. The timer must run while water drains, not start after — starting late would measure nothing.

16. (A) Time = volume / rate. 600 / 24 = 25 min; 900 / 24 = 37.5 min. Reading 24 as the time ignores that 24 is a rate (mL per minute), not minutes. Multiplying rate by volume (24 x 600) gives a meaningless 14400. Dividing rate by volume inverts the formula. Only volume divided by rate yields the correct times.

18. (A) Rates: pot 1 = 720 / 12 = 60 mL/min; pot 2 = 720 / 16 = 45 mL/min; gap = 60 - 45 = 15 mL/min. 21. (A) Paddy needs water to stand, so it belongs in the slow-draining plot A (6 mL/min). Groundnut needs loose, well-drained, airy soil, so it belongs in the fast-draining plot B (54 mL/min). Shortest time (X, 4 min) is the fastest, not the slowest. Largest volume (Z) does not mean slowest — its rate is high. Smallest volume (W) does not by itself mean clay; its rate of 40 is middling. The further loss on strong heating (90 to 82 g), with the soil turning pale, is the dark humus burning off — paling confirms organic matter is gone. The water was already removed in the drying step, so it cannot account for the second drop. Mineral sand and silt do not burn away. Adding oxygen would not lower weight, and that is not what strong heating does to humus — it burns it off.

24. (C) Loss = 100 minus final mass: A lost 8 g, C lost 15 g, B lost 22 g. Heating to dryness and weighing the loss is precisely how retained-water content is compared, so 'cannot reveal' is wrong.

25. (C) Bubbles come from air trapped in the soil's pore spaces escaping when water displaces it. Pressing compacts the clod, closing pores, so less air is trapped and fewer bubbles form. The bubbles are air, not water, so 'destroyed the water' is irrelevant. The clod releases air to the water, it does not absorb air from it. And it is the presence of trapped air — not its absence — that produces bubbles.

26. (D) Bubbles rising from a clod dropped in water are trapped air escaping (experiment 1 = air); mist condensing on cool glass after heating is the soil's own water turning to vapour and back to liquid (experiment 2 = water). Mist is condensed water, not gas, so 'both prove air' is wrong. The conclusions are not reversed — bubbles mean air, mist means water. And soil naturally contains both air and water, so 'neither proves anything' is false.

27. (C) Drying evaporates the water; the pores it leaves are then occupied by air, so the air fraction rises as the water fraction falls. Mineral grains are solid and do not evaporate. Humus does not boil off merely on sun-drying, and air does not disappear — it expands to fill the vacated pores. Air does not turn into water; that is the reverse of what drying does.

28. (B) Sand (largest grains) drained fastest at 4 min, loam at 8 min, clay (finest) slowest at 20 min. The finest soil took 20 min, not 6, so they are not the same. Drain time at equal volume directly indicates texture, so it is judgeable.

29. (A) Worm burrows are open channels that admit air and let water percolate; remove the worms and those channels are lost, so percolation falls and aeration worsens in the worm-free field. Worms do not clog channels — they create them. Worms affect structure and water movement, not merely colour. And killing worms removes, not adds, loosening, so percolation and aeration drop rather than improve.

30. (D) Fast (instant) drainage requires wide pores between coarse particles; maximal wetness requires fine, tightly-packed pores that retain water. These structures are opposites, so a soil cannot maximise both — percolation rate and water-holding capacity are inversely related. They are not the same property. Humus improves balance but cannot make a soil simultaneously the fastest-draining AND the most retentive. Clay's slow drainage is the opposite of 'drains instantly,' so the clay claim is self-contradictory.

31. (C) Sand drains fastest and holds least, so it firms up first (bed 1); clay drains slowest and holds most, so it stays soggy longest (bed 3); loam is intermediate (bed 2). Clay does not shed water fastest — it is the slowest. Sand does not stay soggiest — it holds the least water. Different textures dry at different rates, so they could not all firm up together.

32. (C) Aeration is worst where pores stay full of water. Pot 1's clay, standing in water, keeps its fine pores saturated, starving roots of air — the worst. Pot 2's sand drains freely, so air re-enters its wide pores once the tray is emptied; being flooded once does not permanently exclude air. Pot 4's dry sand is full of air, not devoid of it. Pot 3's moist loam still holds plenty of air in its open pores. Only standing water in fine clay pores chokes off air.

33. (A) Well-rotted compost (humus) improves structure in both directions: in pure sand it boosts water-holding capacity (and nutrients), and in heavy clay it opens the structure so it drains and aerates better. So it helps both beds. It does improve sandy soil's retention, so 'only the clay' is wrong. Compost opens pores and feeds soil rather than sealing it. And it cannot transmute sand into clay or clay into sand — it moderates each toward a better loam-like balance.

34. (B) Only a direct count of micro-organisms, mites and worms shows living things. Bubbling in water shows trapped air, not life. Misting on a heated tube shows moisture (condensed water vapour), not breathing. Mass loss on strong heating is mostly evaporated water plus burnt humus — humus is dead organic matter, so the loss does not by itself prove living organisms. Each non-life option misreads a physical property as evidence of life.

35. (D) Soil organisms need moisture to live, humus as food, and air for respiration — all abundant in loam, scarce in salty sand. Organisms do not favour the largest particles; loam's value is its balanced structure with humus, not coarse grains. Fast drainage is not what supports life — water and food do. Cold does not maximise life; many organisms need moderate warmth.

36. (B) Clay's fine particles pack so tightly that its pores are tiny and resist water flow — that is why a clay bowl holds water and why clay soil drains slowly. Tight packing slows flow, it does not force water straight through fast. Clay actually holds a lot of water (high water-holding capacity), not none. And clay's percolation rate is far lower than sand's despite both being mineral — particle size and packing, not just composition, set the rate.

37. (A) Standing water saturates clay's tiny pores, shutting out air, so gram roots are starved of oxygen and rot — 'more water' harms an aeration-loving crop. Clay drains slowly, so plot B did not dry out from fast drainage. Flooding leaches nutrients downward, not upward to the surface. Gram is not a water crop; plot A, which thrived, was the correctly managed one, so the standing-water plot is the failure, not the reverse.

38. (D) Paddy needs standing water, so it suits the water-logging clay; gram favours moisture-holding clayey-loam; cotton prefers a light, well-drained sandy-loam; groundnut wants loose, fast-draining sand for its pods. The other matchings put paddy in fast-draining soil (it would dry out), groundnut in water-logging clay (its pods rot), or cram all crops into clay despite their differing water needs.

43. (B) Soil pollution is the addition of harmful substances; toxic effluent (i) and plastic waste (ii) both fit, poisoning or choking the soil. 60. (C) Rate = volume / time. Soil X: 720 / 9 = 80 mL/min. Soil Y: 720 / 15 = 48 mL/min. So X is the faster drainer, and Y's longer time means it is slower, not faster — a longer clock for the same volume signals a lower rate. Equal volume with different times gives unequal rates, so they are not equal. Multiplying 720 x 9 (= 6480) misuses the formula; rate is volume divided by time, not multiplied.
