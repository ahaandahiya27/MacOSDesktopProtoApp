**Instructions:** Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Most farm work in rural India is done by women not men; the Green Revolution had a MIXED record (more grain but degraded soil, depleted groundwater, pollution, health harm and debt); and natural/organic farming responds by cutting chemical inputs rather than abandoning food production. Do not write on this paper — mark answers on your answer sheet.

1. A family grows rice on a small plot, eats almost all of it, and sells nothing in any normal year. In a good monsoon year they have a tiny surplus they exchange with a neighbour. This farming is best classified as:
   (A) commercial farming, because they exchanged a surplus once
   (B) intensive farming, because the plot is small
   (C) subsistence farming, since the primary purpose is to feed the household rather than to sell
   (D) horticulture, because rice is a food crop

2. Two farmers each have one hectare. Farmer X uses lots of labour, irrigation and inputs to get a very high yield from that hectare; Farmer Y spreads simple effort thinly over the land for a modest yield. The contrast between X and Y is best described as:
   (A) intensive versus extensive use of the same land
   (B) subsistence versus commercial purpose
   (C) kharif versus rabi season
   (D) organic versus Green Revolution farming

3. A farmer switches from growing food for her own family to growing cotton entirely for sale to a textile mill. The single most important change in how we would now classify her farming is that it has become:
   (A) intensive, because cotton needs more care
   (B) commercial, because the crop is now grown chiefly to sell for income
   (C) rain-fed, because cotton is a kharif crop
   (D) organic, because she stopped growing food

4. Why can the very same field be called 'subsistence' by one observer and 'commercial' by another in different years?
   (A) because the soil type changes from year to year
   (B) because the classification depends on the farmer's purpose, which can change from feeding the family to selling for income
   (C) because the field physically moves between seasons
   (D) because subsistence and commercial mean exactly the same thing

5. A landless worker is hired by the day to weed and harvest on someone else's commercial farm. She herself owns no land and keeps none of the crop. Within the chapter's picture of Indian farming, she is best understood as:
   (A) part of the farm labour input, on which Indian agriculture heavily depends
   (B) a subsistence farmer feeding her own family
   (C) a commercial farmer selling her own crop
   (D) irrelevant to agriculture since she owns no land

6. A flower-and-vegetable grower near a city sells everything she raises to restaurants. Two labels could fit her at once. The pair that correctly describes her farming is:
   (A) cereal farming and subsistence farming
   (B) horticulture and commercial farming
   (C) extensive farming and rain-fed farming
   (D) the Green Revolution and terrace farming

7. A development officer says, 'Commercial farming is always better for the country than subsistence farming.' The most accurate response from the chapter is:
   (A) yes, because selling crops is always more useful than eating them
   (B) no, because subsistence farming always produces more food than commercial farming
   (C) the two are identical, so the comparison is meaningless
   (D) each suits different families and conditions; commercial farming earns income but can raise risk and cost, while subsistence farming secures a family's food

8. A farmer with a large holding cannot afford much labour or irrigation, so he plants a hardy crop across the whole area and waits for the rain, accepting a low yield per hectare. His approach is an example of:
   (A) intensive irrigated farming
   (B) commercial horticulture
   (C) the Green Revolution package
   (D) extensive, largely rain-fed farming

9. Which statement best captures why 'all Indian farming is the same' is a mistaken claim?
   (A) farming differs by soil, climate, season, water source and purpose, so practices vary widely across the country
   (B) because every farm uses exactly the same seeds and tools
   (C) because all farms are commercial and irrigated
   (D) because Indian farming never changed after ancient times

10. A farmer wants a crop to mature on the natural rains of the wet season without needing canal water. The season she should sow in is:
   (A) the kharif (monsoon) season, sown with the arriving southwest monsoon
   (B) the rabi (winter) season
   (C) the zaid (short summer) season
   (D) any season, since rainfall is the same all year

11. Wheat is typically a rabi crop. A farmer who insists on sowing wheat in June, as the monsoon arrives, would most likely find that:
   (A) it grows perfectly, since all crops grow in all seasons
   (B) it becomes a kharif crop and yields more than ever
   (C) the crop struggles, because wheat suits the cool, drier winter season rather than the hot, wet monsoon
   (D) it turns into a zaid crop and ripens in two weeks

12. A farmer in a region served mainly by the northeast monsoon plans her main crop around when that rain arrives. She should expect the heaviest rain around:
   (A) June to September
   (B) March to May
   (C) evenly through all twelve months
   (D) October to December

13. The southwest monsoon arrives late and weak one year. The farms hit hardest will be those that are:
   (A) rain-fed, because they depend on the monsoon alone for water
   (B) irrigated by canals and tube wells, because pumps need rain to work
   (C) growing rabi crops, because they are sown during the monsoon
   (D) practising crop rotation, because rotation requires heavy rain

14. A farmer wants to grow a quick crop in the brief, hot window between harvesting his winter crop and sowing his monsoon crop. He is planning a:
   (A) rabi crop, grown through the long cool winter
   (B) zaid crop, grown in the short summer between rabi and kharif
   (C) kharif crop, sown with the southwest monsoon
   (D) perennial crop that grows for several years

15. Put these in correct seasonal order through one year starting after the spring: monsoon crops, winter crops, short summer crops. The right sequence is:
   (A) winter (rabi) into short summer (zaid) into monsoon (kharif)
   (B) monsoon (kharif) into short summer (zaid) into winter (rabi)
   (C) all three together, since seasons overlap completely
   (D) short summer (zaid) into monsoon (kharif) into winter (rabi)

16. A farmer notices that a crop sown with the first heavy June rains is harvested around the start of the cool season. This timing tells us it is most likely a:
   (A) rabi crop, which is sown in winter and harvested in spring
   (B) kharif crop, sown in the monsoon and harvested as the rains end
   (C) zaid crop, which is grown in the short summer
   (D) crop that ignores all seasons

17. Why do many rabi crops in dry-winter regions need irrigation while many kharif crops in the same regions do not?
   (A) because rabi crops dislike water and irrigation tricks them into growing
   (B) because the monsoon rains fall mainly in winter
   (C) because kharif crops fall in the rainy monsoon while rabi crops grow in the comparatively dry winter, so they must be watered artificially
   (D) because kharif crops are grown only with canal water

18. A government wants to reduce crop failures when the monsoon is unreliable. The most directly relevant investment, from the chapter, is to:
   (A) expand irrigation such as canals, wells and drip systems so crops get water even in weak-monsoon years
   (B) ban kharif crops so farming only happens in winter
   (C) encourage every farm to depend more on rainfall alone
   (D) convert all fields to a single crop grown every year

19. Two neighbouring fields have the same crop, but one is on fertile river-silt soil and the other on poorer soil. The likely reason the first yields more is that:
   (A) fertile soil needs no nutrients at all to feed plants
   (B) the richer soil is actually desert sand blown in by wind
   (C) alluvial soil, made of silt brought by rivers, is rich in nutrients and holds moisture well
   (D) river silt blocks roots and lowers yield

20. A farmer adds plenty of well-rotted compost and manure to a tired field and the crop improves. The best explanation is that the organic matter, or humus, has:
   (A) removed all water from the soil so roots stay dry
   (B) turned the soil into solid rock
   (C) killed the soil organisms that crops depend on
   (D) added nutrients and helped the soil hold moisture, making it more fertile

21. A farmer grows a nutrient-hungry crop in the same field every single year and the yields slowly fall. The chapter's recommended remedy is to:
   (A) grow the very same crop even more often to strengthen the soil
   (B) stop adding any humus so the soil can rest
   (C) remove all the topsoil and replace it with sand
   (D) rotate different crops through the field so the soil is not drained of one set of nutrients

22. A pulse crop is rotated into a field after a cereal, and the next cereal crop grows better. The most likely reason, consistent with the chapter, is that crop rotation:
   (A) physically spins the field to mix the soil
   (B) lets different crops use and return different nutrients, so the soil keeps a better nutrient balance
   (C) removes every nutrient from the soil at once
   (D) stops crops from growing in that field again

23. On a steep hillside, a farmer cuts the slope into level steps before planting. Two benefits follow. They are best described as:
   (A) the steps remove the need for any soil or water
   (B) the steps turn the hill into desert sand
   (C) the steps hold rainwater and reduce soil being washed downhill, allowing crops on steep land
   (D) the steps make crops grow without seeds

24. A farmer is deciding between saving seeds from her own harvest and buying high-yield seeds from a company. The most balanced, chapter-consistent reasoning is that:
   (A) saved seeds are cheap and suited to local conditions, while bought high-yield seeds can raise yields but cost money and often need more water and chemicals
   (B) saved seeds always out-yield every modern seed in every field
   (C) bought seeds are free and never need extra water or chemicals
   (D) the choice makes no difference because all seeds behave identically

25. A farmer relies only on rainfall and has no canal, well or pump. The single biggest risk to her harvest, by the chapter's logic, is:
   (A) too much canal water flooding the field
   (B) her pump breaking down
   (C) the drip system clogging
   (D) a weak or late monsoon, since rain is her only water source

26. Why is a low raised embankment, or bund, built around or across a field useful to a farmer?
   (A) it is a high-yield seed that grows faster
   (B) it holds water in the field and helps stop soil from being washed away
   (C) it is a chemical that kills pests
   (D) it is a short summer crop

27. India's many old tanks, bunds and step-wells, alongside modern canals and tube wells, best show that managing water for crops is:
   (A) a problem invented only after the Green Revolution
   (B) something Indian farmers never thought about
   (C) a long-standing concern that farmers have addressed with both traditional and modern methods
   (D) needed only in regions that never receive any rain

28. A survey of who actually sows, weeds, transplants and harvests on small farms across rural India would most likely find that the majority of this work is done by:
   (A) men only

   (A) tractors are never used anywhere in Indian farming
   (B) no men ever take part in Indian farming

30. An economist notes that much of the field work done by women in rural India is unpaid family labour that does not show up in wage records. The most accurate conclusion is that:

   (B) women's contribution to farming can be undercounted, even though they do a large share of the actual work
   (C) only paid male labour counts as farming

31. A policy aims to support 'farmers' with training and credit but quietly assumes every farmer is a man who owns land. Why might this poorly serve rural farming?

   (B) because all farmers are wealthy landowners anyway
   (C) because it overlooks the many women and landless labourers who do much of the actual farm work
   (D) because farm labour is no longer needed in India

32. Recognising that women carry out most farm operations should most reasonably influence agricultural programmes to:
   (A) stop offering any support to farms with women
   (B) direct training, tools and support to the women who actually do the work
   (C) assume women will not benefit from better tools
   (D) replace all women workers with imported labour

33. A claim says, 'Because tractors and machines now do most field work, the role of women in Indian farming has ended.' The best correction is:
   (A) yes, machines now do everything, so no one works the fields
