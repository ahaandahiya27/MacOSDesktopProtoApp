## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** With a near-point source, rays graze the disc's rim and continue straight, spreading apart on their way to the wall; the farther the wall lies beyond the disc, the more those rays have spread, so the shadow is largest when the disc is near the lamp and smallest (close to the disc's own size) when the disc is pressed near the wall. Moving the disc toward the wall therefore shrinks the shadow. It cannot grow without limit — that would be the case if the disc moved toward the lamp, not the wall. Shadow size plainly does depend on positions, so 'never depends' is false. A shadow is an absence of light, not a refracted band, so no coloured fringe is produced by an opaque disc in straight-line light.

**2. (C)** Light travels straight, so a ray from the top of the sign passes through the pinhole and lands low on the screen, and a bottom ray lands high — that crossing is what inverts the picture and it happens at the hole regardless of box length. Making the screen farther from the hole lets the already-crossed rays continue diverging, so the inverted picture grows larger. Box length never flips the image upright; the inversion is fixed by the single crossing at the hole. A pinhole does not act like a prism, so no dispersion band appears.

**3. (A)** Each lamp casts its own full shadow of the pole; where the two full shadows overlap, both lamps are blocked and the strip is fully dark, but in the flanking strips one lamp's rays are blocked while the other lamp's rays still arrive, so those points receive half the light and look grey. Light travels straight and an opaque pole does not bend rays around itself. The wall's colour is fixed; it does not 'turn grey' — it simply receives less light. Light does not slow and smear beside a solid object in this chapter's straight-line model.

**4. (B)** Because light goes straight, only rays lying on the single straight line through all three holes, the eye and the lamp can reach the eye; sliding the middle card breaks that line and no straight path remains, so the lamp disappears. The light is not bounced card-to-card — these are holes, not mirrors. If light bent through pinholes it could curve around the shifted card and still be seen, but it cannot. Equal hole size is irrelevant; alignment, not size, is what matters.

**5. (C)** A flat slab has two parallel faces; the ray bends one way entering and the equal-but-opposite amount leaving, so it emerges travelling in its original direction merely offset to the side — hence the line stays parallel. A lens has non-parallel curved faces and would change direction, which the slab does not. Any slight colour separation in a parallel slab recombines because the faces are parallel, but the question is about direction, and the beam stays one straight line. Inside any uniform medium light still travels straight, not randomly.

**6. (D)** Sunlight comes in straight rays; when the Sun is high the rays graze the pole top almost straight down, making a short shadow, but when the Sun is low the grazing ray slants and travels far across the ground before it would have hit, so the shadow is long. The pole's height is fixed. Light does not bend around the pole — a shadow is precisely where straight rays are blocked. A shadow is missing light, not a reflected image cast by the ground.

**7. (A)** The normal bisects the angle between the incident and reflected rays because both make equal angles with it; so each ray is at half of 110 degrees, i.e. 55 degrees from the normal, and the angle of incidence is 55 degrees. The full 110 degrees is the sum of the two equal angles, not one of them. Subtracting 90 has no physical basis here. The 35 degrees would be the angle to the mirror surface (90 minus 55), not the angle of incidence, which is measured from the normal.

**8. (B)** Every individual ray obeys angle-in-equals-angle-out at the point it strikes, on smooth and rough surfaces alike; the cardboard merely presents countless tiny facets pointing different ways, so the obediently-reflected rays scatter and no organised image forms — this is diffuse reflection. The law is universal, so it does not 'fail' on the rough surface. There are not two different reflection rules. Roughness scatters light, it does not reflect more strongly.

**9. (D)** Paper is rough, so it scatters incoming room light in all directions (diffuse reflection): that scattered light reaches your eyes from every part of the page, letting you read the print, but because the rays are sent every which way no coherent image of your face can form. Regular reflection would give a face-image, which paper does not. Paper emits no light of its own. If it absorbed all light you would see only black, not white print on a page.

**10. (D)** A calm surface acts like a flat mirror giving regular reflection and a clean image; ripples tilt the surface into many small slopes, so reflected rays scatter in many directions — diffuse reflection — and the single organised image dissolves into shimmer. The light is reflected, not bent down into the water (refraction) nor swallowed (absorption). No prism-like splitting into colours is involved; the image simply scatters.

**11. (C)** A plane mirror forms each object's image as far behind the mirror as the object is in front, so the photo (65 cm in front) has its image 65 cm behind, making object-to-image 65 + 65 = 130 cm. Using your own 90 cm gives your image distance, not the photo's. 65 cm is only the one-way distance, not the round trip to the image. Adding 90 and 25 mixes unrelated distances and has no meaning here.

**12. (B)** The image is always as far behind the mirror as the man is in front, so as he closes in at 1.2 m/s his image closes in at 1.2 m/s from the other side; the man-to-image gap therefore shrinks at 1.2 + 1.2 = 2.4 m/s. Counting only the man ignores that the image also moves. Halving the speed has no basis. The gap does change — it shrinks twice as fast as the man moves, it does not stay fixed.

**13. (D)** A plane mirror always makes an image the same size as the object no matter how near or far it stands; distance only changes how far behind the mirror the image sits, never its size, so the image stays 1.5 m tall. It does not shrink with distance — that confuses how big the image looks (its apparent size) with its true size. It cannot grow to match distance. The 6 m figure mixes the round-trip distance with height, which are unrelated.

**14. (A)** Each image lies as far behind the mirror as its owner stands in front: the boy's image is 2 m behind, the sister's is 6 m behind, both on the same side, so the gap between the two images is 6 − 2 = 4 m. Adding 2 and 6 would be right only if the images sat on opposite sides of the mirror, which they do not. Doubling the distances is the object-to-image trip, not the image-to-image gap. Twice the sister's distance ignores the boy entirely.

**15. (D)** The image distance behind equals the object distance in front; moving the object from 8 cm to 13 cm shifts the image from 8 cm behind to 13 cm behind, i.e. 5 cm farther back — the same 5 cm the object moved. The image moves the same amount as the object, not double. Moving the object away moves the image away too, not closer. The image certainly moves when the object moves; it tracks one-to-one.

**16. (C)** The lamp sits midway, so it is 4 ÷ 2 = 2 m in front of each mirror; a plane mirror puts the image as far behind as the object is in front, so the first image is 2 m behind. The full 4 m is the mirror-to-mirror gap, not the image distance. Halving again to 1 m has no basis. The image is not at the surface — it is 2 m behind, matching the object's 2 m in front.

**17. (D)** A plane mirror laterally inverts (swaps left and right) and reverses the reading order, so 'PEN' becomes N-E-P with each letter also flipped about a vertical line: P, E and N each bulge or open to one side, so none is symmetric and all appear backwards. It is not unchanged — every letter here is asymmetric. The mirror swaps left-right, not top-bottom, so it is not upside down. The reading order does reverse to N-E-P, but each letter is flipped too, so 'NEP' drawn the normal way round is wrong.

**18. (B)** Lateral inversion reverses the left-to-right order of the whole display and mirrors each symbol. Reversing 2 1 : 0 5 gives the visual sequence 5 0 : 1 2, and mirroring those seven-segment shapes turns 5 into 2 and 2 into 5 (1 and 0 stay), yielding 2 0 : 1 5, i.e. 20:15. It is not unchanged — 2 and 5 are not vertically symmetric. Plain '50:12' forgets that 5 and 2 themselves flip in the mirror. '12:50' wrongly keeps the colon's neighbours and mis-mirrors the digits.

**19. (D)** A plane mirror flips left and right about the vertical axis (the 12-6 line), so a hand at 4 (lower right) reflects to the lower left, which on a clock face is the 8 position. It does not stay at 4 — only hands lying on the 12 or 6 line are unchanged. The mirror does not flip top-to-bottom, so 10 is wrong. Reflecting across the horizontal axis would give 8 only by coincidence of reasoning; the correct mirror axis here is vertical, and 4 maps to 8, while a horizontal flip would send 4 to 2, which is the wrong axis.

**20. (C)** Each plane mirror laterally inverts once; the pupil copied a once-reversed version, and viewing that in a second mirror reverses it again, and two reversals cancel, restoring the original readable note. Two reversals do not add up to 'more reversed' — they undo each other. Mirrors swap left-right, not top-bottom, so it is not upside down. 'Still reversed' would be true after one mirror, but the second mirror flips it back.

**21. (D)** A plane mirror reverses left and right, so a letter is unchanged only if its left half mirrors its right half about a vertical centre line: H and X satisfy this, but E and K bulge only to the right and so flip into backwards shapes. E and K are NOT vertically symmetric, so that set is wrong. Not all capitals are symmetric — most are not. Vertically symmetric letters like H and X do survive unchanged, so 'none' is false.

**22. (B)** A rear-view mirror laterally inverts, so to be read as 'AID' it must be painted pre-reversed: the letters in reverse order AND each letter mirror-flipped, so that the mirror's flip undoes it to 'AID'. Writing it normally would appear reversed in the mirror. Upside-down writing is wrong because the mirror swaps left-right, not top-bottom. 'DIA' with normally-drawn letters reverses only the order, not each letter's handedness, so the mirror would not restore 'AID'.

**23. (D)** The student sees the writing reversed (one flip). A plane mirror applies a second left-right flip, and two flips cancel, so the mirror shows the message the right way round and readable. It is not doubly reversed — the two reversals undo each other, not stack. A mirror swaps left-right, not top-bottom, so it is not upside down. A mirror absolutely can fix already-reversed text, which is exactly what happens here.

**24. (A)** A plane mirror makes a virtual image: the reflected rays in front of the mirror only seem to diverge from a point behind it, but no light actually crosses that point, so a card placed there catches nothing. The image is not merely mislocated — it is virtual everywhere behind the mirror. Card thickness or paleness is irrelevant; even a perfect screen catches nothing. A plane-mirror image is erect, not inverted, and 'cancelling out' is not why screens fail — the absence of real light is.

**25. (A)** A plane mirror and a convex mirror always give virtual, screen-uncatchable images, while a concave mirror viewing a distant object gives a real, inverted, screen-catchable image — so the shared 'virtual' property excludes the concave-distant case. The plane mirror does not magnify (same size) and the convex diminishes, so 'magnify' is wrong. It is the concave, not the plane/convex, that makes the real image, so 'only plane and convex form real' is backwards. The plane mirror gives an erect, not inverted, image, so 'plane and convex invert' is false.

**26. (B)** The inward-curving bowl is a concave mirror; viewed from beyond its focus it forms a real, inverted, image (here enlarged) — matching the upside-down face. The outward-bulging back is a convex mirror, which always gives a virtual, erect, diminished image — matching the tiny upright face. The two are not swapped; bowl is concave (inverted), back is convex (erect). A ladle reflects, it is not a lens. The bowl is not flat, so it is not a plane mirror.

**27. (D)** A concave mirror's image depends on where the object sits: a distant object gives a diminished, inverted, real image, while a very close object gives an enlarged, erect, virtual image — so neither 'always magnifies' nor 'always upright' holds. Both are therefore false. Claiming 'always magnifies' fails for the distant small image; claiming 'always upright' fails for the inverted distant image; so neither half of the original statement survives.

**28. (A)** All three rely on the converging power of a concave mirror: it brings parallel sunrays to a hot focus (cooker), reverses that to a parallel beam when the source sits at the focus (headlight), and held close gives an enlarged erect virtual image (dentist). A convex mirror only diverges and diminishes, useless for focusing or beaming. A plane mirror neither focuses nor enlarges. The three are not a mix — they are all concave.

**29. (C)** A concave mirror gives a real, inverted image of a distant object; as the object approaches and finally comes very close to the mirror, the image switches to virtual, erect and enlarged — the shaving-mirror effect. The order is real-then-virtual, not the reverse. It does not stay real for all distances (close up it is virtual). It is not always diminished — that describes a convex mirror, not a concave one.

**30. (C)** A concave mirror with the bulb at its focus reverses the focusing action: rays leaving the focus reflect off the curved surface as a parallel (or near-parallel) bundle, giving a tight forward beam. A convex mirror diverges light into a wide weak spread, the opposite of what a torch needs. A plane mirror cannot collimate a diverging bulb into a beam. A lens is not a mirror, and converging to a single point ahead would not give a long straight beam down the path.

**31. (C)** Only a concave mirror does BOTH: enlarged erect virtual image when the object is very close (the tooth) and small inverted real image when the object is distant (the wall). A convex mirror always diminishes and never inverts, so it cannot give the small inverted far image. A plane mirror never magnifies nor inverts. A lens transmits light, not a mirror that reflects it; the device here reflects off a curved surface.

**32. (D)** A convex mirror bulges outward and diverges reflected rays, so it always forms a virtual, erect, diminished image and sweeps in a wide field of view — ideal for corner security mirrors where everyone looks small. A concave mirror converges and can invert/enlarge, narrowing rather than widening the useful view. A flat mirror's field of view is no wider than the same-size convex mirror's; in fact it is narrower. A concave mirror would magnify, not give the wide diminished view described.

**33. (A)** A convex mirror always gives an erect, diminished, virtual image and a wide field of view, so distant cars stay upright and a broad stretch is visible — exactly what is needed. A concave mirror forms a real inverted image of distant objects (cars upside down) and a narrower useful field, so it fails the 'erect' and 'wide' requirements. A plane mirror's view is narrower than the convex's for the same size. A concave lens is not a mirror and cannot serve as the reflecting side mirror.

**34. (B)** Thicker-in-the-middle glass is a convex (converging) lens: held close it magnifies an erect virtual image (a magnifying glass), and far from a bright distant scene it focuses a small inverted real image onto a wall — both behaviours are signatures of a convex lens. A concave lens is thinner in the middle and only ever gives a small erect virtual image, never an inverted real one. The disc is transparent glass that light passes through, so it is a lens, not a mirror; a convex mirror reflects and never throws a real image on a wall.

**35. (C)** A convex lens forms a real, inverted image of a distant object (the wall picture you can catch on a screen) and a virtual, erect, enlarged image of a very close object (the magnifying-glass effect). So the order is real-inverted then virtual-erect. It is not 'both real' — the close magnified print is virtual. It is not reversed (trees virtual, print real). And a convex lens definitely can make a screen image of distant objects, so 'both virtual' is wrong.

**36. (A)** A concave (diverging) lens is thinner in the middle and spreads parallel rays outward, so they leave more spread out and never converge to a concentrated hot spot — hence no scorching. It transmits light, it does not reflect it back like a mirror. It does not absorb all the light — you can see through it. A simple lens does not disperse sunlight into a rainbow; that is a prism's job, and even so dispersion would not stop heating by itself — the real reason is the spreading of the rays.

**37. (D)** A convex lens converges light, so it can both burn the Sun to a spot (P) and act as a magnifier giving an enlarged erect image close up (Q). R, which makes a distant scene look small and upright, behaves like a concave (diverging) lens, whose image is always diminished and erect — so R is not convex. 'All three' wrongly lumps the diverging R in. 'P and R' pairs a converging lens with a diverging one. 'Q and R' wrongly groups by 'upright' — both convex (close) and concave lenses can look upright, so uprightness alone does not identify converging lenses; the burning spot and magnification do.

**38. (C)** Thinner-in-the-middle glass is a concave (diverging) lens, and it always forms a diminished, erect, virtual image whatever the object's position — matching the small upright letters. A convex lens (thicker in the middle) magnifies close print, not shrinks it. A concave mirror reflects and would invert a distant object, and is not transparent glass you look THROUGH. A convex mirror also reflects rather than transmits; here you are looking through the glass, so it is a lens, not a mirror.

**39. (A)** Converging devices bring parallel rays to a focus: a concave mirror (curved inward) and a convex lens (thicker in the middle) both do this. The convex mirror and the concave lens both diverge, spreading rays apart, so they are the non-converging pair. 'Concave mirror and concave lens' mixes one converger with one diverger. 'Convex mirror and convex lens' likewise pairs a diverger with a converger. The clean rule: concave MIRROR and convex LENS converge; convex MIRROR and concave LENS diverge.

**40. (B)** Correct shapes: a convex lens is thicker in the middle, a concave lens is thinner in the middle, a concave mirror curves inward (like a bowl), and a convex mirror bulges outward (like a dome). The first distractor swaps every lens and mirror descriptor. The second nonsensically assigns lens thicknesses to mirrors and 'flat' to a concave lens. None of these four curved devices is a flat plate, so 'all flat' is false.

**41. (A)** A real image from any mirror in this chapter is always inverted, so a real, erect, enlarged image simply cannot be made by one mirror — the description is self-contradictory. A concave mirror held very close gives an enlarged erect image, but that one is VIRTUAL, not real. A convex mirror always diminishes, never enlarges. A plane mirror gives a same-size virtual image and cannot enlarge or make a real image. Hence none qualifies.

**42. (A)** All three parts of the correct row hold: a concave mirror gives a real inverted image of a distant object, a convex mirror always gives a virtual erect diminished image, and a convex lens gives a real inverted image of a distant object. The first wrong row makes a plane mirror's image 'real' (it is virtual) and a convex mirror 'magnified' (it diminishes). The second wrongly says a concave mirror 'always' virtual (it gives real for distant objects) and a concave lens 'real' (always virtual). The fourth claims a convex mirror gives a screen image (never) and that a concave mirror is never real (it is, for distant objects).

**43. (B)** The clean pairing is: concave MIRROR + convex LENS converge; convex MIRROR + concave LENS diverge. Notice the mirror and lens that converge have OPPOSITE names (concave/convex) — a classic trap. 'Concave mirror and concave lens converge' is wrong because a concave lens diverges. 'Convex mirror and convex lens converge' is wrong because a convex mirror diverges. Not all four converge — two of them spread light apart.

**44. (B)** Each pairing on the correct card holds: a plane mirror gives a virtual same-size image, a concave lens gives a virtual diminished image at any distance, and a convex lens gives a real inverted image of a distant object. The first wrong card falsely calls a plane mirror 'real inverted' and a convex mirror 'real magnified.' The second falsely says a concave mirror is 'always' virtual and a concave lens 'real.' The third falsely claims a convex mirror makes a real screen image and that a concave mirror is never real.

**45. (C)** A convex mirror always forms an erect, virtual, diminished image regardless of object position — erect like a plane mirror but smaller, which is exactly the description. A concave mirror used near gives an enlarged erect image (not smaller). A concave mirror used on a distant object gives an INVERTED real image, not erect. A concave lens is diminished and erect but it is a lens, not a mirror, and the question asks for a mirror.

**46. (D)** Only a real image can land on a screen, and a convex lens forms a real inverted image of a distant window — catchable on paper. A flat bathroom mirror gives a virtual image (behind the mirror, no real light there). A convex side mirror always gives a virtual image. A magnifying glass held close gives a virtual enlarged image. So only the convex-lens picture of the distant window is real and catchable.

**47. (B)** A calm lake mirroring a hill is regular reflection; sunlight split by a prism is dispersion; a lens projecting an inverted picture of a distant window on a wall is a real image formed by a convex lens. The order is reflection, dispersion, real-image-from-convex-lens. The lake is not dispersion; the prism is not reflection; and the wall picture is a real image from a convex lens, not a virtual one from a concave lens. The third option mislabels all three phenomena.

**48. (A)** A camera lens and a magnifying glass are both convex (converging) lenses, and a torch reflector is a concave (converging) mirror — all three bring parallel rays toward a focus, so none is odd; they all converge. The magnifying glass does not diverge — it converges. The camera lens focuses, it does not spread light out. The torch reflector converges to a beam, it does not scatter randomly.

**49. (D)** The prism's two faces are inclined to each other; each colour deviates by a slightly different amount, and because the faces are NOT parallel the colours that begin separating at the first face keep diverging through the second, so a spread band emerges. The cube's parallel faces let any separation recombine into white. The faces are clear glass, not tinted — colour is separated, not added. Ordinary glass disperses, so no rare rainbow glass is needed. Prism faces are flat, not curved like a lens, and curvature is not what produces the colours.

**50. (A)** In a prism red bends the least and violet bends the most, so from least- to most-bent the order is red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet (the forward VIBGYOR list read from the red end). The reverse list (violet first) would be most-bent to least-bent, not least to most. 'Red, yellow, blue' omits four colours. The scrambled green-red-violet sequence is not the spectral order at all.

**51. (D)** A prism disperses white light by separating the many colours already mixed in it; a single pure colour like indigo has nothing to separate, so it simply bends and emerges as indigo. A prism cannot manufacture the other six colours from one. It cannot turn one colour into white. And it does not block colours — the indigo passes through, merely deviated.

**52. (B)** The first prism spreads white light into its colours and the second, reversed, prism brings them back together into white — recombining the very colours that were separated. This proves the colours were components of the white light to begin with, not anything the prisms made or destroyed. The first prism only separates, it does not manufacture colours; the second only recombines, it does not destroy them. White cannot be a single pure colour if a prism splits it. The second prism adds nothing — it merges the existing colours back to white.

**53. (C)** Each raindrop disperses sunlight like a small prism, separating the seven colours that white sunlight already contains — no colour is created. Raindrops cannot manufacture colours; they only spread existing ones. They do not make 'only red and violet.' The colours come from dispersed white sunlight, not from the blue sky. So the rainbow reveals, rather than creates, the colours in sunlight.

**54. (B)** White appears only when the colours reach the eye fast enough that the eye cannot separate them and blends them together; a slowly turning disc lets each colour register on its own, so streaks remain visible. The paints do not fade with slow spinning — they are simply seen separately. Spinning does not convert paint into white paint; the blending happens in the eye, not on the disc. The slow disc reflects the same painted colours as the fast one — it does not gain extra colours.

**55. (C)** Projecting an inverted picture of a distant window onto a wall is a real image from a convex lens; even scattering off chalk-white card is diffuse reflection; a clock read backwards in a mirror is lateral inversion. The first wrong option mislabels all three. The second calls the lens image virtual-from-concave (it is real-from-convex), the card 'absorption' (it scatters, not absorbs), and the clock 'dispersion'. The third shuffles the labels so none lines up with its demonstration.

**56. (B)** A convex mirror bulges outward and diverges reflected rays, so for any object it gives a virtual, erect, diminished image with a wide field of view — exactly why vehicles use it for the rear view. It does not converge rays; converging belongs to the concave mirror used in torch reflectors. It does not enlarge; a dentist uses a CONCAVE mirror for close magnification. It never forms a real screen image — its images are always virtual.

**57. (C)** A close enlarged upright face needs a concave mirror (its near-object virtual magnified image); a wide rear view needs a convex mirror (wide field, diminished erect); focusing sunlight to a hot point needs a concave mirror (converging). So the order is concave, convex, concave. A convex mirror cannot enlarge the face, and a concave mirror would not give the wide erect rear view. A plane mirror neither magnifies the face nor focuses sunlight, and a convex mirror diverges sunlight rather than focusing it.

**58. (A)** Both behaviours — magnifying close print and projecting a far lamp inverted on a wall — are signatures of a convex lens, and because the light passes through the glass to form the images on the far side, it is a lens, not a reflecting mirror. A concave lens cannot project a real inverted image; it only gives small erect virtual ones. A concave mirror would indeed give both image types, but it reflects rather than transmits, so it is excluded by 'it is a lens, not a mirror.' A convex mirror neither magnifies nor projects a real image, and a mirror does not transmit light.

**59. (A)** A convex lens does NOT always form a virtual image: for a distant object it forms a real inverted image (catchable on a screen), so 'always virtual' is the false claim. A plane mirror's image truly is virtual and same-size. A convex mirror's image truly is always virtual and diminished. A concave mirror truly forms a real inverted image of a distant object. So the convex-lens 'always virtual' statement is the lone falsehood.

**60. (B)** A broad source like a tube light is made of many points spread along its length; at the halo a point on the wall has the marble blocking only some of those points while the rest of the tube is still in view, so it receives partial light and looks grey, with full darkness only where the whole tube is hidden. A tiny torch bulb is nearly a point source, so it hides all-or-nothing and gives a crisp edge. The marble does not bend light around itself, the wall does not colour the light, and light does not slow and smear past a solid object — the halo is simply partial illumination from an extended source.
