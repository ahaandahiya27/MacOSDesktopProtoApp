Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

Wool and silk are grouped together because both are:

  • Aplant fibres
  • Banimal fibres✓
  • Csynthetic fibres
  • Dmineral fibres

Working

✓

Wool (from sheep, goat, yak, camel) and silk (from the silkworm) both come from animals, so they are animal fibres.

Q2

Cotton, jute and flax are grouped together because all three are:

  • Aanimal fibres
  • Bsynthetic fibres
  • Cplant fibres✓
  • Dmineral fibres

Working

✓

Cotton, jute and flax all come from plants, so they are plant fibres made of cellulose.

Q3

The protein that makes up wool fibre is:

  • Afibroin
  • Bcellulose
  • Ckeratin✓
  • Dcasein

Working

✓

Wool is made of the protein keratin — the same tough protein found in hair, nails and horn.

Q4

The protein that makes up silk fibre is:

  • Akeratin
  • Bfibroin✓
  • Ccellulose
  • Dgluten

Working

✓

Silk is made of the protein fibroin, secreted by the silkworm.

Q5

Plant fibres such as cotton are chemically made of:

  • Aprotein
  • Bcellulose✓
  • Ckeratin
  • Dnylon

Working

✓

Plant fibres such as cotton are made of cellulose, not protein.

Q6

Which of the following animals does **not** yield wool?

  • AYak
  • BCamel
  • CGoat
  • DWoolly dog✓

Working

✓

Sheep, yak, goat and camel all yield wool; a "woolly dog" does not — it is not a wool-yielding animal.

Q7

Pashmina shawls are made from the fine inner wool of the:

  • AMerino sheep
  • BChangthangi goat✓
  • CAngora rabbit
  • DBactrian camel

Working

✓

Pashmina is the fine inner wool of the Changthangi goat of Ladakh.

Q8

The soft, fine hair found *underneath* the coarse outer hair of some sheep is:

  • Athe burr
  • Bthe fleece grease
  • Cthe fine inner wool used for weaving✓
  • Dkeratin scale

Working

✓

Beneath the coarse outer (beard) hair lies the soft, fine inner wool that is actually woven into fabric.

Q9

The complete coat of wool removed from a sheep in one piece is called the:

  • Afleece✓
  • Bsliver
  • Croving
  • Dyarn

Working

✓

The whole coat of wool removed in one piece during shearing is the fleece.

Q10

The correct order of the first two steps of wool processing is:

  • Ascouring, then shearing
  • Bshearing, then scouring✓
  • Csorting, then shearing
  • Ddyeing, then shearing

Working

✓

Wool processing begins with shearing (cutting the fleece), then scouring (washing it).

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

Shearing is best described as:

  • Apainlessly cutting off the fleece using clippers✓
  • Bwashing the wool to remove grease
  • Cdyeing the wool in different colours
  • Dtwisting fibres into yarn

Working

✓

Shearing is the painless cutting off of the fleece using clippers (the outer skin layer is dead).

Q12

Shearing does not hurt the sheep because:

  • Asheep have no nerves
  • Bthe uppermost layer of skin is dead, like our hair and nails✓
  • Cthe wool is removed surgically
  • Dthe sheep is asleep

Working

✓

Like our own hair and nails, the uppermost skin layer that carries the wool is dead, so cutting it does not hurt the sheep.

Q13

Scouring is carried out mainly to:

  • Acolour the wool
  • Bremove grease, dust and dirt from the sheared wool✓
  • Csort the wool by length
  • Dtwist the fibres into thread

Working

✓

Scouring washes the sheared wool in tanks to remove grease, dust and dirt.

Q14

In the sequence "Shearing → ____ → Sorting → ...", the missing step is:

  • Areeling
  • Bscouring✓
  • Cdyeing
  • Dspinning

Working

✓

The sequence is Shearing → Scouring → Sorting, so the missing step is scouring.

Q15

After scouring and sorting, the small fluffy fibres picked out of the hairy skin are called:

  • Aburrs✓
  • Bfleece
  • Ccocoons
  • Dslivers

Working

✓

The small fluffy fibres picked out of the hairy skin are the burrs that must be removed.

Q16

Sorting is the process of:

  • Awashing the fleece in tanks
  • Bseparating the hairy skin into wool of different textures✓
  • Ccolouring wool in many shades
  • Dunwinding silk from a cocoon

Working

✓

Sorting separates the hairy skin (with its wool) into wool of different textures.

Q17

Natural wool is white, black or brown; the step that gives it many colours is:

  • Ascouring
  • Bshearing
  • Cdyeing✓
  • Dreeling

Working

✓

Natural wool comes in a few shades; dyeing gives it the wide range of colours we see.

Q18

The last stage that turns processed wool fibres into thread is:

  • Ashearing
  • Bscouring
  • Cspinning into yarn✓
  • Dreeling

Working

✓

The final step turns the processed fibres into thread by spinning into yarn.

Q19

"Sorter's disease" is an occupational hazard of wool workers caused by:

  • Aa fungus on mulberry leaves
  • Bthe bacterium *Bacillus anthracis* (anthrax) in raw wool✓
  • Cinhaling synthetic fibre dust
  • Dexcess dyeing chemicals

Working

✓

Sorter's disease is anthrax, caused by *Bacillus anthracis* spores present in raw wool.

Q20

Sorter's disease mainly affects the worker's:

  • Askin only
  • Blungs (it is a respiratory infection)✓
  • Ceyes
  • Dbones

Working

✓

Anthrax inhaled from raw wool is a respiratory infection, so it mainly attacks the lungs.

  • Bfloriculture and apiculture

Q28

A single cocoon yields one continuous silk thread that can be up to about a kilometre long.

Q31

Before reeling, cocoons are usually placed in boiling water or steam in order to:

  • Afeed the pupa
  • Bloosen the gum and kill the pupa so the thread can be unwound✓
  • Cdye the silk
  • Dhatch the eggs faster

Working

✓

Cocoons are boiled or steamed to loosen the gum (sericin) and kill the pupa so the long thread can be unwound unbroken.

Q39

A reason a cotton shirt feels cooler in summer than a polyester one is that cotton is more:

  • Awaterproof
  • Bbreathable and absorbs sweat✓
  • Celastic
  • Dshiny

Q40

Why does a woollen sweater keep you warm in winter? Because wool fibres:

  • Agenerate heat chemically
  • Btrap a layer of air that is a poor conductor of heat✓
  • Care good conductors of heat
  • Dreflect all body heat back instantly

Working

✓

Wool fibres trap a layer of still air, and trapped air is a poor conductor of heat, keeping body warmth in.
