## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** In a cell symbol the longer thin line is the positive (+) terminal and the shorter thick line is the negative (−) terminal. So the pair stands for the positive terminal and the negative terminal.

**2. (C)** Cells form a battery only when joined in a row with the positive terminal of one touching the negative terminal of the next. Joining like-to-like (+ to +) makes them oppose; an air gap breaks the loop.

**3. (C)** Putting two cells positive-against-positive makes their pushes oppose and cancel. With equal cells the net push is zero, so no current flows and the bulb does not glow.

**4. (A)** A battery of two cells joined correctly (+ to −) gives a larger total push than one cell, so more current flows and the bulb glows more brightly. (The trap is to think "more cells = dimmer" — that is the misconception in Q53.)

**5. (C)** A small circle with a cross inside it is the standard symbol for a bulb. (A cell is two parallel lines; a fuse is a line with a small box/loop.)

**6. (B)** A plain straight line is simply a connecting wire. A cell, a bulb and a switch all have extra marks added to the line.

**7. (B)** A small gap with a tilted arm lifted away from one contact is the symbol for an **open** switch: the loop is broken there, so current does not flow.

**8. (C)** The switch in the loop is open, so the circuit is broken and no current can flow. With an open switch the bulb does not glow at all (not "dim", not "fading").

**9. (C)** A broken wire, a dead cell and a fused filament would all stop the bulb glowing — so each is a possible reason. A **complete closed loop** would let the bulb glow, so it is NOT a reason for failure. The "not a possible reason" answer is the closed-loop option.

**10. (B)** A bulb glows only when current can flow, and current flows only round a complete closed loop. An open circuit or an open switch stops it; the number of cells is not the essential condition.

**11. (C)** Four 1.5 V cells joined correctly in a row add up: 4 × 1.5 = 6 V. (Dividing instead of adding gives the trap 0.375 V; using just one cell gives 1.5 V.)

**12. (A)** Same cell and same bulb, but Q gives a dimmer bulb — so less current flows through Q. More opposition to current means higher resistance, so wire Q has the higher resistance. (A perfect insulator or a broken wire would give no glow at all, not a dim one.)

**13. (C)** Of the choices, only the copper wire is a good conductor, so only it completes the circuit and lets the bulb glow. Wood, rubber and plastic are insulators.

**14. (D)** Rubber is an insulator: it does not let current pass, so the loop is not completed electrically and the bulb stays dark.

**15. (C)** Whenever current flows through a wire, the wire warms up — this is the heating effect of current. (Only special high-resistance wires get hot enough to glow; an ordinary wire just warms, but it does heat.)

**16. (C)** Same current flows in both. The wire with higher resistance turns more electrical energy into heat, so the thin high-resistance wire X gets hotter than the thick low-resistance wire Y.

**17. (A)** Nichrome is chosen for heater coils because it has a very high resistance (so it produces a lot of heat) and a high melting point (so it can glow red-hot without melting). Copper, being a far better conductor, would barely heat.

**18. (D)** More current through the same wire produces more heat each second (the heating effect grows with the current). It does not stay the same or fall.

**19. (B)** Tungsten is used for bulb filaments because of its extremely high melting point: it can be heated white-hot to give light without melting away.

**20. (A)** Electric iron, toaster and immersion water heater all work by the heating effect (a high-resistance element gets hot). The bell, crane magnet and loudspeaker use the magnetic effect; fan, mixer and pump use motors (magnetic effect too).

**21. (A)** A toaster heats food using the heating effect of current; a ceiling fan uses a motor, which runs on the magnetic effect of current. In order: heating, then magnetic.

**22. (D)** A fuse is a short piece of wire with a **low melting point**. When the current is too high it melts and breaks the circuit. It does not store charge or increase current, and it is deliberately NOT a high-melting-point conductor.

**23. (C)** The fuse protects by **melting and breaking** the circuit when the current rises above a safe value, cutting off the current before wires overheat. It does not safely carry any current, nor merely glow.

**24. (A)** The fuse rating should match the safe limit, so fit a 5 A fuse: it carries normal current but melts if the current goes above 5 A. A 50 A fuse never protects; a 0.5 A fuse would melt during normal use; no fuse means no protection.

**25. (B)** Too many high-power appliances on one socket draw a very large total current — this is overloading. (A short circuit is specifically bare live and neutral wires touching, which is a different fault.)

**26. (B)** The right action is to find and remove the fault, then fit a fresh fuse of the **correct** rating. Using a thicker wire, a much higher rating, or taping the ends all defeat the fuse's protective purpose.

**27. (D)** A miniature circuit breaker (MCB) does the fuse's job but can simply be switched back on after it trips, instead of being replaced. An electromagnet, a cell and a filament do not protect a circuit this way.

**28. (B)** A wire that never melts removes the protection: if a fault sends a huge current, nothing breaks the circuit, so the wiring can overheat and start a fire. That is the danger of "fuse-replaced-by-thick-wire".

**29. (C)** Oersted saw the compass needle move only when an electric **current was switched on** in the wire. A dead wire, a coat of paint, or a shorter length do not deflect the needle.

**30. (D)** Oersted's experiment showed that an electric current in a wire produces a **magnetic field** around the wire — the magnetic effect of current. It is not "only heat", "sound", or "light only".

**31. (D)** The deflection lasts only while current flows. Switch the current off and the magnetic effect vanishes, so the needle swings back to its normal north–south direction. It does not stay deflected, spin or melt.

**32. (A)** Winding the wire into a coil around a soft-iron core concentrates the magnetic effect, giving a much stronger magnet than a single straight wire carrying the same current.

**33. (B)** Each of more turns, more current, and keeping an iron core inside the coil makes an electromagnet **stronger**. None of them weakens it or destroys its magnetism.

**34. (D)** The key advantage is control: an electromagnet can be switched on to lift a load and switched off to drop it — a permanent magnet cannot be turned off. (It is not "always magnetic", not plastic, and it does need current.)

**35. (D)** An electromagnet attracts only magnetic materials — iron and steel — and not aluminium, copper, plastic, etc. So at a scrap yard it picks up the iron and steel but leaves the rest.

**36. (C)** While current flows, the soft-iron core strongly increases the magnetic effect; when the current stops, soft iron loses its magnetism almost at once. That is exactly why soft iron (not steel) is used and why the magnet can be switched off.

**37. (B)** An electromagnet's magnetism depends on the current. The moment the current is switched off, it loses its magnetism. Painting the core, adding turns, or using copper wire do not switch it off.

**38. (B)** In an electric bell the coil wound on the iron core becomes the **electromagnet**; it is the part that becomes a magnet and pulls the iron armature towards it when current flows. The gong, spring and hammer do not become magnets.

**39. (A)** When the armature is pulled across and the contact at the screw breaks, the circuit opens: the current stops, the electromagnet loses its magnetism, and the spring pulls the armature back. (It does not become permanent, and the current does not grow.)

**40. (A)** As the armature returns, the contact is remade, current flows again, the electromagnet pulls once more — the contact keeps making and breaking, so the strike repeats again and again, giving a continuous buzzing.

**41. (B)** One full cycle is: current flows → electromagnet pulls the armature → hammer hits the gong → the contact breaks → current stops → the spring returns the armature → the contact remakes (and it all repeats). The other sequences put the steps out of order.

**42. (A)** With the same wire and same current, the coil **with** the soft-iron core is the stronger magnet, because the iron core greatly boosts the magnetic effect.

**43. (A)** Without changing the battery, the most sensible single change to strengthen the electromagnet is to wind many more turns of wire on the same iron core. Removing the core, using fewer turns, or a wooden core would all weaken it.

**44. (C)** A current flowing through a wire produces **both** a heating effect (the wire warms) and a magnetic effect (a magnetic field forms) at the same time — they are two effects of the one current, not one or the other, and they occur while the current flows.

**45. (B)** An electric bell works through an electromagnet, so it uses the **magnetic** effect. A toaster, immersion heater and filament bulb all use the heating effect.

**46. (A)** Adding a second identical cell in the same direction (+ to −) makes a two-cell battery whose pushes add, driving a larger current, so the bulb glows brighter. (Cells "sharing the work" or "cancelling out" are misconceptions.)

**47. (D)** Joined positive-to-positive and negative-to-negative, the two cells push against each other and cancel out, so effectively no current flows and the bulb does not glow.

**48. (D)** Opening the switch breaks the loop, so no current can flow and the bulb goes out. (The cell is not used up, and the resistance does not drop to zero; the switch does not become a fuse.)

**49. (A)** With the same current, the wire of higher resistance produces far more heat. Nichrome has a much higher resistance than copper, so the nichrome coil glows red-hot while the copper wire stays cool. (Copper does carry the current; the difference is resistance, not magnetism or melting point.)

**50. (A)** The correct pairing is switch — to make or break the circuit. (A bulb gives light, not over-current protection; a fuse breaks the circuit, it does not store charge; a cell supplies energy, it does not measure current.)

**51. (B)** An immersion rod heats water using the heating effect; an electric bell rings using an electromagnet, which is the magnetic effect. In order: heating, then magnetic.

**52. (C)** In the external wires, current is taken to flow out of the positive terminal — the longer thin line — round the circuit back to the shorter (negative) line. It does not start from the middle or from the bulb.

**53. (B)** The claim is wrong. Adding a cell correctly (+ to −) increases the total push, so more current flows and the bulb gets **brighter**, not dimmer. (Connecting it the wrong way is what reduces the push.)

**54. (B)** For the same large current, the thin fuse wire has a higher resistance and a low melting point, so it heats up the fastest and melts first — before the thicker household wiring can overheat. That is why the fuse is the deliberate weak link.

**55. (D)** The claim is wrong. A fuse does not conduct the extra current away; it protects precisely by **melting and breaking** the circuit when the current is too high. (It also does not increase the current.)

**56. (A)** A current produces a magnetic field, which deflects a nearby compass needle. So if a compass needle deflects when the supply is switched on, current is flowing — and you never had to cut the wire. Colour, weight and length do not change with current.

**57. (A)** More current (from two cells) and more turns both strengthen the electromagnet, so it can lift **more** paper clips than before. The count increases, not decreases.

**58. (B)** The springy metal strip pulls the armature back once the magnet releases it, so the contact is remade and the make-break cycle repeats. It does not keep the magnet on, melt, or measure voltage.

**59. (D)** A tiny break in a wire leaves a gap in the loop, making it an **open** circuit: no current flows, so the bulb stays dark even with a closed switch and a fresh cell. (A break is not a closed circuit and not a short circuit.)

**60. (D)** Statements about switching on/off, attracting iron and steel, and lifting more when made stronger are all true. The **incorrect** statement is that an electromagnet attracts plastic, aluminium and wood as strongly as iron — it attracts only magnetic materials like iron and steel.
