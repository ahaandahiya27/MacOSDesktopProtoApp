# Olympiad Test Paper — Science (Class 7)
## Chapter 4: Heat

| | |
|---|---|
| **Subject** | Science (NCERT Class 7) |
| **Chapter** | 4 — Heat |
| **Total Questions** | 60 (Multiple Choice, single correct) |
| **Maximum Marks** | 240 |
| **Suggested Time** | 90 minutes |

**Marking scheme:** **+4** for each correct answer · **−1** for each wrong answer · **0** if unattempted.

**Instructions:** Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Temperatures are in degrees Celsius (°C) unless a question states otherwise. Thermometer scales are described in words — picture the marks as you read. No calculator is needed — all numbers are designed to work by hand. Most questions need two to four reasoning steps; watch for traps. Do not write on this paper — mark answers on your answer sheet.

---

1. A small cup of boiling water at 100 °C and a large bucket of warm water at 40 °C are placed side by side. Which statement is correct?
   (A) Both hold exactly the same amount of heat
   (B) The cup has the higher temperature, but the bucket may hold more total heat because it has far more water
   (C) The bucket has the higher temperature but the cup holds more heat
   (D) The cup holds more heat because hotter always means more heat

2. Heat is best described as:
   (A) a measure of how hot or cold a body is
   (B) the average vibration of one atom
   (C) the number marked on a thermometer
   (D) a form of energy that flows from a hotter body to a colder one

3. Two identical mugs are filled — one with 100 mL of water at 90 °C, the other with 200 mL of water at 90 °C. Which has the higher **temperature**?
   (A) the 100 mL mug
   (B) the 200 mL mug
   (C) both are at the same temperature
   (D) the one that cools first

4. When a hot iron rod is dipped into a beaker of cold water and left alone, heat flows:
   (A) from the water to the rod until the rod is hotter
   (B) in both directions equally, so nothing changes
   (C) from the rod to the water until both reach the same temperature
   (D) only if the water is stirred

5. A spoon left in a cup of hot tea slowly becomes warm. This shows that:
   (A) the spoon is making its own heat
   (B) heat flows from the hotter tea to the cooler spoon
   (C) temperature and heat are the same thing
   (D) the tea is gaining heat from the spoon

6. Most thermometers work because the liquid inside them:
   (A) expands (takes up more space) when heated and contracts when cooled
   (B) changes colour when heated
   (C) turns into a gas when heated
   (D) gets heavier when heated

7. When the bulb of a thermometer is warmed, the liquid thread rises up the tube. The thread rises because the liquid:
   (A) expands and needs more room, so it climbs the narrow tube
   (B) is pushed up by air from below
   (C) becomes lighter than the glass
   (D) is boiling inside the bulb

8. The tube of a thermometer is made very **narrow** mainly so that:
   (A) less liquid is needed to fill it
   (B) the liquid cannot escape
   (C) even a small expansion of liquid moves the thread a large, easy-to-read distance
   (D) the glass stays cooler

9. A laboratory thermometer is dipped in melting ice and the liquid settles at the mark halfway between the 0 and 10 lines. The temperature is:
   (A) 5 °C
   (B) 0 °C
   (C) 10 °C
   (D) 15 °C

10. Which range is typical for a **clinical** thermometer used to measure human body temperature?
    (A) 35 °C to 42 °C
    (B) −10 °C to 110 °C
    (C) 0 °C to 100 °C
    (D) 0 °C to 50 °C

11. A clinical thermometer has a sharp narrow bend (a "kink") in its tube. The kink is there to:
    (A) make the thermometer cheaper
    (B) let the liquid expand faster
    (C) stop the liquid thread from slipping back, so the reading holds until you read it
    (D) measure two temperatures at once

12. Why does a **laboratory** thermometer usually have **no kink**?
    (A) Laboratory liquids never expand
    (B) A kink would crack the glass in a lab
    (C) It is read while still in contact with the substance, so the thread must move freely up and down
    (D) Laboratory thermometers are always digital

13. A laboratory thermometer is marked from −10 °C to 110 °C. It would be the wrong tool to measure:
    (A) the temperature of warm milk at 45 °C
    (B) the temperature of ice-cold water at 4 °C
    (C) the temperature of a beaker of water being heated to 80 °C
    (D) the exact body temperature of a feverish patient to one-tenth of a degree

14. Before using a clinical thermometer, you give it a few sharp jerks/shakes. The reason is to:
    (A) warm it up before use
    (B) clean the bulb
    (C) bring the thread back down below normal body temperature so the next reading is correct
    (D) test whether the glass is strong

15. A clinical thermometer reads in the range 35 to 42 with small marks every 0.1 between the numbers. The liquid stops on the 2nd small mark past 37. The reading is:
    (A) 37.0 °C
    (B) 39.0 °C
    (C) 37.02 °C
    (D) 37.2 °C

16. The normal temperature of a healthy human body is about:
    (A) 42 °C
    (B) 37 °C
    (C) 30 °C
    (D) 25 °C

17. On a laboratory thermometer scale, the big numbered marks are 10 apart and there are 10 equal small divisions between them. Each small division therefore stands for:
    (A) 1 °C
    (B) 0.1 °C
    (C) 10 °C
    (D) 5 °C

18. A laboratory thermometer reads three small divisions above the 60 mark, where each small division is 1 °C. The temperature is:
    (A) 60 °C
    (B) 90 °C
    (C) 30 °C
    (D) 63 °C

19. A patient's clinical thermometer shows the thread halfway between the 38 and 39 marks. The reading is:
    (A) 38.0 °C
    (B) 38.5 °C
    (C) 39.0 °C
    (D) 37.5 °C

20. Normal human body temperature of about 37 °C is the same as roughly 98.6 on the Fahrenheit scale. A child with a reading of 37 °C is therefore:
    (A) at about the normal body temperature
    (B) running a high fever
    (C) dangerously cold
    (D) below the range any thermometer can show

21. Heat travelling through a solid metal rod, from the hot end to the cold end, without the rod itself moving, is an example of:
    (A) convection
    (B) radiation
    (C) conduction
    (D) reflection

22. One end of a metal spoon is held in a flame. After a while the other end becomes hot even though it was never in the flame. This is because:
    (A) the heat radiated across the air gap
    (B) hot metal floated up to the handle
    (C) the spoon created its own heat
    (D) heat was conducted along the metal from the hot end to the cold end

23. Conduction is the **main** way heat travels through:
    (A) liquids only
    (B) solids
    (C) gases only
    (D) empty space

24. Of the four objects below, heat will travel **fastest** by conduction through the:
    (A) wooden ruler
    (B) plastic comb
    (C) rubber band
    (D) iron nail

25. A metal handle and a wooden handle are left in the same warm room for hours. When you touch them, the metal feels **colder** than the wood even though both are at room temperature. This is because:
    (A) metal is actually at a lower temperature than wood
    (B) wood is warmer than the room
    (C) your hand makes mistakes about metal only
    (D) metal conducts heat away from your hand quickly, so your skin cools faster and feels colder

26. A cook stirs hot soup with a metal spoon and leaves it in the pot; soon the spoon's handle is too hot to hold. Replacing it with a wooden spoon helps because wood:
    (A) is a good conductor, spreading heat away from the soup
    (B) is a poor conductor (insulator), so little heat reaches the handle
    (C) absorbs the soup's heat and stores it safely
    (D) reflects the heat back into the soup

27. Heat travelling through water or air by the actual **movement of the heated fluid** is called:
    (A) conduction
    (B) convection
    (C) radiation
    (D) insulation

28. When water in a pan is heated from below, the warm water at the bottom:
    (A) stays at the bottom because it is heavier
    (B) instantly turns to steam
    (C) conducts heat to the top without moving
    (D) becomes lighter, rises, and cooler water sinks to take its place, setting up a circulating current

29. Why is a room heater most effective when placed near the **floor** for heating, while an air-conditioner is fixed **high** on the wall?
    (A) Warm air rises and cool air sinks, so heating from low and cooling from high lets convection currents spread air through the whole room
    (B) It is only about which is cheaper to install
    (C) Electric wires must be near the floor
    (D) The position makes no difference to how the air moves

30. In a beaker of water heated at the centre of the bottom, a few coloured crystals dropped in will trace a path that:
    (A) stays still at the bottom
    (B) shoots straight up and disappears
    (C) rises in the middle and sinks along the sides, showing a convection current
    (D) sinks straight down only

31. Heat from the Sun reaches the Earth across empty space. It travels by:
    (A) conduction, through the air
    (B) convection, through rising hot air
    (C) all three equally
    (D) radiation, which needs no material medium

32. Which mode of heat transfer can take place even through a **vacuum** (no air, no liquid, no solid)?
    (A) conduction
    (B) radiation
    (C) convection
    (D) none — heat always needs a medium

33. Standing near a glowing campfire, you feel warmth on your face even though the hot air rises straight up and does not blow toward you. The warmth you feel reaches you mainly by:
    (A) conduction through the ground
    (B) convection blowing sideways
    (C) the fire pushing cold air away
    (D) radiation travelling straight from the fire to your skin

34. All warm objects give off heat as radiation. Compared with a cool object, a **hotter** object:
    (A) gives off no radiation
    (B) radiates less heat each second
    (C) radiates more heat each second
    (D) radiates the same as a cold object

35. A teacher claims: "Radiation can pass through empty space, but conduction and convection both need some material (matter) to travel through." This statement is:
    (A) false — all three need matter
    (B) false — only convection needs matter
    (C) false — radiation also needs matter
    (D) true

36. A material that lets heat pass through it easily is called a heat **conductor**. Which list contains only good conductors?
    (A) wood, plastic, copper
    (B) air, wool, rubber
    (C) glass, cork, iron
    (D) copper, aluminium, iron

37. A material that does **not** let heat pass through easily is called an **insulator**. Which is the best insulator?
    (A) trapped air (as in wool or a sweater)
    (B) iron
    (C) aluminium
    (D) silver

38. Cooking pots are often made with a metal body but a **wooden or plastic handle**. The reason is that the metal body should ____ heat while the handle should ____ heat.
    (A) block; conduct
    (B) reflect; absorb
    (C) conduct; not conduct (insulate)
    (D) store; release

39. Which everyday object depends on a material being a **poor** conductor of heat?
    (A) a copper-bottomed cooking pan
    (B) the metal blade of a knife
    (C) the plastic or wooden handle of a frying pan
    (D) an aluminium cooking foil

40. A jacket "keeps you warm" not by making heat but by:
    (A) being a poor conductor that slows the escape of your body's heat
    (B) producing heat from the cloth
    (C) reflecting sunlight onto you
    (D) conducting your heat away quickly

41. Two cups of equally hot tea are left out: cup P is bare metal, cup Q is wrapped in a thick woollen cloth. After ten minutes:
    (A) cup Q is hotter, because wool is a poor conductor and slows heat loss
    (B) cup P is hotter
    (C) both are equally hot
    (D) cup Q is hotter only if the tea was stirred

42. Why do **woollen** clothes keep us warmer than an equal thickness of smooth cotton in winter?
    (A) Wool produces heat from the body's sweat
    (B) Wool traps a lot of air, and trapped air is a poor conductor that slows heat loss
    (C) Wool conducts the body's heat to the surface faster
    (D) Wool is heavier, and heavy things are always warmer

43. Wearing **two thin sweaters** can feel warmer than **one thick one** of the same total thickness because the two-layer arrangement:
    (A) traps an extra layer of air between the sweaters, and trapped air is a poor conductor
    (B) is heavier
    (C) produces more heat
    (D) reflects more light

44. A bird fluffs up its feathers on a cold day. This keeps it warmer because fluffing:
    (A) traps more air among the feathers, and trapped air slows heat loss
    (B) makes its body produce more heat
    (C) makes the feathers conduct heat better
    (D) reflects sunlight onto the bird

45. In **summer**, light-coloured (white) clothes are preferred over dark clothes because light colours:
    (A) absorb more heat from sunlight
    (B) make the body produce less heat
    (C) conduct heat to the skin faster
    (D) reflect away most of the sunlight, so the body absorbs less heat and stays cooler

46. In **winter**, dark-coloured clothes can feel warmer in the daytime Sun because dark colours:
    (A) reflect sunlight away
    (B) trap more body heat than light colours
    (C) absorb more of the Sun's heat than light colours do
    (D) produce heat from the Sun's light

47. Two identical metal cans, one painted shiny white and one painted dull black, are left in bright sunshine. After an hour:
    (A) the white can is hotter
    (B) the black can is hotter, because dark surfaces absorb more radiation
    (C) both are at exactly the same temperature
    (D) the white can is hotter only if it is bigger

48. A coastal town has a **sea breeze** during the day. This means that during the day the wind blows:
    (A) from the sea toward the land
    (B) from the land out to the sea
    (C) straight upward only
    (D) there is no wind in the daytime

49. During the **day**, the sea breeze blows from sea to land because:
    (A) the sea heats up faster than the land
    (B) the land heats up faster than the sea, so air rises over the warm land and cooler air flows in from over the sea
    (C) the land stays cooler than the sea all day
    (D) the Sun pulls air toward the water

50. At **night** along the coast, a **land breeze** blows from land to sea. This happens because at night:
    (A) the sea cools faster than the land
    (B) the land cools faster than the sea, so air rises over the warmer sea and cooler air flows out from the land
    (C) the land stays hotter than the sea all night
    (D) there is no temperature difference at night

51. A fisherman notices the wind blowing **out to sea** in the early morning before sunrise. This is a:
    (A) sea breeze, caused by the warm land
    (B) sign of a coming storm only
    (C) breeze that needs the Sun directly overhead
    (D) land breeze, because at night the land has cooled below the sea's temperature

52. Sea breezes and land breezes are both examples of which mode of heat transfer at a large (coastal) scale?
    (A) conduction
    (B) convection
    (C) radiation
    (D) reflection

53. The sea breeze and the land breeze blow in **opposite** directions because:
    (A) the wind always reverses every hour
    (B) by day the land is warmer than the sea, while by night the land is cooler than the sea
    (C) the sea only heats up at night
    (D) the Moon reverses the wind

54. A clinical thermometer is **not** suitable for measuring the temperature of boiling water at 100 °C because:
    (A) its scale only goes up to about 42 °C, far below 100 °C
    (B) its liquid would freeze
    (C) it has no kink
    (D) it is too long

55. To follow the temperature of water as it is heated steadily from cold up to nearly boiling, the right instrument is a:
    (A) clinical thermometer, because of its kink
    (B) clinical thermometer, because it reads in °C
    (C) any thermometer, since all read up to 110 °C

56. On a cold morning, the metal frame of a bicycle feels colder to the touch than its rubber grips, although both have been outside all night at the same temperature. The best explanation is:
    (A) the metal is genuinely many degrees colder than the rubber
    (B) the rubber grips were warmed by the Sun overnight
    (C) metal conducts heat away from your hand faster than rubber, so the metal feels colder
    (D) rubber makes its own heat

57. Tea stays hot longer in a thermos flask partly because the flask has a near-**vacuum** between its two walls. The vacuum chiefly stops heat loss by:
    (A) radiation
    (B) reflection of light
    (C) conduction and convection, since both need matter to travel through
    (D) absorption of sunlight

58. A laboratory thermometer reads exactly halfway between the −10 and the 0 marks. The temperature is:
    (A) 5 °C
    (B) −5 °C
    (C) −10 °C
    (D) 0 °C

    (A) absorbs the most sunlight, warming the house
    (B) conducts heat into the walls quickly
    (C) reflects most of the sunlight, so the house stays cooler inside
    (D) produces a cooling breeze
