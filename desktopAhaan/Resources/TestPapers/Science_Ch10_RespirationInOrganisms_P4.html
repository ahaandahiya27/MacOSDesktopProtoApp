Worked Solutions

1. (A) Food such as glucose stores chemical energy in its bonds; respiration breaks the glucose down (with oxygen, aerobically) and releases that stored energy in a usable form. Energy is not created from nothing in cells, nor is it absent from food until oxygen reaches the lungs, and the stomach digests rather than destroys the energy.

2. (D) Aerobic breakdown (with oxygen) splits glucose fully into carbon dioxide and water and yields far more energy per glucose than anaerobic breakdown, which only partly breaks the glucose down into products such as alcohol or lactic acid. Equal starting glucose does not mean equal energy released, and the partial products do not store extra usable energy for the cell.

3. (A) Breathing is the mechanical movement of air in and out; respiration is the chemical release of energy inside cells. Faster breathing supplies extra oxygen so the muscle cells can respire faster, but the energy is released in those cells, not in the lungs. The two processes are linked, not unconnected, and faster breathing supports rather than slows respiration.

4. (A) Air enters the nostrils, is cleaned and warmed in the nasal cavity, passes down the windpipe (trachea), branches into the bronchi, reaches the thin-walled alveoli, and there oxygen crosses into the blood. The nasal cavity comes before the trachea, and the trachea comes before the bronchi, so paths that reverse these are wrong.

5. (B) Respiring seeds use oxygen and give out carbon dioxide; the carbon dioxide turns limewater milky, directly evidencing respiration even without visible breathing. Feeling cool/dry, floating, or a candle merely staying lit are not tests for the carbon dioxide that respiration produces.

6. (B) During hard, fast effort the oxygen supply cannot keep up, so muscle cells partly respire anaerobically and accumulate lactic acid, causing cramp. Rest restores oxygen, which breaks the lactic acid down and relieves the cramp. Human muscles make lactic acid (not alcohol) anaerobically, and the cramp is caused by too little oxygen, not too much.

7. (B) Yeast in dough respires the sugar without enough oxygen (anaerobic respiration), giving off carbon dioxide; the trapped bubbles make the dough rise. Yeast does not photosynthesise (it is not green and has no chlorophyll), and the rise is from gas bubbles, not boiling water vapour.

8. (C) Lactic acid built up while oxygen ran short during exertion; continued fast breathing after stopping supplies the extra oxygen needed to break that lactic acid down. The lungs are not mechanically stuck, human muscles make lactic acid (not alcohol), and the goal is to clear lactic acid, not to make more.

9. (C) The values rise from 13 (asleep) to 40 (sprinting) as activity increases, showing breathing rate climbs with muscular effort to meet higher oxygen demand and clear more carbon dioxide. The data do not show the rate highest at rest, falling with activity, or fastest during sleep.

10. (B) The rubber sheet represents the diaphragm. Pushing it up models the diaphragm doming up (relaxing), which shrinks the chest space and forces air out of the balloon — exhalation. Pulling the sheet down would model inhalation; pushing up does not enlarge the chest space, and this models breathing, not the heartbeat.

11. (A) Limewater turning milky indicates carbon dioxide. Y turning slightly milky shows ordinary air contains a little carbon dioxide, while X turning milky much faster shows exhaled air contains far more. So room air is not free of carbon dioxide, it does not contain more than exhaled air, and the change is due to carbon dioxide, not water vapour.

12. (A) Oxygen falls from about 21% to 16% (some used, but most still present) while carbon dioxide rises from about 0.04% to 4% (a large increase). So exhaled air is not oxygen-free, the body does not add oxygen, and the amounts clearly change — evidence that respiration consumed oxygen and produced carbon dioxide.

13. (A) A trained body supplies and uses oxygen more efficiently, so it meets the same oxygen demand with fewer breaths — A's lower rate reflects fitness. A higher rate does not prove stronger lungs; respiration has not stopped in A; and all working bodies, trained or not, still need oxygen.

14. (D) Anaerobic respiration (fermentation) by yeast yields alcohol (the smell) and carbon dioxide (the fizz and the inflated balloon) — one process explains all three. Yeast does not photosynthesise, the changes are chemical not boiling, and the yeast is breaking sugar down, not rebuilding it into sugar.

15. (A) 15 breaths per minute times 45 minutes equals 675 breaths. Adding 15 and 45 gives 60; multiplying by an extra factor of ten gives 6750; and 450 corresponds to only 30 minutes, not 45.

16. (D) On inhaling, the diaphragm contracts and flattens downward and the ribs swing up and out, enlarging the chest space so air is drawn in. Doming up and ribs moving down would shrink the space (exhalation), the diaphragm cannot flatten and let ribs move in at once, and the lungs do not move air by themselves.

17. (B) Respiration in cells never stops in a living body; even at rest the cells need oxygen and release carbon dioxide, so breathing must continue. Cells do not stop respiring in sleep, lungs do not make glucose, and breathing is essential at all times, not only during movement.

18. (B) Burning needs oxygen. Exhaled air has less oxygen (used in respiration) and more carbon dioxide, so the candle dies sooner. Exhaled air does not have more oxygen, it is not oxygen-free (the candle does burn briefly), and it is the lower oxygen, not water vapour, that snuffs the flame.

19. (A) Insects breathe through small openings called spiracles, and air passes through a branching network of tubes called tracheae straight to the tissues. Stomata and lenticels are plant pores; gills, bronchi, nostrils and alveoli belong to other animals' systems, not the insect tracheal system.

20. (C) A given volume of water contains far less oxygen than the same volume of air, so a fish must pass a large amount of water over its gills to extract enough. It is not that fish respire vastly faster, nor that gills physically cannot use still water, nor that water's weight sweeps oxygen away.

21. (C) A frog's moist skin lets it absorb dissolved oxygen underwater, so it need not surface; a fish's gills work only on oxygen dissolved in water and collapse in air, so the fish suffocates on land despite the surrounding air. Frogs do not have gills as adults storing oxygen in the stomach, and both animals keep respiring; their organs simply suit different surroundings.

22. (D) The trachea (windpipe) is the chief airway between the throat and the lungs; if it is blocked or cut, air cannot reach healthy lungs and breathing fails. Energy is released in cells (not the windpipe), the windpipe does not manufacture oxygen, and it carries air, not blood.

23. (B) As the single breath's oxygen is used up during sustained effort, the muscles increasingly respire anaerobically, producing lactic acid. Muscles cannot photosynthesise, one breath cannot keep full aerobic respiration going indefinitely, and living muscle cannot simply stop respiring.

24. (A) An earthworm absorbs oxygen through its moist skin, a cockroach takes air through spiracles, and a fish uses gills. The other sets misassign these — earthworms lack gills/lungs/spiracles, cockroaches do not use stomata or moist skin, and fish use neither spiracles nor lenticels.

25. (C) A leaf in bright light does both: it respires (always) and photosynthesises (in light). Because photosynthesis outpaces respiration in strong light, the net effect is carbon dioxide taken in and oxygen given out. The plant does not stop respiring by day, does not stop photosynthesising in light, and being sealed does not switch either process off.

26. (D) Respiration uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and water, so exhaled air has less oxygen, more carbon dioxide, and more water vapour than inhaled air. It is not richer in oxygen, not oxygen-free (it still holds about 16% oxygen), and clearly differs from inhaled air.

27. (C) A gas that snuffs a flame and turns limewater milky is carbon dioxide. Oxygen would relight a glowing splint, hydrogen pops when lit, and yeast does not 'breathe out' nitrogen — fermentation gives off carbon dioxide.

28. (B) Without light a plant cannot photosynthesise, but it goes on respiring all night — using oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide. So the air gains carbon dioxide and loses oxygen. The plant does not photosynthesise in the dark, it does not become inactive, and respiration continues (it does not stop) in darkness.

29. (A) First the muscles act (diaphragm flattens, ribs rise), enlarging the chest space, which lowers the pressure inside the lungs, and only then air rushes in: iii → ii → iv → i. Air cannot flow in first, the chest cannot enlarge before the muscles move it, and the pressure does not drop before the chest expands.

30. (C) A control with dead (boiled) peas isolates the variable: since only the live peas milken the limewater, the carbon dioxide must come from living respiration, not the apparatus or mere presence of seeds. Boiled peas do not respire (so release no carbon dioxide), limewater does not milken by itself, and sealed peas in the dark are respiring, not photosynthesising.

31. (D) High-altitude air is thinner (less dense), so each breath delivers less oxygen, and the body breathes more often to make up the shortfall. Mountain air is not rich in carbon dioxide, cold does not by itself drive the diaphragm faster, and lungs do not physically shrink in cold to cause this.

32. (C) The drop moves inward because oxygen is being consumed (the carbon dioxide is mopped up), showing the animal steadily uses oxygen — the hallmark of ongoing respiration to release energy. An animal does not photosynthesise, breathing without respiration would not consume oxygen, and the oxygen is being used, not merely stored.

33. (A) Both cases show the universal feature of life: every living cell respires all the time, using oxygen and giving off carbon dioxide — hence the baby's steady breathing and the seeds milkening limewater. Plants respire too (not only animals), respiration is not limited to movement, and neither baby nor seeds photosynthesise in the dark.

34. (C) A fish breathes with gills; a cockroach draws air through spiracles into a network of tracheae. Cockroaches do not use gills nor fish spiracles, earthworms have no lungs and adult frogs no gills, and frogs do not use spiracles nor fish moist skin.

35. (B) Complete breakdown with oxygen (to carbon dioxide and water) releases far more energy per glucose than the incomplete anaerobic route ending in lactic acid. Lactic acid is a leftover, not a richer energy store; equal glucose does not mean equal energy; and at rest the body favours the high-yield aerobic route, not the anaerobic one.

36. (D) Nasal hairs and mucus filter dust and germs from incoming air, protecting the lungs by delivering cleaner air. The nose does not add oxygen, release energy (that happens in cells), or convert carbon dioxide into oxygen.

37. (A) Leaves exchange gases through stomata, woody stems through lenticels, and roots take in oxygen from air held in the spaces between soil particles. Gills, spiracles and alveoli are animal structures, and the leaf/stem pores must not be swapped.

38. (D) On exhaling, the ribs drop down and in and the diaphragm relaxes upward (domes up), shrinking the chest so the pressure inside the lungs rises and air is pushed out. Ribs moving up/out and a flattening diaphragm describe inhalation, and a flattening diaphragm cannot accompany shrinking and rising pressure.

39. (C) All three milken the limewater, so all three release carbon dioxide — the candle by burning, the mouse and seeds by respiration. The living things do not absorb the carbon dioxide here, the seeds release it (not oxygen) in the dark, and carbon dioxide comes from respiration too, not only from fire.

40. (D) Holding a long phrase delays normal breathing, so oxygen runs low and carbon dioxide builds up; the gasping restores oxygen and clears the carbon dioxide. The lungs do not store alcohol, the purpose is gas exchange not cooling, and breathing does not manufacture glucose.

41. (C) A glowing splint relighting identifies oxygen. In bright light photosynthesis exceeds respiration, so the plant gives off surplus oxygen. Carbon dioxide would not relight a splint, respiration consumes rather than releases oxygen, and aquatic plants do not ferment water.

42. (C) The increase is 30 − 18 = 12 breaths; as a fraction of the resting 18 that is 12/18 = 2/3. One-half would be 9, doubling would be a rise of 18, and one-third would be 6 — none match the actual rise of 12.

43. (D) While the breath is held, cells keep respiring and carbon dioxide builds up in the blood, triggering the urge to breathe. Oxygen is being used up, not piling up; lungs do not make lactic acid; and resting human muscles do not release alcohol.

44. (B) Waterlogged mud has lost the air spaces, so the worm's skin cannot collect enough oxygen and it surfaces; the fish, in open water, can still extract dissolved oxygen through its gills. Worms surface for oxygen, not to drink; worms have no gills and fish no skin breathing; and the problem is too little oxygen, not too much.

45. (D) These are the alveoli; their very thin walls let oxygen diffuse easily and quickly into the surrounding blood. Thin walls do not store gas, alveoli do not manufacture oxygen, and they do not pump blood (that is the heart's job).

46. (B) Gills extract oxygen already dissolved in the water; the fish does not 'breathe' water itself. Fish have no lungs taking in water, they cannot make oxygen from water, and they do need oxygen — water flow is for gas exchange, not cooling.

47. (C) Whatever the organ — gills or lungs — the oxygen is ultimately used by cells to release energy from food (glucose) through respiration. Animals do not photosynthesise, breathing organs do not make glucose, and carbon dioxide is a waste product, not turned into energy.

48. (B) Seeds in the dark cannot photosynthesise, so the carbon dioxide that milkens limewater and the oxygen loss that snuffs the candle can only come from respiration — clinching proof that plants respire. Oxygen bubbles in light show photosynthesis, growth and flower movements are not direct tests for respiration.

49. (B) 20 breaths in 40 seconds is 20 ÷ 40 = 0.5 breaths per second; over 60 seconds that is 30 breaths per minute. Taking 20 ignores the time scaling, 13 corresponds to scaling the wrong way, and 60 would need 20 breaths in only 20 seconds.

50. (D) Oxygen allows complete breakdown of glucose to carbon dioxide and water, the route that frees the most energy; without it only partial breakdown (and less energy) is possible. Oxygen does not multiply glucose, is not itself energy, and its role is chemical breakdown, not cooling.

51. (D) Exhaled air is far richer in carbon dioxide than inhaled air, so over many breaths net carbon dioxide accumulates and turns the limewater milky. Inhaled (room) air alone has only a trace of carbon dioxide, the milkiness is due to carbon dioxide not water vapour, and inhaled and exhaled air differ markedly — the result is not chance.

52. (A) The athlete rises 12→24, an increase of 12 on a base of 12 (a 100% rise); the untrained rises 18→24, an increase of 6 on a base of 18 (about a 33% rise). The athlete's fractional increase is larger, not smaller or equal, and both clearly do change.

53. (A) Less dissolved oxygen means the fish must move more water over its gills to extract enough, so gill pumping speeds up. Warm water does not carry oxygen more easily, a fish cannot switch to breathing air, and it still needs oxygen for respiration.

54. (D) Respiration to release energy occurs in every living cell of plants and animals alike. Plants respire too (not only photosynthesise), respiration does not stop during sleep, and all of a human's cells respire, not just the lung cells.

55. (B) An adult frog takes in oxygen through its moist, moist skin and through its lungs — it does not keep fish-like gills. It does not use insect spiracles, and it certainly continues to respire and breathe out of water.

56. (A) Aerobic respiration combines glucose with oxygen to give carbon dioxide, water and energy. Leaving out oxygen describes the anaerobic case, swapping oxygen for carbon dioxide on the left is wrong, and reversing the arrow describes photosynthesis, not respiration.

57. (C) Both the flame and the animal consume oxygen and give off carbon dioxide; in a sealed jar the oxygen runs out and carbon dioxide accumulates, so the flame dies and the animal struggles. Neither releases oxygen or absorbs carbon dioxide, neither needs sunlight here, and neither makes glucose.

58. (C) Enlarging the chest lowers the pressure inside the lungs below outside pressure, and air flows in from high to low pressure — the lungs do not actively pull air. Lungs are not magnets, the diaphragm enlarges the chest rather than pushing air from the stomach, and air flow here is driven by pressure, not temperature.

59. (D) Yeast respiring sugar without enough oxygen produces both alcohol (valued by brewers) and carbon dioxide (which raises bakers' dough), so one anaerobic process serves both. Yeast does not photosynthesise, aerobic respiration would not make the alcohol, and the products come from respiration, not boiling.

60. (B) Relaxing the diaphragm and rib muscles lets the chest spring back smaller, which raises the pressure inside the lungs above the outside and pushes air out — exhalation. Relaxing cannot enlarge the chest or keep it fixed, and the lungs do not manufacture oxygen to expel air.
