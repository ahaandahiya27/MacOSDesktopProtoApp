Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

"Financial infrastructure" is best described as the part of a country's infrastructure that:

  • Abuilds the physical roads, bridges and power lines a city needs
  • Bsupplies drinking water and sanitation to households
  • Cmoves and manages money — banks, payment systems and stock markets✓
  • Druns the schools and hospitals of a region

Working

✓

Financial infrastructure is the network of banks, payment systems and stock markets that *moves and manages money* — distinct from physical infrastructure (roads, power) and social infrastructure (schools, hospitals).

Q2

The deepest reason financial infrastructure is said to *complement* physical infrastructure is that:

  • Aa road or factory still needs money — loans and payments — to be built and run, which the financial system supplies✓
  • Bboth are built by the same engineers
  • Cbanks and roads are always located in the same buildings
  • Dphysical infrastructure is useless and only money matters

Working

✓

It *complements* physical infrastructure because building and running a road or factory needs loans and a way to pay — money the financial system supplies. The two work together; neither alone is enough.

Q3

  • Afixed deposit account
  • Bsavings account
  • Cloan account
  • Dcurrent account✓

Q5

A fixed deposit pays a higher rate than a savings account chiefly because:

  • Athe bank likes long-term customers more
  • Bthe money is committed for a set period, so the bank can lend it out for longer without fearing a sudden withdrawal✓
  • Cthe government forces banks to do so
  • Dfixed deposits are riskier for the saver

Working

✓

The higher rate rewards the *commitment*: because the money is parked for a set term, the bank can lend it out for longer without fearing a sudden withdrawal, so it pays the saver more.

Q6

The chessboard-and-rice story (one grain on the first square, doubling each square) is used in the chapter to illustrate the idea of:

  • Asimple interest paid once a year
  • Binflation eating away savings
  • Cthe spread between loan and deposit rates
  • Dcompounding — earning interest on interest, so small amounts grow huge✓

Working

✓

Doubling grains on each chess square explodes to an astronomical total — the chapter's image for **compounding**, where interest earns interest and small amounts grow huge.

Q7

Compounding differs from simple interest because under compounding you earn interest on:

  • Athe principal plus the interest already added in earlier years✓
  • Bonly the original principal, every year
  • Cthe bank's profit, not your money
  • Dnothing until the deposit matures

Working

✓

Under compounding you earn interest on the principal *plus the interest already added*; under simple interest you earn only on the original principal each year.

Q8

By the "Rule of 72," money earning 8% compound interest a year will roughly double in about:

  • A6 years
  • B8 years
  • C9 years✓
  • D72 years

Working

✓

Rule of 72: doubling time ≈ 72 ÷ rate. Here 72 ÷ 8 = **9 years**.

Q9

Two friends each save ₹100. Asha starts at age 10, Bina at age 20, both at the same compound rate. Asha's deposit will be worth more at age 40 mainly because:

  • Aher money had ten extra years to compound, and compounding rewards time✓
  • Bshe deposited more money
  • Cthe bank gives children a higher rate
  • DBina's account was a current account

Working

✓

Same money, same rate — but Asha's deposit compounds for ten extra years. Compounding rewards *time*, so the earlier start wins.

Q10

A bank pays 3% interest on a ₹2,000 deposit and charges 7% on a ₹2,000 loan made from it. In one year the bank's gross earning from the gap (the "spread") is:

  • A₹40
  • B₹60
  • C₹80✓
  • D₹140

Working

✓

The bank earns the gap on the principal: (7% − 3%) × ₹2,000 = 4% × 2,000 = **₹80**.

A bank keeps part of its deposits as **reserves** so depositors can withdraw on demand; lending out *every* rupee would leave it unable to pay people back.

A bank is a **middleman** for money: it pools savers' deposits (paying them interest) and lends to borrowers (charging more), connecting the two.
