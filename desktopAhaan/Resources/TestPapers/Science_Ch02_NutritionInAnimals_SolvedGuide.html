Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

The five steps of nutrition occur in a fixed order. Which sequence is correct?

  • AIngestion → Absorption → Digestion → Assimilation → Egestion
  • BDigestion → Ingestion → Absorption → Egestion → Assimilation
  • CIngestion → Digestion → Absorption → Assimilation → Egestion✓
  • DIngestion → Digestion → Assimilation → Absorption → Egestion

Working

✓

The five steps run: ingestion (taking food in) → digestion (breaking it down) → absorption (passing nutrients to the blood) → assimilation (cells using them) → egestion (removing undigested waste). Only option C lists this exact order. The others scramble it — e.g. putting absorption before digestion is impossible, because nothing can be absorbed until it is first broken small enough to cross the gut wall.

Q2

A student says, "Once I swallow food, my body can use it straight away." The flaw in this statement is that the food must still be:

  • Aingested only
  • Bdigested and absorbed before cells can use it✓
  • Cegested before it can be used
  • Dassimilated before it is digested

Working

✓

Swallowed food is large and locked in complex molecules; cells can use it only after it is *digested* into simple molecules and then *absorbed* into the blood. So the missing steps are digestion and absorption. "Ingested only" is what already happened; egestion and assimilation come later, and assimilation can never precede digestion.

Q3

Which step of nutrition actually delivers nutrients **into the blood**?

  • Adigestion
  • Begestion
  • Cassimilation
  • Dabsorption✓

Working

✓

Absorption is precisely the step in which the simple nutrients pass from the small intestine into the bloodstream. Digestion only breaks food down (no blood transfer yet); assimilation is cells *using* what is already in the blood; egestion removes waste.

Q4

"Assimilation" is best described as the step in which:

  • Aabsorbed nutrients are used by cells for energy, growth, and repair✓
  • Bcomplex food is broken into simple molecules
  • Cfood is taken into the mouth
  • Dundigested waste leaves the body

Working

✓

Assimilation is the use of absorbed nutrients by body cells for energy, growth, and repair. Option B describes digestion, C describes ingestion, and D describes egestion — classic step-mix-up distractors.

Q5

Removal of **undigested** food from the body is called egestion. This is different from excretion because excretion removes:

  • Athe same undigested food, just by another name
  • Bwaste produced inside cells (such as during energy release)✓
  • Cwater only
  • Dbacteria from the gut

Working

✓

Egestion removes *undigested food* that never entered the cells. Excretion is different: it removes wastes produced *inside the body's cells* (for example during the release of energy). So they are not the same process under a different name (A), nor merely water (C) or bacteria (D).

Q6

Why can't a single molecule of starch from a roti simply pass straight into your blood the moment you swallow?

  • Abecause starch is poisonous until cooked
  • Bbecause the starch molecule is too large until enzymes break it into simple sugar✓
  • Cbecause the blood does not touch the stomach
  • Dbecause starch is absorbed only in the large intestine

Working

✓

Starch is a giant molecule; the gut wall only lets through small, simple sugar molecules. Until enzymes (starting with salivary amylase) cut the starch into simple sugars, it is too large to be absorbed. It is not poisonous (A), the blood does reach the gut wall via capillaries (C), and starch is mostly absorbed in the small intestine, not the large (D).

Q7

A butterfly sipping nectar and a snake swallowing a whole egg differ mainly in their step of:

  • Aabsorption
  • Bassimilation
  • Cegestion
  • Dingestion✓

Working

✓

A butterfly sucking nectar through a proboscis and a snake swallowing prey whole are both *taking food in* — they differ in their mode of **ingestion**. Absorption, assimilation, and egestion are later, internal steps that are broadly similar.

Q8

An Amoeba pushes out finger-like extensions to surround a food particle. These extensions are called:

  • Avilli
  • Bcilia
  • Cflagella
  • Dpseudopodia✓

Working

✓

Amoeba extends temporary cytoplasmic projections called **pseudopodia** ("false feet") to surround food. Villi line the human intestine; cilia and flagella are hair-like motion structures, not feeding arms in Amoeba.

Q9

A housefly cannot chew. It pours digestive juice onto solid food and then sucks up the liquid. This shows that, in the housefly:

  • Ano digestion occurs at all
  • Babsorption happens before ingestion
  • Csome digestion begins **outside** the body before ingestion is complete✓
  • Degestion happens first

Working

✓

The housefly releases digestive juice onto solid food *outside* its body and then sucks up the liquefied result — so **some digestion begins outside the body** before ingestion is complete. Digestion clearly does occur (so not A), and absorption/egestion still come in their normal later order.

Q10

A mosquito's mouthparts and a cow's flat-topped teeth are different because:

  • Amosquitoes are larger than cows
  • Bthe kind of food an animal eats shapes its feeding tools✓
  • Call insects suck and all mammals grind, with no exceptions
  • Dcows do not need to feed

Working

✓

Feeding tools match the food: a mosquito pierces and sucks fluids, a cow grinds tough grass with flat molars. So **the kind of food an animal eats shapes its feeding tools**. Size (A) is irrelevant, the "no exceptions" rule (C) is false (e.g. a butterfly is an insect that sucks while a grasshopper chews), and cows obviously feed (D).

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

An adult human has 32 teeth. Which set is used mainly for **biting and cutting** food?

  • Aincisors✓
  • Bcanines
  • Cpremolars
  • Dmolars

Working

✓

The flat, chisel-edged front teeth, the **incisors**, do the biting and cutting. Canines tear, premolars and molars grind — a different job for each shape.

Q12

A carnivore such as a dog has long, pointed teeth on each side of its front teeth, well suited to **tearing** flesh. These are the:

  • Aincisors
  • Bmolars
  • Ccanines✓
  • Dpremolars

Working

✓

The long, pointed teeth beside the incisors are the **canines**, well shaped for tearing flesh — prominent in carnivores like dogs. Incisors cut, premolars/molars grind.

Q13

The hardest substance in the human body, which forms the outer covering of the crown of a tooth, is:

  • Adentine
  • Bpulp
  • Cenamel✓
  • Dbone

Working

✓

**Enamel** is the hard outer covering of the crown and is the hardest substance in the human body. Dentine lies beneath it, pulp is the soft inner core, and bone is not part of the visible tooth surface.

Q14

The central part of a tooth, which contains nerves and blood vessels, is the:

  • Acrown
  • Benamel
  • Croot
  • Dpulp cavity✓

Working

✓

The **pulp cavity** at the centre of the tooth holds the nerves and blood vessels — which is why a deep cavity hurts. The crown is the visible top, enamel is the outer coat, and the root anchors the tooth in the jaw.

Q15

Tooth decay begins when bacteria act on left-over sugar and release acid that attacks the enamel. The best single reason to brush after sweets is therefore that brushing:

  • Amakes the teeth whiter only
  • Breplaces lost enamel
  • Cremoves the sugar before bacteria can turn it into enamel-attacking acid✓
  • Dkills the tongue's taste buds

Working

✓

Decay starts when mouth bacteria turn left-over sugar into acid that attacks enamel. Brushing **removes the sugar before the bacteria can make that acid** — that is the real protective action. Brushing does not rebuild lost enamel (B), whiteness alone (A) is cosmetic, and it should not harm taste buds (D).

Q16

Counting an adult's grinding teeth: there are 8 premolars and 12 molars. The total number of grinding teeth is:

  • A12
  • B32
  • C8
  • D20✓

Working

✓

Grinding teeth = premolars + molars = 8 + 12 = **20**. (12 counts only molars; 8 counts only premolars; 32 is all the teeth, not just the grinders — three tempting miscounts.)

Q17

Saliva does more than wet the food. The enzyme in saliva begins the digestion of:

  • Astarch✓
  • Bfats
  • Cproteins
  • Dwater

Working

✓

The enzyme in saliva (salivary amylase) begins the digestion of **starch**. Proteins are first attacked in the stomach by pepsin; fats are handled later with bile and pancreatic enzymes; water needs no digestion.

Q18

If you chew a plain piece of bread for a long time without swallowing, it slowly tastes sweeter. This is because:

  • Athe bread absorbs sugar from the tongue
  • Bsalivary amylase breaks starch into simpler sugars✓
  • Cthe acid in saliva caramelises the bread
  • Dbacteria add sugar to the bread

Working

✓

Chewing plain bread tastes sweeter over time because **salivary amylase breaks the starch into simpler sugars**, which taste sweet. The bread does not absorb sugar from the tongue (A), saliva is not hot or acidic enough to caramelise (C), and bacteria do not add sugar that fast (D).

Q19

Three pairs of glands in the mouth release saliva. Apart from starting starch digestion, saliva also helps by:

  • Aabsorbing nutrients into the blood
  • Bstoring undigested waste
  • Cproducing hydrochloric acid
  • Dmoistening food so it can be swallowed easily✓

Working

✓

Besides starting starch digestion, saliva **moistens the food so it can be swallowed** smoothly and rolled into a soft lump. Absorption (A) happens in the intestine, HCl (C) is made in the stomach, and waste storage (B) is the large intestine's job.

Q20

After you swallow, food is pushed down the food pipe by a wave-like squeezing of its muscular wall. This movement is called:

  • Aperistalsis✓
  • Brumination
  • Cemulsification
  • Dabsorption

Working

✓

The wave-like muscular squeezing that pushes food down the food pipe is **peristalsis**. Rumination is cud-chewing in cows, emulsification is bile's action on fat, and absorption is nutrient uptake.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

A person lying flat on their back can still swallow water and it reaches the stomach. The reason peristalsis can move food even against gravity is that:

  • Agravity always helps swallowing
  • Bmuscles of the food pipe contract in a sequence that pushes food forward✓
  • Cthe food pipe is a straight vertical drop
  • Dwater is lighter than air

Working

✓

Swallowing works lying down because the **food-pipe muscles contract in a coordinated sequence** that actively pushes the lump forward — it does not rely on gravity (so A is false, and the pipe is not a straight vertical drop, C). Water being "light" (D) is irrelevant.

Q22

The food pipe (oesophagus) carries food from the mouth region to the:

  • Astomach✓
  • Bliver
  • Csmall intestine
  • Dlarge intestine

Working

✓

The oesophagus carries food from the throat region straight to the **stomach**. The small and large intestines come after the stomach; the liver is a gland off to the side, not on the food's swallowing path.

Q23

The stomach is a J-shaped muscular bag. Its strong walls help digestion mainly by:

  • Aabsorbing all nutrients into the blood
  • Breabsorbing water from waste
  • Cproducing bile
  • Dchurning the food and mixing it thoroughly with digestive juices✓

Working

✓

The stomach's muscular walls mainly **churn the food and mix it thoroughly with gastric juices**. Most absorption happens later in the small intestine (A), bile is made by the liver (C), and water reabsorption is the large intestine's role (B).

Q24

The gastric juice of the stomach contains hydrochloric acid, mucus, and a protein-digesting enzyme called:

  • Aamylase
  • Bpepsin✓
  • Cbile
  • Dtrypsin

Working

✓

The protein-digesting enzyme in gastric juice is **pepsin**. Amylase digests starch (mouth/pancreas), bile is not an enzyme (it emulsifies fat), and trypsin is a pancreatic protein-enzyme, not a stomach one.

Q25

Pepsin works best in a strongly acidic setting. The hydrochloric acid in the stomach therefore has the double job of killing germs in food **and**:

  • Acooling the food
  • Bbreaking down fats into droplets
  • Ccreating the acidic conditions that let pepsin act✓
  • Dabsorbing sugar

Working

✓

Besides killing germs in food, the stomach's hydrochloric acid **creates the acidic conditions that allow pepsin to work** (pepsin needs strong acid). It does not cool food (A), break fats into droplets (B — that is bile), or absorb sugar (D).

Q26

The stomach is itself made largely of protein, yet pepsin and acid do not normally digest the stomach wall. The protection comes from:

  • Abile coating the stomach
  • Ba layer of mucus lining the inner wall✓
  • Cthe wall being made of bone
  • Dthe wall having no proteins at all

Working

✓

Even though the stomach wall is made of protein, **a layer of mucus lines the inner wall** and shields it from the acid and pepsin. The wall is not made of bone (C) and certainly contains proteins (D); bile (A) is not involved in stomach protection.

Q27

The thick, semi-liquid paste of partly digested food that the stomach releases into the small intestine is called:

  • Achyme✓
  • Bbile
  • Ccud
  • Dsaliva

Working

✓

The thick, semi-liquid paste of partly digested food released from the stomach is called **chyme**. Cud is the regurgitated grass of a ruminant, bile is the liver's fat-fluid, and saliva is the mouth's secretion.

Q28

In which order does food pass through these three organs?

  • Astomach → oesophagus → small intestine
  • Bsmall intestine → stomach → oesophagus
  • Coesophagus → stomach → small intestine✓
  • Doesophagus → small intestine → stomach

Working

✓

Following the food's path: mouth → **oesophagus → stomach → small intestine**. Option C is the only correct order; the others place the stomach or small intestine before the oesophagus, which never happens.

Q29

The small intestine is the **longest** part of the food canal (about 6 metres in an adult). The single biggest reason it is so long and coiled is to:

  • Astore undigested food
  • Bprovide a large surface and enough time to digest food and absorb nutrients✓
  • Cmake hydrochloric acid
  • Dchurn food the way the stomach does

Working

✓

The small intestine is long and coiled chiefly to **provide a large surface area and enough time to finish digestion and absorb the nutrients**. It does not store waste (A — that is the large intestine), make HCl (C — the stomach), or churn like the stomach (D).

Q30

Two secretions empty into the first part of the small intestine to complete digestion: bile from the liver and a juice rich in enzymes from the:

  • Agall bladder
  • Bstomach
  • Csalivary glands
  • Dpancreas✓

Working

✓

Into the first part of the small intestine empties bile (from the liver) and an enzyme-rich juice from the **pancreas** (pancreatic juice). The gall bladder only *stores* bile; the stomach and salivary glands act earlier in the canal.

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

The inner wall of the small intestine is covered with millions of tiny finger-like projections. Their main purpose is to:

  • Agrind the food
  • Bgreatly increase the surface area for absorption✓
  • Cmake the intestine acidic
  • Dstore bile

Working

✓

The finger-like projections exist to **greatly increase the surface area for absorption**, so more nutrients pass into the blood per second. They do not grind food (A), make the gut acidic (C), or store bile (D).

Q32

These finger-like projections of the small-intestine wall are called:

  • Acilia
  • Bvilli✓
  • Cpseudopodia
  • Dsphincters

Working

✓

These projections lining the small-intestine wall are the **villi**. Cilia are tiny beating hairs, pseudopodia belong to Amoeba, and sphincters are ring muscles that open and close passages.

Q33

Inside each villus are tiny blood vessels. This tells us that, right at the villus, the step taking place is:

  • Aingestion
  • Bdigestion of protein
  • Cabsorption of nutrients into the blood✓
  • Degestion

Working

✓

Because each villus contains tiny blood vessels (capillaries), the step happening there is **absorption of nutrients into the blood**. Ingestion is at the mouth, protein digestion happens before this in the stomach, and egestion is waste removal at the far end.

Q34

Suppose a disease flattened the villi so the inner wall of the small intestine became smooth. The most direct result would be:

  • Afaster digestion of starch in the mouth
  • Bpoorer absorption of nutrients, so the person stays undernourished even while eating✓
  • Cmore bile produced
  • Dstronger churning by the stomach

Working

✓

Flattening the villi removes most of the absorbing surface, so the most direct effect is **poorer absorption — the person stays undernourished even while eating enough**. It would not speed up mouth digestion (A), increase bile (C), or strengthen stomach churning (D); those are unrelated organs.

Q35

The large intestine is shorter and wider than the small intestine. Its chief job is to:

  • Adigest proteins
  • Babsorb digested sugars into the blood
  • Cproduce pepsin
  • Dabsorb water from the undigested residue✓

Working

✓

The large intestine's chief job is to **absorb water from the undigested residue**, turning it into solid waste. It does not digest proteins (A) or absorb sugars into the blood (B — the small intestine does that), and pepsin is a stomach enzyme (C).

Q36

If the large intestine absorbs **too much** water from the waste, the likely result is:

  • Ahard, dry stools that are difficult to egest (constipation)✓
  • Bloose, watery stools
  • Cvomiting of acid
  • Dfaster digestion of fats

Working

✓

If too much water is pulled out, the leftover becomes **hard, dry stools that are difficult to egest — constipation**. Too *little* water removal would cause loose stools (B); vomiting acid (C) and faster fat digestion (D) are unrelated to large-intestine water uptake.

Q37

The undigested, solid waste is stored for a short time and then removed through the anus. This final step is:

  • Aabsorption
  • Bassimilation
  • Cegestion✓
  • Dingestion

Working

✓

Removing the stored solid waste through the anus is the final step, **egestion**. Absorption and assimilation happen earlier and deal with useful nutrients; ingestion is the very first step.

Q38

The largest gland in the human body, which makes bile, is the:

  • Apancreas
  • Bsalivary gland
  • Cliver✓
  • Dgall bladder

Working

✓

The largest gland in the human body, and the maker of bile, is the **liver**. The pancreas makes enzyme juice, salivary glands make saliva, and the gall bladder only stores bile (it is not a gland that makes it).

Q39

Bile is stored, between meals, in a small sac called the:

  • Arumen
  • Bduodenum
  • Cgall bladder✓
  • Dpancreas

Working

✓

Between meals, bile is stored in the **gall bladder**, a small sac, and released when fatty food arrives. The rumen is a cow's stomach chamber, the duodenum is the start of the small intestine, and the pancreas makes a different juice.

Q40

Bile contains **no digestive enzyme**, yet it greatly helps the digestion of fats. It does this by:

  • Achemically breaking fat into amino acids
  • Bturning fat into starch
  • Cabsorbing the fat into the blood directly
  • Dbreaking large fat globules into tiny droplets so enzymes can act on them more easily✓

Working

✓

Bile carries no enzyme, yet it helps fat digestion by **breaking large fat globules into tiny droplets** (emulsification), giving fat-digesting enzymes far more surface to act on. It does not chemically cut fat into amino acids (A — fats give fatty acids, not amino acids), turn fat to starch (B), or absorb fat directly (C).

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

A patient whose gall bladder has been removed often finds **fatty** meals hardest to handle. This fits the idea that bile mainly assists the digestion of:

  • Afats✓
  • Bstarch
  • Cproteins
  • Dwater

Working

✓

Without a gall bladder, bile is no longer concentrated and released in a burst, so **fatty** meals are hardest to handle — confirming that bile mainly assists the digestion of **fats**. Starch (B), proteins (C), and water (D) do not depend on bile.

Q42

The pancreatic juice contains enzymes that act on carbohydrates, proteins, **and** fats. This makes the pancreas special because most other digestive juices:

  • Aonly absorb water
  • Bcontain no enzymes
  • Care made in the mouth
  • Dact on only one or two food types✓

Working

✓

The pancreatic juice is special because it acts on carbohydrates, proteins, *and* fats together, whereas **most other digestive juices act on only one or two food types** (e.g. saliva on starch alone, gastric juice mainly on protein). It is full of enzymes (so not B), is not made in the mouth (C), and is for digestion, not water absorption (A).

Q43

Cows, buffaloes, goats and deer are grouped together as **ruminants** chiefly because they:

  • Aall eat meat
  • Bdigest food inside a single cell
  • Chave only one stomach chamber
  • Dswallow grass quickly and later return it to the mouth to chew again✓

Working

✓

Ruminants are grouped together because they **swallow grass quickly and later bring it back to the mouth to chew again** (chewing the cud). They are plant-eaters, not meat-eaters (A); they have a four-chambered, not single-chambered, stomach (C); and they digest in organs, not inside one cell (D refers to Amoeba).

Q44

The partly digested grass that a cow brings back up to chew slowly while resting is called:

  • Acud✓
  • Bbile
  • Cchyme
  • Dmucus

Working

✓

The partly digested grass a cow returns to its mouth to chew at leisure is called **cud**. Chyme is the human stomach paste, bile is the liver's fluid, and mucus is a protective slime.

Q45

A ruminant's stomach has four chambers. The largest chamber, where microbes start breaking down the grass, is the:

  • Aabomasum
  • Brumen✓
  • Creticulum
  • Domasum

Working

✓

The largest of the four chambers, where microbes first attack the grass, is the **rumen**. The reticulum, omasum, and abomasum are the smaller, later chambers.

Q46

Grass is rich in cellulose. Cows can live on grass even though, like humans, they make no enzyme to digest cellulose. They manage because:

  • Athey cook the grass first
  • Bcellulose needs no digestion
  • Cmicrobes living in the rumen break the cellulose down for them✓
  • Dtheir saliva contains cellulase

Working

✓

Cows cannot make a cellulose-digesting enzyme themselves; they manage because **microbes living in the rumen break the cellulose down for them**. They do not cook grass (A), cellulose certainly needs digestion (B), and their saliva does not contain cellulase (D) — the microbes supply that ability.

Q47

A human eats the same grass and gets almost nothing from it, but a cow thrives on it. The best single reason is that the human:

  • Alacks both the rumen microbes and the re-chewing system that break down cellulose✓
  • Bhas no stomach
  • Ccannot swallow grass
  • Ddigests grass in the mouth

Working

✓

A human gets little from grass because the human **lacks both the rumen microbes and the cud re-chewing system** that together break down cellulose. The human does have a stomach (B), can swallow grass (C), and does not digest grass in the mouth (D) — none of those is the real reason.

Q48

In which chamber of a ruminant's stomach does acid-and-enzyme digestion happen, much like in a human stomach?

  • Arumen
  • Breticulum
  • Cabomasum✓
  • Domasum

Working

✓

Acid-and-enzyme digestion, like that in a human stomach, happens in the **abomasum**, the "true stomach" chamber. The rumen and reticulum host microbial breakdown, and the omasum mainly absorbs water.

Q49

Re-chewing the cud is useful because grinding the grass more finely:

  • Amakes the grass taste sweeter
  • Bturns cellulose into protein instantly
  • Cremoves the need for any stomach
  • Dexposes more surface for microbes and enzymes to act on✓

Working

✓

Re-chewing the cud grinds the grass finer, which **exposes more surface for microbes and enzymes to act on**, speeding cellulose breakdown. It does not change taste (A), turn cellulose into protein (B), or remove the need for a stomach (C).

Q50

Amoeba is a single-celled animal. It captures its food by surrounding it with pseudopodia. The food then ends up trapped inside a:

  • Avillus
  • Bgall bladder
  • Cfood vacuole✓
  • Drumen

Working

✓

After the pseudopodia wrap around the food, it is trapped inside a **food vacuole**, where enzymes digest it. A villus and gall bladder are human structures; the rumen is a cow's stomach chamber.

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

Inside the Amoeba, digestive enzymes are released **into the food vacuole**. This is comparable, in a human, to enzymes acting:

  • Ainside the cavity of the stomach and intestine✓
  • Binside the brain
  • Con the skin surface
  • Din the blood only

Working

✓

In Amoeba the enzymes are released into the food vacuole — a closed pocket where digestion happens. The human parallel is enzymes acting **inside the cavity of the stomach and intestine** (also enclosed spaces). It is not in the brain (B), on the skin (C), or only in the blood (D).

Q52

Which statement correctly compares nutrition in Amoeba and in a human?

  • Aonly the human carries out digestion; Amoeba does not
  • Bthe human takes in food but never assimilates it
  • CAmoeba absorbs food but never egests
  • Dboth carry out all five steps, but Amoeba does them within one cell while a human uses many organs✓

Working

✓

The correct comparison: **both Amoeba and humans carry out all five steps of nutrition, but Amoeba does them within a single cell while a human uses many specialised organs.** Amoeba does digest (so A is false), it does egest (C false), and humans do assimilate (B false).

Q53

The way an Amoeba engulfs a food particle with its pseudopodia is most similar to which everyday image?

  • Aa hand cupping and wrapping around a marble✓
  • Ba straw sucking up juice
  • Ca sieve straining water
  • Da knife cutting bread

Working

✓

Pseudopodia flowing around and wrapping a food particle is most like **a hand cupping and wrapping around a marble** — surrounding and enclosing it. A straw sucking (B) is more like a butterfly's proboscis, a sieve straining (C) is like baleen filtering, and a knife cutting (D) is like incisors.

Q54

After an Amoeba has digested and absorbed the useful part of its meal, the leftover undigested matter is:

  • Athrown out when the vacuole reaches the cell surface (egestion)✓
  • Bkept forever inside the cell
  • Cturned into a new pseudopodium
  • Dpassed to a neighbouring cell

Working

✓

Leftover undigested matter in Amoeba is **thrown out when the food vacuole reaches the cell surface and discharges its contents** — this is egestion in a single cell. It is not kept forever (B), recycled into a pseudopodium (C), or passed to a neighbour (D).

Q55

A pseudopodium is a "temporary" structure, unlike a human's stomach. The key difference this points to is that Amoeba:

  • Ahas permanent organs for each step
  • Bmakes structures as needed rather than having fixed organs✓
  • Ccannot move at all
  • Ddigests food outside its body

Working

✓

A pseudopodium being temporary tells us that Amoeba **makes structures as it needs them rather than having fixed organs** — a single cell improvises. So it does *not* have permanent organs (A), it certainly can move (C), and its digestion occurs inside the cell, not outside (D).

Q56

The glucose absorbed from your food is finally "burnt" inside your cells to release energy. The tiny structures in the cell where this energy release mainly happens are the:

  • Avilli
  • Bmitochondria✓
  • Cfood vacuoles
  • Dsalivary glands

Working

✓

Glucose is "burnt" to release energy mainly inside the **mitochondria**, the cell's energy structures. Villi absorb nutrients, food vacuoles are Amoeba's digestion pockets, and salivary glands make saliva — none release the cell's energy.

Q57

After a meal, a growing child uses some of the absorbed amino acids to build new muscle. This use of absorbed nutrients to build and repair tissue is the step called:

  • Aingestion
  • Bdigestion
  • Cegestion
  • Dassimilation✓

Working

✓

Using absorbed amino acids to build new muscle and repair tissue is the step called **assimilation**. Ingestion is taking food in, digestion is breaking it down, and egestion is waste removal.

Q58

Q59

Trace one mouthful of rice through the body. In which organ is its **starch digestion first started** and in which is the resulting sugar **mainly absorbed**?

Working

✓

Starch is first attacked by salivary amylase in the **mouth**; the resulting simple sugar is **mainly absorbed in the small intestine** (through the villi).
