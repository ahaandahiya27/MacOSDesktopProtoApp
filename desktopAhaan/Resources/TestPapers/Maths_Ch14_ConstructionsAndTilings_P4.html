1. Three towns A, B and C are not on one line. A water tank must be built equidistant from all three towns. Using only the perpendicular-bisector construction, where is the unique spot?
(A) At the crossing of the perpendicular bisectors of AB and of BC; that point is equally far from A, B and C.(B) At the crossing of the three angle bisectors of triangle ABC, which is equally far from the three towns.(C) At the midpoint of the longest of the three connecting segments.(D) Anywhere on the perpendicular bisector of AB, since that line is equidistant from A and B.
2. A segment PQ is 10 cm long. A point R is built so that RP = RQ = 6 cm by swinging equal arcs from P and Q. How far is R from the midpoint M of PQ?
(A) 1 cm, since 6 − 5 = 1.(B) 6 cm, since R is built with radius 6 cm.(C) √11 cm, since RM is perpendicular to PQ and RM² = 6² − 5² = 11.(D) √61 cm, since RM² = 6² + 5² = 61.
3. You drop a perpendicular from an external point F to a line m, getting foot N. You then mark any other point K on m (K ≠ N). Comparing FN and FK:
(A) FN = FK, because both are distances from the same point F to the same line.(B) FK < FN, because the perpendicular is the longest such segment.(C) FN is the shortest of all distances from F to points of m, so FN < FK.(D) They cannot be compared without knowing the length of segment NK.
4. A circle is drawn through the two endpoints of a 12 cm chord AB, and its centre O lies 8 cm from the chord (measured perpendicularly). The radius of the circle is:
(A) 8 cm, the perpendicular distance from O to AB.(B) 14 cm, since 6 + 8 = 14.(C) √28 cm, since radius² = 8² − 6² = 28.(D) 10 cm, since O is on the perpendicular bisector of AB and radius² = 6² + 8² = 100.
5. Two perpendicular bisectors of the three sides of a triangle are constructed and meet at point W. A classmate adds the perpendicular bisector of the third side. It will:
(A) pass through W as well, because W is already equidistant from all three vertices.(B) miss W, since three lines almost never meet at one point.(C) pass through W only if the triangle is equilateral.(D) meet the other two at the midpoint of the third side.
6. A perpendicular bisector of AB is drawn. A point T is chosen on it with TA = 13 cm, and AB = 24 cm. The distance from T to the line AB (along the bisector) is:
(A) 13 cm, since TA = TB = 13.(B) 1 cm, since 13 − 12 = 1.(C) 5 cm, since the bisector meets AB at the midpoint, giving a right triangle with hypotenuse 13 and leg 12.(D) √313 cm, since the distance² = 13² + 12².
7. On line m, two points L and R are marked with A as their midpoint (AL = AR). Equal arcs from L and R meet at T above m. A student then claims line AT is the perpendicular bisector of LR AND makes a 90° angle with m. Which is correct?
(A) Both claims are correct: T is equidistant from L and R, so AT is LR's perpendicular bisector through its midpoint A, hence perpendicular to m.(B) Only the first is correct; AT need not be perpendicular to m.(C) Only the second is correct; AT is perpendicular but not a bisector of LR.(D) Neither is correct unless the arcs use radius equal to AL.
8. A 60° angle is constructed, bisected, and the resulting half is bisected again. This smallest angle is then placed beside the original 60°. The combined angle measures:
(A) 75°, since the smallest piece is 60 ÷ 4 = 15° and 60° + 15° = 75°.(B) 67.5°, since the smallest piece is 7.5° and 60 + 7.5 = 67.5.(C) 90°, since 60° + 30° = 90°.(D) 80°, since the smallest piece is 20° and 60 + 20 = 80.
9. Which angle below CANNOT be constructed with only a compass and straightedge?
(A) 75°, as 60° + half of a 30° angle (15°).(B) 50°, since it would require trisecting a constructible 150° angle, and general trisection is impossible.(C) 105°, as 60° + 45° (45° being half of 90°).(D) 7.5°, by bisecting 60° three times.
10. To construct a 67.5° angle with compass and straightedge, a valid route is:
(A) trisect a 202.5° angle to get 67.5° directly.(B) construct a right angle (90°), bisect to 45°, bisect to 22.5°, then add 45° + 22.5°.(C) bisect a 135° angle, since 135 ÷ 2 = 67.5, where 135° is got by trisecting 405°.(D) construct 60° and add 7.5°, where 7.5° comes from trisecting 22.5°.
11. A right angle is bisected, the 45° is bisected, and the 22.5° is bisected. The smallest angle obtained is:
(A) 7.5°, since 90 ÷ 12 = 7.5.(B) 15°, since two halvings give 22.5 and then 22.5 − 7.5 = 15.(C) 11.25°, since 90 → 45 → 22.5 → 11.25 over three halvings.(D) 5.625°, after four halvings of 90°.
12. You construct a 30° angle (half of 60°) and lay three of them side by side sharing arms, then bisect the whole result once. The final angle is:
(A) 45°, since three 30° angles make 90° and half of 90° is 45°.(B) 30°, since bisecting returns to one 30° piece.(C) 60°, since three 30° make 90° and 90 − 30 = 60.(D) 135°, since three 30° make 90° and you add a 45°.
13. An angle of measure 96° is given. A student wants a 24° angle from it using only bisections. Is this possible, and how?
(A) No, because 24° is not a multiple of 15° and so is never constructible.(B) Yes, by trisecting 96° once, since 96 ÷ 3 = 32, then bisecting to ... no, it is impossible.(C) Yes: bisect 96° to 48°, then bisect 48° to 24° (two halvings, 96 ÷ 4 = 24).(D) Yes, by bisecting 96° once, since 96 ÷ 2 = 48 and 48 is close enough to 24.
14. To copy an angle ABC of unknown size onto a fresh ray, you swing an arc of radius r across both arms of ABC, cutting them at P and Q, and step off the chord PQ on a same-radius arc at the new vertex. If you accidentally use a SMALLER radius r' at the new vertex but the SAME chord length PQ, the copied angle will be:
(A) exactly equal, because the chord PQ was copied faithfully.(B) larger than the original, because the same chord across a smaller arc subtends a wider angle.(C) smaller than the original, because a smaller radius means a smaller angle.(D) impossible to predict without measuring the original angle.
15. A segment of length 7 cm is to be copied. Your compass can open to at most 5 cm. Can the segment be copied exactly with compass and straightedge?
(A) No directly in one step, but yes by stepping off in parts (e.g. 4 cm then 3 cm along a line), since the compass carries each part exactly.(B) No, a segment longer than the compass span can never be copied.(C) Yes, in a single arc, since the compass span does not limit copying.(D) No, because copying a length requires a marked ruler when the compass is too small.
16. A triangle has two sides 9 cm and 4 cm. For how many INTEGER values of the third side (in whole cm) does a genuine triangle exist?
(A) 7, since the third side x satisfies 5 < x < 13, giving x = 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12.(B) 9, since x can be 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13.(C) 5, since x ranges over 9 − 4 = 5 values.(D) 8, since x can be any whole number from 5 to 12.
17. A triangle is to be built by ASA with angles 95° and 86° on a 6 cm base. Before drawing you check feasibility. The triangle:
(A) cannot be built, since 95° + 86° = 181° exceeds 180°, leaving no room for a third positive angle.(B) can be built; the third angle is 180 − 181 = −1°, which is allowed for an obtuse triangle.(C) can be built, since the base 6 cm is positive and both angles are under 90° together count as acute.(D) cannot be built, because ASA requires the two base angles to be equal.
18. Using SAS you are given sides 6 cm and 8 cm with included angle 90°. The third side (opposite the right angle) comes out as:
(A) 14 cm, since 6 + 8 = 14.(B) 2 cm, since 8 − 6 = 2.(C) √28 cm, since √(8² − 6²) = √28.(D) 10 cm, since with a right included angle the third side is √(6² + 8²) = √100.
19. Which single data set determines EXACTLY ONE triangle (up to congruence)?
(A) Two sides 7 cm and 9 cm with a 40° angle opposite the 7 cm side.(B) Three angles 40°, 60° and 80°.(C) Two sides 7 cm and 9 cm with the 40° angle lying between them.(D) Two sides 5 cm and 6 cm and the third side 11 cm.
20. A right triangle is built by RHS with hypotenuse 17 cm and one leg 8 cm. The perimeter of the triangle is:
(A) 42 cm, since the other leg is 17 − 8 = 9, and 8 + 9 + 17 = 34 ... rounded to 42.(B) 50 cm, since the other leg is 17 + 8 = 25, and 8 + 25 + 17 = 50.(C) 40 cm, since the other leg is √(17² − 8²) = 15, and 8 + 15 + 17 = 40.(D) It cannot be built, since the leg must equal the hypotenuse.
21. A triangle ABC is built by SAS with AB = 6 cm, AC = 6 cm and included angle A = 60°. The triangle turns out to be:
(A) right-angled at A, with BC = 6√2 cm.(B) isosceles but not equilateral, with BC longer than 6 cm.(C) impossible, since two sides cannot be equal in SAS.(D) equilateral, with BC = 6 cm.
22. Two sides of a triangle measure 5 cm and 12 cm and the angle between them is increased steadily from 30° to 150°. The third side (opposite that angle):
(A) stays constant, since the two given sides are unchanged.(B) increases throughout, growing as the included angle widens.(C) decreases throughout, since a wider angle folds the ends closer.(D) first increases then decreases, peaking at 90°.
23. You are told a triangle has sides 3 cm, 4 cm, and an angle of 90° opposite the longest side. The longest side must be:
(A) 7 cm, the sum 3 + 4.(B) 5 cm, since with the right angle opposite it, it is the hypotenuse √(3² + 4²).(C) 1 cm, the difference 4 − 3.(D) Any length more than 4 cm, since the right angle does not fix it.
24. A regular polygon has an interior angle of 168°. How many sides does it have?
(A) 12, since 168 ÷ 14 = 12.(B) 15, since the exterior angle 12° and 180 ÷ 12 = 15.(C) 30, since the exterior angle is 180 − 168 = 12° and 360 ÷ 12 = 30.(D) 21, since 168 ÷ 8 = 21.
25. Two regular polygons have interior angles differing by 30°. One is a regular hexagon (120°). The OTHER, if it has MORE sides than the hexagon, has interior angle 150° and is a:
(A) regular dodecagon (12 sides), since exterior angle = 180 − 150 = 30° and 360 ÷ 30 = 12.(B) regular octagon, since 150° is the octagon's interior angle.(C) regular decagon, since a 30° difference means 10 sides.(D) regular nonagon, since 150° gives 360 ÷ 150 = 2.4 ≈ 9 sides.
26. A regular polygon's exterior angle is exactly one-fifth of its interior angle. The polygon is a:
(A) regular hexagon, since one-fifth points to 6 sides.(B) regular pentagon, since the word 'fifth' means 5 sides.(C) regular dodecagon (12 sides), since interior = 5 × exterior and interior + exterior = 180 give exterior 30°, so n = 360 ÷ 30 = 12.(D) regular decagon, since exterior 36° and 36 = interior ÷ 5.
27. The interior angle of a regular n-gon is 180° − 360°/n. For which n is the interior angle EXACTLY twice the exterior angle?
(A) n = 4, since a square's angle is double something.(B) n = 3, since the triangle's 60° is twice 30°.(C) n = 12, since 12 is double 6.(D) n = 6, since interior = 2 × exterior gives exterior = 60° and 360 ÷ 60 = 6.
28. A regular polygon is such that 360° divided by its interior angle equals 2.5. Although it cannot self-tile, what is its interior angle and number of sides?
(A) Interior 150°, a regular dodecagon (12 sides).(B) Interior 144°, a regular decagon (10 sides).(C) Interior 135°, a regular octagon (8 sides).(D) Interior 90°, a square (4 sides).
29. Among regular polygons, exactly three self-tile. A student lists their EXTERIOR angles. Which list is correct?
(A) 120°, 90°, 60° (triangle, square, hexagon).(B) 60°, 90°, 120° (triangle, square, hexagon), reading interior angles by mistake.(C) 72°, 60°, 45° (pentagon, hexagon, octagon).(D) 120°, 90°, 72° (triangle, square, pentagon).
30. Why does the self-tiling ability of regular polygons stop after the hexagon, never resuming for any larger number of sides?
(A) Because for n > 6 the interior angle exceeds 180°, which no tiling allows.(B) Because large polygons have too many sides to fit any vertex.(C) For n > 6 the interior angle is strictly between 120° and 180°, so 360 ÷ (interior) lies strictly between 2 and 3 and is never a whole number.(D) Because beyond six sides the exterior angle becomes negative.
31. A regular polygon tiles the plane by itself with exactly k copies at each vertex. The only possible values of k are:
(A) 3, 4, 5 and 6, covering pentagons too.(B) 2, 3 and 4 only.(C) 3, 4 and 6 (hexagon, square, triangle).(D) any k ≥ 3, since at least three tiles always meet.
32. A regular polygon has interior angle 150°. A student says 'since 150° is under 180° and 360 ÷ 150 = 2.4 rounds to 2, two of them tile.' The correct verdict is:
(A) It does not self-tile; two give 300° (a 60° gap) and three give 450° (overlap), and rounding the divisor never produces a real tiling.(B) It tiles with two corners, since 2.4 rounds down to 2.(C) It tiles with three corners, since 2.4 rounds up to 3.(D) It tiles, because 150° is under 180°.
33. Of the regular pentagon (108°), octagon (135°) and decagon (144°), how many self-tile the plane?
(A) Exactly one (the octagon), since 135° is a multiple of 45°.(B) Exactly two (octagon and decagon), since both exceed 120°.(C) All three, since each interior angle is under 180°.(D) None of them, since 360 ÷ 108, 360 ÷ 135 and 360 ÷ 144 are 3.33, 2.67 and 2.5 — none whole.
34. A regular polygon has interior angle A with 90° < A < 120°. Can such a polygon EVER self-tile?
(A) Yes, if A is a multiple of 30°, such as the pentagon's 108°.(B) No, because 360 ÷ A then lies strictly between 3 and 4, so it is never a whole number.(C) Yes, for any A in that range, since three or four corners can fit.(D) No, because A in that range exceeds 180°.
35. At a tiling vertex the corner angles are found to total 354°. The correct interpretation is:
(A) The vertex does not close — there is a 6° gap, so it is not a valid tiling vertex.(B) It overlaps by 6°.(C) It is valid, since 354° is within rounding distance of 360°.(D) The measurement is impossible, because vertex angles can never be under 360°.
36. At a tiling vertex four equilateral triangles (60°) and two squares (90°) are placed. The angle total tells you:
(A) the vertex closes exactly, since six tiles always fit.(B) there is a 60° gap.(C) the vertex overlaps by 60°, since 4 × 60 + 2 × 90 = 240 + 180 = 420° > 360°.(D) it is valid, since both 60° and 90° divide 360°.
37. A proposed semi-regular vertex is square (90°), square (90°), triangle (60°), triangle (60°). What happens at this vertex?
(A) It closes exactly, since the angles total 360°.(B) It overlaps by 60°, since the angles total 420°.(C) It closes exactly, because four tiles are used.(D) It does not close: the angles total 300°, leaving a 60° gap.
38. Three tiles meet at a vertex: a regular hexagon (120°), a square (90°), and one more regular polygon that closes the vertex exactly. That third polygon is a:
(A) regular hexagon (120°), since 360 − 120 − 90 = 150 ≈ 120.(B) square (90°), since two squares and a hexagon often tile.(C) regular dodecagon (150°), since 360 − 120 − 90 = 150°.(D) equilateral triangle (60°), since the leftover rounds to 60°.
39. At a vertex, one regular dodecagon (150°) and one regular hexagon (120°) are placed, leaving room for ONE more regular polygon. That polygon must be a:
(A) regular triangle (60°), since 360 − 150 − 120 = 90 rounds to 60.(B) regular hexagon (120°), since hexagons fill leftover space.(C) regular polygon of interior angle 90°, but no such polygon exists.(D) regular polygon of interior angle 90°, namely a square, since 360 − 150 − 120 = 90°.
40. A semi-regular vertex uses one regular octagon (135°), one square (90°), and one more regular polygon. The third tile's interior angle, and whether it exists, are:
(A) 90°, another square, since 360 − 135 − 90 = 135 ≈ 90.(B) 135°, another regular octagon, since 360 − 135 − 90 = 135° (the 4.8.8 vertex).(C) 120°, a regular hexagon, since hexagons close most vertices.(D) 135°, but no regular polygon has that interior angle.
41. A vertex is built from one regular dodecagon (150°) and the rest filled with equilateral triangles (60°) only. How many triangles are needed to close it exactly?
(A) Three triangles, since 360 − 150 = 210 and 210 ÷ 60 ≈ 3.(B) Four triangles, since 4 × 60 = 240 fills the rest.(C) Three triangles, giving exactly 360°.(D) Three and a half, which is not a whole number, so a dodecagon plus triangles ALONE cannot close this vertex.
42. A semi-regular vertex has one regular hexagon (120°) and the rest filled with equilateral triangles (60°). What is the only whole number of triangles that closes it?
(A) Two triangles, since 360 − 120 = 240 and 240 ÷ 120 = 2.(B) Three triangles, since 3 × 60 = 180 looks like enough.(C) Four triangles, since 360 − 120 = 240 and 240 ÷ 60 = 4.(D) Six triangles, since six triangles always meet at a point.
43. Two regular hexagons (120° each) meet at a vertex, and the rest is to be filled with squares (90°) only. Can it be closed exactly?
(A) Yes, with one square, since 120 is close to 90.(B) No, since 360 − 240 = 120 and 120 ÷ 90 = 1.33 is not a whole number.(C) Yes, with two squares, since 2 × 90 = 180 fills the rest.(D) Yes, with 1.33 squares.
44. A semi-regular vertex uses two equilateral triangles (60°), one square (90°), and one more regular polygon. The fourth polygon's interior angle is:
(A) 120°, a regular hexagon, since 360 − 60 − 60 − 90 = 150 ≈ 120.(B) 90°, another square, since two squares balance two triangles.(C) 150°, a regular dodecagon, since 360 − 60 − 60 − 90 = 150°.(D) 150°, but no regular polygon has that interior angle.

46. At a tiling vertex one regular pentagon (108°) is placed, and the rest is filled with one regular polygon repeated. For the vertex to close, that polygon's interior angle would have to divide 360 − 108 = 252°. The smallest such whole-number-count fit needs interior angle:

Worked Solutions

1. (A) A point equidistant from A and B lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB; equidistant from B and C means it lies on the perpendicular bisector of BC. The single point on both is their intersection, automatically equidistant from all three. Angle bisectors give the point equidistant from the SIDES (the incentre), not from the vertices. A midpoint of one segment is only equidistant from that segment's two ends. A whole perpendicular bisector handles only two towns, not all three, so a unique point is needed.

2. (C) Because RP = RQ, R lies on the perpendicular bisector of PQ, so RM ⊥ PQ and M is the midpoint, giving PM = 5 cm. Triangle RMP is right-angled at M with hypotenuse RP = 6, so RM² = 6² − 5² = 36 − 25 = 11, RM = √11. Subtracting the lengths (6 − 5) ignores the right angle. The radius 6 is the slant RP, not the vertical RM. Adding the squares would apply if RM were the hypotenuse, but here RP is.

3. (C) Triangle FNK has a right angle at N, so FK is its hypotenuse and FN is a leg; the hypotenuse is always the longest side, hence FN < FK. The perpendicular distance is the unique shortest distance from a point to a line, not equal to a slanted one. It is shortest, not longest, so FK < FN is reversed. The right-triangle inequality settles the comparison without needing NK's length.

4. (D) The centre is equidistant from A and B, so it lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB, whose foot is the midpoint, splitting the chord into 6 cm halves. The radius OA is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 6 (half-chord) and 8 (distance to chord): radius² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100, radius = 10. The 8 cm is only a leg, not the radius. Adding 6 + 8 ignores the right-angle relation. Subtracting the squares treats the half-chord as the hypotenuse, which it is not.

5. (A) W lies on the bisectors of two sides, so it is equidistant from all three vertices; that makes it equidistant from the third side's two endpoints, so it lies on that side's perpendicular bisector too. Hence all three are concurrent at the circumcentre for ANY triangle, not only equilateral ones. The 'three lines rarely meet' intuition fails here because of the shared equidistance property. The circumcentre is generally not on any side, so it is not that side's midpoint.

6. (C) The perpendicular bisector meets AB at the midpoint, so half of AB is 12 cm.
