## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** The residue left after boiling a clear filtrate is made of substances that were dissolved and survive evaporation — dissolved inorganic salts like nitrates and phosphates, which the chapter classes as inorganic impurities. Organic matter (food bits, faeces, oil) is carried as solids or floating film, here in fractions (i) and (ii), so it is misplaced to call the salt residue organic. The residue is not dried-out microbes — pathogens are the living fraction (iv), not a salt cake. Dissolving in water does not convert a salt into an organic compound; classification depends on the substance, not on whether it dissolves. And not everything in sewage is organic — the very point of the sorting is that dissolved inorganics form a separate class.

**2. (B)** Pathogens are living microbes that enter chiefly with human and medical waste, so a clinic and households dominate that class. Dissolved inorganic salts (nitrates, phosphates, metal salts) come largely from industrial effluent like a dye-mill and from fertiliser-laden field runoff, so those dominate the dissolved-inorganic class. Fields contribute nutrients and runoff, not the heaviest pathogen load. A dye-mill releases chemicals and dissolved salts, not the main microbe load, and a clinic certainly does not add only clean rainwater. The sources are not interchangeable; the whole reason sewage is mixed is that different sources add different dominant contaminant classes.

**3. (B)** Large objects are caught first by the bar screen (the rag), then heavy sand and grit settle in the grit chamber, then in the settling tank the sludge sinks while the oily scum is skimmed off the top, then the clarified water is aerated so aerobic bacteria consume dissolved organic matter, and finally the settled sludge is sent to the air-free digester where anaerobic bacteria release biogas. Grit removal never precedes screening, because screens protect the works from the bulky debris first. Aeration of dissolved organics happens after, not before, the scum-and-sludge separation in the settling tank. And settling/skimming come after grit removal, not before it.

**4. (D)** Each unit has a distinct job. The bar screen stops bulky floating objects like a sack. The grit-and-sand chamber lets heavy mineral particles sink by slowing the flow. The settling (sedimentation) tank captures the fine suspended solids that slipped past the grit chamber, settling them as sludge. The aeration tank is where aerobic bacteria consume the dissolved organic matter that no physical step can remove. The grit chamber cannot catch a sack, nor does the bar screen catch grit. The settling tank, not the aeration tank, removes fine suspended mud, and dissolved organic matter is consumed by aeration, not settled out or digested in the sludge digester.

**5. (C)** Bubbling air means oxygen-needing aerobic bacteria, and their job is to consume dissolved organic matter so the water clears — that is Tank P. Sealing from air means oxygen-free anaerobic bacteria, and their action on sludge releases biogas — that is Tank Q. The trainee swapped both: he called the aerated cleaning tank anaerobic and biogas-making, and the sealed gas-making tank aerobic and cleaning, so both claims are wrong. It is not just one error; both halves are reversed. And the two tanks do not use the same bacteria — they deliberately use opposite types under opposite air conditions.

**6. (D)** Biogas comes from anaerobic bacteria decomposing organic sludge, so only the organic settled solids feed the digester usefully. Grit is inert mineral sand that the bacteria cannot ferment into gas, and it is taken out early precisely so abrasive sand neither wears equipment nor contaminates the sludge. So feeding grit to the digester is pointless. The digester acts on settled organic sludge, not on the skimmed floating scum. And settling does not transform sand into organic matter — what settles in the grit chamber stays mineral grit.

**7. (B)** Treatment removes most solids and organic matter, so the effluent looks much cleaner than raw sewage, but the chapter is explicit that cleaner is not the same as potable — the treated water may still carry germs and is not declared safe to drink. Looking clear does not certify it as drinking water. Treatment makes the water cleaner, never dirtier than the raw inlet. And added clearness does not mean added nutrients making it safe; extra dissolved nutrients would be a pollution problem, not a sign of drinkability.

**8. (D)** Bulky floating debris like plastic and branches is the bar screen's job, and heavy washed-in road sand is the grit chamber's job — those two units bear the brunt of a storm surge, so they are checked first. The aeration tank grows bacteria and is not built to trap plastics, and the digester decomposes organic sludge, not sand. The settling tank captures fine suspended solids, not bulky plastics, and the aeration tank does not remove grit. Matching each load to the unit physically designed for it points to the screen and the grit chamber.

**9. (C)** Grit removal sits early precisely to stop abrasive mineral sand from wearing moving equipment and from mixing into the organic sludge handled later, which would degrade the sludge and the products derived from it. Sand is inert, not bacterial food, and it does not make effluent drinkable. Mineral sand does not dissolve into nitrate, and bacteria cannot turn sand into biogas. The bar screen is upstream of the grit chamber and has coarse bars that pass sand, so it cannot rescue the situation downstream — the sand reaches the later units and does the damage.

**10. (D)** A floor scraper moving settled solids plus a surface skimmer taking off a greasy film, with no air being bubbled, describes the settling tank, where the dense bottom material is sludge and the floating film is scum. The aeration tank would show vigorous bubbling and is not where scum-and-sludge layers are skimmed and scraped this way; biogas is a gas, not a surface film here. Bottom and top must not be swapped — sludge sinks and scum floats. And the sealed digester has no open scraping-and-skimming arms exposed like this, nor does it hold grit and 'manure' as its two layers.

**11. (C)** The aeration tank is the only step where aerobic bacteria consume the dissolved organic matter that physical steps cannot remove, so skipping it leaves that dissolved organic load in the water. Plastics are still caught because the bar screen is untouched. Grit is still removed because the grit chamber remains. The bar screen still works — none of the screening, grit removal or settling is what was deleted. The unique loss from dropping aeration is the unremoved dissolved organic matter.

**12. (D)** The chlorination step is a final disinfection to destroy harmful microbes that physical and biological treatment did not remove; it targets pathogens, not dissolved salts. Nitrates and phosphates are a dissolved inorganic load that chlorination is not designed to take out. Settling heavy grit is the grit chamber's job, done early, not chlorination's. Chlorination does not make biogas — that is the anaerobic digester. And chlorine kills germs rather than washing away every dissolved salt, so the original claim is mistaken.

**13. (A)** After settling, the sludge (the solids) is decomposed in the sealed, air-free digester by anaerobic bacteria, producing biogas and a digested sludge later dried as manure, while the clarified water (the liquid) is sent to the aeration tank where aerobic bacteria clean out its dissolved organics. The two streams must not be swapped: water is not digested for biogas, and sludge is not aerated. The streams are handled in different units, not together in one digester. And the clarified water is not turned into manure, nor is sludge dumped back into the inlet.

**14. (A)** A village biogas unit works by anaerobic (air-free) decomposition that releases biogas, exactly like the sealed sludge digester at a treatment plant. The aeration tank is the opposite — it uses air and does not produce biogas. The grit chamber is a physical settling unit using no sunlight and yielding grit, not manure or gas. The bar screen merely sieves large debris and produces no flammable gas. Only the anaerobic digester matches both the air-free condition and the biogas product.

**15. (D)** Faeces is decaying organic matter; dissolved nitrate is a dissolved inorganic salt; intestinal bacteria are living disease-causing microbes — each fits one box cleanly. Faeces is organic, not a dissolved salt; nitrate is an inorganic salt, not a living thing; and bacteria are living pathogens, not dissolved inorganics, so the second option scrambles all three. The third option ignores that faeces and bacteria are not dissolved salts at all. The chapter's three contaminant classes map one-to-one onto these three examples.

**16. (B)** Dissolved organic matter passes straight through screening, grit removal and settling, all of which act on solids; only the aeration tank's aerobic bacteria consume it. So a high dissolved-organic outflow, with the physical units normal, points to failing aeration. The bar screen strains bulky objects, not dissolved matter. The grit chamber settles mineral sand and does nothing to dissolved organics. The settling tank removes suspended sludge, not dissolved organic matter. By elimination, aeration is the culprit.

**17. **18. (B)** Biogas is a gas produced when anaerobic bacteria decompose sludge in the sealed digester; scum is the floating oily and greasy material skimmed off the open settling tank's surface — they differ in physical state, in origin process and in use, so they are not the same substance. **20. **21. **22.
