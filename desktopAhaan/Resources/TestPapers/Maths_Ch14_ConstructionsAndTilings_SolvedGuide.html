Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

In a construction, a "straightedge" is used to:

  • Ameasure exact lengths in centimetres
  • Bdraw straight lines only (it carries no markings)✓
  • Cmeasure angles like a protractor
  • Dswing arcs of equal radius

Working

✓

A straightedge is an *unmarked* ruler — its only job is to draw straight lines. It carries no scale (so it cannot measure lengths) and is not a protractor; swinging arcs is the compass's job.

Q2

To construct the perpendicular bisector of a segment XY, you draw arcs from X and from Y. For the construction to work, those arcs must be drawn with:

  • Aany two different radii
  • Ba radius exactly equal to half of XY
  • Ca radius equal to the full length XY only
  • Dthe same radius from both X and Y, that radius being more than half of XY✓

Working

✓

Both arcs must be the SAME radius (so each crossing is equidistant from X and Y), and that radius must exceed half of XY or the arcs won't reach far enough to cross. "Exactly half" makes the arcs meet only on the segment (no upper/lower crossings); different radii destroy the equidistance.

Q3

Every point lying on the perpendicular bisector of segment AB is:

  • Aequidistant from A and B✓
  • Bcloser to A than to B
  • Cthe midpoint of AB and nothing else
  • Dalways inside segment AB

Working

✓

The perpendicular bisector is precisely the locus of points equidistant from A and B. The midpoint is just ONE such point; the whole line is equidistant, and points on it can lie far outside the segment.

Q4

The perpendicular bisector construction crosses the segment XY at its midpoint AND meets it at an angle of:

  • A45°
  • B60°
  • C90°✓
  • D30°

Working

✓

By definition the perpendicular bisector meets the segment at a right angle, 90°, while also cutting it in half. That is exactly the "perpendicular" in the name.

Q5

Why does the equal-arc construction land exactly on the midpoint of XY, with no measuring?

  • ABecause the compass secretly measures half the length
  • BBecause each arc-crossing is the same distance from X as from Y, so the line through them is the segment's mirror line✓
  • CBecause any two arcs always cross at a midpoint
  • DBecause the straightedge is marked at its centre

Working

✓

Each arc-crossing is found at equal radius from X and from Y, so it is equidistant from both endpoints — it lies on the segment's mirror line. Two such crossings fix that mirror line, which cuts XY at its midpoint. No length is ever measured; equidistance does the work.

Q6

A regular polygon's interior angle must satisfy which condition for the polygon to tile the plane by itself?

  • AIt must divide 360° a whole number of times✓
  • BIt must be a multiple of 90°
  • CIt must be less than 90°
  • DIt must be greater than 100°

Working

✓

For corners to surround a point with no gap/overlap, copies of the interior angle must fill exactly 360°, i.e. 360° ÷ (interior angle) must be a whole number. Being a multiple of 90° or "small" is neither necessary nor sufficient (120° tiles though it isn't a multiple of 90°).

Q7

Four squares meet exactly at a point in a square tiling because:

  • Asquares are the only four-sided shapes
  • Bsquares have four equal sides
  • C4 × 90° = 360°, filling the full turn with no gap or overlap✓
  • Dtwo squares already make 360°

Working

✓

A square's interior angle is 90°, and four of them give 4 × 90° = 360° — a full turn with no gap or overlap. Having four equal sides (B) is true but is not the tiling reason; two squares give only 180°.

Q8

How many equilateral triangles meet at each vertex in a triangular tiling?

  • A3
  • B4
  • C5
  • D6✓

Working

✓

Each interior angle of an equilateral triangle is 60°, and 360° ÷ 60° = 6, so six triangles meet at every vertex.

Q9

How many regular hexagons meet at each vertex when hexagons tile the plane?

  • A2
  • B3✓
  • C4
  • D6

Working

✓

A regular hexagon's interior angle is 120°, and 360° ÷ 120° = 3, so three hexagons meet at each vertex (the honeycomb pattern).

Q10

A regular pentagon has interior angle 108°. Why can't regular pentagons tile the plane by themselves?

  • APentagons have an odd number of sides
  • B108° is too large to fit any tiling
  • C360 ÷ 108 is not a whole number, so they leave a gap or overlap at a vertex✓
  • DPentagons are not regular polygons

Working

✓

A regular pentagon's interior angle is 108°, and 360 ÷ 108 ≈ 3.33 — not a whole number. Three pentagons give 324° (a 36° gap) and four give 432° (overlap), so they cannot surround a point cleanly.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

To construct a 60° angle with compass and straightedge, you essentially build the corner of which figure?

  • AAn equilateral triangle✓
  • BA square
  • CA regular hexagon
  • DA right-angled isosceles triangle

Working

✓

The 60° construction copies the corner of an equilateral triangle: equal-radius arcs build three equal sides, whose corners are all 60°. A square's corner is 90°, a hexagon's 120°.

Q12

Bisecting a 60° angle with only a compass and straightedge produces an angle of:

  • A15°
  • B45°
  • C20°
  • D30°✓

Working

✓

Bisecting halves the angle: 60° ÷ 2 = 30°. (Bisecting again would give 15°, the trap option A.)

Q13

A student bisects a right angle, then bisects one of the two resulting angles. The smaller of the final pieces measures:

  • A45°
  • B30°
  • C22.5°✓
  • D15°

Working

✓

Bisect 90° → two 45° angles. Bisect one 45° → two 22.5° pieces. The smallest piece present is 22.5°. (45° is the in-between stage; 30° comes from bisecting 60°, not 90°.)

Q14

Which of these can be constructed using only a compass and straightedge?

  • ATrisecting a general 60° angle into three 20° angles
  • BA 30° angle (by bisecting a constructed 60° angle)✓
  • CA 20° angle directly
  • DA 1° angle by repeated bisection

Working

✓

A 30° angle is constructible — bisect a constructed 60°. Trisecting a general angle (so 60° → three 20°s) is provably impossible with these tools, so a 20° angle "directly" is out, and an exact 1° cannot be reached by halving from 60°/90° either.

Q15

The seven pieces of a tangram are cut from a single square and rearranged into the shape of a swan. The swan's total area compared with the original square is:

  • Alarger, because the swan looks bigger
  • Bsmaller, because gaps appear
  • Cexactly the same✓
  • Dit depends on how the pieces are turned

Working

✓

Rearranging the same seven pieces only moves them; nothing is added or removed, so the swan has exactly the same area as the original square. "Looks bigger" and "gaps appear" are perception traps.

Q16

The deep reason rearranging tangram pieces conserves area is that:

  • Asliding, turning or flipping a piece changes its position but not its size✓
  • Bthe pieces are all triangles
  • Ca square always has the largest area
  • Dseven pieces always cover the same region

Working

✓

Sliding, turning or flipping a piece is a rigid motion: it changes position, never size. So a fixed set of pieces re-adds to the same total area no matter the arrangement.

Q17

A regular octagon has an interior angle of 135°. Can regular octagons tile the plane on their own?

  • AYes, four of them meet at a vertex
  • BYes, three of them meet at a vertex
  • CYes, two of them meet at a vertex
  • DNo, because 360 ÷ 135 is not a whole number✓

Working

✓

A regular octagon's interior angle is 135°, and 360 ÷ 135 ≈ 2.67 — not a whole number. Two octagons give 270° (a 90° gap), three give 405° (overlap), so octagons cannot tile alone.

Q18

At a tiling vertex, two regular octagons (135° each) meet along with one more regular polygon that fits exactly. That polygon must have an interior angle of:

  • A45°
  • B90°✓
  • C120°
  • D60°

Working

✓

Two octagons contribute 2 × 135° = 270°. The leftover to reach a full 360° is 360° − 270° = 90°, so the third polygon's interior angle is 90°.

Q19

The polygon in the previous situation (one that fills the leftover angle beside two octagons) is a:

  • Aregular hexagon
  • Bregular triangle
  • Csquare✓
  • Dregular pentagon

Working

✓

A regular polygon with a 90° interior angle is a square. (This is the classic octagon–square semi-regular tiling.)

Q20

Three regular pentagons (108° each) are placed around a point. The angle still left uncovered is:

  • A36°✓
  • B0° (they tile)
  • C72°
  • D45°

Working

✓

Three pentagons give 3 × 108° = 324°. The angle left uncovered is 360° − 324° = 36°. (A 0° leftover would mean they tile, which they don't.)

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

If your compass slipped so the two arcs for a perpendicular bisector had DIFFERENT radii, the line you draw would:

  • Astill be the exact perpendicular bisector
  • Bnot pass through the midpoint and not be perpendicular — it would tilt and miss✓
  • Cbecome an angle bisector instead
  • Dbe parallel to XY

Working

✓

Unequal radii destroy the equidistance: the two crossings are no longer the same distance from X and Y, so the line through them tilts, misses the midpoint, and is not perpendicular. It is neither the true perpendicular bisector nor an angle bisector.

Q22

A "construction" in this chapter is special because the figure is exact by:

  • Acareful measuring with a ruler
  • Btracing over a printed shape
  • Cusing a protractor accurately
  • Dgeometry — equal radii and equidistance — not measurement✓

Working

✓

A construction is *exact by geometry* — equal radii force equal distances, guaranteeing the result without any measuring. Ruler/protractor measuring (A, C) or tracing (B) would only be approximate; that is precisely what makes constructions special.

Q23

Which statement about trisecting and bisecting angles is TRUE?

  • ABisecting a general angle is possible, but trisecting a general angle is impossible with these tools✓
  • BBoth bisecting and trisecting a general angle are easy with compass and straightedge
  • CTrisecting is possible but bisecting is not
  • DNeither can be done without a protractor

Working

✓

With compass and straightedge any angle can be BISECTED, but trisecting a *general* angle is provably impossible (you can make 30° = half of 60°, but not 20° = a third). So only A is true.

Q24

To get a 15° angle using only a compass and straightedge, the cleanest route is to:

  • Aconstruct 60°, bisect to 30°, bisect again to 15°✓
  • Bconstruct 45° and subtract 30°
  • Ctrisect a 45° angle
  • Dit cannot be constructed at all

Working

✓

Construct 60° (equilateral corner), bisect to 30°, bisect again to 15°. Subtracting 30° from 45° isn't a single clean construction step here, and trisecting a 45° is impossible; 15° certainly IS constructible, ruling out D.

Q25

A regular dodecagon (12 sides) has interior angle 150°. Around a point you place one dodecagon corner. To fill the rest of the 360° with two MORE equal polygons, each of those must have interior angle:

  • A90°
  • B60°
  • C120°
  • D105°✓

Working

✓

One dodecagon corner is 150°. The remaining angle is 360° − 150° = 210°, shared by two equal polygons: 210° ÷ 2 = 105° each.

Q26

Which set of regular polygons tile the plane EACH BY ITSELF?

  • ATriangle, square, pentagon
  • BSquare, hexagon, octagon
  • CTriangle, square, hexagon✓
  • DPentagon, hexagon, heptagon

Working

✓

Only triangle (60°), square (90°) and hexagon (120°) have interior angles dividing 360° a whole number of times, so only this trio tiles each on its own. Pentagons (108°) and octagons (135°) do not.

Q27

A regular polygon has interior angle 144° (a regular decagon). The number of such polygons that would meet at a tiling vertex if they tiled would be 360 ÷ 144 = 2.5. This tells you the decagon:

  • Atiles, with 2 meeting at a vertex
  • Bcannot tile by itself, because 2.5 is not a whole number✓
  • Ctiles, with 3 meeting at a vertex
  • Dtiles, with 2.5 meeting at a vertex

Working

✓

360 ÷ 144 = 2.5 is not a whole number, so a whole number of decagons can never surround a point — the decagon cannot tile by itself. "2.5 tiles meet" (D) is meaningless; you can't have half a tile-corner at a vertex.

Q28

Constructing the perpendicular bisector of segment XY gives you, as a free bonus, the:

  • Aexact midpoint of XY✓
  • Bangle bisector of angle X
  • Ca 60° angle at X
  • Da tiling of the plane

Working

✓

The perpendicular-bisector construction cuts XY exactly in half, so it hands you the midpoint for free — no measuring. It does not produce an angle bisector at X, a 60° angle, or a tiling.

Q29

A rectangle is cut along a diagonal into two triangles, which are then slid together into a different shape. The total area has:

  • Adoubled
  • Bhalved
  • Cnot changed✓
  • Dchanged by the length of the diagonal

Working

✓

Cutting and rearranging is rigid motion only — the two triangles are merely slid into a new outline, so the total area is unchanged (conservation of area under rearrangement). The diagonal's length has nothing to do with total area.

Q30

To bisect a given angle, from the vertex you draw an arc cutting both arms; then from those two crossing points you swing equal arcs to meet at a point P. The ray from the vertex to P works because P is:

  • Athe midpoint of the original arc only
  • Balways 30° from each arm
  • Cthe centre of the angle's circle
  • Dequidistant from both arms, so the ray splits the angle evenly✓

Working

✓

P is built at equal distances from the two crossing points on the arms, which makes it equidistant from both arms. A point equidistant from both arms lies on the angle's bisector, so the ray V→P splits the angle into two equal halves.

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

Equilateral triangles tile the plane. If instead you used the triangles to surround a point and only FIVE met there, the angle covered would be:

  • A360° (they still tile)
  • B300°, leaving a 60° gap✓
  • C240°
  • D320°

Working

✓

Five equilateral triangles give 5 × 60° = 300°, which is 60° short of a full 360°, leaving a 60° gap. (Six are needed to actually tile.)

Q32

Which is the correct first step to construct a 60° angle at the end A of a ray AB?

  • AFrom A, draw an arc crossing the ray; from that crossing, with the SAME radius, swing an arc cutting the first arc✓
  • BPlace the protractor at A and mark 60°
  • CMeasure 60 mm along the ray
  • DFold the paper at A

Working

✓

With compass and straightedge you start by swinging an arc from A across the ray, then from that crossing point swing the SAME radius to cut the arc — building the equilateral corner. Protractors, measuring or folding are not construction steps.

Q33

Squares tile the plane and so do regular hexagons. The reason hexagons need only THREE at a vertex while squares need FOUR is that:

  • Ahexagons are bigger than squares
  • Bhexagons have more sides so more must meet
  • Ca hexagon's interior angle (120°) is larger than a square's (90°), so fewer fill 360°✓
  • Dit is just a coincidence

Working

✓

The count is 360° ÷ (interior angle). A hexagon's 120° is larger than a square's 90°, so fewer hexagons fill the 360° (three vs four). It has nothing to do with physical size or "more sides means more meet".

Q34

A point is constructed at equal distance from both endpoints of a segment, on the same side. Joining it to a second such point on the other side produces a line that is the segment's:

  • Aangle bisector
  • Bperpendicular bisector✓
  • Ctangent
  • Dparallel

Working

✓

Two points each equidistant from both endpoints (one on each side) lie on the segment's mirror line; joining them gives the perpendicular bisector. Equidistance from the *endpoints* (not from arms) is the perpendicular-bisector signature.

Q35

The interior angle of a regular polygon must divide 360° evenly for a self-tiling. The largest regular polygon (most sides) that can tile by itself is therefore the:

  • Asquare
  • Bregular pentagon
  • Cregular octagon
  • Dregular hexagon✓

Working

✓

Self-tiling needs the interior angle to divide 360° evenly. Among regular polygons that holds only for the triangle, square and hexagon; the hexagon has the most sides of these, so it is the largest self-tiler. The octagon (135°) does not divide 360°.

Q36

Tiling (tessellation) is best defined as:

  • Acovering a region with shapes leaving no gaps and no overlaps✓
  • Bcovering a region with shapes that may overlap a little
  • Cdrawing a shape with a compass
  • Dcutting a square into seven pieces

Working

✓

Tiling (tessellation) means covering a region with shapes leaving NO gaps and NO overlaps. "May overlap a little" (B) violates the definition; the others describe constructions/tangrams.

Q37

At a tiling vertex the corner angles meeting there must add to EXACTLY 360°. If they add to less than 360°, the result is:

  • Aan overlap of tiles
  • Ba perfect tiling
  • Ca gap (uncovered wedge)✓
  • Da 90° corner

Working

✓

Less than 360° at a vertex means the corners don't reach all the way round — an uncovered wedge, i.e. a gap. (More than 360° would force an overlap.)

Q38

If the angles meeting at a tiling vertex add to MORE than 360°, the result is:

  • Aa gap
  • Ban overlap (tiles pile on top of each other)✓
  • Ca perfect fit
  • Da perpendicular bisector

Working

✓

More than 360° means the corners run past a full turn and pile on top of one another — an overlap. (Less than 360° gives a gap; exactly 360° is the perfect fit.)

Q39

A mixed tiling uses squares (90°) and equilateral triangles (60°). At one vertex three triangles and two squares meet. The angles there total:

  • A330°
  • B300°
  • C390°
  • D360°✓

Working

✓

Three triangles + two squares = 3 × 60° + 2 × 90° = 180° + 180° = 360°. The angles fill a full turn exactly, so this is a valid vertex.

Q40

A mixed tiling uses regular hexagons (120°) and equilateral triangles (60°). At a vertex two hexagons already meet; the number of triangles needed to complete the full 360° is:

  • Atwo triangles✓
  • Bone triangle
  • Cthree triangles
  • Dfour triangles

Working

✓

Two hexagons contribute 2 × 120° = 240°, leaving 360° − 240° = 120° to fill. Equilateral triangles are 60° each, so 120° ÷ 60° = 2 triangles complete the vertex. One triangle gives only 300° (a gap); three give 420° and four give 480° (overlaps).

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

A 90° angle is constructed and then bisected twice in succession (each time taking the smaller new angle and halving it). After the two bisections, the smallest angle present measures:

  • A45°
  • B30°
  • C22.5°✓
  • D60°

Working

✓

Bisect 90° → 45°; bisect 45° → 22.5°. After two bisections the smallest angle present is 22.5°. (45° and 30° are intermediate/distractor values.)

Q42

Which of these angles is NOT directly constructible by combining a constructed 60° angle with bisections of it?

  • A30°
  • B15°
  • C90° (as 60° + 30°)
  • D20°✓

Working

✓

From a constructed 60° you can bisect to 30°, bisect again to 15°, and add to make 90° (= 60° + 30°). But 20° would require trisecting 60°, which is impossible — so 20° is NOT constructible this way.

Q43

You construct a 60° angle, then bisect it, then place the 30° angle next to the original 60°. The total angle formed is:

  • A45°
  • B30°
  • C120°
  • D90°✓

Working

✓

The original is 60°; bisecting gives a 30° angle; placing the 30° beside the 60° gives 60° + 30° = 90°. (120° would be 60° + 60°.)

Q44

A tangram square is rearranged into a tall thin "tree" and into a wide squat "boat", using all seven pieces each time. The tree and the boat have:

  • Aequal areas✓
  • Bdifferent areas, the boat larger
  • Cdifferent areas, the tree larger
  • Dareas that cannot be compared

Working

✓

Both the "tree" and the "boat" use all seven pieces of the same square, so by conservation of area under rearrangement they have exactly equal areas. Appearance (tall vs wide) does not change total area.

Q45

Which statement is the correct reason only triangles, squares and hexagons tile among regular polygons?

  • AThey are the only shapes with equal sides
  • BOnly their interior angles (60°, 90°, 120°) divide 360° a whole number of times✓
  • CThey are the only convex polygons
  • DLarger polygons are simply too heavy to tile

Working

✓

The reason is pure arithmetic: only 60°, 90° and 120° divide 360° a whole number of times (6, 4 and 3 times). Equal sides, convexity and "weight" are irrelevant to whether a regular polygon tiles.

Q46

A regular polygon whose interior angle is 120° will, at a tiling vertex, have how many of its corners meeting?

  • A2
  • B4
  • C3✓
  • D6

Working

✓

360° ÷ 120° = 3, so three of its corners meet at a vertex (this is the hexagon case).

Q47

The interior angle of an equilateral triangle is 60°. The number meeting at a tiling vertex (six) multiplied by 60° gives:

  • A360°✓
  • B300°
  • C420°
  • D240°

Working

✓

6 × 60° = 360°, a full turn — which is exactly why six equilateral triangles tile around a point.

Q48

You want to construct a 45° angle with compass and straightedge. The correct route is to:

  • Atrisect a 135° angle
  • Bmeasure it with a protractor
  • Cbisect a 60° angle
  • Dconstruct a right angle (90°) and bisect it✓

Working

✓

Construct a right angle (90°) and bisect it: 90° ÷ 2 = 45°. Bisecting 60° gives 30°, not 45°; trisecting 135° is impossible; a protractor isn't a construction.

Q49

The perpendicular bisector of a segment is, in precise terms, the set of ALL points that are:

  • Ainside the segment
  • Bthe same distance from both endpoints✓
  • Ccloser to the left endpoint
  • Dexactly the midpoint

Working

✓

Precisely, the perpendicular bisector is the set of ALL points the same distance from both endpoints (the locus). The midpoint (D) is just one of those points, not the whole set.

Q50

Three regular hexagons (120° each) meet at a vertex. The angle they cover is:

  • A240°
  • B300°
  • C360°✓
  • D480°

Working

✓

3 × 120° = 360°, a full turn — so three hexagons exactly surround a vertex with no gap or overlap.

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

A student claims "any regular polygon can tile the plane." The simplest single counterexample is the:

  • Asquare
  • Bregular pentagon✓
  • Cequilateral triangle
  • Dregular hexagon

Working

✓

The regular pentagon (108°) is the simplest counterexample: 108° does not divide 360°, so it cannot tile alone. Triangle, square and hexagon all DO tile, so they're not counterexamples.

Q52

To construct a 90° angle (a perpendicular at a point P on a line) the construction relies on building a:

  • A60° angle and doubling it
  • Bregular pentagon
  • Ctangram
  • Dperpendicular bisector / equal-arc construction so the line meets at a right angle✓

Working

✓

A right angle is built from the equal-arc / perpendicular construction, which by design meets the line at 90°. Doubling a 60° gives 120° (not 90°); a pentagon or tangram is irrelevant.

Q53

Two equilateral triangles and two squares are arranged around a point. The total of their corner angles is:

  • A300°✓
  • B360°
  • C330°
  • D270°

Working

✓

2 × 60° + 2 × 90° = 120° + 180° = 300°. That is 60° short of 360°, so it is NOT a valid vertex — but the question only asks the total, which is 300°.

Q54

A regular polygon's interior angle is 140°. The value 360 ÷ 140 is between 2 and 3 (about 2.57). This polygon:

  • Atiles with 2 meeting at a vertex
  • Btiles with 3 meeting at a vertex
  • Ccannot tile the plane by itself✓
  • Dtiles with 2.57 meeting at a vertex

Working

✓

360 ÷ 140 ≈ 2.57 is not a whole number, so no whole number of these polygons can surround a point — it cannot tile by itself. ("2.57 meeting" is meaningless.)

Q55

Bisecting an angle requires finding a point equidistant from both arms. The tool that locates such a point exactly, without measuring, is:

  • Aa protractor
  • Bequal-radius compass arcs✓
  • Ca marked ruler
  • Da set square

Working

✓

Equal-radius compass arcs locate a point equidistant from both arms exactly, without measuring — that is what makes angle bisection a true construction. A protractor or marked ruler would only measure approximately.

Q56

A floor is to be tiled with regular pentagons only. Pushing three pentagons (108° each) snug at a corner leaves a wedge. Four pentagons would overshoot. This shows pentagons:

  • Atile with three at a vertex
  • Btile with four at a vertex
  • Ctile only if cut in half
  • Dcannot tile, since neither 3 nor 4 of them makes exactly 360°✓

Working

✓

Three pentagons give 324° (a 36° wedge left) and four give 432° (overlap); since neither hits exactly 360°, regular pentagons cannot tile the plane. Cutting them in half changes the shape, so that doesn't count.

Q57

Which pair of facts is BOTH true?

  • AA general angle can be bisected with compass and straightedge; pentagons cannot tile the plane✓
  • BA general angle can be trisected with compass and straightedge; pentagons tile the plane
  • CConstructions need a protractor; hexagons cannot tile
  • DThe perpendicular bisector misses the midpoint; squares cannot tile

Working

✓

True facts: a general angle CAN be bisected with these tools, and regular pentagons CANNOT tile. Option B reverses both (trisection is impossible; pentagons don't tile), and C, D each contain a false claim (constructions need no protractor; hexagons DO tile; the perpendicular bisector hits the midpoint; squares DO tile).

Q58

The interior angle of a regular polygon increases as the number of sides increases. This is why, among regular polygons, the ability to self-tile STOPS after the hexagon: any polygon with more sides has an interior angle that is:

  • Aless than 90°, too small
  • Bmore than 120° but not a whole-number divisor of 360°✓
  • Cexactly 360°
  • Dnegative

Working

✓

Beyond the hexagon, each regular polygon's interior angle exceeds 120° (heptagon ≈128.6°, octagon 135°, …) yet never again divides 360° a whole number of times, so none of them can self-tile. The angle is never less than 90°, never 360°, never negative.

Q59

A 30° angle has been constructed (by bisecting 60°). Constructing one more 30° angle beside it and then a third gives a total angle of:

  • A60°
  • B120°
  • C90°✓
  • D30°

Working

✓
