# Social Science — Chapter 2: Understanding the Weather · Paper 2 (Advanced) Solutions

**1. (D)** **Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at *one place and one time* — today's heat, drizzle and breeze; **climate** is the long-term *average* of that weather over many years. Confusing the snapshot with the average is the classic trap.

**2. (A)** Nearly all weather — clouds, rain, storms — happens in the **troposphere**, the lowest layer where almost all the air's water vapour sits. The stratosphere above is calm and dry.

**3. (B)** The five weather elements are temperature, precipitation, atmospheric pressure, wind and **humidity**. Altitude and latitude shape weather but are *places on Earth*, not elements you read off instruments.

**4. (C)** Pressure is quoted in hectopascals (hPa), so **1003 hPa** is the **atmospheric pressure**. Temperature is the °C, humidity the RH%, and wind the "breeze from the west."

**5. (D)** Rainfall is caught and measured by the **rain gauge**. The barometer reads pressure, the hygrometer humidity, the anemometer wind speed.

**6. (A)** A rain gauge reading in millimetres is the **depth** the water would stand on level ground if none drained or soaked away — not a volume. That is why two places can both report "20 mm" regardless of their size.

**7. (B)** Both gauges record **the same rainfall in mm**: a wider funnel catches more water *but* spreads it over a wider cylinder, so the depth comes out the same. Depth does not depend on funnel width.

**8. (C)** The **wind vane** swings to show the direction the wind blows *from*; the anemometer's spinning cups measure how *fast* it blows.

**9. (D)** A wind vane points *into* the wind — toward where the air comes from. Pointing east means the air arrives from the east, so it is named an **east wind**.

**10. (A)** Winds are named for where they come **from** because **that direction tells you the kind of air arriving** — a sea wind is moist, a desert wind dry, a polar wind cold. The name is really a forecast in disguise.

**11. (B)** Relative humidity is **how much vapour the air holds compared with the most it could hold at that temperature, written as a percentage**. At 100% the air is saturated and dew or rain can form.

**12. (C)** Humidity is measured with the **hygrometer**. (A common version uses a wet-and-dry-bulb pair of thermometers.)

**13. (D)** Cooler air can hold **less** vapour, so as the evening cools the *same* amount of moisture fills a larger share of the (now smaller) limit — relative humidity **rises**, even though no water was added. Keep cooling and it reaches 100% and dew forms.

**14. (A)** On a clear, still night **the ground radiates its heat away to the open sky and cools; the chilled air just above it can no longer hold all its vapour, which condenses** as dew. Clouds or wind would prevent that cooling.

**15. (B)** **Clouds act like a blanket**, absorbing and returning the heat the ground radiates at night. With a clear sky that heat escapes straight to space, so cloudless nights turn colder.

**16. (C)** A **falling** barometer signals **approaching unsettled or stormy weather**: low pressure goes with rising air that cools, condenses and builds clouds. Rising pressure means settled, sinking air.

**17. (D)** **Warm air expands, grows less dense and rises**, so it presses down less — lowering the pressure beneath it. That is why hot regions and storm centres are low-pressure areas.

**18. (A)** Air flowing from high pressure to low pressure *is* **wind**. The bigger the pressure difference, the stronger the wind.

**19. (B)** Precipitation is any water falling from the sky: rain, **snow, sleet and hail**. Fog hangs in the air rather than falling, and wind/dust are not water.

**20. (C)** Hailstones grow **high inside tall storm clouds where the air is below freezing; up-and-down currents toss them, adding icy layers** until they are heavy enough to fall — so hail can crash down even on a hot summer afternoon.

**21. (D)** The scientific study of the atmosphere and weather is **meteorology**; a person who does it is a meteorologist. Astronomy studies the stars, geology the rocks, cartography maps.

**22. (A)** India's official weather body is the **India Meteorological Department (IMD)**, which records data and issues forecasts and warnings. ISRO runs space, NDMA manages disasters, Survey of India makes maps.

**23. (B)** An Automated Weather Station's value is that it **records continuously and transmits data automatically, even from remote spots** where no observer could be posted — filling gaps in the network.

**24. (C)** The old animal-and-plant signs worked because **living things sense the humidity and pressure changes that precede rain** — ants, frogs and pine cones react to real physical cues, not magic or chance.

**25. (D)** Pine-cone scales swell shut in damp air, so a **closed cone hints at high humidity and possibly rain**; open scales mean dry air. It is a natural hygrometer.

**26. (A)** In much of the world weather moves **from west to east**, so a red *sunset* (clear air to the west, lit through to clouds) means fine weather is approaching, while a red *sunrise* means the clear belt has already passed eastward and cloud may follow — the kernel of truth in the old rhyme.

**27. (B)** A heat wave is declared when **the temperature stays unusually high, well above normal, for several days running** — a sustained spell, not one hot afternoon.

**28. (C)** The chief danger of a heat wave is **heatstroke and dehydration**, hitting the very young, the elderly and outdoor workers hardest. Forecasts let them take shelter and drink water.

**29. (D)** Forecasts save lives because warnings let people **evacuate before a cyclone, store water before a drought and stay indoors during a heat wave** — preparation, not just knowing what to wear.

**30. (A)** On a cyclone red alert the right move is to **shift people from low, exposed areas to safe shelters and secure boats and loose objects in advance** — acting on the warning rather than waiting for the wind.

**31. (B)** The day warms because **the Sun climbs higher through the morning and heats the ground more strongly**; the warmed ground then warms the air. The Earth's distance from the Sun barely changes over a day.

**32. (C)** The hottest time lags past noon because **the ground keeps absorbing more heat than it loses for a while after the Sun's peak**, so the air goes on warming into the early afternoon.

**33. (D)** The day's temperature **range = maximum − minimum**, found from a max–min thermometer. It tells how much the temperature swung that day.

**34. (A)** Monday's range = 34 − 22 = **12 °C**; Tuesday's = 30 − 24 = 6 °C. So **Monday had the larger range, by 6 °C**.

**35. (B)** Coasts have a smaller day–night swing because **the sea warms and cools slowly and moderates the air over nearby land**. Inland, without that buffer, days are hotter and nights colder.

**36. (C)** Up a mountain it gets colder because **the air is thinner and is warmed mainly from the ground up**, so temperature falls with height — even though you are technically a little nearer the Sun.

**37. (D)** Pressure **falls with height because there is less air above you pressing down**. That is why climbers carry oxygen and sealed packets puff up high on a mountain.

**38. (A)** Fog and cloud are the same tiny droplets; the only difference is that **fog sits on the ground while cloud floats higher up**. Walk into a hill fog and you are walking through a cloud.

**39. (B)** Tall, piled-up clouds that bring thunderstorms are the **cumulus / cumulonimbus** family; low grey sheets that drizzle are stratus; high wispy ones are cirrus.

**40. (C)** Thin, wispy, high ice-crystal clouds are **cirrus**, often the first hint that the weather may change in a day or two.

**41. (D)** We see lightning before we hear thunder because **light travels far faster than sound** — the flash arrives almost instantly while the bang lags behind.

**42. (A)** Sound covers about 1 km every 3 seconds, so a 3-second gap puts the storm roughly **1 km away**. (A 9-second gap would mean about 3 km.)

**43. (B)** In a thunderstorm the safest place is **indoors, away from open fields, water and tall lone objects**. A solitary tree or hilltop is exactly where lightning tends to strike.

**44. (C)** Forecasts are given as probabilities because **the atmosphere is so complex and ever-changing that even good data leaves some uncertainty** — not because forecasters are careless.

**45. (D)** A ten-day forecast is **less reliable than a one-day forecast because small errors grow over time** — the further ahead you look, the fuzzier it gets.

**46. (A)** Satellites add to ground stations by being able to **photograph whole cloud systems and track storms across regions** from above — a view no single ground gauge can give.

**47. (B)** A farmer should use a heavy-rain forecast to **adjust sowing, harvesting or drainage** so the crop is protected and the water used well — turning the warning into a plan.

**48. (C)** Muggy air feels sticky because high humidity **slows the evaporation of sweat from the skin**, so the body cannot shed heat and we feel hotter.

**49. (D)** The same temperature feels cooler in a dry desert and hotter on a humid coast because **humidity decides how fast sweat evaporates** — fast evaporation cools you, slow evaporation does not.

**50. (A)** A drinking **straw** shows air pressure at work: you lower the pressure inside, and the *outside* air pressing on the drink pushes it up the straw — proof that air has weight and pushes.

**51. (B)** A daytime sea breeze blows inland because **the land heats faster than the sea; the air over the warm land rises and cooler sea air flows in to replace it**. At night the land cools faster and the flow reverses (land breeze).

**52. (C)** The sea-breeze chain runs **(iii) the Sun warms the coast → (ii) the land heats and its air rises → (i) cooler sea air moves in to replace it.** That replacing flow is the breeze.

**53. (D)** A drought is essentially a long shortfall of **rainfall over a season or more**, leaving too little water for crops and people.

**54. (A)** One cloudburst can leave a drought because **the water may run off fast without soaking in or refilling reservoirs**, so the long-term shortfall remains. Droughts are about sustained totals, not a single storm.

**55. (B)** Water evaporating, rising, cooling, condensing into clouds and falling again is the **water (hydrological) cycle** — the engine behind all precipitation.

**56. (C)** Condensation, which makes clouds and dew, is water changing from **gas (vapour) to liquid**. Evaporation is the reverse.

**57. (D)** A balloon expands as it rises because **the surrounding air presses on it less — pressure falls with height** — so the gas inside pushes out further.

**58. (A)** Instruments sit in a white, louvred Stevenson screen so they **read the true air temperature in shade with free airflow**, shielded from direct sun and rain that would give false highs.

**59. (B)** At the same 30 °C the **RH 85%** town feels worse, because the muggy air stops sweat from evaporating and cooling the body; the RH 30% town's dry air lets sweat work.

**60. (C)** Modern forecasting has leapt forward thanks to **dense networks of stations and AWS, satellites, and computers that model the atmosphere** from all that data — folklore alone could never reach today's accuracy.
