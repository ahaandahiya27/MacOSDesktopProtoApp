Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

A 'constitution' is best described as:

  • Aa yearly budget of government spending
  • Bthe supreme document spelling out a nation's basic principles, laws, government, and citizens' rights and duties✓
  • Ca collection of a country's folk songs
  • Da treaty signed with a foreign country

Working

✓

A constitution is the supreme rulebook setting up government, listing rights and duties, and stating a country's goals. It is not a budget, a songbook, or a foreign treaty.

Q2

The chapter calls the Constitution the nation's:

  • Aoldest storybook
  • Bdaily newspaper
  • Csupreme 'rulebook'✓
  • Droyal family tree

Working

✓

The Constitution is the supreme 'rulebook' of the nation. It is not a storybook, newspaper, or family tree.

Q3

Which of these does a constitution do?

  • Adecide the result of every cricket match
  • Bchoose each citizen's job
  • Cwrite the lyrics of the national songs
  • Dset up the three organs of government and arrange checks and balances among them✓

Working

✓

A constitution establishes the three organs and their checks and balances (plus rights, duties and goals). It does not decide sports, jobs, or song lyrics.

Q4

A constitution also states a country's:

  • Aweather forecast for the year
  • Blist of tourist hotels
  • Clong-term goals and values✓
  • Drecord of cricket scores

Working

✓

A constitution sets out the nation's long-term goals and values. Weather, hotels and scores are unrelated.

Q5

Why does a country need a constitution?

  • Ato lay down agreed basic rules so government and citizens know their powers, rights and limits✓
  • Bso that no laws ever have to be made again
  • Cto make the country the largest in the world
  • Dto remove the need for any government

Working

✓

A constitution provides agreed basic rules defining powers, rights and limits for government and citizens. It does not end lawmaking, enlarge territory, or abolish government.

Q6

Officials such as judges and ministers take a pledge to:

  • Auphold the Constitution✓
  • Bobey a foreign ruler
  • Cserve only their own family
  • Dignore the courts

Working

✓

Officials pledge to uphold the Constitution, showing it is the supreme authority. They do not pledge to a foreign ruler, their family, or to ignore courts.

Q7

That even top officials must pledge to uphold the Constitution shows that:

  • Aofficials are free to ignore it whenever they wish
  • Bonly ordinary citizens must follow it
  • Cthe Constitution applies only on Republic Day
  • Dthe Constitution stands above every individual, including the most powerful✓

Working

✓

The pledge shows the Constitution is supreme over everyone, even the powerful. It is not ignorable, citizens-only, or limited to one day.

Q8

India's Constitution was written by the:

  • ABritish Parliament
  • BUnited Nations
  • CSupreme Court of India
  • DConstituent Assembly✓

Working

✓

The Constituent Assembly, formed in 1946, wrote India's Constitution. It was not written by the British Parliament, the UN, or the Supreme Court.

Q9

The Constituent Assembly was formed in:

  • A1947
  • B1946✓
  • C1950
  • D1976

Working

✓

The Constituent Assembly was formed in 1946. 1947 is independence, 1950 is when the Constitution took effect, and 1976 is when Fundamental Duties were added.

Q10

After Partition, the Constituent Assembly's membership changed from 389 to about:

  • A100 members
  • B545 members
  • C15 members
  • D299 members✓

Working

✓

Membership fell from 389 to about 299 after Partition. 15 was the number of women members, not the total; 100 and 545 are unrelated.

Q11

The Constituent Assembly included how many women members?

  • A15✓
  • Bnone at all

  • D299

Working

✓

The Assembly included 15 women. It was not all-male; 299 was the post-Partition total and 150 is invented.

Q12

The Constituent Assembly was chaired by:

  • ADr. B.R. Ambedkar
  • BMahatma Gandhi
  • CJawaharlal Nehru
  • DDr. Rajendra Prasad✓

Working

✓

Dr. Rajendra Prasad chaired the Constituent Assembly. Dr. Ambedkar headed the Drafting Committee; Gandhi and Nehru held other roles.

Q13

The Drafting Committee that prepared the text was headed by:

  • ADr. Rajendra Prasad
  • BDr. B.R. Ambedkar✓
  • CSardar Patel
  • DDr. S. Radhakrishnan

Working

✓

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar headed the Drafting Committee. Dr. Rajendra Prasad chaired the Assembly itself; the others held different roles.

Q14

The difference between Dr. Rajendra Prasad's and Dr. Ambedkar's roles was that:

  • APrasad chaired the whole Assembly while Ambedkar headed the committee that drafted the text✓
  • Bboth headed the Drafting Committee together
  • CAmbedkar chaired the Assembly and Prasad drafted the text
  • Dneither had any role in the Constitution

Working

✓

Prasad chaired the Constituent Assembly; Ambedkar headed the Drafting Committee that wrote the text. The other options swap or deny their roles.

Q15

India's Constitution was adopted on:

  • A15 August 1947
  • B26 January 1950
  • C26 November 1946
  • D26 November 1949✓

Working

✓

The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949. 15 August 1947 is independence, 26 January 1950 is when it came into effect, and 1946 is when the Assembly formed.

Q16

The Constitution came into effect on:

  • A26 November 1949
  • B15 August 1947
  • C26 January 1950✓
  • D1 January 1950

Working

✓

It came into effect on 26 January 1950, now Republic Day. The 1949 date is adoption, 1947 is independence, and 1 January is unrelated.

Q17

Republic Day, 26 January, marks the day:

  • Athe Constitution came into effect, making India a republic✓
  • BIndia became independent from Britain
  • Cthe Constituent Assembly was formed
  • Dthe Fundamental Duties were added

Working

✓

Republic Day marks the Constitution taking effect in 1950. Independence was 15 August 1947, the Assembly formed in 1946, and Duties were added in 1976.

Q18

The claim 'Republic Day is when India became independent' is wrong because:

  • AIndia is not independent at all
  • Bindependence was on 15 August 1947, while 26 January 1950 marks the Constitution coming into effect✓
  • CRepublic Day and Independence Day are the same date
  • DIndia became independent in 1950

Working

✓

Independence Day is 15 August 1947; Republic Day (26 January 1950) marks the Constitution's coming into force. They are different dates, and independence was 1947, not 1950.

Q19

Which sequence is in correct order (earliest first)?

  • AIndependence (1947), Assembly formed (1946), effect (1950), adopted (1949)
  • BConstitution adopted (1949), Independence (1947), Assembly formed (1946), effect (1950)
  • CAssembly formed (1946), Independence (1947), Constitution adopted (1949), comes into effect (1950)✓
  • Dcomes into effect (1950), adopted (1949), Independence (1947), Assembly formed (1946)

Working

✓

The order is 1946 (Assembly), 1947 (Independence), 1949 (adopted), 1950 (effect). The other options scramble these dates.

Q20

The three great influences on India's Constitution were the freedom struggle, India's civilisational heritage, and:

  • Athe rules of a single foreign king
  • Buseful learnings from other countries' constitutions✓
  • Cthe wishes of the British Parliament alone
  • Da vote of the United Nations

Working

✓

The three influences were the freedom movement, India's heritage, and learnings from other countries. It was not one king's rules, the British Parliament alone, or a UN vote.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

The freedom movement shaped the Constitution by:

  • Acopying Britain's monarchy exactly
  • Brejecting all Indian traditions
  • Cbanning all elections
  • Dbringing in the values and goals India had fought for, such as liberty and equality✓

Working

✓

The freedom struggle infused the Constitution with hard-won values such as liberty and equality. It did not copy the monarchy, reject Indian traditions, or ban elections.

Q22

The claim 'India simply copied its Constitution from one other country' is wrong because the Assembly:

  • Ainvented every idea from scratch with no outside study
  • Bstudied many sources and chose what suited India, weaving them with its own values✓
  • Ccopied only the British monarchy
  • Drefused to look at any other country

Working

✓

The Assembly studied many constitutions and selected what fit India, blending them with the freedom movement's values and India's heritage. It neither copied one country, invented everything alone, nor refused outside ideas.

Q23

India borrowed the idea of a parliamentary system mainly from:

  • ABritain✓
  • BIreland
  • Cthe USA
  • Dancient Rome

Working

✓

The parliamentary system was drawn from Britain. Directive Principles came from Ireland and the Fundamental Rights / judicial review idea from the USA.

Q24

The idea of Fundamental Rights and judicial review was influenced by the:

  • ABritain
  • BIreland
  • CFrance
  • DUSA✓

Working

✓

Fundamental Rights and judicial review drew on the US example. Britain influenced the parliamentary system and Ireland the Directive Principles.

Q25

The Directive Principles of State Policy were inspired partly by:

  • ABritain
  • Bthe USA
  • CIreland✓
  • DJapan

Working

✓

The Directive Principles were inspired partly by Ireland. Britain shaped the parliamentary system and the USA the Fundamental Rights idea.

Q26

The Constitution sets up which three organs of government?

  • Alegislature, executive and judiciary✓
  • Barmy, navy and air force
  • Cpresident, king and pope
  • Dcentre, state and village only

Working

✓

The three organs are the legislature, executive and judiciary. The armed forces, those leaders, and tiers of government are not the three organs.

Q27

'Checks and balances' arranged by the Constitution mean that the three organs:

  • Acan limit one another so no single organ becomes too powerful✓
  • Bare all merged into one body
  • Cnever affect one another at all
  • Dare each abolished in turn

Working

✓

Checks and balances let each organ limit the others, preventing any from dominating. They are not merged, isolated, or abolished.

Q28

Why does the Constitution keep the three organs separate yet able to check each other?

  • Ato make the government slower for its own sake
  • Bto prevent any one organ from misusing power and to protect citizens✓
  • Cto give the judiciary control over elections
  • Dto remove the need for any laws

Working

✓

Separation with checks guards against the misuse of power and protects citizens. It is not slowness for its own sake, judicial control of elections, or removing laws.

Q29

'Fundamental Rights' are:

  • Agoals the government should aim for but cannot be enforced in court
  • Bduties that every citizen must perform
  • Cpowers belonging only to ministers
  • Dbasic rights guaranteed to citizens that can be enforced through the courts✓

Working

✓

Fundamental Rights are court-enforceable basic rights of citizens. Non-enforceable goals are Directive Principles, citizen responsibilities are Fundamental Duties, and they are not ministers' powers.

Q30

A key feature of Fundamental Rights is that a citizen can:

  • Anever have them questioned by anyone
  • Bgo to court if these rights are violated✓
  • Conly enjoy them on Republic Day
  • Dtrade them away for money

Working

✓

Fundamental Rights are enforceable - a citizen can approach the courts if they are violated. They are not beyond all process, day-limited, or tradeable.

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

Which is an example of a Fundamental Right being enforced?

  • Aa citizen plants trees to protect the environment
  • Bthe government builds new roads
  • CParliament debates a new tax
  • Da citizen asks a court to protect a right the government has violated✓

Working

✓

Enforcing a Fundamental Right means using the courts when a right is violated. Planting trees is a duty; road-building and tax debates are ordinary government work.

Q32

Fundamental Rights differ from Directive Principles mainly because Fundamental Rights:

  • Aare only goals, while Directive Principles are enforceable
  • Bapply only to ministers
  • Cwere added only in 1976
  • Dcan be enforced in court, while Directive Principles cannot✓

Working

✓

Fundamental Rights are court-enforceable; Directive Principles are non-enforceable guidelines. The other options reverse this or confuse them with the Duties added in 1976.

Q33

'Fundamental Duties' are:

  • Arights that can be enforced in court
  • Bpowers reserved for the President
  • Cresponsibilities of citizens, such as respecting the National Flag and protecting the environment✓
  • Dgoals only the government must meet

Working

✓

Fundamental Duties are citizens' responsibilities, like respecting the flag and protecting the environment. They are not enforceable rights, presidential powers, or government-only goals.

Q34

Protecting the environment, as listed in the Constitution, is an example of a:

  • AFundamental Duty of citizens✓
  • BFundamental Right enforceable in court
  • CDirective Principle binding only on the courts
  • Dpower of the army

Working

✓

Protecting the environment is a Fundamental Duty. It is not a court-enforceable right, a court-binding principle, or an army power.

Q35

Fundamental Duties were added to the Constitution in:

  • A1950
  • B1947
  • C1976✓
  • D1992

Working

✓

Fundamental Duties were added by amendment in 1976. 1950 is when the Constitution took effect, 1947 is independence, and 1992 brought Panchayati Raj.

Q36

'Directive Principles of State Policy' are:

  • Agoals and guidelines for the government that cannot be enforced in court✓
  • Brights that citizens can enforce in court
  • Cduties every citizen must perform
  • Dthe powers of the police

Working

✓

Directive Principles are non-enforceable goals guiding government policy. Enforceable rights are Fundamental Rights, citizen responsibilities are Duties, and they are not police powers.

Q37

Although Directive Principles cannot be enforced in court, they:

  • Aguide the government in making its policies and laws✓
  • Bhave no purpose at all
  • Ccan be ignored completely by everyone
  • Doverride the Fundamental Rights

Working

✓

Directive Principles steer the government's policymaking even though courts cannot enforce them. They are not purposeless, ignorable, or superior to Fundamental Rights.

Q38

The main contrast between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is that:

  • Aboth are equally enforceable in court
  • Bboth are merely optional suggestions
  • CRights are enforceable in court while Principles are guiding goals that are not✓
  • DPrinciples are enforceable but Rights are not

Working

✓

Fundamental Rights can be enforced in court; Directive Principles are guiding goals that cannot. The other options confuse or reverse this.

Q39

The Constitution is called a 'living document' because it:

  • Acan never be changed once written
  • Bwas written by living people
  • Ccan be changed through amendments as the country's needs evolve✓
  • Dis read aloud every single day

Working

✓

A 'living document' can be amended as needs change. It is not unchangeable, named for its authors, or a daily reading.

Q40

An 'amendment' to the Constitution is:

  • Aa change to it, rigorously debated in Parliament before being accepted✓
  • Ba yearly election of the President
  • Ca treaty with a foreign country
  • Da court verdict in a single case

Working

✓

An amendment is a change to the Constitution, carefully debated in Parliament. It is not an election, a treaty, or a court verdict.

Q42

  • Aforbidden entirely
  • Bdone secretly by one person
  • Cpossible, but deliberately not easy or careless✓

Rigorous debate means the Constitution can change but only carefully, not easily or carelessly. It is neither forbidden, secret one-person work, nor automatic.

Q43

  • Acannot respond to change at all
  • Bwas copied from another country in 1992
  • Cadapts to new needs through amendment✓
  • Dwas abolished and rewritten

Working

✓

The 1992 Panchayati Raj amendment shows the Constitution adapting to new needs. It is not unresponsive, foreign-copied, or rewritten.

Q44

Q45

Democratic means the people govern through free and fair elections. King-rule, religious rule and wealthy-few rule are other forms.

Q52

✓

Q53

  • Amaking one family the richest in the land
  • Breducing inequality so wealth and opportunity are shared more fairly✓
  • Cabolishing the government
  • Dbanning all trade

Working

✓

Socialist (from Latin socius, 'companion') aims to reduce inequality and share wealth and opportunity fairly. It is not about enriching one family, abolishing government, or banning trade.

Q54

'Justice' in the Preamble means:

  • Apunishment for everyone
  • Bfair treatment for all - social, economic and political✓
  • Cfreedom only for the rich
  • Dthe rule of the strongest

Working

✓

Justice means fair treatment - social, economic and political - so no one is wronged. It is not blanket punishment, rich-only freedom, or might-makes-right.

Q55

'Liberty' in the Preamble is the freedom of every citizen to:

  • Aignore every law of the land
  • Bthink, speak, believe and worship as they choose✓
  • Ctake others' property freely
  • Drule the country alone

Working

✓

Liberty (Latin liber, 'free') is the freedom to think, speak, believe and worship. It is not lawlessness, theft, or sole rule.

Q56

'Equality' in the Preamble means:

  • Aequal status and equal opportunity for all, with no discrimination✓
  • Beveryone must earn exactly the same wage forever
  • Conly equal voting and nothing else

Working

✓

Equality means equal status and opportunity with no discrimination. It is broader than identical wages or voting alone, and certainly not wealth-limited.

Q57

'Fraternity' in the Preamble means a spirit of:

  • Cbrotherhood and unity among all citizens, respecting one another's dignity✓

✓

Fraternity (Latin frater, 'brother') is brotherhood and unity, with respect for each person's dignity. It is not rivalry, family-only loyalty, or foreign obedience.

Q58

Q59
