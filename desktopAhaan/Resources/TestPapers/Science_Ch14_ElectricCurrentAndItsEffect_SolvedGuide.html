Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

In the symbol for a single electric cell, the **longer thin line** and the **shorter thick line** stand for, respectively:

  • Athe positive terminal and the negative terminal✓
  • Bthe negative terminal and the positive terminal
  • Cthe wire and the switch
  • Dtwo equal terminals with no fixed sign

Working

✓

In a cell symbol the longer thin line is the positive (+) terminal and the shorter thick line is the negative (−) terminal. So the pair stands for the positive terminal and the negative terminal.

Q2

A battery is correctly built from three identical cells by joining them so that:

  • Athe positive terminal of one cell touches the positive terminal of the next
  • Ball three negative terminals are joined at one point
  • Cthe positive terminal of one cell joins the negative terminal of the next, in a row✓
  • Dthe cells are stacked with a gap of air between each pair

Working

✓

Cells form a battery only when joined in a row with the positive terminal of one touching the negative terminal of the next. Joining like-to-like (+ to +) makes them oppose; an air gap breaks the loop.

Q3

Two identical cells are placed in a torch the **wrong way** — positive terminal facing positive terminal in the middle. The bulb will:

  • Aglow twice as bright
  • Bglow at normal brightness
  • Cnot glow, because the cells oppose each other✓
  • Dglow, but only half as bright

Working

✓

Putting two cells positive-against-positive makes their pushes oppose and cancel. With equal cells the net push is zero, so no current flows and the bulb does not glow.

Q4

A circuit has one cell and one bulb, and the bulb glows dimly. A student replaces the single cell with a battery of two cells joined end-to-end (+ to −) the right way. The bulb now:

  • Aglows more brightly✓
  • Bglows more dimly
  • Cgoes out completely
  • Dglows exactly as before

Working

✓

A battery of two cells joined correctly (+ to −) gives a larger total push than one cell, so more current flows and the bulb glows more brightly. (The trap is to think "more cells = dimmer" — that is the misconception in Q53.)

Q5

The symbol drawn as a **small circle with a cross (×) inside it** represents a:

  • Acell
  • Bswitch
  • Cbulb✓
  • Dfuse

Working

✓

A small circle with a cross inside it is the standard symbol for a bulb. (A cell is two parallel lines; a fuse is a line with a small box/loop.)

Q6

Which circuit symbol is drawn simply as a **plain straight line**?

  • Aa cell
  • Ba connecting wire✓
  • Ca bulb
  • Dan open switch

Working

✓

A plain straight line is simply a connecting wire. A cell, a bulb and a switch all have extra marks added to the line.

Q7

In a circuit diagram an electrician draws a small gap in the line with a tilted arm lifted away from one contact. This symbol means the switch is:

  • Aclosed, and current flows
  • Bopen, and current does not flow✓
  • Ca fuse that has melted
  • Da battery being charged

Working

✓

A small gap with a tilted arm lifted away from one contact is the symbol for an **open** switch: the loop is broken there, so current does not flow.

Q8

A simple circuit is wired as: cell → wire → bulb → wire → **open switch** → back to cell. With the switch in this state the bulb is:

  • Aglowing brightly
  • Bglowing dimly
  • Cnot glowing✓
  • Dglowing and then slowly fading

Working

✓

The switch in the loop is open, so the circuit is broken and no current can flow. With an open switch the bulb does not glow at all (not "dim", not "fading").

Q9

A torch bulb does **not** light when its switch is pressed. Which of these is **not** a possible reason?

  • Aone of the connecting wires is broken
  • Bthe cell is dead
  • Cthe circuit is a complete closed loop✓
  • Dthe bulb's filament is fused

Working

✓

A broken wire, a dead cell and a fused filament would all stop the bulb glowing — so each is a possible reason. A **complete closed loop** would let the bulb glow, so it is NOT a reason for failure. The "not a possible reason" answer is the closed-loop option.

Q10

For a bulb to glow in a circuit, the essential condition is that:

  • Athe circuit must be open at one point
  • Bthe circuit must form a complete closed loop✓
  • Cthere must be at least three cells
  • Dthe switch must be open

Working

✓

A bulb glows only when current can flow, and current flows only round a complete closed loop. An open circuit or an open switch stops it; the number of cells is not the essential condition.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

A battery is made of four identical 1.5 V cells joined correctly in a row (+ to −). The total voltage pushing current round the circuit is about:

  • A1.5 V
  • B0.375 V
  • C6 V✓
  • D4 V

Working

✓

Four 1.5 V cells joined correctly in a row add up: 4 × 1.5 = 6 V. (Dividing instead of adding gives the trap 0.375 V; using just one cell gives 1.5 V.)

Q12

Two pieces of wire, P and Q, are each connected in turn across the same cell with the same bulb. With P the bulb is bright; with Q the bulb is dim. The most likely explanation is that:

  • Awire Q has a higher resistance than wire P✓
  • Bwire Q is a perfect insulator
  • Cwire P is broken inside
  • Dwire Q has no resistance at all

Working

✓

Same cell and same bulb, but Q gives a dimmer bulb — so less current flows through Q. More opposition to current means higher resistance, so wire Q has the higher resistance. (A perfect insulator or a broken wire would give no glow at all, not a dim one.)

Q13

Which of the following is the best **conductor** of electric current, and would let a bulb glow if used as the connecting link?

  • Aa dry wooden stick
  • Ba rubber band
  • Ca copper wire✓
  • Da plastic ruler

Working

✓

Of the choices, only the copper wire is a good conductor, so only it completes the circuit and lets the bulb glow. Wood, rubber and plastic are insulators.

Q14

A student joins a bulb to a cell using a strip of **dry rubber** in place of one wire. The bulb does not glow because rubber is:

  • Aa good conductor
  • Ba low-melting-point metal
  • Cmagnetic
  • Dan insulator, so the loop is not completed electrically✓

Working

✓

Rubber is an insulator: it does not let current pass, so the loop is not completed electrically and the bulb stays dark.

Q15

Electric current passing through a wire always makes the wire:

  • Acooler than the surroundings
  • Bglow with light, whatever the wire
  • Cwarmer, because of the heating effect of current✓
  • Dlose all of its resistance

Working

✓

Whenever current flows through a wire, the wire warms up — this is the heating effect of current. (Only special high-resistance wires get hot enough to glow; an ordinary wire just warms, but it does heat.)

Q16

Two wires of the **same length and material** carry the same current. Wire X is thin and has high resistance; wire Y is thick and has low resistance. Compared with Y, wire X gets:

  • Acooler
  • Bexactly as hot
  • Chotter✓
  • Dmagnetised but not heated

Working

✓

Same current flows in both. The wire with higher resistance turns more electrical energy into heat, so the thin high-resistance wire X gets hotter than the thick low-resistance wire Y.

Q17

The coil inside an electric room heater is made of **nichrome** rather than copper because nichrome:

  • Ahas a very high resistance and a high melting point, so it heats up and glows without melting✓
  • Bis a much better conductor than copper
  • Cis magnetic
  • Dis cheaper than any other metal

Working

✓

Nichrome is chosen for heater coils because it has a very high resistance (so it produces a lot of heat) and a high melting point (so it can glow red-hot without melting). Copper, being a far better conductor, would barely heat.

Q18

As the current through a given heating wire is increased, the amount of heat produced in the wire each second:

  • Adecreases
  • Bbecomes zero
  • Cstays exactly the same
  • Dincreases✓

Working

✓

More current through the same wire produces more heat each second (the heating effect grows with the current). It does not stay the same or fall.

Q19

The filament of an electric bulb glows white-hot when current flows. It is made of **tungsten** mainly because tungsten:

  • Ais a magnetic metal
  • Bhas an extremely high melting point, so it can get white-hot without melting✓
  • Cis an insulator
  • Dmelts very easily at a low temperature

Working

✓

Tungsten is used for bulb filaments because of its extremely high melting point: it can be heated white-hot to give light without melting away.

Q20

Which set of appliances **all** work mainly on the **heating effect** of electric current?

  • Aelectric iron, toaster, immersion water heater✓
  • Belectric bell, crane magnet, loudspeaker
  • Cceiling fan, mixer, water pump
  • Dcompass, bar magnet, torch

Working

✓

Electric iron, toaster and immersion water heater all work by the heating effect (a high-resistance element gets hot). The bell, crane magnet and loudspeaker use the magnetic effect; fan, mixer and pump use motors (magnetic effect too).

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

An electric toaster and a ceiling fan are both running. The toaster mainly uses the ____ effect of current, while the fan mainly uses the ____ effect.

  • Aheating; magnetic✓
  • Bmagnetic; heating
  • Cheating; heating
  • Dmagnetic; magnetic

Working

✓

A toaster heats food using the heating effect of current; a ceiling fan uses a motor, which runs on the magnetic effect of current. In order: heating, then magnetic.

Q22

An electric fuse is best described as a short piece of wire that:

  • Ais a very good conductor with a high melting point
  • Bincreases the current in the circuit
  • Cstores electric charge for later use
  • Dhas a low melting point and melts to break the circuit when the current is too high✓

Working

✓

A fuse is a short piece of wire with a **low melting point**. When the current is too high it melts and breaks the circuit. It does not store charge or increase current, and it is deliberately NOT a high-melting-point conductor.

Q23

The fuse "protects" the appliances in a circuit. It does this by:

  • Astaying solid and carrying any current, however large
  • Bglowing to warn the user without breaking the circuit
  • Cmelting and **breaking** the circuit when the current rises above a safe value✓
  • Dmaking the current flow faster

Working

✓

The fuse protects by **melting and breaking** the circuit when the current rises above a safe value, cutting off the current before wires overheat. It does not safely carry any current, nor merely glow.

Q24

A house circuit is rated to carry safely up to 5 A. Which fuse should be fitted in it?

  • Aa 5 A fuse, so it melts only if the current goes above the safe limit✓
  • Ba 50 A fuse, so it never melts
  • Ca 0.5 A fuse, so it melts during normal use
  • Dno fuse, to avoid interruptions

Working

✓

The fuse rating should match the safe limit, so fit a 5 A fuse: it carries normal current but melts if the current goes above 5 A. A 50 A fuse never protects; a 0.5 A fuse would melt during normal use; no fuse means no protection.

Q25

Too many high-power appliances are switched on at the same socket. The current grows very large and the fuse melts. This situation is an example of:

  • Aa short circuit caused by bare wires touching
  • Boverloading the circuit✓
  • Cthe magnetic effect of current
  • Dthe cell being connected the wrong way

Working

✓

Too many high-power appliances on one socket draw a very large total current — this is overloading. (A short circuit is specifically bare live and neutral wires touching, which is a different fault.)

Q26

After a fuse has melted ("blown"), the correct action is to:

  • Areplace it with a thicker copper wire so it can never blow again
  • Bfind and remove the fault, then fit a new fuse of the correct rating✓
  • Cjoin the two melted ends together with tape and reuse it
  • Dreplace it with a fuse of a much higher rating to avoid trouble

Working

✓

The right action is to find and remove the fault, then fit a fresh fuse of the **correct** rating. Using a thicker wire, a much higher rating, or taping the ends all defeat the fuse's protective purpose.

Q27

A modern device that does the fuse's job but can simply be **switched back on** after it trips, instead of being replaced, is the:

  • Atungsten filament
  • Belectromagnet
  • Celectric cell
  • DMCB (miniature circuit breaker)✓

Working

✓

A miniature circuit breaker (MCB) does the fuse's job but can simply be switched back on after it trips, instead of being replaced. An electromagnet, a cell and a filament do not protect a circuit this way.

Q28

Someone replaces a blown 5 A fuse with a thick copper wire that will never melt. The danger of doing this is that:

  • Athe bulb will glow more dimly
  • Bthe circuit can now overheat and catch fire without anything breaking the current✓
  • Cthe cells will be charged the wrong way
  • Dthe magnetic effect will become too strong

Working

✓

A wire that never melts removes the protection: if a fault sends a huge current, nothing breaks the circuit, so the wiring can overheat and start a fire. That is the danger of "fuse-replaced-by-thick-wire".

Q29

In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted noticed that a magnetic compass needle placed near a wire **moved** when:

  • Athe wire was simply lying on the table with no current
  • Bthe wire was painted a different colour
  • Can electric current was switched on in the wire✓
  • Dthe wire was made shorter

Working

✓

Oersted saw the compass needle move only when an electric **current was switched on** in the wire. A dead wire, a coat of paint, or a shorter length do not deflect the needle.

Q30

Oersted's experiment showed that an electric current flowing in a wire produces:

  • Aonly heat and never any magnetism
  • Blight only
  • Csound but no magnetism
  • Da magnetic field around the wire✓

Working

✓

Oersted's experiment showed that an electric current in a wire produces a **magnetic field** around the wire — the magnetic effect of current. It is not "only heat", "sound", or "light only".

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

A compass needle lies still below a straight wire. When the current in the wire is switched on, the needle swings aside. If the current is then switched **off**, the needle:

  • Akeeps pointing in the deflected direction for ever
  • Bmelts
  • Cstarts spinning continuously
  • Dswings back to its original (north–south) direction✓

Working

✓

The deflection lasts only while current flows. Switch the current off and the magnetic effect vanishes, so the needle swings back to its normal north–south direction. It does not stay deflected, spin or melt.

Q32

A current-carrying wire is wound into a coil around a soft-iron nail to make an **electromagnet**. Compared with the straight wire alone, the coil-and-iron arrangement gives:

  • Aa much stronger magnetic effect✓
  • Bno magnetic effect at all
  • Ca weaker magnetic effect
  • Donly a heating effect, never magnetism

Working

✓

Winding the wire into a coil around a soft-iron core concentrates the magnetic effect, giving a much stronger magnet than a single straight wire carrying the same current.

Q33

Three things are tried on the same electromagnet, one at a time: (i) more turns of wire, (ii) more current, (iii) keeping an iron core inside the coil. Each of these, on its own, makes the electromagnet:

  • Aweaker
  • Bstronger✓
  • Close its magnetism
  • Dreverse into an insulator

Working

✓

Each of more turns, more current, and keeping an iron core inside the coil makes an electromagnet **stronger**. None of them weakens it or destroys its magnetism.

Q34

The biggest reason an electromagnet is more useful than an ordinary permanent bar magnet for lifting and dropping iron scrap is that an electromagnet:

  • Ais always magnetic and can never be switched off
  • Bworks without any electric current
  • Cis made of plastic
  • Dcan be switched on to lift and switched off to drop the load✓

Working

✓

The key advantage is control: an electromagnet can be switched on to lift a load and switched off to drop it — a permanent magnet cannot be turned off. (It is not "always magnetic", not plastic, and it does need current.)

Q35

A crane at a scrap yard uses a large electromagnet. As the operator drives the crane over a mixed heap, the electromagnet will pick up:

  • Anothing at all, because electromagnets cannot lift things
  • Bevery object in the heap, whatever it is made of
  • Conly the plastic and rubber objects
  • Diron and steel objects, but not the aluminium, copper or plastic✓

Working

✓

An electromagnet attracts only magnetic materials — iron and steel — and not aluminium, copper, plastic, etc. So at a scrap yard it picks up the iron and steel but leaves the rest.

Q36

In a working electromagnet the soft-iron core is essential because the iron:

  • Ablocks the current completely
  • Bkeeps the magnetism for ever, even with no current
  • Cstrongly increases the magnetic effect while current flows, then loses it when current stops✓
  • Dcools the coil down

Working

✓

While current flows, the soft-iron core strongly increases the magnetic effect; when the current stops, soft iron loses its magnetism almost at once. That is exactly why soft iron (not steel) is used and why the magnet can be switched off.

Q37

An electromagnet stops working (loses its magnetism) the moment the:

  • Airon core is painted
  • Belectric current is switched off✓
  • Cnumber of turns is increased
  • Dwire is made of copper

Working

✓

An electromagnet's magnetism depends on the current. The moment the current is switched off, it loses its magnetism. Painting the core, adding turns, or using copper wire do not switch it off.

Q38

In an electric bell, the part that becomes a magnet and pulls the iron arm (armature) towards it when current flows is the:

  • Agong
  • Belectromagnet (coil on the iron core)✓
  • Cspring
  • Dhammer head

Working

✓

In an electric bell the coil wound on the iron core becomes the **electromagnet**; it is the part that becomes a magnet and pulls the iron armature towards it when current flows. The gong, spring and hammer do not become magnets.

Q39

In an electric bell, the moment the electromagnet pulls the armature across and the hammer strikes the gong, the contact at the screw is **broken**. The immediate result is that:

  • Athe current stops, the electromagnet loses its magnetism, and the spring pulls the armature back✓
  • Bthe current grows larger and the bell melts
  • Cthe electromagnet becomes a permanent magnet
  • Dthe bell rings just once and then stops for good

Working

✓

When the armature is pulled across and the contact at the screw breaks, the circuit opens: the current stops, the electromagnet loses its magnetism, and the spring pulls the armature back. (It does not become permanent, and the current does not grow.)

Q40

An electric bell rings continuously (a buzzing sound) rather than giving a single strike because:

  • Athe contact keeps making and breaking, so the cycle repeats again and again✓
  • Bthe battery voltage keeps rising
  • Cthe electromagnet never switches off once started
  • Dthe gong is itself a magnet

Working

✓

As the armature returns, the contact is remade, current flows again, the electromagnet pulls once more — the contact keeps making and breaking, so the strike repeats again and again, giving a continuous buzzing.

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

Which single sequence correctly describes one cycle of an electric bell, starting from the switch being pressed?

  • Ahammer hits gong → current stops → electromagnet pulls armature → spring breaks
  • Bcurrent flows → electromagnet pulls armature → hammer hits gong → contact breaks → current stops → spring returns armature → contact remakes✓
  • Cspring pulls armature → contact breaks → current flows for ever
  • Dgong becomes a magnet → armature melts → bell stops

Working

✓

One full cycle is: current flows → electromagnet pulls the armature → hammer hits the gong → the contact breaks → current stops → the spring returns the armature → the contact remakes (and it all repeats). The other sequences put the steps out of order.

Q42

A current-carrying coil with **no** iron core is wound with the same wire and carries the same current as an identical coil **with** a soft-iron core inside it. The coil with the iron core is:

  • Athe stronger magnet✓
  • Bthe weaker magnet
  • Cexactly equal in strength
  • Dnot a magnet at all

Working

✓

With the same wire and same current, the coil **with** the soft-iron core is the stronger magnet, because the iron core greatly boosts the magnetic effect.

Q43

A student wants to make her electromagnet **stronger** without changing the battery. The most sensible single change is to:

  • Awind many more turns of wire on the same iron core✓
  • Bremove the iron core
  • Cuse fewer turns of wire
  • Dreplace the iron core with a wooden stick

Working

✓

Without changing the battery, the most sensible single change to strengthen the electromagnet is to wind many more turns of wire on the same iron core. Removing the core, using fewer turns, or a wooden core would all weaken it.

Q44

Which statement about the heating and magnetic effects is correct?

  • Aa current produces only a heating effect, never a magnetic one
  • Ba current produces only a magnetic effect, never a heating one
  • Ca current passing through a wire produces both a heating effect and a magnetic effect at the same time✓
  • Dheat and magnetism are produced only after the current is switched off

Working

✓

A current flowing through a wire produces **both** a heating effect (the wire warms) and a magnetic effect (a magnetic field forms) at the same time — they are two effects of the one current, not one or the other, and they occur while the current flows.

Q45

Which device makes use of the **magnetic effect** of electric current?

  • Aan electric toaster
  • Ban electric bell✓
  • Can immersion water heater
  • Dan incandescent filament bulb

Working

✓

An electric bell works through an electromagnet, so it uses the **magnetic** effect. A toaster, immersion heater and filament bulb all use the heating effect.

Q46

A bulb is joined to a single cell and glows. A second identical cell is now added in the **same direction** (+ to −), making a two-cell battery. Compared with one cell, the bulb is now:

  • Abrighter, because the two cells together push a larger current✓
  • Bdimmer, because two cells share the work
  • Coff, because two cells cancel out
  • Dunchanged, because cells make no difference

Working

✓

Adding a second identical cell in the same direction (+ to −) makes a two-cell battery whose pushes add, driving a larger current, so the bulb glows brighter. (Cells "sharing the work" or "cancelling out" are misconceptions.)

Q47

Two cells are joined positive-to-positive and negative-to-negative (facing against each other) before being put in a circuit with a bulb. The bulb:

  • Aglows extra brightly
  • Bglows with twice the current
  • Cglows normally
  • Ddoes not glow, because the cells push against each other and cancel out✓

Working

✓

Joined positive-to-positive and negative-to-negative, the two cells push against each other and cancel out, so effectively no current flows and the bulb does not glow.

Q48

A circuit is drawn as: cell → closed switch → bulb → back to cell. The bulb glows. A student then opens the switch only. The bulb goes out because:

  • Athe cell has been used up
  • Bthe switch turned into a fuse
  • Cthe bulb's resistance dropped to zero
  • Dthe loop is now broken, so no current can flow✓

Working

✓

Opening the switch breaks the loop, so no current can flow and the bulb goes out. (The cell is not used up, and the resistance does not drop to zero; the switch does not become a fuse.)

Q49

A nichrome heater coil and a copper connecting wire carry the **same current**. The nichrome coil becomes red-hot while the copper wire stays cool mainly because:

  • Anichrome has a much higher resistance than copper, so far more heat is produced in it✓
  • Bcopper carries no current
  • Cnichrome is magnetic and copper is not
  • Dcopper has a lower melting point than nichrome

Working

✓

With the same current, the wire of higher resistance produces far more heat. Nichrome has a much higher resistance than copper, so the nichrome coil glows red-hot while the copper wire stays cool. (Copper does carry the current; the difference is resistance, not magnetism or melting point.)

Q50

Which of these correctly pairs a circuit component with its job?

  • Aswitch — to make or break the circuit✓
  • Bbulb — to break the circuit when current is high
  • Cfuse — to store charge
  • Dcell — to measure the current

Working

✓

The correct pairing is switch — to make or break the circuit. (A bulb gives light, not over-current protection; a fuse breaks the circuit, it does not store charge; a cell supplies energy, it does not measure current.)

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

An immersion rod (used to heat a bucket of water) and an electric bell are compared. They depend, respectively, on the ____ effect and the ____ effect of current.

  • Amagnetic; heating
  • Bheating; magnetic✓
  • Cheating; heating
  • Dmagnetic; magnetic

Working

✓

An immersion rod heats water using the heating effect; an electric bell rings using an electromagnet, which is the magnetic effect. In order: heating, then magnetic.

Q52

A circuit is drawn with the cell symbol's longer line on the left. Current in the external wires is taken to flow out of the:

  • Ashorter thick line (negative terminal) round to the longer line
  • Bmiddle of the cell symbol
  • Clonger thin line (positive terminal) round to the shorter line✓
  • Dbulb towards the switch only

Working

✓

In the external wires, current is taken to flow out of the positive terminal — the longer thin line — round the circuit back to the shorter (negative) line. It does not start from the middle or from the bulb.

Q53

A child claims: "Adding a second cell the right way always makes a bulb dimmer." This claim is:

  • Acorrect, because more cells slow the current
  • Bwrong; correctly adding a cell increases the push and makes the bulb brighter✓
  • Ccorrect only for tungsten bulbs
  • Dwrong; the bulb always goes out

Working

✓

The claim is wrong. Adding a cell correctly (+ to −) increases the total push, so more current flows and the bulb gets **brighter**, not dimmer. (Connecting it the wrong way is what reduces the push.)

Q54

The reason a thin fuse wire melts before the thick household wiring overheats is that, for the same large current, the thin fuse wire:

  • Acarries no current at all
  • Bhas a higher resistance and a low melting point, so it heats up fastest and melts first✓
  • Cis a magnet
  • Dis a better conductor than the household wire

Working

✓

For the same large current, the thin fuse wire has a higher resistance and a low melting point, so it heats up the fastest and melts first — before the thicker household wiring can overheat. That is why the fuse is the deliberate weak link.

Q55

A safety claim says: "A fuse protects a circuit by **conducting** the extra current safely away without ever breaking." This claim is:

  • Acorrect — that is exactly how a fuse works
  • Bwrong — a fuse increases the current instead
  • Ccorrect only for MCBs
  • Dwrong — a fuse protects precisely by **melting and breaking** the circuit when the current is too high✓

Working

✓

The claim is wrong. A fuse does not conduct the extra current away; it protects precisely by **melting and breaking** the circuit when the current is too high. (It also does not increase the current.)

Q56

Which observation would best convince you that a current is flowing in a hidden wire, **without** cutting the wire open?

  • Aa nearby compass needle deflects when the supply is switched on✓
  • Bthe wire changes colour
  • Cthe wire becomes heavier
  • Dthe wire becomes longer

Working

✓

A current produces a magnetic field, which deflects a nearby compass needle. So if a compass needle deflects when the supply is switched on, current is flowing — and you never had to cut the wire. Colour, weight and length do not change with current.

Q57

A coil electromagnet lifts at most 5 paper clips with one cell. With **two cells** joined correctly (more current) and **more turns** added, the number of paper clips it can lift will most likely:

  • Aincrease✓
  • Bdecrease
  • Cstay exactly the same
  • Ddrop to zero

Working

✓

More current (from two cells) and more turns both strengthen the electromagnet, so it can lift **more** paper clips than before. The count increases, not decreases.

Q58

In an electric bell, the role of the **springy metal strip** carrying the armature is to:

  • Akeep the electromagnet switched on permanently
  • Bpull the armature back so the contact is remade and the cycle repeats✓
  • Cmelt when the current is too high
  • Dmeasure the voltage of the cell

A tiny break in a wire leaves a gap in the loop, making it an **open** circuit: no current flows, so the bulb stays dark even with a closed switch and a fresh cell. (A break is not a closed circuit and not a short circuit.)
