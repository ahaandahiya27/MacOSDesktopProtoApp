Worked Solutions

1. (B) Two independent axes apply. Purpose: she grows to feed her family and sells nothing, so it is subsistence, not commercial. Effort per area: she pours heavy labour and water onto one hectare, so it is intensive, not extensive. 'Commercial and intensive' wrongly flips the purpose to selling. 'Subsistence and extensive' wrongly calls heavy per-area effort thin/extensive. 'Commercial and extensive' is wrong on both counts.

2. (C) Intensive (and extensive) farming is defined by how heavily land is worked per unit area, not by purpose, so pairing 'intensive' with the eat-or-sell axis is the mismatch. Subsistence and commercial are correctly defined by purpose (eating vs selling). Extensive is correctly defined by effort per unit of land. Only the intensive pairing crosses the wrong axis.

3. (B) Cultivating fruits, vegetables and flowers is horticulture, and selling the whole output for cash makes the purpose commercial, so both apply. Subsistence is wrong because she keeps nothing to eat. 'Cereal farming' is wrong because she grows no grains. 'Terrace farming' describes step-cut hillsides, which is not stated, and subsistence is again wrong.

4. (C) Subsistence versus commercial is decided by purpose, and purpose can change year to year, so the same unchanged field can earn either label. Soil type does not flip annually. Season (kharif/rabi) is a different axis and does not set the subsistence/commercial label. Intensity (intensive/extensive) is a separate axis from purpose, so it is not a synonym for subsistence/commercial.

5. (D) She supplies the labour input that Indian farming relies on, and in rural India most such work is done by women. She is not a subsistence farmer because she keeps none of the crop, and not a commercial farmer because she owns and sells nothing. Far from being outside agriculture, landless labourers are central to the harvest, so 'irrelevant because landless' is false.

6. (A) The chapter treats the two as suited to different situations, not one as universally superior: commercial farming brings cash but also cost and market risk, while subsistence farming guarantees a family's own food. 'Selling always beats eating' ignores food security. Subsistence farms need not out-produce commercial ones. The two differ in purpose and are not identical, so the comparison is meaningful.

7. (D) Spreading limited effort thinly over a large area for a low per-hectare yield is extensive, and depending on the rains makes it rain-fed, so both apply. 'Intensive' is wrong because effort per area is low. 'Irrigated' is wrong because he waits on rain rather than supplying water artificially. Each alternative gets at least one axis wrong.

8. (A) Indian farming varies along several axes together, soil, climate, season, water source and purpose, so practices are far from uniform. Saying every farm uses identical seeds and tools, or that all farms are commercial and irrigated, merely restates uniformity in another form, which is false. And farming has changed greatly, including the Green Revolution, so 'never changed' is also wrong.

9. (D) After spring comes the short hot zaid window, then the southwest monsoon brings kharif, and after the rains the cool rabi winter follows: zaid -> kharif -> rabi. Starting with rabi or placing zaid after kharif breaks the calendar that begins just after spring, and putting kharif first ignores the spring starting point.

10. (D) Sowing with the June monsoon and harvesting as the rains end is the kharif pattern, and kharif crops can live on monsoon rain. Rabi crops grow in the dry winter and usually need irrigation, the reverse timing. Zaid fills the short hot gap, not the monsoon. The last option pairs the wrong season label with the right water source.

11. (A) Kharif crops grow during the rainy monsoon and can live on rain, whereas rabi crops grow in the dry winter when little rain falls, so they need artificial watering. Rabi crops need water like any crop; irrigation supplies it rather than tricking them. The monsoon falls in the rainy season, not winter. And kharif crops there rely largely on monsoon rain, not canals.

12. (C) The northeast monsoon brings rain mainly from October to December, so that is when she can count on it. June to September is the southwest monsoon, a different system. March to May is the hot pre-monsoon stretch. Indian rainfall is concentrated in monsoon periods, not spread evenly through the year.

13. (B) A rain-fed farm depends on the monsoon as its only water source, so a weak monsoon hurts it most; an irrigated farm can draw on wells and canals and is buffered. Tube wells and canals do not stop working merely because the monsoon is weak. Growing the same crop does not equalise their water security. And less rain cannot help a rain-fed crop grow better.

14. (B) Wheat is matched to the cool, comparatively dry winter and is harvested in spring, so sowing it in the hot, wet monsoon mismatches its needs and the crop suffers. Crops are not season-neutral, which is why kharif, rabi and zaid exist. Renaming it kharif or zaid does not change wheat's growing requirements, and no crop ripens in two weeks.

15. (A) Irrigation supplies water artificially, so it cushions farms against a weak or late monsoon and directly reduces monsoon-driven failures. Banning kharif crops would waste the monsoon's rain. Pushing more reliance on rainfall would increase vulnerability, not reduce it. And monocropping the same crop yearly drains soil nutrients and does nothing for water supply.

16. (C) The short, hot stretch between the rabi harvest and kharif sowing is the zaid season, so a quick crop there is a zaid crop. A rabi crop occupies the cool winter, and a kharif crop is sown with the southwest monsoon, neither of which is the in-between window. A multi-year perennial is the opposite of a quick short-season crop.

17. (D) The false statement misnames rabi: rabi is the cool-winter crop, not the monsoon crop sown in June. The other three are accurate: kharif is sown with the southwest monsoon in the hot rainy season; rabi grows in the cool drier winter and is harvested in spring; and zaid is the short hot in-between crop.

18. (D) Alluvial soil is fertile silt deposited by rivers, carrying nutrients and retaining moisture, which is why it yields more. It is river-borne silt, not wind-blown desert sand. Even fertile soil supplies nutrients to plants, which is precisely why it is fertile. And alluvium makes a productive bed for roots rather than blocking them.

19. (A) Humus is decomposed plant and animal matter; mixing it in supplies nutrients and improves the soil's ability to hold moisture, so the crop does better. It helps retain water rather than removing it, it loosens and enriches soil rather than hardening it into rock, and it supports rather than destroys the living matter that keeps soil healthy.

20. (A) Crop rotation works because different crops use and replenish different nutrients, so no single nutrient is exhausted and the soil stays balanced, which is why the next cereal does better. Rotation means changing the crop grown, not spinning the field. It conserves nutrients rather than stripping them all at once, and it keeps the field productive rather than ending cultivation.

21. (C) Terrace farming cuts a slope into level steps so rainwater is held on each step and soil is not washed away, allowing crops on steep ground. The steps still need soil and water; they manage them better. They conserve soil rather than turning land to sand, and every crop still needs seeds, so terracing cannot remove that need.

22. (A) A purely rain-fed farm depends entirely on the monsoon, so a weak or late monsoon is its chief threat. The other risks all assume irrigation equipment she does not have: there is no canal to flood the field, no pump to break and no drip line to clog. That is exactly why irrigation, where available, makes a farm less vulnerable.

23. (A) A bund is a low earthen embankment that traps water in the field and reduces soil erosion, which is why farmers build them for water and soil management. It is not a seed, because it does not grow; not a chemical, because it does not kill pests; and not a crop, because it is an earthen structure, not a plant.

24. (B) The mix of ancient tanks and bunds with modern canals and wells shows that storing and supplying water for crops has mattered across the ages, addressed by both old and new means. It was not invented in the Green-Revolution era, the very existence of these structures proves farmers thought hard about water, and water management matters in seasonally rained regions too, not only where it never rains.

25. (A) The honest comparison is a trade-off: saved seeds are cheap and locally suited, while bought high-yield seeds can raise yields but bring extra cost and usually more water and chemicals, which is why many farmers blend the two. Saved seeds do not always out-yield modern ones, bought seeds are not free and often demand more inputs, and seeds clearly differ, so the choice does matter.

26. (B) Blending traditional and modern methods lets a farmer earn decent yields while guarding the soil and limiting costs, which is the balance the chapter highlights. The aim is to protect the land, not harm it; there is no law banning all modern tools; and the point is to combine the strengths of both approaches, not to disprove that chemicals can raise yields, which they can.

27. (B) The Green Revolution combined high-yielding seeds, irrigation, chemical fertilisers and pesticides, and machinery; it concerned farming inputs and yields, not how goods were traded. A switch to barter is not part of it, so it is the odd one out. The other three were all core components of the package.

28. (B) The Green Revolution's national-scale success was raising food-grain output so India could feed itself, though it also degraded soil, depleted groundwater, polluted and harmed health. Saved seeds do not always out-produce high-yield seeds. The revolution raised, not lowered, output. And both approaches clearly affect how much food is produced, so 'no effect' is false.

29. (C) Drawing water from underground faster than it is replenished lowers the water table, so heavy pumping depletes groundwater and threatens future supply. Pumping removes water, so it cannot raise the table or flood the region, it clearly does affect groundwater rather than leaving it unchanged, and it cannot manufacture new underground rivers.

30. (C) Output did rise, so the assertion is true, but the claim of no harmful side effects is false: the revolution degraded soil, depleted groundwater, polluted soil and water, harmed health and pushed many farmers into debt. So the reason is wrong while the assertion stands, which rules out the options calling the assertion false or the reason true.

31. (B) The pattern ties together: intensive Green-Revolution methods lifted yields but also drained soil and groundwater and raised input costs, so the same approach produced both the gains and the later troubles. The problems are clearly linked to the method, the region did adopt the revolution, and the debt traces to the high cost of seeds and chemicals, not to low yields.

32. (A) Sustained heavy chemical use pollutes soil and water and wears down the soil's natural fertility, the chain the chapter describes. It does not make soil richer and cleaner, the reverse of what happens; chemicals do not simply vanish without effect; and they have no power to increase rainfall.

33. (B) The accurate verdict is mixed: yields rose, but at the cost of degraded soil, falling groundwater, chemical pollution, health harms and farmer debt. It did not reduce the food supply, since output rose; it clearly had measurable effects; and it did not benefit soil and groundwater, which were among the very things it harmed.

34. (D) The defining difference is that the organic farm shuns synthetic fertilisers and pesticides and uses natural inputs instead. Organic wheat still grows in soil, and the organic farm does produce wheat, so the no-soil and no-wheat claims are wrong. Using more chemical pesticide would make it the chemical-intensive farm, not the organic one.

35. (D) Reducing chemical inputs and heavy irrigation lessens water pollution and slows groundwater depletion, so over time groundwater can improve. Organic farming still uses water and soil, so 'no water, no soil' is wrong; natural inputs do not refill aquifers overnight; and organic crops do grow and use water, just more sustainably.

36. (B) Organic and natural farming are framed as a response because they consciously cut chemical inputs after the Green Revolution showed the damage those inputs caused. Older traditional farming did not rely on heavy chemicals, the chemical era is the new thing being responded to, organic methods are not a 1960s invention, and natural inputs like manure and neem have been used for ages.

37. (A) Sustainable farming means meeting present needs while protecting soil, water and the capacity to farm in the future. Using up resources as fast as possible, or ignoring the future to chase this year's profit, is the opposite of sustainability, and farming that produces no food today fails the 'meets today's needs' part of the definition.

38. (B) Practices that protect and maintain soil health, such as rotation, adding humus and stopping erosion, are together called soil conservation. Irrigation is about supplying water, not protecting soil; mechanisation refers to using machines, not conserving soil; and exporting soil is not a farming practice at all.

39. (A) A sensible middle path combines natural methods with measured modern inputs, balancing yield, soil health and cost while she transitions, the blended approach the chapter endorses. Piling on chemicals worsens the long-term harm she is escaping, stopping farming sacrifices her livelihood, and ignoring soil health to chase yield repeats the Green Revolution's mistake.

40. (C) The ending '-cide' means 'killer', so a pesticide is a substance that kills crop pests, which corrects the teacher's claim. '-cide' does not mean water, seed or garden, so the other readings of the word are all false.

41. (D) Hydroponics grows plants in nutrient-rich water, feeding minerals straight to the roots, which proves plants can grow without soil. Plants do need nutrients, so 'no nutrients' is false; they are not restricted to bare rock; and they certainly are not limited to deserts.

42. (C) Because hydroponics grows plants in nutrient-rich water rather than soil, it is valuable for urban farming and places where the soil is poor. It needs water, so it cannot work without any water; it supplies nutrients through the water rather than removing the need for them; and it is a modern technique, not one limited to ancient times.

43. (C) The chapter stresses that in rural India most agricultural operations are carried out by women, so a survey of hands-on field work would mostly find women. It is not men only, nor machines only, since much of the labour is manual, and it is local rural labour rather than imported foreign workers.

44. (C) If much of women's work is unpaid and unrecorded, the records understate it, yet women still perform a large share of the real work, so their contribution is undercounted, not small. Unpaid work is still genuine farm labour, so 'only paid male labour counts' is wrong, and farming plainly still depends heavily on human labour.

45. (D) The programme fails because a large share of farm work is done by women and by landless labourers who may own no land, so assuming every farmer is a male owner leaves them out. It is not because women contribute nothing, which is false, nor because all farmers are wealthy owners, and farm labour is still essential, so support for workers matters.

46. (D) The correction is twofold: Indian farming is far from fully mechanised, and women continue to do most of the actual field work, so their role has not ended. Saying machines do everything overstates mechanisation, claiming women never worked the fields contradicts the chapter, and asserting men always did all the work is the very misconception being challenged.

47. (C) If women do most of the work, then aiming training, tools and support at them reaches the people actually farming, which is the sensible response. Withdrawing support from farms with women, or assuming women cannot benefit from better tools, ignores the very workers carrying the load, and replacing them with imported labour neither follows from the fact nor reflects how rural farming operates.

48. (D) Machines speed up heavy tasks and reduce manual labour, most usefully on larger holdings, which is the core benefit of mechanisation. It does not remove the need for soil, seeds or water, which crops still require; it does not guarantee wealth, since machines also cost money; and it does not restrict farming to one season.

49. (C) Heavy machinery fits larger holdings and can be costly or impractical on very small, fragmented plots, so the small farmer's caution is reasonable. Mechanisation is not equally easy and cheap for every farm, it is certainly used in India so it is not impossible, and it reduces rather than wholly removes the need for human labour, which farms still rely on.

50. (A) Earning little despite a good harvest because prices crash is the problem of uncertain market prices, a real challenge for farmers. It is not a weak monsoon, since the harvest was good, nor barren soil, since the field produced well, and the issue is the low price, not a total absence of buyers.

51. (C) Erratic rains, heatwaves and shifting seasons are signs of climate change, which makes the timing of farming unreliable. It is not an excess of irrigation water, nor soil becoming too fertile, which would not disrupt planning, and it is not a sudden loss of farm labour, which is a separate matter.

52. (D) Repeated division shrinks and scatters holdings, which makes efficient farming and the use of machinery harder, a real challenge the chapter notes. It does the opposite of enlarging farms, it does not improve soil fertility, and it cannot guarantee higher crop prices, which depend on the market.

53. (B) Today's farmers face shrinking, fragmented land, climate change, unstable prices, water stress and debt, and naming these is accurate. The other options are unreal: farmers do not enjoy abundant land and perfect prices, they clearly do face difficulties, and India grows a wide range of crops, so 'no crops' is false.

54. (C) The fair lesson is a trade-off across time: chemical-boosted yields give short-term output gains while imposing long-term costs on soil, water and health, exactly what the region shows. Saying it brings only benefits ignores the documented harms, only costs ignores the real rise in output, and it is plainly connected to the soil and water it degraded.

55. (D) Choosing a natural input like neem over a synthetic pesticide is a hallmark of organic farming. The Green-Revolution package is defined by chemical inputs, the reverse of this choice. Selling crops abroad is unrelated to which pesticide is used, and she is still controlling pests, just by natural means rather than abandoning the effort.

56. (B) The accurate summary holds three threads together: the Green Revolution's record was mixed, women do most rural farm work, and many farmers combine old and new methods. Calling the Green Revolution purely good ignores its harms and misnames the typical worker, claiming all farming is identical denies its real variety, and saying women do no work while machines do everything contradicts the chapter on both counts.

57. (B) Farming evidence reaching back to the 7th-8th millennium BCE and persisting to the present shows agriculture is an ancient, continuous Indian tradition. It is therefore not a recent invention, not wholly borrowed from abroad, and certainly not abandoned, since it continues today.

58. (A) India has several major soils, alluvial, black, red and others, suited to different crops, so what grows well varies by place. Saying every field has identical soil restates the teacher's error. Soil type clearly affects which crops thrive, and the variety of soils is precisely why many different crops grow across the country, not just one.

59. (A) Rice grown in the wet monsoon season is a kharif crop, while wheat grown in the cool dry winter is a rabi crop, so the pairing is kharif then rabi. Reversing them mislabels rice as rabi and wheat as kharif. Zaid is the short hot in-between crop and fits neither rice in the monsoon nor wheat in winter.

60. (D) Women are central to rural farming because they do the majority of the physical field tasks, from sowing to harvest. It is not because they own most farmland, since land is often not in their name, nor because they alone run heavy machinery. And they are very much in the fields doing the work, not managing only from a distance.
