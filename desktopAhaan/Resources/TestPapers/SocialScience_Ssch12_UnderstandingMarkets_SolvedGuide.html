Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

A 'market' is best described as:

  • Aonly a large building with many shops
  • Bonly an online shopping app
  • Conly a government office
  • Dany arrangement, physical or online, where people buy and sell goods and services✓

Working

✓

A market is any arrangement where buying and selling happen, physical or online. It is not limited to a building, an app, or a government office.

Q2

The claim 'a market is always a physical place you can walk into' is wrong because:

  • Amarkets exist only inside malls
  • Bonly village haats are real markets
  • Ca market can be an online app or website too - what makes it a market is the exchange, not the building✓
  • Dmarkets never involve money

Working

✓

A market is defined by buying and selling at a price, which can happen online. It is not mall-only, haat-only, or money-free.

Q3

The 16th-century Hampi Bazaar is used in the chapter as an example of a:

  • Amodern shopping app
  • Bgovernment tax office
  • Cgrand historical market✓
  • Dcertification mark

Working

✓

Hampi Bazaar illustrates a glorious historical market. It is not an app, a tax office, or a certification mark.

Q4

Markets exist mainly because people:

  • Aare forbidden to make anything at home
  • Bmust obey the government's shopping orders
  • Cwant to avoid all trade
  • Dhave needs and wants they cannot all produce themselves✓

Working

✓

Markets exist so people can obtain the goods and services they need and want but cannot all make themselves. They are not about a home-making ban, government orders, or avoiding trade.

Q5

The difference between a 'need' and a 'want' is best shown by:

  • Aboth are exactly the same thing
  • Bneeds are luxuries and wants are essentials
  • Conly the government decides which is which
  • Dfood and water are needs, while a video game is a want✓

Working

✓

Needs are essentials like food and water; wants are extras like a video game. The terms are not identical, not reversed, and not government-decided.

Q6

Every market must have at least:

  • Aa king, an army and a temple
  • Bgold coins and nothing else
  • Cbuyers, sellers and an agreed price✓
  • Da government inspector present at all times

Working

✓

A market needs buyers, sellers and an agreed price. A king/army/temple, gold coins alone, or a permanent inspector are not the defining features.

Q7

The 'price' in a market is:

  • Aalways fixed by the buyer alone
  • Bthe cost of building the shop
  • Cthe amount at which a buyer will buy and a seller will sell✓
  • Da tax paid to the government

Working

✓

Price is the amount at which buyer and seller agree to trade. It is not buyer-only, the shop's building cost, or a tax.

Q8

If a market has goods and sellers but no one willing to buy, it lacks a key feature:

  • Abuyers✓
  • Bsellers
  • Cgoods
  • Da building

Working

✓

Without willing buyers the market is missing one of its three essentials. Sellers and goods are present, and a building is not essential.

Q9

'Negotiation' (bargaining) in a market is:

  • Athe government setting one fixed price for all
  • Ba tax added at the till
  • Cthe study of old coins
  • Dbuyer and seller discussing until they agree on a price✓

Working

✓

Negotiation is bargaining between buyer and seller to agree a price. It is not government price-fixing, a tax, or numismatics.

Q10

A 'just right' price is one that is:

  • Aas low as absolutely possible, even at a loss to the seller
  • Bhigh enough for the seller to profit and low enough for the buyer to be willing to pay✓
  • Cas high as the seller can possibly demand
  • Dalways set by the buyer alone

Working

✓

The 'just right' price balances the seller's profit and the buyer's willingness. It is not rock-bottom (loss-making), sky-high, or buyer-set.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

The belief 'the lower the price, the better; a seller should always sell as cheap as possible' is wrong because:

  • Alow prices are illegal
  • Btoo low a price means no profit or a loss, so the seller may stop selling✓
  • Cbuyers dislike low prices
  • Dthe government bans cheap goods

Working

✓

A price too low leaves the seller with no profit or a loss, so they may quit selling - bad for everyone. Low prices are not illegal, disliked by buyers, or banned.

Q12

A buyer offers 40 rupees and the seller asks 60 rupees; they settle at 50 rupees. This settling is an example of:

  • Aa government subsidy
  • Bnegotiation reaching a 'just right' price✓
  • Can export deal
  • Da certification mark

Working

✓

Bargaining from 40 and 60 to 50 rupees is negotiation arriving at a 'just right' price. It is not a subsidy, an export, or a mark.

Q13

'Demand' is:

  • Athe quantity sellers are willing to sell
  • Bthe cost of making a good
  • Cthe quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at a particular price✓
  • Dthe tax on a good

Working

✓

Demand is what consumers will and can buy at a price. What sellers will sell is supply; making-cost and tax are different.

Q14

'Supply' is:

  • Athe quantity consumers wish to buy
  • Bthe quantity sellers are willing and able to sell at a particular price✓
  • Cthe price printed on a pack
  • Dthe government's subsidy

Working

✓

Supply is what sellers will and can sell at a price. What consumers want is demand; the printed price is MRP; government help is a subsidy.

Q15

The claim 'when more people want something, its price falls' is wrong because:

  • Amore demand always lowers the price
  • Bdemand has no effect at all on price
  • Conly the government sets all prices
  • Dwhen many buyers chase a limited supply, the price usually RISES✓

Working

✓

Strong demand against limited supply tends to push price up, not down. Demand does affect price, and prices are usually not all government-set.

Q16

In general, demand pushes price ____ and plentiful supply pushes price ____.

  • Adown ... up
  • Bup ... up
  • Cup ... down✓
  • Ddown ... down

Working

✓

Demand pushes price up while plentiful supply pushes it down; the 'just right' price is where they meet. The other pairings are wrong.

Q17

The 'just right' market price is found:

  • Awhere demand and supply meet✓
  • Bwhere demand is zero
  • Cwhere supply is zero
  • Dwhere the government forbids all sales

Working

✓

The settling price is where demand and supply balance. Zero demand or supply, or a sales ban, would not yield a working price.

Q18

A sudden shortage of onions, with demand unchanged, will most likely make their price:

  • Afall to zero
  • Brise✓
  • Cstay exactly the same forever
  • Dbecome illegal

Working

✓

Less supply against steady demand pushes the price up. It does not fall to zero, stay fixed, or become illegal.

Q19

The claim 'if there is no physical shop, there is no real market' is wrong because:

  • Aonline stores never have prices
  • Bonly buildings can host trade
  • Conline sellers are not real sellers
  • Dan online store with buyers, sellers and prices is just as much a market as a haat or mall✓

Working

✓

A market is wherever buyers and sellers meet at a price, including online. Online stores do have prices and real sellers, and trade needs no building.

Q20

An example of an 'online market' is:

  • Aa shopping app where many sellers offer goods to buyers✓
  • Bthe 16th-century Hampi Bazaar
  • Ca weekly village haat
  • Da city shopping mall

Working

✓

A shopping app is an online market. Hampi Bazaar, a haat and a mall are physical markets.

Q21

A 'haat' is:

  • Aa permanent online app
  • Ba market open every single day
  • Ca government tax office
  • Da periodic rural market that gathers on fixed days of the week✓

Working

✓

A haat is a periodic rural market held on fixed days. It is not an app, a daily market, or a tax office.

Q22

A 'domestic' market is one in which trade happens:

  • Abetween two or more different countries
  • Bwithin a single country✓
  • Conly on the internet
  • Donly in ancient times

Working

✓

A domestic market is trade within one country; an international market crosses borders. It is not internet-only or ancient-only.

Q23

'Export' means:

  • Abuying goods from another country and bringing them home
  • Bselling goods within one's own village
  • Cstoring goods in a warehouse
  • Dselling goods produced in one country to a buyer in another country✓

Working

✓

Export is selling a country's goods to a buyer abroad. Buying from abroad is import; village selling and storage are neither.

Q24

'Import' means:

  • Aselling one's goods to another country
  • Bbuying goods from other countries and bringing them into one's own✓
  • Cmaking goods in a factory
  • Dholding a periodic rural market

Working

✓

Import is bringing in goods from abroad. Selling abroad is export; making goods is production; a periodic rural market is a haat.

Q25

India buying crude oil from another country is an example of:

  • Aan import✓
  • Ban export
  • Ca domestic sale
  • Da subsidy

Working

✓

Bringing oil in from abroad is an import. Selling abroad is export, trade within India is domestic, and a subsidy is government support.

Q26

A 'wholesaler' is a trader who:

  • Abuys goods in bulk from producers and sells them to retailers✓
  • Bsells small quantities directly to final consumers
  • Cuses up the goods as the final buyer
  • Dinspects goods for the government

Working

✓

A wholesaler buys in bulk from producers and sells to retailers. Selling small amounts to consumers is a retailer's job; the final buyer is a consumer; an inspector is a regulator.

Q27

A 'retailer' is a shopkeeper who:

  • Abuys in bulk from producers to resell to shops
  • Bmanufactures the goods
  • Csells goods in small quantities to final consumers for use✓
  • Dexports goods abroad

Working

✓

A retailer sells small quantities to consumers. Bulk-buying for resale to shops is a wholesaler, making goods is a producer, and selling abroad is exporting.

Q28

The main difference between a wholesaler and a retailer is that a wholesaler:

  • Adeals in bulk and sells to retailers, while a retailer sells small amounts to consumers✓
  • Bsells to consumers, while a retailer buys in bulk
  • Cmakes the goods, while a retailer exports them
  • Dinspects goods, while a retailer taxes them

Working

✓

Wholesalers handle bulk and sell to retailers; retailers sell small quantities to consumers. The other options reverse or confuse these roles.

Q29

Buying a single notebook from a neighbourhood shop is buying from a:

  • Aretailer✓
  • Bwholesaler
  • Cproducer
  • Dexporter

Working

✓

A neighbourhood shop selling you one notebook is a retailer. A wholesaler sells in bulk, a producer makes goods, and an exporter sells abroad.

Q30

A 'distributor' mainly:

  • Auses up the goods as the final consumer
  • Bprints the MRP on packs
  • Csupplies goods from manufacturers and wholesalers to retailers, especially across distances✓
  • Dsets the country's tax rates

Working

✓

A distributor moves goods from makers and wholesalers to retailers, often over distances. The final user is a consumer; MRP and tax-setting are different.

✓

  • Dexporter

Working

✓

Combining many sellers' offers in one app is an aggregator's role. A producer makes goods, a wholesaler sells bulk, and an exporter sells abroad.

Q33

The usual chain from maker to user runs:

  • Aconsumer, then retailer, then producer, then wholesaler
  • Bretailer, then producer, then consumer, then wholesaler
  • Cproducer, then wholesaler, then distributor, then retailer, then consumer✓
  • Dconsumer, then producer, then retailer, then wholesaler

Working

✓

Goods flow producer to wholesaler to distributor to retailer to consumer. The other orders scramble the chain.

Q34

In the supply chain, the 'consumer' is the:

  • Atrader who buys in bulk to resell
  • Bfactory that makes the good
  • Cperson who finally buys and uses the good✓
  • Dapp that lists many sellers

Working

✓

The consumer is the final buyer and user. The bulk reseller is a wholesaler, the maker is a producer, and the listing app is an aggregator.

Q35

The Surat textile market is used in the chapter to illustrate:

  • Aan online aggregator app
  • Ba government tax office
  • Ca certification mark for food
  • Da real-life supply chain moving cloth from producers to consumers✓

Working

✓

Surat's textile market shows a living supply chain for cloth. It is not an app, a tax office, or a food mark.

Q36

In the Surat textile example, the goods pass through several hands before reaching you because:

  • Aeach step (bulk buying, distribution, retailing) moves the cloth closer and in usable quantities to the buyer✓
  • Bthe law requires exactly five owners
  • Cthe cloth must be taxed at each separate shop
  • Dconsumers are forbidden to buy directly

Working

✓

Each link adds value by moving and dividing the cloth toward the buyer. There is no five-owner law, per-shop tax rule, or ban on direct buying as the reason.

Q37

The belief 'middlemen like wholesalers and retailers are useless and just make things expensive' is wrong because they:

  • Ado nothing at all in the chain
  • Bonly raise prices with no benefit
  • Creplace the producers entirely
  • Dadd real value - buying in bulk, storing, reaching far shops and splitting goods into small amounts✓

Working

✓

Middlemen add genuine value by bulk-buying, storing, distributing far and selling small near you, making goods cheaper to move and easier to buy. They are not useless or mere price-raisers, and they do not replace producers.

Q38

A wholesaler buying 1,000 shirts and a retailer selling them one at a time near your home shows that middlemen:

  • Aserve no purpose and waste money
  • Bmake goods more convenient to buy in small, nearby quantities✓
  • Cforce everyone to buy 1,000 shirts at once
  • Dstop goods from ever reaching consumers

Working

✓

The wholesaler-retailer split lets you buy a single shirt conveniently nearby - clear value. Middlemen are not purposeless, do not force bulk buying, and do help goods reach consumers.

Q39

Without retailers, an ordinary buyer wanting just one item would have to:

  • Apay no money at all
  • Bget the item free from the government
  • Cnever obtain the item under any circumstances
  • Dbuy in bulk straight from producers, which is costly and inconvenient✓

Working

✓

Without retailers you'd buy bulk directly from producers - costly and inconvenient, showing why retailers add value. It would not become free, government-given, or impossible.

Q40

Beyond making money, markets benefit society by:

  • Akeeping different communities strictly apart
  • Bbanning all conversation between people
  • Cserving only the government
  • Dbuilding trusted relationships and acting as melting pots of culture✓

Working

✓

Markets build trust and mix cultures, connecting people, traditions and ideas. They do not separate communities, ban conversation, or serve only the government.

Q47

Working

✓

Public goods are available to all and not used up by one person's use, usually government-provided. They are not rich-only, single-use, or export-only.

Q48

✓

A street light serves everyone without being used up - a public good. A chocolate, a sold shirt and a private bicycle are private goods used by one owner.

Q49

Curbing pollution protects society and the environment. It is not done for profit, to encourage pollution, or to ban all manufacturing.

Q50

Q55

'MRP' printed on a pack is the:

Q56

✓

Charging above MRP is not allowed, as MRP is the legal maximum consumer price. It is not a mere suggestion, a requirement, or a subsidy.

✓

Q60
