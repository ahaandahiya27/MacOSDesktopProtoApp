Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

Historians call the period from roughly the 2nd century BCE to the 3rd century CE the 'Age of Reorganisation' chiefly because:

  • Aone new empire reorganised the whole subcontinent under a single ruler
  • Bold regions were repeatedly reshaped into many new, competing kingdoms✓
  • Cthe Mauryan administration was reorganised into larger provinces
  • Dforeign rulers reorganised India into a single Silk-Route state

Working

✓

The name marks how the political map kept being rearranged: after the Mauryas broke up, regions were reshaped into many rival kingdoms that fought and allied for power. There was no single unifying empire in this age, and the reorganisation was of the whole map of kingdoms, not merely of Mauryan provinces.

Q2

What actually triggered the breakup of the Maurya Empire?

  • Aan Indo-Greek army stormed and burned the Mauryan capital
  • Bthe Kushanas under Kanishka annexed it
  • Ca Satavahana invasion from the Deccan toppled it
  • Dits last emperor was assassinated by his own commander-in-chief✓

Working

✓

The empire broke from WITHIN: around 185 BCE the last Maurya ruler was assassinated by his commander-in-chief, Pushyamitra Shunga. The foreign peoples entered the northwest only later, after the breakup had weakened the frontier; they did not cause the collapse.

Q3

The general who killed the last Maurya emperor around 185 BCE and began a new dynasty was:

  • AKharavela of Kalinga
  • BPushyamitra Shunga✓
  • CKanishka the Kushana
  • DGautamiputra Satakarni

Working

✓

Pushyamitra Shunga, the Mauryan commander-in-chief, assassinated the last emperor and founded the Shunga dynasty. Kharavela ruled Kalinga, Kanishka was a later Kushana, and Gautamiputra was a Satavahana, none of whom ended Mauryan rule.

Q4

'The invaders came only after the Mauryas fell.' This ordering of events best supports the conclusion that:

  • Athe internal breakup came first and left the northwest weak, which then let invaders in✓
  • Bthe invaders' attacks were the cause of the Mauryan collapse
  • Cthe Mauryas and the invaders ruled India at the same time
  • Dthe northwest had never belonged to the Mauryas

Working

✓

Because the breakup is dated before the invasions, it cannot have been caused by them; instead the collapse left the frontier undefended, and the Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Kushanas exploited that opening afterwards. Cause precedes effect here.

Q5

Which single statement best captures the chapter's overall picture of the Age of Reorganisation?

  • Aendless warfare made it a cultural dark age with no achievements
  • Bintense political competition and rich cultural blending went hand in hand✓
  • Cit was a peaceful age with no wars, only trade
  • Devery kingdom in it was ruled by foreign invaders

Working

✓

The chapter's big idea is that this was at once an age of fierce war-and-alliance competition AND a time of remarkable cultural confluence: new art schools, busy trade and great Sanskrit texts. War and cultural flowering happened together; one did not cancel the other.

Q6

Apart from open warfare, the chief means by which kingdoms of this age won and secured territory was:

  • Amatrimonial alliances between royal families✓
  • Bholding elections among the nobles
  • Csending out Buddhist missionaries
  • Dabolishing every rival kingdom's identity

Working

✓

Kings married their children into other dynasties to seal peace and join lands, a deliberate political tool that often achieved what war could not. These were monarchies, not elective states, and the alliances kept rival identities alive rather than abolishing them.

Q7

A king gives his daughter in marriage to a neighbouring prince to end a long rivalry and link their lands. The chapter would read this mainly as:

  • Aan act of diplomacy, politics conducted through marriage✓
  • Ba purely private family celebration with no wider meaning
  • Ca religious sacrifice to win the gods' favour
  • Da method of collecting annual tribute

Working

✓

A matrimonial alliance was a tool of POLITICS, not just a wedding: it cemented friendship and merged territories between dynasties. It was diplomatic, not a private affair, not a religious rite, and not a tribute payment.

Q8

Why would a ruler of this age perform the ashvamedha (horse-sacrifice) yajna?

  • Ato proclaim himself a supreme king, claiming every land his freed horse crossed unchallenged✓
  • Bto select the next emperor by drawing lots
  • Cto formalise a marriage alliance with another dynasty
  • Dto mark the first day of a new calendar era

Working

✓

The ashvamedha was a Vedic ritual asserting supreme kingship: a horse was let loose and all the territory it wandered across unopposed was claimed by the king. It was about declaring paramount power, not elections, marriages or calendars.

Q9

That a widowed Satavahana queen is recorded as performing the ashvamedha yajna is used by the chapter to show that:

  • Aonly men ever wielded power in these kingdoms
  • Bthe ritual had nothing to do with claiming kingship
  • Cthe Satavahanas had given up Vedic rituals entirely
  • Droyal women could exercise real political and ritual authority✓

Working

✓

A queen performing this supreme-kingship ritual shows that Satavahana royal women held genuine authority, consistent with kings taking mother-names and queens donating land and commissioning inscriptions. It contradicts the idea that women were mere royal decoration.

Q10

The Shunga dynasty is best remembered in the chapter for:

  • Abuilding a sea-trading empire that reached Rome
  • Breviving Vedic rituals and adorning the Bharhut Stupa✓
  • Ccontrolling the Silk Route across Central Asia
  • Dissuing the Shaka Samvat calendar

Working

✓

The Shungas of north and central India revived Vedic ritual and are linked to the carved railings of the Bharhut Stupa. Sea trade with Rome was the southern and Satavahana story, the Silk Route the Kushanas', and the Shaka era the Shakas'.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

The Shungas revived Vedic ritual, yet the Bharhut Stupa they adorned is a Buddhist monument. This pairing best illustrates that:

  • Athe Shungas had secretly become Buddhist rulers
  • Bsupporting one tradition did not prevent the making of fine art for another in this age✓
  • Cthe Bharhut Stupa was really a Vedic temple
  • DBuddhism had completely vanished by Shunga times

Working

✓

A Vedic-reviving dynasty still connected with carved railings on a Buddhist stupa shows the age's mixing of traditions; reviving one set of rituals did not stop art for another. It does not make the Shungas Buddhist, nor the stupa a Vedic temple, and Buddhism was clearly still alive.

Q12

In this chapter, a 'stupa' is:

  • Aan anthology of Tamil love-and-heroism poems
  • Ba dome-shaped Buddhist monument, often with carved railings and reliefs✓
  • Ca low dam built across a river to feed canals
  • Da free-standing Greek-style victory pillar

Working

✓

A stupa is a dome-shaped Buddhist monument, frequently decorated with carved railings depicting religious stories. The poem anthology is Sangam literature, the river dam is an anicut, and the pillar describes something like the Heliodorus pillar.

Q13

The Shungas held power mainly in:

  • Athe far south, in the Tamil country
  • Bthe Deccan plateau
  • Cthe Central Asian steppe
  • Dnorth and central India✓

Working

✓

The Shungas were a north-and-central-Indian dynasty. The Tamil south belonged to the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas, the Deccan to the Satavahanas, and Central Asia to peoples like the Kushanas.

Q14

The Satavahanas built their power in which region?

  • Athe Tamil far south
  • Bnorth and central India
  • Cthe northwest frontier
  • Dthe Deccan plateau✓

Working

✓

The Satavahanas were the great dynasty of the Deccan. The Tamil south had the three crowned kingdoms, north-central India the Shungas, and the northwest saw the Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Kushanas.

Q15

Satavahana coins that show ships are taken as direct evidence that the dynasty:

  • Aworshipped the sea as a god
  • Bhad lost all its land territory
  • Ccarried on a flourishing sea trade✓
  • Dbuilt warships to fight the Kushanas

Working

✓

Ships stamped on Satavahana coins point to active maritime trade, which reached as far as Rome. The coins are read as evidence of commerce, not sea-worship, territorial loss, or naval war with the inland Kushanas.

Q16

A Satavahana king bears the name 'Gautamiputra'. What does this naming custom tell us?

  • Akings were named after the god they worshipped
  • B'Gautami' was the dynasty's founding city
  • Cthe name marks him out as a Buddhist monk
  • Dkings could be named after their mothers, signalling the standing of royal women✓

Working

✓

'Gautamiputra' means 'son of Gautami', a mother-name. The Satavahana custom of mother-names reflects the real influence of royal women. It is not a god's name, a city, or a monastic title.

Q17

Royal women like Gautami Balashri are known to us mainly because they:

  • Adonated land and had their gifts recorded in inscriptions✓
  • Bled Satavahana armies into battle against Rome
  • Ccomposed the Sangam poems of the Tamil south
  • Ddesigned the Gandhara style of sculpture

Working

✓

Gautami Balashri and other Satavahana royal women donated land and had inscriptions carved recording their gifts, which is how historians know of them. They were not army commanders against Rome, Tamil Sangam poets, or Gandhara sculptors.

Q18

Mother-names, land-donating queens and a queen's ashvamedha together overturn which common belief about these kingdoms?

  • Athat the Satavahanas lived in the Deccan
  • Bthat coins can serve as historical evidence
  • Cthat the age saw frequent warfare
  • Dthat queens were merely decorative and only kings ever held power✓

Working

✓

All three facts show royal women wielding real authority, directly contradicting the belief that queens were mere decoration. The other statements are accepted facts that this evidence does not challenge.

Q19

The Satavahanas' trade reaching as far as Rome is best explained by their:

  • Asharing a land border with the Roman Empire
  • Bmembership of the Roman Empire as a province
  • Ccontrol of Deccan ports and sea routes to the west✓
  • Dcontrol of the overland Silk Route through China

Working

✓

Sea routes from the Deccan's western ports linked the Satavahanas to Roman markets. They did not border Rome, were never a Roman province, and the overland Silk Route was the Kushanas' domain, not theirs.

Q20

King Kharavela, famous in this chapter, ruled the kingdom of:

  • Athe Deccan, as a Satavahana ruler
  • BGandhara, as a Kushana ruler
  • Cthe Tamil south, as a Chera ruler

Kharavela was the Chedi king of Kalinga. He was neither a Satavahana of the Deccan, a Kushana of Gandhara, nor a Chera of the Tamil south.

An inscription is text cut into a lasting material such as stone or metal, frequently recording deeds, gifts or beliefs. A stamped coin, an oral poem and a wall painting are different kinds of evidence.

Sangam literature, the oldest Tamil poetry on love, heroism and generosity, gives the Sangam Age its name. It is named for a body of poetry, not for battles, a royal election, or a Buddhist council.

Sangam literature expresses themes of love, heroism and generosity. It is Tamil court-and-life poetry, not a manual of Vedic ritual, a Kushana chronicle, or Silk-Route trade law.

Q27

Finding Roman gold coins in the Tamil south is best explained by the fact that the southern kingdoms:

✓

Roman coins in the south reflect brisk sea trade between the Tamil kingdoms and Rome. They were never Roman subjects, did not mint in Rome, and their kings were crowned at home.

Q28

Which pairing of region and power is correct for this age?

  • BKalinga, the Cheras

✓

The Pandyas were a Tamil southern kingdom. The Cholas (also Tamil south) did not rule the Deccan (that was the Satavahanas), Kalinga was the Chedis' (not the Cheras'), and the northwest belonged to the invaders, not the Pandyas.

Q29

The Grand Anicut (Kallanai) built by the Cholas was:

  • Ca coastal fort built to keep out Roman ships
  • Dan ancient dam across the Kaveri to spread its water over the delta✓

Working

✓

The Grand Anicut is an ancient dam across the Kaveri that diverted water across the delta, making it a rich rice region. It is not a stupa, a pillar, or a fort.

Q30

An 'anicut', as the word's Tamil roots show, is:

  • Aa low dam built to raise river water and divert it into canals✓
  • Ba royal marriage treaty between dynasties
  • Ca dome-shaped Buddhist monument
  • Da counted calendar era

Working

✓

From Tamil anai ('dam') + kattu ('to build'), an anicut is a low dam that lifts river water into canals. It is not a marriage treaty (alliance), a monument (stupa), or a calendar (samvat).

Q31

Why did the Grand Anicut matter so much to Chola prosperity?

  • Ait blocked Roman ships from entering Chola ports

  • Dit marked the boundary with the Pandya kingdom

Working

✓

By spreading the Kaveri's water across the delta, the Anicut created one of India's richest rice lands, feeding the population and armies behind Chola power. It was an irrigation work, not a naval barrier, a fortress, or a boundary marker.

✓

The Cheras and Pandyas prospered on sea trade, reaching as far as Rome. The Silk Route was the Kushanas' overland network; they received no Kushana tribute and held no Central Asian mines.

✓

Roman familiarity with Tamil-coast goods points to lively maritime commerce between south India and the Mediterranean: trade, not conquest, colonisation, or Tamil rule in Italy.

Both the Deccan Satavahanas and the southern Tamil kingdoms traded with Rome by SEA, using their coastal ports. Neither relied on the overland Silk Route, which connected China to the Mediterranean through the Kushana lands.

A Greek envoy raising a pillar in devotion to an Indian deity shows cultural blending, with the Indo-Greeks absorbing Indian beliefs. It is the opposite of forcing Greek gods, shunning Indian religion, or destroying temples.

✓

Much of our knowledge of the Indo-Greek kings comes from their coins, which preserve names and portraits. The coins were real trade currency, not Roman-minted, and carried no Tamil Sangam poetry.

The Indo-Greeks managing to enter India is itself a consequence of:

✓

The Mauryan collapse left the northwest poorly defended, opening the door to the Indo-Greeks. The southern Cholas did not invite them, the Kushana Silk Route came later, and the Shaka era is unrelated to their arrival.

Q39

Though they arrived as conquerors, the Indo-Greeks and Kushanas were deeply influenced by India's culture, producing blends in which Indian themes stayed dominant. They neither erased Indian culture, nor stayed wholly separate, nor unified all India.

A Buddhist figure rendered in Greek forms is textbook cultural confluence, traditions flowing together into something new. It is not destruction of a culture, nor influence-free, nor a Vedic revival.

Cultural confluence is the flowing-together of cultures to make new shared styles, with a strong local character surviving, exactly what happened in Indian art and coinage of this age. It is neither replacement, nor strict separation, nor unchanging spread.

Why does the chapter stress that without coins and inscriptions we would know far less about this age?

  • Abecause no one living then could read or write
  • Bbecause the period left behind no buildings at all
  • Cbecause all written histories of it were translations from Rome
  • Dbecause so much of our evidence for its rulers, trade and religion is physical, not written chronicles✓

Working

✓

Coins (ships, titles, gods) and inscriptions (Hathigumpha, Nashik, Naneghat) carry much of what we know, since continuous written chronicles are scarce, hence their value. It is not that no one was literate, that nothing was built, or that Rome wrote its history.

Q59

Which sequence places these events in correct chronological order (earliest first)?

  • AKanishka's Kushana rule, then the Maurya breakup, then the rise of the Satavahanas
  • BMaurya breakup, then the rise of the Satavahanas, then Kanishka's Kushana rule✓
  • Cthe rise of the Satavahanas, then Kanishka's Kushana rule, then the Maurya breakup
  • DKanishka's Kushana rule, then the rise of the Satavahanas, then the Maurya breakup

Working

✓

The Maurya breakup (c. 185 BCE) came first; the Satavahanas rose in the 2nd century BCE; Kanishka's Kushanas peaked in the 2nd century CE. So breakup, then Satavahanas, then Kanishka is the right order.

Q60

A student writes: 'The Age of Reorganisation proves that political division and cultural creativity can occur together.' The best support from the chapter is:

  • Athe whole subcontinent was peacefully unified under one ruler
  • Bconstant wars among many kingdoms coincided with new art schools, busy trade and great Sanskrit works✓
  • Call art and trade stopped until a single empire returned
  • Dforeign rulers banned every form of Indian culture

Working

✓

The age shows division and creativity side by side: rival kingdoms warred and allied while Gandhara and Mathura art, far-reaching trade and Sanskrit literature flourished. The age was not unified, art and trade did not stop, and Indian culture was not banned.
