## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** "Drizzling right now" is what is happening this moment — that is weather. "Cherrapunji is one of the wettest places on Earth" describes a long-term pattern over decades — that is climate. So Ahaan = weather, teacher = climate. The trap (B) reverses the two; the catch is that a single moment is always weather, never climate.

**2. (B)** Averaging weather over thirty years to find the steady pattern is the very definition of climate. Weather (A) is hour-to-hour; a season (C) is a yearly recurrence; a microclimate (D) is a small local area — none fits a 30-year average for a whole town.

**3. (C)** Weather and climate describe the *same* atmosphere and can describe the same place and rainfall — the ONLY thing that separates them is the length of time you look at: a moment is weather, decades give climate. Hence (C), not (A), (B) or (D).

**4. (D)** Most of the world uses four seasons; India is famous for adding the rainy season, the monsoon, as a fifth. "Harvest", "dry" and "windy" seasons (A, B, C) are not the chapter's named extra season.

**5. (C)** The six ṛitus are vasanta, grīṣhma, varṣhā, śharad, hemanta and śhiśhir. "Maru" (desert) is not a ṛitu — it is a distractor built from the Thar. Vasanta, Varṣhā and Śhiśhir are all genuine ṛitus, so the odd one out is (C).

**6. (A)** Seasons recur every year because the Earth revolves around the Sun, so the same conditions return annually. Reversing monsoon winds (B) explain the two monsoons, not the yearly return of all seasons; the Sun does not grow hotter each summer (C); festivals (D) follow the seasons, they don't cause them.

**7. (C)** India uses six ṛitus because that finer scheme reads a monsoon-ruled year better than four seasons — a four-season model has no slot for the all-important rains. Age (A), borrowing from Europe (B) and festival-matching (D) are not the chapter's reason.

**8. (A)** High Himalayas = alpine, Northern Plains = subtropical, Thar = arid, west coast = tropical wet. Only (A) lists them in that order; the other options scramble the pairing.

**9. (D)** A country stretching from snowy mountains to a hot desert to rainy coasts cannot have one climate: its range of latitude, altitude and nearness to the sea produces many climates side by side. Calendars (A), a "different Sun" (B) or states "controlling weather" (C) are not real causes.

**10. (C)** Lower hills with hill stations that stay mild are temperate. Arid (A) is the dry Thar; alpine (B) is the freezing high Himalayas; tropical wet (D) is the rainy west coast — none describes mild lower hills.

**11. (B)** Very hot summers, cold winters and good wheat country describe the subtropical Northern Plains. Tropical wet (A) is the rainy coast; alpine (C) is freezing mountains; maritime (D) means a mild coastal climate — the opposite of "hot summers and cold winters".

**12. (D)** The Equator is hotter mainly because the Sun's rays strike it almost straight down, concentrating their heat on a small area. The "closer to the Sun" idea (A) is the classic misconception — the distance difference is tiny. There is air over the Equator (C) and the poles do get some sunlight (B); both are false.

**13. (C)** Slanting polar rays are weakened two ways: spread over a wider area AND passing through more air on a longer journey. Reflecting off ice (A), losing heat to winds (B) and being blocked by mountains (D) are not the chapter's two reasons.

**14. (D)** Kanniyakumari is warm and Kashmir cold because of how far each lies from the Equator — that is latitude. Altitude (A) would matter for a mountain-vs-plain pair, distance from the sea (B) for a coast-vs-inland pair, winds (C) for a seasonal contrast — but the equator-to-far-north comparison is a latitude effect.

**15. (B)** The two reasons given are: the air higher up is thinner and holds less heat, AND you are farther from the ground, which is the air's main heater. You do not move "closer to the cold Sun" (A — the Sun isn't cold and you barely move toward it); (C) and (D) are invented.

**16. (B)** Same latitude rules out latitude; the high plateau town is cooler because of its greater altitude — temperature falls as you climb. Distance from the sea (C) and rainfall (D) are not what sets these two apart.

**17. (C)** Peaks are snowbound while the plains below swelter — the towering height of the peaks (altitude) is the deciding factor, since temperature drops with elevation. Latitude (A) is similar for peak and plain; sea distance (B) and winds (D) don't explain the snow line.

**18. (D)** Same latitude, so latitude is ruled out; Mumbai is milder because the sea moderates its temperature (water warms and cools slowly). "Higher altitude" (A) is false for coastal Mumbai; Nagpur is not nearer the Equator (B); it certainly gets sunshine (C).

**19. (A)** The sea moderates because water heats and cools much more slowly than land, storing summer heat and releasing it gently in winter. (B) is the reverse and would make coasts extreme; (C) and (D) are false — the sea does change temperature, just slowly.

**20. (A)** A small temperature range means an even, moderate climate — typical of places near the sea, which the ocean keeps mild. A high inland plateau (B), a desert (C) and a very high latitude (D) all give large ranges (big extremes), the opposite of small.

**21. (C)** Mumbai's small ~14°C range is explained by its nearness to the moderating sea, which evens out summer and winter. Altitude (A) is low at the coast; lower latitude (B) would affect overall warmth, not the smallness of the range; rainfall (D) does not set the temperature range.

**22. (D)** The loo gets its heat by crossing the hot deserts of Arabia and Afghanistan before reaching north India. The Bay of Bengal (A) and the Western Ghats (C) would add moisture, not dry heat; the snowy Himalayas (B) would chill, not heat, the wind.

**23. (B)** Cold Himalayan winds chilling the foothills show that winds carry the qualities (here, cold) of the land they have crossed. Winds do not change a place's latitude (A) or altitude (C), nor move it nearer the sea (D).

**24. (A)** The same plain can be hot under a desert wind and cold under a mountain wind because air picks up the character of wherever it has travelled — heat from deserts, cold from snowy mountains. Air is not "created fresh" (B), does not always warm as it moves (C), and certainly carries heat and moisture (D).

**25. (B)** Flat Thar lying open to hot winds while ranges shield the land behind them shows the climate role of topography (relief) — the shape of the land blocking or admitting winds. It is not about latitude (A), distance from the sea (C) or the monsoon (D).

**26. (C)** A small patch with its own special climate is a microclimate. A season (A) and a ṛitu (D) are time periods, not places; a rain shadow (B) is the dry leeward side of a mountain, not a shady forest patch.

**27. (B)** Topography means a region's physical surface features — mountains, hills, valleys, slopes and coasts. It is not population/cities (A), festivals (C) or rainfall alone (D).

**28. (D)** A concrete city warmer than the green countryside is a human-made microclimate (an urban heat island). It is not a season (A), a cyclone (B) or a rain shadow (C).

**29. (C)** *Mausim* (Arabic) means "season" — which is why "monsoon" really refers to seasonal winds. Rain (A), wind (B) and flood (D) are tempting because we link the monsoon to rains and winds, but the literal meaning is "season".

**30. (D)** Strictly, "monsoon" names the seasonal winds that reverse direction with the seasons; people only loosely use it to mean the rains (A) those winds bring. It is not the six ṛitus (C) or the ocean itself (B).

**31. (D)** The whole monsoon turns on one fact: land heats and cools faster than the ocean, creating the pressure differences that move the winds. The Moon (A), a "fixed" Equator temperature (B) and rivers pushing air (C) are all false.

**32. (B)** In summer the hot land becomes a low-pressure zone; since air flows from high to low pressure, moist sea air rushes inland. If the land were high pressure (A) the air would flow out (that is winter); (C) and (D) describe no inflow.

**33. (A)** Air always flows from high pressure to low pressure — that is what drives the monsoon winds. Option (B) is backwards; (C) and (D) are false.

**34. (A)** The summer monsoon is the southwest monsoon and the winter monsoon is the northeast monsoon — in that order, (A). Option (B) reverses them; (C) and (D) invent names.

**35. (C)** The summer monsoon brings the main rains because its winds blow from the moist sea onto the land, carrying water vapour that falls as rain. Land-to-sea winds (A) are the dry winter monsoon; (B) and (D) are false.

**36. (B)** The winter (northeast) monsoon is mostly dry because its winds blow from the land out toward the ocean, carrying little moisture. Sea-to-land winds (A) are the wet summer monsoon; (C) and (D) are false.

**37. (D)** Part of the winter monsoon crosses the Bay of Bengal, picking up moisture and bringing some rain to south-east India. The Arabian Sea (A) feeds the summer monsoon's west coast; the Thar (B) and Himalayas (C) would not add moisture to these winds.

**38. (B)** The winds reverse because the land–sea temperature difference flips between summer and winter, flipping which side is high pressure. The Earth's tilt (A) and the Moon's orbit (C) don't reverse twice a year in this way, and the Ghats (D) do not move.

**39. (A)** The rains reach the southern tip in early June and cover the whole country by mid-July. End of June (B) is too soon; September (C) and October (D) are too late.

**40. (C)** Both a sea breeze and the summer monsoon work by warm land creating low pressure that draws in cooler sea air — the monsoon is just that breeze on a country-sized scale. The Moon (A), rivers (B) and melting snow (D) are not the principle.

**41. (C)** The dry leeward region behind the Western Ghats, left in the "shadow" of the rain, is a rain shadow. A microclimate (A) is a small local area, a cold desert (B) is a high-altitude dry zone, and a delta (D) is a river mouth — none names this dry plateau.

**42. (D)** The west coast is wet because the Western Ghats force the moist monsoon winds to rise and rain on the western slopes, leaving the Deccan drier. The Deccan is not nearer the Equator (A); the west coast is not higher (B); river count (C) does not decide rainfall.

**43. (B)** Orographic rain forms because rising air expands, cools, and can hold less water vapour, so the vapour condenses into rain. Rising air does not warm or hold more vapour (A); it does not dry instantly (C) or sink on the windward side (D).

**44. (A)** Without the Ghats there would be nothing to force the moist winds up, so the west coast would receive much less rain. "Much more" (B) and "more rain → flood" reasoning are backwards; "desert overnight" (C) overstates it; "no change" (D) ignores the Ghats' role.

**45. (C)** The monsoon exists only because land heats and cools faster than the ocean. If both heated at the same speed, there would be no pressure difference and no monsoon at all. It would not strengthen (A), reverse once (B) or shift to winter (D).

**46. (D)** Mawsynram in Meghalaya receives the highest average annual rainfall in the world. Cherrapunji (A) is nearby and very wet but not the highest; Mumbai (C) and Munnar (B) are wet but far below Mawsynram.

**47. (D)** The windward side faces the wind, where moist air is forced up, cools and drops heavy rain. The sheltered, dry side (A) is the leeward side; (B) and (C) are unrelated to the term.

**48. (B)** Festivals like Bihu, Pongal, Onam and Baisakhi are tied to the seasons and the farming cycle. They are not tied to fuel prices (A), the number of cities (C) or each state's latitude (D).

**49. (A)** Monsoon failure means poor rains, so crops suffer, water grows scarce and food prices rise. Overflowing rivers and floods (B) come from too much rain, not failure; warmer winters (C) and vanishing ṛitus (D) are not its effects.

**50. (C)** Because most farming depends on the monsoon, failed rains shrink harvests, push up food prices and force some workers to migrate to cities. Festivals are not "cancelled by law" (A); the sea keeps moderating the coast (B); the Himalayas do not grow (D).

**51. (B)** The periodic eastward shift of warm Pacific water that weakens India's monsoon is El Niño. The loo (A) is a hot land wind, a glacial burst (C) is a Himalayan flood, and a rain shadow (D) is a dry mountain region.

**52. (A)** Since the 1800s, climate change has been driven mainly by human activities, especially burning fossil fuels (plus deforestation). "Natural processes alone" (B) was true long ago, not since the 19th century; (C) and (D) are not the main driver.

**53. (A)** The natural greenhouse effect matters because greenhouse gases trap some of the Sun's heat, keeping Earth warm enough for life. They do not block all rays (B) or cool Earth to freezing (C), and they do not create the monsoon winds (D).

**54. (B)** Human warming happens when burning fossil fuels and clearing forests add extra greenhouse gases, thickening the heat-trapping "blanket" so more heat is trapped. Thinning the blanket (A) would cool, not warm; (C) and (D) are false.

**55. (A)** The cause-effect chain runs: coal burnt → CO₂ released → extra heat trapped (greenhouse effect) → climate shifts → monsoon disrupted → crop fails. Burning coal does not cool the sea (B), freeze rivers (C) or grow forests (D), so only (A) is a valid chain.

**56. (D)** Mitigation attacks the *cause* of warming — boosting renewable energy and planting trees both cut or absorb greenhouse gases. Cutting forests (A), burning more coal (B) and wasteful consumption (C) all worsen warming, the opposite of mitigation.

**57. (A)** Mitigation slows the warming by attacking its cause (cutting emissions, adding trees); resilience helps communities withstand and recover from impacts like floods. Option (B) swaps the two; (C) oversimplifies; (D) is false — each addresses one side, not neither.

**58. (D)** A cyclone forms when the sea-air pressure drops much lower than its surroundings, so air and moisture rush in and spiral up. High pressure (A) pushes air out, equal pressure (C) gives no storm, and being "colder than the land" (B) is not what triggers a cyclone.

**59. (C)** The eerie calm with clear sky is the cyclone's "eye" passing overhead; the fierce winds of the surrounding ring will return, so people must stay sheltered. Going outside (A) or to the coast (B) is dangerous; lighting a fire (D) is irrelevant.

**60. (B)** The fiercest winds are in the ring of cloud surrounding the eye, not in the calm "eye" itself (A) — the common misconception. They are not far out at sea (C) or only after the storm ends (D).
