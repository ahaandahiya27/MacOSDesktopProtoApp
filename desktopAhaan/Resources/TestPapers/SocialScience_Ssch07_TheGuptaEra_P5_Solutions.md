## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** Correct: Pataliputra served both dynasties but they are separated by centuries with the Kushanas in between, and unlike the Maurya ideal the Guptas only directly held the north and centre, so neither the shared city nor the shared name proves continuity. The claim that the Mauryas never used Pataliputra is false and contradicts the textbook. The claim of direct descent is invented; the Guptas were a distinct later dynasty. The claim that both ruled the whole subcontinent identically is wrong because the Gupta polity left the south and northeast to their own rulers, which is precisely part of the refutation.

**2. (B)** Correct: a court poet writing in praise of his patron produces a partial source, so the conquests are plausible but must be triangulated with coins and other evidence rather than swallowed whole. Saying court poetry is worthless overcorrects and would wrongly discard the true premises (i) and (ii). The 'rival king' idea contradicts the fact that Harisena was Samudragupta's own court poet. Saying Samudragupta never fought wars is flatly wrong: he is remembered above all as an expansionist warrior king.

**3. (A)** Correct: the marvel is specific to this pillar's phosphorus-rich iron and its passive layer, demonstrating advanced skill on one object, not that all ancient iron is rust-proof. The bronze claim is false; it is an iron pillar. Saying iron never corrodes repeats the very error being criticised. The annual-repainting claim is invented; the resistance is inherent to the metal, not a coating.

**4. (B)** Correct: each source supports one inference, and together they show royal self-projection (coin), documented administration (copper plate) and long-distance maritime trade (graffiti). Graffiti by visiting merchants shows travel and trade, not Gupta rule over Socotra. The coin and graffiti show coinage and trade continued, so 'abandoned coinage and trade' is contradicted by the very evidence listed. The copper plate is itself a written record, so 'no written records' is self-contradicting.

**5. (C)** Correct: the Licchavi match lent the young dynasty prestige and the Vakataka match (with Prabhavati later governing as regent) projected Gupta influence outward, showing a consistent political use of marriage. Calling them purely religious ignores their clear political payoff. Marriages were not tax-collection devices. The Guptas did not surrender their throne; they gained allies and influence.

**6. (C)** Correct: Samudragupta ruled in the 4th century CE, so dating him to the 2nd century BCE misplaces him by roughly six centuries into the Mauryan era. It is not a minor slip of decades but a span-of-dynasties error. The Guptas did not immediately follow the Mauryas; the Kushanas lay between. The date is wrong too, not merely the dynasty, since no Gupta ruled in the 2nd century BCE.

**7. (A)** Correct: the student selects only the flattering half of Faxian's own report and ignores his note on the chandalas, so the 'fair to everyone' conclusion is unsupported by the complete source. Nothing here shows the document is forged or that Faxian never visited. The student did not reject the source; he over-relied on part of it. The source is Faxian's travel account, not a prashasti, so the 'wrong type of source' label is inaccurate.

**8. (D)** Correct: tax-free grants subtract from taxable land and transfer local authority, so cumulative grants erode revenue and direct control, a weakness that tells when invasions like the Hunas' arrive. Tax-free land paid no tax, so 'doubled the taxes' is the reverse of the truth. Grants did not abolish armies. Grants did not confine merchants or end trade; that conflates a fiscal policy with maritime commerce.

**9. (D)** Correct: the lyre projects cultured patronage, the archer projects martial power, and 'great king of kings' projects supremacy, so the combined message is a multi-faceted, paramount ruler. A humble servant making no claim contradicts the supreme title. A renouncing pilgrim contradicts a king minting royal coins. A foreign merchant contradicts both the kingly imagery and the royal title.

**10. (B)** Correct: the Aryabhatiya is a compact treatise that systematised and extended known results (place-value, Earth's rotation, an accurate pi), so 'gathered and advanced' fits better than 'invented from nothing'. It is not a poetry work; that confuses Aryabhata with Kalidasa. It is not a prashasti; that confuses it with the Prayaga inscription. Mathematics had a long prior history, so 'invented the subject' is false.

**11. (B)** Correct: the senses suggest a still Earth under a moving sky, so attributing the motion to the Earth contradicts immediate appearance and required real abstraction. No one can see the Earth spin, so it was not 'obvious'. The idea concerns rotation, not a flat Earth on a pillar. It is the Earth's spin, not the Sun racing nightly, that the proposal invokes.

**12. (D)** Correct: both attributions are swapped wrongly, since Aryabhata wrote the Aryabhatiya and Harisena (not Aryabhata) wrote the Prayaga Prashasti praising Samudragupta. It is not a single error; both halves are wrong. It is false that kings wrote treatises and scientists wrote royal eulogies as a rule. Kalidasa wrote neither of these two works, so the 'Kalidasa wrote both' option introduces a new false claim.

**13. (B)** Correct: a calculating system taken up worldwide transformed global mathematics, giving it broader and longer reach than a single localised artwork, however fine. Ajanta paintings did survive, so the premise of the first distractor is false. The place-value system was not forgotten; it spread and endured. Paintings are valid historical evidence, so dismissing them entirely is wrong.

**14. (D)** Correct: a regent governs in place of a monarch unable to rule, usually a child heir, which is exactly Prabhavati's role for her sons rather than reigning as sovereign. A traveller to holy places is a pilgrim. A composer of praise-verse is a court poet. An envoy arranging marriages is an ambassador. Only the regent definition fits.

**15. (D)** Correct: defeating the Shakas gave the Guptas the western lands and ports, strengthening their reach into Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean trade. The conquest had nothing to do with China or the Pacific. The Mauryan dynasty had ended centuries earlier and was not under Shaka protection. Zero was an Indian mathematical achievement, not a Shaka secret.

**16. (B)** Correct: the Gupta state mixed direct rule over the north-centre core with looser reliance on grants and allied kings at the margins, so neither pure model fits alone. It was not the whole-subcontinent direct rule of the Maurya ideal. It was not a leaderless alliance; there was a strong emperor. It controlled far more than just Pataliputra. The blend answer captures both halves.

**17. (B)** Correct: a partisan inscription and an independent traveller's report check each other, so agreement strengthens a claim and disagreement flags it for further study. They were not by the same author for the same king; one is Harisena's eulogy, the other Faxian's travel record. The prashasti is partial, not unbiased, and Faxian is not pure invention. Both being written does not make them unusable; written sources are central evidence.

**18. (C)** Correct: titles asserting kingship over kings presuppose subordinate rulers beneath a paramount one, mirroring the tiered Gupta political order. A village headman has no kings beneath him, so that reading empties the title of meaning. There was no elected council holding the real power. A temple priest reading ignores that these are sovereign, not priestly, titles.

**19. (C)** Correct: choosing copper shows an intent to make land rights permanent and provable over generations, evidence of an organised, documented administration. Copper plates were not chosen for sea-voyage portability to Rome. Copper is durable precisely so the grant could NOT be easily erased. Melting them for coins would defeat the purpose of recording a lasting grant.

**20. (D)** Correct: 'Vikramaditya' and cultural patronage are the hallmarks of Chandragupta II, whose reign is remembered for art and learning rather than for the great conquests. The inscription explicitly omits conquests, so it does not fit conquest-famed Samudragupta. Chandragupta I is the founder of imperial status, not the 'Vikramaditya' patron of culture. Chandragupta Maurya is a different dynasty centuries earlier.

**21. (A)** Correct: the coin is deliberate royal self-projection, whereas Faxian was an independent foreign pilgrim, so their biases differ in source and strength rather than being equal. Faxian did not mint coins; he wrote travel notes. He was not a paid court official. And neither is entirely bias-free, so 'no bias at all' is also wrong; the point is the kinds of bias differ.

**22. (D)** Correct: traders' graffiti demonstrates trade and travel, which is a commercial footprint, not proof of conquest or rule. The graffiti was left by Indian merchants, not invading Socotrans. Socotra was a distant island, not the Gupta capital (Pataliputra was). And the graffiti is genuine evidence of Indian merchants reaching the island, not a forgery.

**23. (B)** Correct: 'Golden Age' rightly praises the period's culture and science, but inferring social equality is the error because the chandalas faced harsh exclusion. It is not true that brilliance went with full equality; that is the misconception itself. The era clearly produced major culture, so 'no notable culture' is false. And its art and science were exceptional, so calling the Golden Age label the flaw is backwards.

**24. (A)** Correct: contemporary kingdoms in the south, northeast and Deccan show the Guptas held the north-centre while others ruled their regions, so India was not one Gupta realm. These were full kingdoms, not Gupta provinces or villages. India was not a Chinese province. The conclusion that follows is shared, multi-kingdom subcontinent, not Gupta monopoly.

**25. (C)** Correct: placing Samudragupta's conquests in the 2nd century BCE puts a Gupta ruler centuries before the empire's own start date, contradicting the 4th-6th century CE frame. The other three (Chandragupta I in the early 4th century, the Hunas in the 6th, Aryabhata around 500) all sit comfortably inside the stated span and so are consistent, not contradictory.

**26. (C)** Correct: the pillar is evidence of skilled metallurgy (corrosion-resistant iron) while the coin is evidence of economy and royal imagery, so they illuminate different facets. Neither is reducible to bare metal weight. The pillar shows domestic iron skill, not Roman imports. And the iron pillar itself disproves any claim that the Guptas worked only gold.

**27. (C)** Correct: conquest (Samudragupta/Prayaga Prashasti), patronage (Chandragupta II/Kalidasa) and universal-sovereign titles (Chakravartin) jointly match the conqueror-patron-overlord description. The visitors-and-art set is about outsiders and art, not a king's three-fold image. The Hunas-and-decline set is about the empire's fall. The administration-only set covers governance, not conquest and universal sovereignty together.

**28. (D)** Correct: 'tireless creativity' is justified by sustained, many-sided creative output in science, literature, scholarship, medicine and art together. A single coin in one year is too thin to support 'tireless' across fields. Huna invasions are destructive pressure, not creativity. Tax collection is administration, not the creative flowering the phrase names.

**29. (A)** Correct: the 'II' only separates the two Chandraguptas; the succession is Chandragupta I, then Samudragupta, then Chandragupta II, so the Samudragupta coin predates the Chandragupta II coin. 'II' does not mean later than every king. The rulers did not reign simultaneously. Chandragupta II followed, not preceded, Samudragupta, so his coin is the younger.

**30. (D)** Correct: the assertion stands (treat it critically) but the reason is wrong, since the problem is partisan authorship, not the stone medium, which aids preservation. 'Both true and reason explains' fails because the reason is false. The assertion is not false; court inscriptions are not neutral. And the prashasti is not reliable in every detail, so the all-false option is wrong too.

**31. (D)** Correct: coins are authentic, dateable sources that also carry deliberate royal messaging, so the balanced stance is 'real evidence, read critically'. The coins were used in a trading economy, not unused. They do carry images and titles, which is exactly what must be read critically. And physical objects like coins are valid evidence alongside inscriptions, not to be set aside.

**32. (D)** Correct: Aryabhata and Varahamihira were scientists, but Kalidasa (poet-playwright) and Harisena (court poet of the prashasti) were literary figures, so two names are misplaced. Harisena was not a scientist, so 'only Kalidasa' is wrong. Aryabhata was indeed a scientist, so calling him misplaced is false. And the scholars had distinct specialisms, so 'none misplaced' is wrong.

**33. (B)** Correct: drawing students from afar shows Nalanda's international reputation as a centre of advanced learning. Nothing suggests Indians were barred; that inverts the openness the fact implies. There is no basis for a 'military fort' reading. And the Guptas patronised learning rather than banning study within their borders.

**34. (A)** Correct: differing reports each capture part of a complex society, so the historian synthesises them into 'brilliance alongside inequality' rather than discarding either. Keeping only the cheerful report repeats the cherry-picking error. Disagreement does not make accounts forgeries. And one favourable report does not settle the matter or disprove another observer.

**35. (C)** Correct: cumulative grants drained revenue and weakened central control, leaving the state less able to repel the Huna invasions, so an internal weakness and an external shock combine. The grants reduced, not strengthened, the treasury. The Hunas were Central Asian invaders, not grant-holders. And the grants did not lure the Hunas; the Hunas migrated for their own reasons through the northwest.

**36. (A)** Correct: repeated entries by varied peoples over centuries mark the northwest as a recurring overland gateway, a geographic pattern transcending the Guptas. Many groups, not only the Hunas, used it, so it is not Gupta-specific. The route was passable, not sealed. And it was an overland gateway, not a seaport, so arrival by ship is wrong.

**37. (C)** Correct: an agrahara is a land/village grant (often tax-free) to brahmins or temples; a prashasti is a eulogistic inscription of a king's deeds; they are different kinds of thing. They are not the same object. The 'swapped correctly' option reverses both definitions, so it is still wrong. And neither is a gold coin.

**38. (B)** Correct: to codify is to organise and record existing knowledge systematically, so Ayurveda was structured, not invented from nothing. Codifying is the opposite of destroying texts. It means writing knowledge down, not hiding it as a secret. And it does not mean translating into Greek or originating abroad; Ayurveda is the indigenous 'science of life'.

**39. (C)** Correct: a Hindu royal emblem alongside patronage of Buddhist art shows support for multiple traditions, not one exclusive faith. They did not worship only Greek gods. They were not Buddhist-only banning Hindu symbols; Garuda is a Hindu emblem they used. And Garuda's choice as Vishnu's vahana was religiously meaningful, not random decoration.

**40. (A)** Correct: the argument over-reads a praise-title; 'Sun of Valour' honours bravery while the artistic flowering reflects patronage, so equating valour with the arts confuses two distinct facts joined only by a name. Valour and art are not literally identical. 'Vikramaditya' means 'Sun of Valour', not 'King of Kings'. And Chandragupta II's reign genuinely was a cultural peak, so the premise about the arts is true, not false.

**41. (A)** Correct: Prabhavati's documented regency is concrete evidence of real political power without claiming women generally ruled. The 'commanded every army' option overstates well beyond the evidence. The 'only foreign queens' claim is invented and Prabhavati was a Gupta princess. And the 'no role at all' option is contradicted by her case itself.

**42. (A)** Correct: the Meghadutam is Kalidasa's poem, not a science treatise, while the other two are scientific/encyclopedic works, so it is the misfit. The Aryabhatiya is a mathematics-astronomy treatise, not a love poem. The Brihat Samhita is Varahamihira's encyclopedic work, not a prashasti. And since the Meghadutam is poetry, 'none misclassified' is false.

**43. (B)** Correct: independent sources of different kinds converging is strong corroboration, since shared bias or coincidence is unlikely across them. A single source would not already prove everything; that is why corroboration matters. The three were not all by one author; they are independent. And convergence does not license ignoring physical objects; coins and graffiti are themselves physical evidence.

**44. (C)** Correct: sound method is authorship -> bias -> cross-check -> accept corroborated parts, which is exactly the discipline the chapter models with the Prayaga Prashasti. Accepting everything first reverses the method and invites error. Rejecting it whole discards genuine evidence. Assuming secret enemy authorship is an unfounded leap, not careful reasoning.

**45. (D)** Correct: a grant-and-ally system can run smoothly when unchallenged but loses central revenue and control over time, so it is more brittle under prolonged strain like the Huna invasions. Grants did not guarantee added central taxes; they removed taxable land. The two systems are not identical in effect; that is the whole contrast. And tax-free grants by definition returned less, not more, revenue.

**46. (C)** Correct: two strong examples justify excellence in those specific fields, not blanket superiority everywhere, so the overreach is 'every field'. Both the pillar and zero were genuine Indian achievements, so 'nothing' is wrong. They belong to the Gupta age, not the Mauryan. And two examples cannot establish leadership in every field, so 'no correction needed' is mistaken.

**47. (B)** Correct: dating it to the Mauryan era detaches the text from its real Gupta-age context and the flowering around 500 CE, distorting how we understand it, while a misspelling does not move it in time. Dates do matter for context. The Aryabhatiya was not Mauryan; it is Gupta-age. And a misplaced date and a misspelling are not of equal historical weight.

**48. (D)** Correct: a titled copper-plate grant shows the documented land-grant system AND the king's supreme-overlord self-presentation in one object. Overseas trade and the Iron Pillar are unrelated to a land grant. Aryabhata and Kalidasa concern science and literature, not a grant deed. The Hunas and Ayurveda are about decline and medicine, not this document's two themes.

**49. (C)** Correct: even the leading power need not rule everywhere, and the Pallavas governed the south from Kanchipuram on their own, so Gupta prominence does not subordinate them. There is no basis for the Pallavas ruling the north or being mightier. The two were distinct dynasties, not one under two names. And the Guptas clearly were a major power, so 'no power at all' is false.

**50. (D)** Correct: 'Classical' rests on enduring cultural models, since politically the subcontinent was shared among several kingdoms, not unified. The Guptas did not unify the whole subcontinent, so that basis fails. The period produced rich culture, so 'no notable culture' is false. And cultural achievement and political unity are not the same thing, which is the very distinction at issue.

**51. (A)** Correct: shared sovereign titles and military fame mean a conquest-praising fragment could fit either king, so dating and corroboration are needed before attribution. Chandragupta II also won wars (the Shakas), so 'only Samudragupta fought' is false. 'Chakravartin' was a general claim of paramount kings, not unique to one. And '-gupta' marks the Gupta, not the Maurya, dynasty, so the Chandragupta Maurya reading is doubly wrong.

**52. (A)** Correct: conquest and the prashasti belong to Samudragupta; cultural patronage and the 'Chandra' pillar to Chandragupta II, which keeps the two distinct. It is false that neither had military activity, since Samudragupta was a great conqueror. The 'pillar/prashasti' option swaps the correct attributions. And neither is Chandragupta Maurya, a separate Mauryan ruler centuries earlier.

**53. (C)** Correct: the equality claim is unsound because the chandalas were harshly excluded, so it must be struck. The other three — enduring science, lasting literature, and skilled art and metallurgy — are all well-supported reasons the era is celebrated and rightly stay on the list.

**54. (C)** Correct: a Gupta king is being slotted into the role of a foreign pilgrim, mixing two categories, when the touring pilgrim was Faxian. Kumaragupta did not travel from China; he was a Gupta ruler. It is not a mere spelling issue. And the pilgrim was Faxian, not Samudragupta, so naming another Gupta king repeats the same category error.

**55. (A)** Correct: useful ideas spread peacefully via trade and scholarship, so worldwide adoption shows influence, not Gupta rule abroad. Adoption did not require conquest. The system clearly did leave India and spread, so 'never left' is false. And mathematical ideas plainly travel without armies, so the 'only conquest transmits ideas' claim is wrong.

**56. (B)** Correct: Samudragupta's conquests and the Huna invasions establish real warfare, refuting 'purely peaceful' while the culture still stands. Codifying Ayurveda is a cultural achievement and says nothing about the absence of war. Gold coins do not show the empire lacked an army. And Kalidasa's plays are literature, not evidence that no war occurred.

**57. (B)** Correct: the prashasti's purpose is eulogy; the copper-plate's purpose is to document a land grant, so they serve praise versus legal-administrative ends. They are not both mere praise. The 'grant praises conquests / prashasti records land' option swaps the two purposes. And inscriptions were certainly meant to be read and to last, not mere decoration.

**58. (A)** Correct: the best summary keeps both the real, world-influencing achievements and the enduring inequality together, matching the full evidence without overstatement. Saying it left nothing ignores its huge contributions. Saying it had equality but no culture inverts the facts on both counts. And crediting the Mauryan state confuses two dynasties separated by centuries.

**59. (A)** Correct: marriage alliances, obligation-creating land grants and reliance on allied kings are exactly the 'web of relationships' that bound the realm, contrasting with direct rule everywhere. Zero, the pillar and Ajanta are cultural-scientific achievements, not the bonds of governance. Faxian, Socotra and Nalanda concern travel and learning. The Hunas, the Pallavas and Kamarupa describe rivals and decline, not the Guptas' own binding ties.

**60. (D)** Correct: an accurate pi and the rotation insight are genuinely impressive for the era, but a handful of results does not equal all of modern science, so the leap is an overstatement. The results were sound, not wrong. Modern science is not identical to Gupta science; centuries of further discovery followed. And these are Gupta-age, not Mauryan, achievements, so dismissing them as Mauryan is mistaken.
