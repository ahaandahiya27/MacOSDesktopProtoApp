Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

India's "First Urbanisation" refers to:

  • AThe sixteen mahaajanapadas
  • BThe Maurya Empire
  • CThe Harappan (Indus / Sindhu-Sarasvati) civilisation✓
  • DThe Chola kingdom

Working

✓

India's First Urbanisation was the Harappan (Indus / Sindhu-Sarasvati) civilisation, with its planned cities, drains and writing. The mahaajanapadas, Maurya Empire and Cholas all came much later, in or after the SECOND Urbanisation — so they are time-period traps.

Q2

After the Harappan urban order broke down in the early 2nd millennium BCE, urban life in the Subcontinent was largely absent for about:

  • AA hundred years
  • BThree hundred years
  • CFive thousand years
  • DA thousand years✓

Working

✓

After the Harappan order broke down in the early 2nd millennium BCE, urban life was largely absent for about a thousand years before cities returned in the Ganga plains. "Five thousand years" confuses the age of the whole story with the length of the gap.

Q3

A historian argues that the Harappan collapse was not just a loss of buildings but a loss of "urban order." The BEST evidence for this claim is that after the collapse:

  • APeople built even larger cities elsewhere immediately
  • BIron tools suddenly appeared everywhere
  • CPeople largely returned to simple village life, losing trade, writing and administration✓
  • DThe mahaajanapadas were formed within a few years

Working

✓

"Urban order" means trade, writing, sanitation and administration held together — not just buildings. The clinching evidence is that people slipped back into simple village life, losing those systems. Building larger cities (A) or forming mahaajanapadas (D) happened only much later; iron (B) belongs to the later Second Urbanisation.

Q4

Which of these did NOT disappear when the Harappan urban order broke down — because it actually became widespread only LATER?

  • APlanned drainage
  • BThe Harappan writing system
  • CLarge fortified citadels
  • DThe use of iron tools✓

Working

✓

Drainage, writing and citadels were all features of the Harappan cities that were lost. Iron, however, was NOT a Harappan feature at all — the Harappans used copper and bronze — and iron only became widespread later. So iron did not "disappear with" the Harappans; it had not yet arrived.

Q5

The wave of city-building that began in the Ganga plains in the 1st millennium BCE is called the:

  • AVedic Urbanisation
  • BThird Urbanisation
  • CSecond Urbanisation✓
  • DHarappan revival

Working

✓

The 1st-millennium-BCE wave of city-building in the Ganga plains is the Second Urbanisation (the Harappan being the First). "Third" and "Vedic Urbanisation" are invented labels; "Harappan revival" wrongly implies the old civilisation came back.

Q6

Place these in correct chronological order, earliest first: (i) the sixteen mahaajanapadas, (ii) the Harappan civilisation, (iii) the rise of janapadas, (iv) the first empires.

  • A(ii) → (iii) → (i) → (iv)✓
  • B(i) → (ii) → (iii) → (iv)
  • C(ii) → (i) → (iii) → (iv)
  • D(iii) → (ii) → (i) → (iv)

Working

✓

Correct order: Harappan civilisation (ii) → janapadas form (iii) → these merge into the sixteen mahaajanapadas (i) → the first empires (iv). Option C wrongly puts the mahaajanapadas before the janapadas they grew out of.

Q7

A historian studying the Second Urbanisation relies mainly on TWO kinds of sources. They are:

  • AArchaeology and ancient literature✓
  • BPhotographs and newspapers
  • CTelevision and the internet
  • DCoins and the Constitution

Working

✓

Because there are no written histories from that age, historians use two main sources: archaeology (digging up city remains) and ancient literature (books that name and describe the cities). Photographs, TV and the Constitution are all modern, so the other options are anachronisms.

Q8

Why can a historian be MOST confident that the Second Urbanisation really happened the way described?

  • ABecause one ancient poem says so
  • BBecause modern cities look old
  • CBecause the Harappans wrote it down
  • DBecause both excavated city remains and ancient books agree about it✓

Working

✓

Confidence is highest when two independent kinds of evidence agree — the excavated city remains AND the ancient books. A single poem (A) or "looking old" (B) is weak; the Harappans (C) did not write about the Second Urbanisation.

Q9

The Sanskrit word "janapada" is made of jana ("people") + pada ("foot"). It therefore means:

  • AA king's army
  • BWhere the people have set foot (settled)✓
  • CA silver coin
  • DA great empire

Working

✓

jana = "people", pada = "foot", so janapada means "where the people have set foot", i.e. where a clan has settled. The other options confuse it with an army, a coin or an empire.

Q10

A janapada was best described as:

  • AA wandering band with no fixed land
  • BA trading guild with no ruler
  • CA territory settled by a clan and led by a rajaa✓
  • DA single fortified gateway

Working

✓

A janapada was a territory settled by a clan and led by a rajaa — settled, not wandering (A), and it did have a ruler, unlike the "no ruler" guild in B. A single gateway (D) is far too small.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

Why are janapadas regarded as among India's earliest states of this period?

  • ABecause they had no rulers at all
  • BBecause settled clans identified with their land, needed a rajaa to lead and protect it, and grew through trade✓
  • CBecause they were built by the Harappans
  • DBecause they used iron coins

Working

✓

Janapadas count as early states because settled clans identified with their land, needed a rajaa to lead and protect them, and grew larger through trade — the marks of an organised state. They did have rulers (so A is wrong) and were not Harappan (C) or coin-based (D).

Q12

By roughly which centuries BCE had janapadas merged into the larger mahaajanapadas?

  • AThe 2nd–1st centuries BCE
  • BThe 4th–3rd centuries CE
  • CThe 20th–19th centuries BCE
  • DThe 8th–7th centuries BCE✓

Working

✓

Janapadas merged into mahaajanapadas by about the 8th–7th centuries BCE. The 2nd–1st centuries BCE (A) is the age of empires, the 20th–19th centuries BCE (C) is Harappan times, and "centuries CE" (B) is far too late.

Q13

The prefix "maha-" in "mahaajanapada" tells you the word means:

  • AA small village
  • BA river crossing
  • CA royal coin
  • DA great janapada✓

Working

✓

"maha-" means "great", so mahaajanapada = "great janapada" (a bigger state formed by merging). The other options ignore the word's parts entirely.

Q14

The most frequently cited list names how many mahaajanapadas?

  • A16✓
  • B12
  • C10
  • D20

Working

✓

The most frequently cited list names 16 mahaajanapadas. 10, 12 and 20 are near-miss number traps.

Q15

The mahaajanapadas stretched from Gandhaara in the northwest to Anga in the east, and to Ashmaka in central India near which river?

  • AThe Godavari✓
  • BThe Sindhu
  • CThe Ganga
  • DThe Kaveri

Working

✓

Ashmaka lay in central India near the Godavari River. The Ganga and Sindhu belong to the north, and the Kaveri lies far to the south — geography traps.

Q16

Why were SO MANY mahaajanapadas concentrated in the Ganga plains rather than elsewhere?

  • AThe plains were cold and dry, which suited horses
  • BThe plains had no rivers, so armies could march freely
  • CFertile soil gave food surpluses, iron was nearby, and trade routes tied the region together✓
  • DThe Harappans had already built their capitals there

Working

✓

The Ganga plains were ideal because fertile soil produced food surpluses, iron sources were nearby, and trade routes linked the region — exactly the conditions big states need. The plains were warm and well-watered (not cold/dry, A; not riverless, B), and the Harappans had not built there (D).

Q17

A typical mahaajanapada capital was protected by ramparts AND, just outside the walls, by a moat. A moat was:

  • AA tall stone watchtower
  • BAn open market square
  • CA deep, wide, water-filled ditch for defence✓
  • DThe king's private palace

Working

✓

A moat is a deep, wide, water-filled ditch outside the walls, for defence. A watchtower (A), market (B) and palace (D) are all parts of a city but not a moat.

Q18

The gateways through a capital's walls were often kept deliberately narrow MAINLY so that:

  • AThe city would look beautiful
  • BRainwater could drain out faster
  • CA few guards could control who and what entered or left✓
  • DElephants could pass two at a time

Working

✓

Narrow gateways let a few guards control who and what entered or left (useful for defence and for taxing trade). Beauty (A), drainage (B) and elephant traffic (D) are not the chapter's reason.

Q19

Rajagriha, Kaushambi and Varanasi were mahaajanapada capitals about 2,500 years ago and are still busy cities today. This remarkable continuity MOST strongly suggests that:

  • ANothing about these places has ever changed
  • BThe Harappans must have founded them
  • CThey were never actually fortified
  • DTheir sites had lasting natural and strategic advantages✓

Working

✓

That cities like Rajagriha and Varanasi are still alive 2,500 years later suggests their sites had lasting natural and strategic advantages that keep attracting people. It does not mean nothing ever changed (A), that the Harappans founded them (B), or that they were unfortified (C).

Q20

Which set lists mahaajanapadas that were MONARCHIES?

  • AVajji and Malla
  • BSabhaa and Samiti
  • CCholas and Cheras
  • DMagadha, Kosala and Avanti✓

Working

✓

Magadha, Kosala and Avanti were monarchies. Vajji and Malla (A) were the republics; sabhaa/samiti (B) are assemblies, not states; Cholas and Cheras (C) were southern kingdoms.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

In each janapada, the rajaa was expected to take counsel from assemblies called the:

  • AUttaraapatha and Dakshinaapatha
  • Bvarna and jaati
  • CCholas and Pandyas
  • Dsabhaa and samiti✓

Working

✓

The advisory assemblies were the sabhaa and samiti. Uttaraapatha/Dakshinaapatha (A) are trade routes, varna/jaati (B) is the social system, and the Cholas/Pandyas (C) are southern kingdoms.

Q22

The words sabhaa and samiti first appear in:

  • AThe Vedas✓
  • BJain texts
  • CBuddhist texts
  • DTamil literature

Working

✓

The words sabhaa and samiti first appear in the Vedas — the oldest texts — showing how ancient the idea of assemblies is. Buddhist, Jain and Tamil texts came later.

Q23

Most members of the sabhaa or samiti were probably:

  • AThe elders of the clan✓
  • BForeign merchants
  • CChildren of the rajaa
  • DSoldiers of the army

Working

✓

The assemblies were made up mostly of the elders of the clan, whose experience commanded respect. Foreign merchants, royal children and soldiers were not their main members.

Q24

"A good rajaa was not supposed to rule by himself or do whatever he liked." This statement BEST shows that even in monarchies:

  • AThe rajaa had no real power
  • BThe people elected the rajaa every year
  • CThe rajaa was expected to take advice from the assemblies and ministers✓
  • DThere was no army at all

Working

✓

That a good rajaa was "not supposed to rule by himself" shows even monarchs were expected to take advice from assemblies and ministers. It does not mean he had no power (A), that he was elected yearly (B — that fits republics, not monarchies), or that there was no army (D).

Q25

In a monarchy, the position of rajaa was usually hereditary. This means the next rajaa was normally:

  • AChosen by the assembly through a vote
  • BA foreign conqueror
  • CThe richest merchant of the city
  • DThe son of the previous rajaa✓

Working

✓

"Hereditary" means the position passed by birth, so the next rajaa was normally the son of the previous one. Being chosen by an assembly (A) describes the gana/sangha republics, not a monarchy.

Q26

Which of the following was NOT typically among a monarch's main duties as described in the chapter?

  • ASelecting his successor by public election✓
  • BKeeping law and order
  • CCollecting revenue (taxes)
  • DMaintaining an army and fortifications

Working

✓

A monarch's duties included revenue (taxes), law and order, and an army with fortifications. He did NOT select his successor by public election — succession was hereditary — so that is the odd one out.

Q27

A mahaajanapada that was NOT a monarchy — where the assembly took major decisions — was called a:

  • Agana or sangha✓
  • Bjanapada
  • Cvarna
  • Dmoat

Working

✓

A non-monarchy where the assembly took major decisions was a gana or sangha. A janapada (B) is just a settled territory, a varna (C) is a social category, and a moat (D) is a ditch.

Q28

Which TWO mahaajanapadas were ganas or sanghas ("early republics")?

  • AMagadha and Kosala
  • BAvanti and Anga
  • CVajji (Vrijji) and Malla✓
  • DGandhaara and Kuru

Working

✓

Vajji (Vrijji) and Malla were the ganas/sanghas. Magadha, Kosala, Avanti, Anga, Gandhaara and Kuru in the other options were monarchies.

Q29

In a gana or sangha such as Vajji, the rajaa was:

  • AAlways the eldest son of the previous rajaa
  • BChosen by the Harappan priests
  • CAppointed by a foreign empire
  • DSelected by the assembly through discussion and, if needed, a vote✓

Working

✓

In a gana/sangha the rajaa was selected by the assembly through discussion and, if needed, a vote — not inherited (A), not chosen by Harappan priests (B, an anachronism), and not appointed by a foreign empire (C).

Q30

The single feature that MOST clearly makes Vajji and Malla "early republics" rather than monarchies is that:

  • AThey had stronger armies
  • BThey were larger in area
  • CTheir assemblies took major decisions and even selected the ruler✓
  • DThey used punch-marked coins

Working

✓

What makes Vajji and Malla republics is that their assemblies took major decisions and even selected the ruler — power lay with the group, not one hereditary king. Army size (A), area (B) and coin use (D) are not the defining feature.

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

A classmate says, "Every mahaajanapada was ruled by an all-powerful king." The BEST correction is:

  • A"True — there were no exceptions."
  • B"No — none of them had kings at all."
  • C"True — but only in the south."
  • D"No — at least Vajji and Malla were ganas/sanghas where the assembly decided major matters."✓

Working

✓

The correct reply is that NOT every mahaajanapada was a monarchy: at least Vajji and Malla were ganas/sanghas where the assembly decided. "No exceptions" (A) and "only in the south" (C) repeat the error; "none had kings" (B) overcorrects, since many WERE monarchies.

Q32

The ganas/sanghas are called "early republics," yet they were NOT modern democracies because:

  • AThey had no assemblies at all
  • BThe rajaa inherited power by birth
  • CDecisions were taken jointly by the heads of leading families, not by every person✓
  • DWomen alone took all decisions

Working

✓

They were "early republics" but not modern democracies because decisions were taken jointly by the heads of leading families, not by every individual. They DID have assemblies (so A is wrong) and the rajaa did NOT inherit power (B describes a monarchy).

Q33

The Harappans were experts in copper and bronze. The big new metal technology of the Second Urbanisation was:

  • AIron✓
  • BGold
  • CSilver
  • DTin

Working

✓

The big new metal of the Second Urbanisation was iron (the Harappans had mastered copper and bronze). Gold, silver and tin are distractors — silver was used for coins, but the transformative tool/weapon metal was iron.

Q34

A student writes, "The Harappan cities used iron tools and weapons." This is wrong because iron:

  • AWas never used in ancient India at all
  • BWas softer than the bronze the Harappans used
  • CWas used only for making coins
  • DBecame widespread only later, by the late 2nd millennium BCE — a technology of the SECOND Urbanisation✓

Working

✓

The student is wrong because iron became widespread only later, by the late 2nd millennium BCE, as a technology of the SECOND Urbanisation — after the Harappans. Iron WAS used in ancient India (so A is false) and it is harder, not softer, than bronze (B), and not used only for coins (C).

Q35

Why was the spread of iron so important for the growth of cities and states?

  • AIron was worshipped as sacred, so people gathered in cities
  • BIron replaced the need for trade routes
  • CIron axes and ploughs cleared forests and worked heavy soil, raising food surpluses that fed cities and armies✓
  • DIron was used to print the first books

Working

✓

Iron mattered because iron axes and ploughs cleared forests and turned heavy soil, creating the food surpluses that fed city populations and armies. It was not worshipped to draw crowds (A), did not replace trade routes (B), and was not used to print books (D).

Q36

Iron tools also made armies stronger. The chapter links this to:

  • AThe disappearance of all warfare
  • BThe growth of bigger kingdoms through war and consolidation✓
  • CThe end of farming
  • DThe invention of writing

Working

✓

Stronger iron weapons made armies more powerful, fuelling wars and the growth of bigger kingdoms through consolidation. Warfare did not disappear (A), farming did not end (C), and writing is unrelated here (D).

Q37

As trade grew, people needed something easier than barter. India's response was its first coins, which were:

  • ASilver punch-marked coins✓
  • BCopper paisa
  • CGold dinars
  • DCowrie shells

Working

✓

India's first coins were silver punch-marked coins. Copper paisa, gold dinars and cowrie shells are later or different forms of money — date/type traps.

Q38

They are called "punch-marked" coins because:

  • AThey were used to punch holes in cloth
  • BThey were round like a fist
  • CThey were given as a reward in boxing
  • DSymbols were punched (stamped) into the soft silver✓

Working

✓

They are "punch-marked" because symbols were punched (stamped) into the soft silver. The fanciful options (cloth-punching, fist shape, boxing) miss the literal meaning.

Q39

The chapter says coins were invented MAINLY in order to:

  • AMake trade easier than swapping goods by barter✓
  • BMake people rich
  • CPay soldiers in gold only
  • DDecorate temples

Working

✓

Coins were invented mainly to make trade easier than barter — a trusted standard-weight piece of metal anyone would accept. Making people rich (B), paying only in gold (C) or decorating temples (D) were not the purpose.

Q40

Why is barter clumsy compared with using coins?

  • ABarter needs a bank
  • BIn barter you must find someone who has what you want AND wants what you have✓
  • CBarter can only be done at night
  • DBarter needs iron tools

Working

✓

Barter is clumsy because you must find someone who has what you want AND wants what you have (a "double coincidence of wants"). Barter needs no bank (A), is not limited to night (C), and does not require iron tools (D).

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

Usually a mahaajanapada made its own coins, but:

  • ANo one ever used another region's coins
  • BOnly the Harappans were allowed to make coins
  • CCoins from neighbouring regions were used and traded as well, even reaching abroad✓
  • DCoins were banned outside the capital

Working

✓

Usually a mahaajanapada minted its own coins, but coins from neighbouring regions were used and traded too, even reaching abroad. So claims that no foreign coins were used (A), that only Harappans minted (B), or that coins were banned outside the capital (D) are wrong.

Q42

The chapter describes Indian society organising itself through a TWO-fold system made up of:

  • Asabhaa and samiti
  • Bjaati and varna✓
  • Cgana and sangha
  • DUttaraapatha and Dakshinaapatha

Working

✓

Society organised itself through a two-fold system of jaati (occupational community) and varna (four broad categories). Sabhaa/samiti (A) are assemblies, gana/sangha (C) are types of state, and the routes (D) are for trade.

Q43

A jaati is best described as:

  • AA water-filled defensive ditch
  • BAn occupational community whose skills and customs pass from parents to children✓
  • CA great trade route
  • DA silver coin

Working

✓

A jaati is an occupational community whose specific skills and customs pass from parents to children. A defensive ditch (A) is a moat, a trade route (C) and a coin (D) are unrelated.

Q44

The varna system named four broad categories. Which varna was expected to defend society and the land and to engage in warfare if necessary?

  • ABrahmins
  • BVaishyas
  • CShudras
  • DKshatriyas✓

Working

✓

The Kshatriyas were expected to defend society and the land and to wage war if necessary. Brahmins handled knowledge and ritual, Vaishyas trade/farming, Shudras service roles.

Q45

According to the chapter, which varna was associated with preserving and spreading knowledge and performing rituals?

  • AKshatriyas
  • BVaishyas
  • CShudras
  • DBrahmins✓

Working

✓

The Brahmins were associated with preserving and spreading knowledge and performing rituals. Kshatriyas (A) were the warriors — the classic mix-up with Q44.

Q46

A student claims, "The varna-jaati system was always rigid and unchanging." The chapter corrects this by noting that:

  • AIt never existed at all
  • BIn the early period it was more flexible, and people could change occupations when circumstances demanded✓
  • CIt applied only to the Harappans
  • DIt became rigid the very day it began

Working

✓

The chapter corrects the "always rigid" idea by noting that early on the system was more flexible, and people could change occupations when circumstances (like a drought) demanded; it grew rigid only later. So "never existed" (A), "only Harappans" (C) and "rigid from day one" (D) are wrong.

Q47

Two famous routes connected India's regions for trade, pilgrimage and travel. The northern one was called the:

  • AUttaraapatha✓
  • BDakshinaapatha
  • CRajagriha road
  • DVindhya path

Working

✓

The northern route was the Uttaraapatha ("uttara" = north). The Dakshinaapatha (B) was the southern route; "Rajagriha road" and "Vindhya path" (C, D) are invented names.

Q48

The Dakshinaapatha route started from Kaushaambi (near today's Prayagraj) and then crossed which range to head south?

  • AThe Vindhya Range✓
  • BThe Aravallis
  • CThe Himalayas
  • DThe Western Ghats

Working

✓

The Dakshinaapatha started near Kaushaambi/Prayagraj and crossed the Vindhya Range to head south. The Himalayas and Aravallis lie to the north/northwest, and the Western Ghats run along the southwest coast — geography traps.

Q49

The two great routes carried not only goods but also:

  • AOnly soldiers, nothing else
  • BPilgrims, armies, ideas and coins✓
  • COnly the rajaa's letters
  • DOnly iron ore

Working

✓

Along the great routes moved not only goods but also pilgrims, armies, ideas and coins — which is how distant regions came to share a culture. The "only…" options (A, C, D) wrongly narrow this.

Q50

Shishupalgarh, an eastern city with a neat square plan and strong fortifications, was the capital of which region?

  • AMagadha
  • BKalinga✓
  • CGandhaara
  • DAvanti

Working

✓

Shishupalgarh, with its square plan and strong fortifications, was the capital of Kalinga in the east. Magadha, Gandhaara and Avanti were elsewhere.

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

Around 400 BCE, which THREE kingdoms emerged in the southern regions of the Subcontinent?

  • AMagadha, Kosala and Avanti
  • BVajji, Malla and Anga
  • CCholas, Cheras and Pandyas✓
  • DKuru, Panchaala and Matsya

Working

✓

Around 400 BCE the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas emerged in the south. Magadha/Kosala/Avanti (A) were northern monarchies, Vajji/Malla/Anga (B) were eastern/republican, and Kuru/Panchaala/Matsya (D) were northern janapadas.

Q52

The south was drawn into trade with the rest of India largely because of its:

  • ACold climate
  • BValuable resources such as gems, gold and spices✓
  • CLack of any rivers
  • DDistance from the sea

Working

✓

The south was drawn into trade because of its valuable resources — gems, gold and spices — which the rest of India wanted. A cold climate (A), lack of rivers (C) or being far from the sea (D) would have discouraged trade, not encouraged it.

Q53

By about 300–200 BCE, the chapter says the Subcontinent had become:

  • AOne vibrant, interconnected land, with goods and culture even reaching beyond India✓
  • BCompletely empty of people
  • CThree separate islands
  • DRuled entirely by the Harappans again

Working

✓

By about 300–200 BCE the Subcontinent had become one vibrant, interconnected land, with goods and culture even reaching beyond India. It was certainly not empty (B), split into islands (C), or back under the Harappans (D).

Q54

Around the same time that India became one interconnected land, the mahaajanapadas:

  • AWere just being founded for the first time
  • BCame to an end, making way for the great empires✓
  • CAll became ganas/sanghas
  • DMoved to the Indus valley

Working

✓

Around that same time the mahaajanapadas came to an end, making way for the great empires. They were not just being founded (A), did not all turn into republics (C), and did not relocate to the Indus (D).

Q55

Which sequence correctly traces a chain of cause and effect from the chapter?

  • ACoins invented → barter began → trade stopped
  • BCities grew → forests appeared → iron disappeared
  • CIron spread → more farmland cleared → food surplus → cities and states grew✓
  • DEmpires formed → janapadas appeared → clans settled

Working

✓

The valid causal chain is: iron spread → more farmland cleared → food surplus → cities and states grew. Option A reverses coins and barter and ends with trade stopping; B has forests appearing and iron vanishing as cities grow (backwards); D reverses the whole timeline (empires before clans).

Q56

Magadha, in today's Bihar, became one of the most powerful mahaajanapadas. From the chapter's logic, the BEST single reason is that it:

  • AHad no rivers, so it was easy to defend
  • BSat on the fertile middle Ganga plain with food surpluses, river transport and nearby resources✓
  • CWas a gana/sangha that elected its rulers
  • DLay far to the south near the Pandyas

Working

✓

Magadha rose because it sat on the fertile middle Ganga plain, giving food surpluses, river transport and access to nearby resources (like iron) — geography and resources gave it the edge. It was not riverless (A) or a southern republic (C, D).

Q57

Working

✓

Coins of one mahaajanapada turning up far away is best explained by trade: coins travelled along the routes between regions. They were not fakes (A); regions did not all share one single coin (C); and coins clearly were used beyond their home city (so D is wrong).
