## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** Path two has just one bulb; path one forces current through two bulbs in a row, which is a harder path. The branch with the easier path carries more current, so the single bulb on path two is brightest. Order of arrival does not decide brightness, since current is not 'used up' on the way. The two-bulb branch is harder, so its bulbs are dimmer, not brighter at the end. And bulbs in different branches need not glow equally.

**2. (B)** A closed bare-wire branch beside the bulb is a short-circuit path that steals almost all the current, so the bulb stays dark. Opening that switch removes the shortcut, so the only remaining path is through the bulb, which now lights. Opening a switch does not raise current everywhere; here it merely redirects it. The switch is in branch P, not the bulb's branch. An open switch makes no push of its own.

**3. (D)** Everything is in ONE series loop, so the same current must pass through every part. A damp thread is a poor conductor, so it limits the current through the whole loop, dimming the bulb. It is not a branch, since the loop has no fork. It adds no push. It did not fully break the loop, because the bulb still glowed faintly.

**4. (C)** When the hinged arm touches both contacts it bridges the gap, which is the CLOSED state, so current flows and the bulb glows. The pupil confused the resting-on-contacts position with 'open'. The arm symbol means open only when it is lifted off a contact. A bridged gap is not a break. A switch certainly controls whether the bulb lights.

**5. (C)** A bare wire across bulb 1 is an easy path beside it, so current skips bulb 1's filament and bulb 1 goes dark. With one less bulb effectively in the loop, the path is easier, so more current flows and bulb 2 brightens. The wire does not stop all current; it redirects it past bulb 1. It adds no push. Bulb 2 is still in the loop, so it does not go dark.

**6. (B)** The circle-with-a-cross is the bulb symbol, and it is present, but the lifted hinged arm is an open switch that breaks the loop, so no current flows and the bulb stays dark. The circle-with-a-cross is indeed the bulb, so that is not the fault. The long-thin and short-thick lines are simply the cell's two terminals, not a sign of a dead cell. A single cell can light a small bulb.

**7. (A)** A bare wire straight across the battery is a very easy path, so almost all the current pours through it rather than through the bulb, leaving the bulb dark, while the heavy current drains the battery quickly. A short adds no push. An extra bare path certainly does affect the circuit by stealing current. The battery is far from unaffected; it discharges rapidly.

**8. (A)** An open switch breaks the only loop, so current stops everywhere immediately and the bulb is dark for that instant. A bulb does not store current to keep glowing. Opening the switch definitely affects the bulb by cutting its current. Opening a switch builds up no push; it simply interrupts the path.

**9. (D)** Switches one-after-another in a single loop are both breaks in that loop; if either is open the loop is incomplete and no current flows, so the bulb is dark. A switch does not give 'half' current when closed; the open one still breaks the loop entirely. One closed switch is not enough when another in series is open. An open switch saves no current; it stops it.

**10. (D)** A ring can look unbroken yet still not conduct if it includes an insulator such as dry plastic in series, which stops the current and keeps the bulb dark — disproving the claim. Two correctly-joined cells would light the bulb, so that is no counter-example. All-copper wiring would also light it. The order of bulb and cell around a single loop does not matter, so that also lights the bulb.

**11. (B)** Two cells pushing forward and two pushing backward in the same row oppose each other and cancel, so the net push is near zero and the bulb is dark or barely lit. The mere count of cells does not help when half oppose the rest. Reversing cells very much matters; it subtracts their push. Four cells do not guarantee leftover push when they are evenly opposed.

**12. (D)** Two forward pushes minus one backward push leaves the net push of about one cell, so the bulb glows roughly as it does on a single cell. Three cells present does not mean triple push when one opposes. One reversed cell does not cancel everything; it only subtracts its own share. The net is two-minus-one, i.e. one cell's worth, not two cells' worth.

**13. (C)** Cells joined the correct way add their pushes regardless of size, so two cells together push harder than the big one by itself, making the bulb brighter with both. A single big cell does not beat the correctly-joined pair. Mismatched cells joined correctly do not cancel; they add. Removing one of two series cells does reduce the total push, so it is not 'no difference'.

**14. (A)** If both cells are reversed but still agree with each other, their pushes still add to the full two-cell amount; the current simply circulates the opposite way, and the bulb glows normally. Reversed cells still push; they are not dead. Reversing does not halve a cell's push. Nor does it double it. What matters is that the two cells agree, which they do.

**15. (D)** A weak but correctly-facing cell still contributes some push in the right direction, so the total rises a little above the two fresh cells — a small brightening, not a full cell's worth. It does not add a whole cell's push because it is nearly dead. A correctly-joined weak cell does not drag the push to zero; only a reversed cell subtracts. And it does add a little, so the glow is not exactly the same.

**16. (C)** A battery is built by joining cells in a row, positive terminal of one to negative terminal of the next; only then do their pushes add. Merely touching cases side by side does not connect them the right way. The cases touching does not chain the terminals correctly. And cells certainly can be combined into a battery when joined end-to-end the proper way.

**17. (B)** More correctly-joined cells raise the push and generally brighten the bulb, but a real filament can only stand so much before it overheats and burns out, so the doubling cannot continue without limit. Brightness is not endlessly proportional to cell count. Adding cells the right way brightens, not dims. And a bulb cannot survive any number of cells; too many destroy it.

**18. (D)** Long-line to long-line means the cells face each other, so their pushes oppose and cancel, leaving the bulb dark or barely lit. Two cell symbols add only when joined positive-to-negative in a row, not facing each other. The orientation of the symbols does represent the real terminal arrangement, so it matters. They do not settle at one cell's brightness; opposing equal pushes cancel toward zero.

**19. (A)** Heating depends on the wire's opposition to current; nichrome opposes much more than copper, so for the same current nichrome gets far hotter and glows while copper barely warms. Copper does carry the current (it is in the loop). In a single series loop the current is the same everywhere, not larger in the nichrome. And copper does not secretly out-heat nichrome; it genuinely heats less.

**20. (A)** A short, thick wire opposes current less, so on the same battery it draws a larger current, and more current means more heat per second, making Y hotter. Length alone does not decide heat; a long thin wire actually limits current. Same material does not mean same heating, because shape changes the opposition. Thin wires carry LESS current, not more, for the same push.

**21. (C)** Heat is produced continuously while current flows, so running twice as long delivers roughly twice the total heat to the water. Time does change the total heat collected, since heating accumulates. The wire does not 'tire' and make less heat over time at steady current. And more time alone increases total heat even without changing the current.

**22. (B)** Being thin and of a high-opposition metal, the filament converts the same current into far more heat than the thick, easy-conducting copper, so only the filament glows. The current is the same in both, not larger in the filament. Current passes through the copper too, since they are in series. The copper's coolness is due to its low opposition, not because it 'cools' the filament.

**23. (D)** Both rely on the heating effect, but they are designed for different temperatures: the bulb filament is pushed white-hot to give light, whereas a heater coil glows only dull red at a lower temperature. Using the same effect does not force the same temperature. A heater genuinely uses the heating effect, not the magnetic one. And they are not the same metal — filaments are tungsten, heaters typically nichrome.

**24. (A)** For a given heating wire, raising the current raises the heat produced each second, so doubling the current increases the heating. Keeping the same wire does not keep heat fixed when the current changes. Current does not 'spend less time' and cool the wire; more current means more heating. And the heating rises with current alone, without needing to thin the wire.

**25. (D)** For the same length on the same battery, the thickest wire opposes least, draws the most current, and so makes the most heat: C then B then A. The thinnest does not automatically make the most heat; it limits the current. Same length and material do not force equal heat when thickness differs. There is no rule that a middle thickness wins; heat simply rises with current.

**26. (B)** Every ordinary wire, copper included, produces at least a little heat when current flows; copper merely produces far less than nichrome, so it feels cool. Copper is not magically zero-heat. It produces less than nichrome, not more. And coiling is not required for heating; a straight wire heats too.

**27. (D)** On the same cell, a shorter, thicker coil opposes current less, draws more current, and so glows hotter. A longer, thinner piece would oppose more and draw less current, glowing cooler. Coating in plastic would not raise the heat produced and would risk melting; it does not increase current. Moving the cell along the same wire changes nothing about the current.

**28. (B)** The magnetic effect of a straight current wraps around the wire, so the direction above the wire is opposite to the direction below it; the needle therefore swings the other way. It does not swing the same way, because the field reverses across the wire. Being below the wire does not shield the compass. And the needle settles to a steady deflection, not endless spinning.

**29. (C)** Because the magnetic effect circles the wire, the left side and the right side point in opposite directions, so the two needles deflect oppositely. They do not swing the same way, since the field reverses across the wire. Two separated compasses do not cancel each other; each responds to the local field. And the needles align with the circling field, not straight at the wire.

**30. (C)** A larger current produces a stronger magnetic effect, so the needle swings through a bigger angle. Adding cells correctly strengthens, not weakens, the current and field. The field very much depends on the current, so the angle is not unchanged. And more current does not cancel the field; it strengthens it.

**31. (C)** Oersted's key discovery was that a flowing current, not a mere wire, creates a magnetic effect that moves the needle; with the current off, nothing happens. A wire alone, with no current, does not deflect the needle. The metal of the wire is not the crucial factor. And the effect needs no prior stroking of the compass.

**32. (D)** A flowing current produces a magnetic effect, so a nearby compass needle will deflect — a clean, non-invasive test through intact insulation. Insulation does not reliably change colour with current. Current does not make an audible hum you can count on through insulation. And current does not add measurable weight to the cable.

**33. (A)** The current's magnetic effect exists only while current flows; switch it off and the effect vanishes, so Earth's field returns the needle toward north-south. The wire does not 'remember' the current and hold the needle. Switching off releases no stored field to deflect it further. And the needle still has Earth's field to settle on, so it does not spin freely.

**34. (C)** Every current-carrying wire, including a heater coil, has a magnetic effect around it; the heater is simply designed to use the heating effect. A wire is not limited to one effect at a time. Heating does not destroy the accompanying magnetic effect. But the coil's magnetic effect is weak and not arranged on an iron core, so it is not a strong electromagnet.

**35. (D)** Since the field circles each wire, 'below the lower wire-position' and 'above the upper' for same-direction currents end up pointing the same way at the compass, so the two effects add and deflect the needle strongly. They do not automatically cancel. The needle settles to a deflection, not endless spinning. And magnetic effects deflect, they do not physically tear the needle off.

**36. (D)** Soft iron drops nearly all its magnetism the moment the current stops, which is why it is used in electromagnets; hard steel keeps much of its magnetism, behaving like a permanent magnet. So they do not both lose it instantly, nor do both keep it forever. And it is the steel, not the soft iron, that retains magnetism — the last option has it backwards.

**37. (A)** An electromagnet's strength rises with both the number of turns and the current; if doubling the turns comes at the cost of a much smaller current, the two changes can roughly offset, so the lift need not double. Turns do affect strength, so they are not irrelevant. Thinner wire (less current) does not strengthen the magnet. And both turns and current matter alongside the core, not the core alone.

**38. (A)** More turns make a stronger electromagnet, so halving the turns weakens it and it lifts fewer pins. Fewer turns do not concentrate current into extra strength. Turns certainly affect strength, so the lift is not unchanged. And there is no magic threshold at 40 turns; 20 turns still gives a working but weaker magnet.

**39. (B)** An electromagnet needs current to be magnetic; with the ends unconnected no current flows, so there is no magnetic effect and the pins are not attracted. A plain soft-iron core is not a permanent magnet on its own. An idle coil stores no magnetism to attract pins. And an unconnected coil neither attracts nor repels.

**40. (B)** Reversing the current swaps the magnet's north and south ends but it remains a magnet, so it still attracts the iron filings. Reversed current still magnetises the core. A magnet attracts unmagnetised iron whichever way it is poled, so it does not repel the filings. And it does not become permanent; it still needs current.

**41. (D)** An electromagnet attracts iron (and steel) but not aluminium, copper, or plastic, so it can pull iron out and leave the rest. Aluminium, copper and brass are all non-magnetic, so none would be lifted. Iron and steel are both magnetic, so a magnet would lift both rather than separate them. And magnets work by being magnetic, not by lightness, so plastic and wood are not lifted.

**42. (B)** A soft-iron core strongly concentrates the magnetic effect, so P is far stronger; wood gives no such boost. Lightness of wood does not help the magnetic effect. The core material makes a large difference, so they are not equal. And wood insulating the coil does nothing to strengthen the magnet.

**43. (C)** More current did strengthen the magnet, but a single observation cannot establish an unlimited faster-than-current rise; in reality the core eventually saturates and the lift levels off. So the pupil's 'forever' conclusion is unsupported. Adding cells does increase the lift, so it is not zero effect. And more current strengthens, not weakens, an electromagnet.

**44. (C)** A soft-iron-cored coil controlled by the button is magnetic only while current flows and loses magnetism at once when it stops — exactly the on/off behaviour wanted. A permanent steel magnet cannot be switched off. A hard-steel core keeps magnetism after the current stops, so it does not switch off cleanly. A coreless copper coil is far too weak to be a 'strong' magnet.

**45. (C)** The bell rings because the electromagnet loses its pull when the contact breaks, letting the armature spring back to remake contact; a permanently magnetic core keeps gripping, so the armature is held and the bell does not repeat. It does not ring faster; it stops repeating. The repeating action depends on the magnetism dropping, so a permanent core breaks it. And a magnet would still attract the armature, so it does grip — that is the problem.

**46. (D)** If the contact can never break, the electromagnet keeps pulling, the armature stays held against the gong, and after the first strike there is no repeat — so a single strike then silence. It does not ring continuously; the breaking step is exactly what makes it repeat. The armature is still pulled, so it does move once. And it does not ring faster; it stops repeating altogether.

**47. (A)** The repeating action needs the armature to spring back after the contact breaks; a stiff, non-flexing bar cannot return to remake contact, so the bell cannot keep ringing. A stiff bar does not give louder repeated strikes; it gives none after the first. Springiness IS needed for the return stroke. And it does not hum a steady tone; the cycle simply fails.

**48. (A)** If the gap is too wide to close even at rest, the contact never makes, so no current flows, the electromagnet never energises, and nothing happens. A wide permanent gap stops the cycle, it does not free it. The armature at rest cannot reach the contact, so there is no first strike. And ordinary low voltage does not jump such a gap, so there is no buzz.

**49. (B)** When the contact breaks the current stops, and a soft-iron electromagnet then loses its magnetism, releasing the armature — so 'keeps full magnetism / held forever' is the wrong step. Current does start the cycle, so that step is fine. The hammer does strike the gong, that step is correct. And the contact breaking is the essential step that makes the bell repeat, so it is not wrong.

**50. (B)** Weak cells push only a feeble current, giving a weak magnetic pull that may be too weak to swing the armature, so the bell rings weakly or not at all. Weak cells do not make a louder bell. The bell's pull depends on the current, so cell strength is not ignored. And weak cells do not speed the cycle; they sap the pull.

**51. (B)** The make-and-break cycle still drives the armature to vibrate rapidly, but with no hammer or gong there is simply no ringing sound. The cycle does not need the gong to run, so the armature does move. It is not held stuck; the contact still breaks each time. And a missing gong does not short or overheat the coil.

**52. (C)** A fuse melts to break the circuit and protect against dangerous current; a bell's contact breaks repeatedly only to make the armature vibrate and ring, which is not protection. Containing a wire does not make something a protective device. Breaking the circuit for a different purpose (ringing) is not protection. And it is the fuse, not the bell, that protects — so the last option is backwards.

**53. (B)** Each fuse should be rated just above its circuit's safe current so it melts before that circuit's wiring overheats — matching the ratings to each circuit. A single 20 A fuse on the 5 A lighting would let dangerous current flow there. A 5 A fuse on the 20 A air-conditioner would blow under normal use. And a fuse far below the normal current would keep nuisance-tripping, so 'lower is always safer' is wrong.

**54. (A)** A thick nail will not melt at the overcurrent, so the excess current keeps flowing through the wiring, which can overheat and ignite — the very risk a fuse prevents. The nail does not 'absorb' current safely. Appliances do not switch themselves off on overload without a fuse. And the nail does not drain current safely to earth; it just conducts the dangerous current onward.

**55. (A)** A fuse must be the weak link: it should melt at a lower current than the wiring can stand, breaking first and protecting the rest. A fuse that carries more without melting would never protect anything. If it stayed cooler than the wiring it would not melt first. And a higher melting point would make it melt LAST, not first — the opposite of what a fuse needs.

**56. (C)** As the current climbs past the fuse's rating, the fuse heats, melts, and breaks the loop, so the bulb goes out. The bulb does not brighten without limit; the fuse cuts the circuit. The current must rise first to make the fuse melt, so 'melts then rises' has the order wrong. And it is the fuse, designed as the weak link, that gives way — not the battery.

**57. (B)** A fuse is destroyed when it blows and must be replaced, whereas an MCB merely trips and can be switched back on after the fault is cleared. The roles are not swapped: the fuse, not the MCB, is the one that is replaced. An MCB is reusable, so not both are thrown away. And both genuinely break the circuit; they do not just sound an alarm.

**58. (A)** A 30 A fuse will not melt until the current is dangerously high — far above what the lighting wiring can safely carry — so the wiring can overheat and ignite while the fuse sits intact. The bigger fuse blows LESS often, not more, which is exactly why it is unsafe. It does conduct fine. And the fuse rating does not change how brightly the lights glow.

**59. (D)** Current must flow first; it heats the thin fuse wire, which glows and then melts, breaking the path and stopping the current. Melting cannot precede heating or the start of current. Glowing and melting also cannot happen before any current flows. And the current does not stop before the heating; the heating is what eventually stops it by melting the wire.

**60. (A)** A fuse works by being the weak link that melts first; a thick copper wire that resists melting would let dangerous current keep flowing and never break the circuit, defeating the purpose. Copper does conduct, so that reason is false. A thick wire melts LESS easily, not too easily. And a fuse must carry LESS than the wiring safely, not more — the last option states the wrong design rule.
