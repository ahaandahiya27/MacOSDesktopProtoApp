## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** Heat is the total thermal energy; it depends on both temperature and the amount (mass) of substance. A small amount of very hot water can hold less total heat than a large amount of moderately warm water, so the litre at 30 °C can contain more heat than the tablespoon at 90 °C. The claim that heat depends only on temperature ignores the amount of substance. Heat and temperature are not the same quantity, so they need not match. And a larger amount is not automatically hotter — temperature is set by how vigorously the particles move, not by how much there is.

**2. (C)** Heat flows from the hotter body to the colder one until both reach the same (equilibrium) temperature; with identical blocks that common value lies between, near 50 °C. Metals readily conduct, so the blocks do not stay separate. Heat stops flowing once temperatures are equal — the cold block cannot overshoot past the hot one. And the hot block warms the cold block, so the pair does not simply drop to the cold value of 20 °C.

**3. (B)** Objects left together in one room reach the same temperature. Metal feels colder because it is a good conductor and pulls heat from the skin quickly, cooling the touch point fast; the sensation is about heat flow, not a lower temperature. The claim that metal always cools lower ignores thermal equilibrium. Plastic does not produce its own heat. And the hand judges both surfaces by the same rule — the difference is conduction speed, not a flaw special to metal.

**4. (C)** Both liquids read 60 °C, so they are at the same temperature, and each is hotter than the room air; heat therefore flows from each liquid into the cooler air. Heat does not flow between the two cups, since they are not in contact and are at the same temperature anyway. Two things at the same temperature are equally hot regardless of how much heat each stores. And being equal in temperature to each other does not stop them losing heat to the colder surroundings.

**5. (B)** A thermometer reads correctly only after its bulb liquid reaches the same temperature as what it touches; the thread then holds steady, and that mark is the temperature. The contents always have a temperature — a steady reading does not mean the temperature vanished. The thermometer is not 'out of heat'; it simply matched the sample. And a thermometer shows temperature, not the total heat content, which would also depend on the amount of substance.

**6. (A)** Temperature does not depend on amount, so a thermometer reads 5 °C in both. The pool holds more heat energy only because it contains so much more water — that is a difference in total heat, not in temperature. More water does not raise the temperature reading. Metal does not 'concentrate' temperature. And 5 °C is well within an ordinary laboratory thermometer's range, so both can be read.

**7. (D)** Clinical thermometers have a kink that traps the thread so the reading stays fixed once the thermometer is removed, letting the nurse read it later at the desk. A laboratory thermometer has no kink, so its thread would drop as it cooled on the way to the desk, and its range is overkill for a fever. The kink is essential, not something to remove. So the laboratory thermometer is the wrong tool for a reading taken away from the body.

**8. (C)** A clinical thermometer reads only about 35–42 °C and its kink locks the thread, so it can neither reach boiling temperatures nor follow a freely changing reading — both fatal for this task. Reading in Celsius does not make it usable beyond its range. The problem is the range and kink, not the length. And the liquid certainly would not freeze in hot water; freezing is not the issue.

**9. (A)** The kink stops the thread from retreating, so it stays at the trapped 39.0 °C; a 37 °C body is not warm enough to push it higher, so the reading stays near 39.0 °C — which is exactly why shaking it down first matters. The thermometer does not reset itself; that is the purpose of shaking. The thread does not fall on its own to 0 °C, again because of the kink. And readings do not accumulate from use to use — the thread simply records the highest temperature reached.

**10. (D)** 38 °C lies inside both scales (35–42 for clinical, −10 to 110 for laboratory), so both can read it; the clinical gives finer divisions near body warmth, while the laboratory's kink-free thread is handy if you watch the milk rise as it heats. The clinical scale begins at 35, so 38 is within it — it is not below the scale. And 38 is well below 110, so it is inside the laboratory range too. Since both cover 38, 'neither can show it' is false.

**11. (D)** Because a body's temperature only ranges over a few degrees around 37 °C, the clinical scale can devote its whole length to 35–42 °C, giving fine divisions for precise fever measurement. The liquid expands just as readily; the narrow range is a design choice, not a limit of the liquid. Cost is not the governing reason — accuracy over the body range is. Both thermometers use Celsius, so a unit difference does not explain the wider laboratory scale.

**12. (B)** A laboratory thermometer has no kink, so its thread moves freely and the reading can shift if conditions or position change; a clinical thermometer's kink locks the thread so its reading is held. Not all thermometers trap their threads — only the clinical kink does. So it is false that both lock or both move freely. And the clinical thermometer, with its kink, is the one that holds its reading steady rather than the one that changes, so the claim that only the clinical reading changes reverses the truth.

**13. (D)** Five small divisions span 5 degrees, so each small division is 5 ÷ 5 = 1 °C. Three divisions above 45 means 45 + 3 = 48 °C. Reading each division as 0.1 °C (giving 45.3) wrongly assumes ten divisions per 5 degrees. Adding the division count to the next number's region (53) double-counts the gap. And 60 ignores the actual marks entirely.

**14. (D)** Each one-degree gap is split into 5 equal divisions, so each division is 1 ÷ 5 = 0.2 °C. Three divisions above 38 means 38 + 3 × 0.2 = 38.6 °C. Treating each division as 0.1 °C (giving 38.3) assumes ten divisions per degree, not five. Adding 3 whole degrees to reach 41 misreads small divisions as whole degrees. And 38.06 misplaces the decimal by a factor of ten.

**15. (A)** Ten divisions per 10 degrees means each division is 1 °C. Four divisions above −10 means moving up (toward warmer) by 4 °C: −10 + 4 = −6 °C. Going the wrong way to −14 treats 'above' as colder. Reading −4 forgets that you start at −10, not −8. And 4 °C ignores the −10 starting mark altogether.

**16. (A)** A has 10 divisions per 10 degrees, so each = 1 °C; B has 5 divisions per 10 degrees, so each = 2 °C. Smaller per-division steps mean finer reading, so A (1 °C) beats B (2 °C). Fewer divisions actually make each division larger, not smaller, so B is coarser. Equal ranges do not guarantee equal precision — division count matters. And A's smallest division is 1 °C, not 0.1 °C, so claiming a 0.1 °C division for A uses a wrong value.

**17. (C)** Starting reading: 20 + 5 = 25 °C; final reading: 20 + 2 = 22 °C; the drop is 25 − 22 = 3 °C. Adding the division numbers (5 + 2) to get 7 is meaningless. Reporting 2 just copies the final division count. And 5 copies the starting division count — neither is the difference.

**18. (A)** Ten divisions across one degree make each division 0.1 °C, so 5 divisions above 37 is 37 + 0.5 = 37.5 °C — the first student is correct. The classmate's 37.05 places the decimal one digit too far, as if each division were 0.01 °C. 37.5 and 37.05 are clearly different numbers. And 42 would require the divisions to be whole degrees, which they are not.

**19. (A)** In a solid metal rod, heat passes from particle to particle by conduction, reaching and melting the nearer wax first and the farther wax later — exactly the observed order. The wax does not generate heat; it merely melts when heat arrives. Solid iron does not flow upward to carry heat — that bodily movement is convection in fluids, not solids. If radiation alone reached each pellet through air, the rod's order and material would not matter, but the sequence along the rod shows conduction through the metal.

**20. (B)** Copper is a good conductor and carries heat to its tip fastest, melting that wax first; glass is poorer and wood poorest, so they follow in that order. Being light does not make wood a good conductor — it is actually an insulator. Glass conducts far less than copper, so it is not the best. And an identical heat source does not make poor and good conductors deliver heat at the same instant; the material decides the rate.

**21. (C)** Metal is a good conductor, so heat from the cocoa travels quickly up the metal spoon to its top, while plastic is an insulator and stays cool at the top — so the metal top feels hotter. Plastic conducts worse, not better, than metal. Sitting in the same cocoa does not equalise the handle temperatures, since the materials conduct at different rates. And heat does travel up a solid by conduction, so 'neither warms' is wrong.

**22. (B)** Conduction relies on particles passing energy to neighbours; solids have tightly packed particles that hand energy along quickly, while a gas's widely spaced particles seldom touch, making conduction feeble. Gases can hold and carry heat (chiefly by convection), so it is false that they hold none. Solids are not inherently colder than gases — temperature is unrelated to the packing. And gas particles actually move fast, but their large spacing, not slowness, makes conduction poor.

**23. (A)** Metal is a good conductor, carrying heat from the hot curry up to the handle; wood is an insulator, so little heat reaches its handle and it stays cool. Wood does not work by reflecting heat back into the curry — it simply conducts poorly. The metal spoon does not create heat; it only transmits the curry's heat. And whether an object floats has nothing to do with conduction along its handle.

**24. (B)** Conduction is the passing of heat from particle to particle, and since both rods are the same metal, both transfer heat by this same process regardless of thickness. Thickness does not switch the mechanism to radiation. Different thickness does not mean different material — they are stated to be the same metal. And a thin rod still conducts; being thin does not stop conduction.

**25. (B)** When water is heated it expands, grows less dense, and rises; cooler denser water sinks to take its place, and this circulation (convection) carries the dye in the looping path described. Conduction does not move the water bodily, so it cannot trace a loop. Radiation does not drive fluid loops within the pan. And the dye is a passive marker — it moves because the water moves, not by itself.

**26. (B)** The heater warms nearby air, which expands, rises, travels along the ceiling, cools at the far wall and sinks — a convection loop, which the smoke makes visible. Air conducts heat very poorly, so the path is not conduction. Radiation travels in straight lines and does not loop around a room. And the smoke's organised circuit is driven by the heater's warmth, so it is not random.

**27. (D)** Air warmed by the stove expands, becomes less dense and rises to collect near the ceiling, while cooler denser air sinks to the floor — a convection current that makes the ceiling thermometer read higher. Conduction through still air is far too slow to carry the stove's heat straight up. Radiation spreads out in all straight-line directions, not only upward, so it would not preferentially pool warmth at the ceiling. And a ceiling is not 'naturally' warmer than a floor in an unheated room — the gradient here is created by the stove's convection.

**28. (C)** Convection requires a fluid — a liquid or gas — whose warmed portions can rise and circulate; in a solid the particles are locked in fixed positions and cannot flow, so heat moves through iron by conduction instead. Paint colour affects radiation, not whether convection can occur in a solid. Heat alone cannot make a rigid solid's particles circulate. And solids certainly can be heated — they just transfer heat by conduction, not convection.

**29. (A)** Convection is driven by warm fluid rising and cold sinking; here the warm water is already on top and the cold below, the stable arrangement, so there is nothing to drive circulation. Hot water is lighter, not heavier, so it does not sit at the bottom. Convection starts when heating happens at the bottom (cold on top, warm below), not the reverse. And water clearly can convect — just not in this already-settled arrangement.

**30. (A)** The warm ground heats the air just above it; that air expands, becomes less dense and rises as a convection current, lifting the smoke. Conduction does not bodily push air upward. Radiation travels in straight rays and does not lift particles by buoyancy. And the smoke rises here because the surrounding air is heated and rising, not because smoke is inherently lighter than all air.

**31. (D)** Radiation travels in straight lines and needs no medium, so heat reaches the face that points at the fire even though the hot air rises straight up and not toward you. Convection here carries hot air upward, away from your face, so it cannot be the cause. Air is a poor conductor and the gap is large, so conduction is negligible. And the fire does not warm you by shoving cold air aside — the direct radiation does the warming.

**32. (B)** Only radiation can cross a vacuum, since it requires no medium; conduction needs touching particles and convection needs a flowing fluid, neither of which exists in empty space. There is no continuous gas filling space to carry convection from the Sun. There is no matter in a vacuum to conduct through. And space, being empty, is very different from air, so the three modes do not act equally there.

**33. (B)** All warm objects radiate, and the hotter an object the more heat it emits each second, so plate X at 300 °C radiates far more than plate Y at 30 °C. Cooler objects radiate less, not more, so plate Y does not win. Equal size and colour do not make their emission equal when their temperatures differ so much. And objects radiate whether in sun or shade — shade is not required.

**34. (A)** A vacuum has no particles, so it cannot support conduction (needs touching particles) or convection (needs a flowing fluid); removing the matter between the walls blocks both. Radiation actually does cross a vacuum, so the gap does not stop it — that is why the inner surfaces are also shiny. Conduction cannot pass through a vacuum, so the 'only convection' claim is wrong. And the vacuum does not stop radiation, so it does not block all three.

**35. (B)** Dull, dark surfaces are good emitters of radiation, so the black surface radiates heat away faster than the shiny silver one at the same temperature. Shiny surfaces are poor emitters, so silver loses heat more slowly, not faster. Equal temperature does not make their emission equal, because surface type matters. And objects radiate their own heat regardless of any light shining on them, so a dark room does not stop emission.

**36. (C)** Radiation needs no medium and crosses a vacuum, so claim 1 is correct. Conduction needs touching particles and convection needs a flowing fluid; a vacuum has neither, so claims 2 and 3 are false. That leaves exactly one correct claim. 'All three' wrongly grants conduction and convection a vacuum path. 'Only two' wrongly counts one of conduction or convection as working. 'None' wrongly denies radiation, which truly does cross a vacuum.

**37. (D)** Copper, aluminium and iron are all metals and good conductors, so this list is all conductors. The list with copper, wood and aluminium hides wood, which is an insulator. The list with iron, plastic and silver hides plastic, an insulator. And the list with aluminium, air and copper hides air, which is one of the best insulators. Only the all-metal list is purely conductors.

**38. (D)** The base is metal (a good conductor) so it passes the stove's heat into the food quickly, while the handle is plastic (an insulator) so it resists conducting heat and stays cool enough to hold safely. Reversing the roles — base resisting, handle conducting — would leave food cold and burn the hand, the opposite of the design. The base does not reflect heat away from food; it conducts heat into it. And the design is about conducting versus insulating, not storing heat then releasing it into the hand.

**39. (C)** Wool traps air and is a poor conductor, so wrapping it around the kettle greatly slows heat from escaping. Aluminium is a good conductor, so it would carry the kettle's heat away to its outer surface quickly — the opposite of what is wanted. Metal does not 'seal in' heat by blocking it; it conducts heat through itself. And the two materials are not equally effective, since wool insulates far better than metal.

**40. (A)** Foam contains many trapped air pockets and is a poor conductor, so heat escapes slowly; a thin metal cup conducts the drink's heat outward fast, cooling it sooner. Foam does not generate heat. Metal is a good conductor, not a better insulator than foam, so the metal cup actually cools faster. And foam does not work by reflecting heat back as light — it works by being a poor conductor with trapped air.

**41. (C)** A handle must be a poor conductor so it stays cool; wood is an insulator and is the right choice. Silver is the best conductor of the four and would get hot fastest — a good conductor makes a bad handle. Copper is also a metal and conducts heat well, so it would heat up. And aluminium, though light, is still a metal and conducts heat well, so 'light metals do not conduct' is false.

**42. (A)** Air's particles are far apart and conduct heat very poorly, so when it is trapped and cannot circulate it lets almost no heat through — making trapped air an excellent insulator. Air conducts far worse than metal, not better. Air does not destroy heat; it simply transmits it slowly. And trapped, still air insulates best — moving air would carry heat away by convection, the opposite of insulating.

**43. (A)** Dark colours absorb more radiation and light colours reflect more, so the black shirt heats up more and the white shirt stays cooler — which is why white is preferred in summer. White reflects rather than absorbs, so the white wearer does not get hotter. Colour clearly does affect absorption, so the two are not the same. And black absorbs rather than reflects, so it does not feel cooler.

**44. (C)** Dark colours absorb more of the Sun's radiation, so in direct winter sun a dark coat warms the wearer more than a white one would — useful in cold weather. Reflecting sunlight would keep the wearer cooler, not warmer, so reflection cannot be the reason. The coat does not manufacture heat; it absorbs the Sun's. And conducting body heat outward faster would cool the wearer, the opposite of warming.

**45. (D)** A dull black surface absorbs more of the Sun's radiation, so the black box warms its inside air more and its thermometer reads higher. White reflects most sunlight, so it absorbs least and stays cooler — it does not absorb the most. Equal sunshine does not give equal readings, because the surfaces absorb different fractions. And black paint does not 'create' heat; it absorbs the Sun's radiation.

**46. (B)** A white roof reflects most of the Sun's radiation, absorbing little, so the rooms stay cooler — the right choice in a hot place. Black reflects little and absorbs much, so it does not reflect sunlight away. Absorbing heat warms a building, so a black roof would make rooms hotter, not cooler. And colour does affect indoor temperature through how much radiation is absorbed, so 'either' is wrong.

**47. (A)** Darker surfaces absorb more solar radiation, so the dark blue car's interior heats up more than the silver-white one's. Pale colours reflect rather than absorb, so the white car absorbs less, not more. Equal sun and time do not equalise them, because the colours absorb different amounts. And the difference is from absorbed radiation, not from paint blocking a breeze.

**48. (C)** Dark colours absorb more of the Sun's radiation and light colours reflect more, so the black cap heats up more and warms the scalp beneath it, while the white cap stays cooler. Pale colours reflect rather than absorb, so the white cap does not warm more. Same cloth and thickness do not make them equal, because colour decides how much radiation is absorbed. And black cloth does not generate its own heat — it simply absorbs the Sun's radiation.

**49. (D)** Land heats faster than water in the day, so air over the warm land rises and cooler sea air flows in to replace it — that inflow is the sea breeze. The sea actually heats more slowly than land, so it is not the sea heating fastest. The Sun does not physically drag air sideways. And the land is the warmer surface in daytime, not the cooler one, so it does not 'draw in' as if cool.

**50. (C)** At night land cools faster than the sea, so the sea stays warmer; air rises over the warm sea and cooler air flows out from the cooled land — that outflow is the land breeze. The land is actually cooler than the sea at night, not warmer. The sea cools more slowly than land, so it is not the sea cooling fastest. And the reversal is caused by the day-night temperature swap between land and sea, not by an automatic rule.

**51. (B)** Land heats faster by day (so it is warmer, air rises there, breeze blows inland) and cools faster by night (so it is cooler, air rises over the warmer sea, breeze blows seaward) — one day-night temperature swap explains both. There is no automatic twelve-hour switch rule; the temperatures cause it. The sea actually stays cooler by day and warmer by night, the reverse of the claim that the sea is warmer by day and cooler by night. And the Moon does not reverse winds; uneven land-sea heating does.

**52. (D)** Breezes are air moving because of uneven heating — warm air rises and cooler air flows in to replace it — which is convection on a coastal scale, with air as the moving fluid. Conduction does not move air bodily across the coast. Radiation is straight-line heat from a source, not the circulation of air. And reflection of sunlight is not a mode of heat transfer that creates winds.

**53. (B)** The driving cause of these breezes is the temperature difference from land and sea heating and cooling at different rates; with no difference, no side is warmer to make air rise, so no breeze forms. Earth's rotation does not by itself create a local sea breeze. Saltiness does not drive these breezes — the temperature difference does. And identical temperatures would remove the difference that breezes need, so they cannot be a requirement for the breeze.

**54. (D)** Before sunrise the land has cooled below the sea overnight, so the sea is warmer; air rises over the warm sea and cool air flows out from the land toward the sea — a land breeze. The land is not warmer than the sea before sunrise, so it is not a sea breeze. The breeze does not require the Sun overhead; it works at night from stored daytime warmth differences. And it is the land, not the sea, that cools faster overnight, so blaming the sea's faster cooling names the wrong surface.

**55. (C)** Two effects help: light colours reflect sunlight so less heat is absorbed, and loose cotton lets air flow and sweat evaporate, carrying heat away — together keeping you cool. Dark clothes absorb, not reflect, sunlight, so dark is worse in summer. Tight clothes restrict airflow and sweat evaporation, so they do not keep you coolest. And in summer we want to lose heat; trapping a warm air layer (what wool does in winter) is the opposite of the goal.

**56. (C)** Loose cotton allows air to move over the skin and soaks up sweat which then evaporates; both processes carry heat away and cool the body. Trapping a still-air layer would keep heat in (good for winter wool, bad for summer cooling), so that is the wrong mechanism. Cotton does not make a cooling chemical. And reflecting body heat 'back inward' would warm the skin, not cool it.

**57. (C)** Wearing two layers traps an extra cushion of still air between them, and trapped air is a poor conductor, so it slows heat escaping and feels warmer than one layer of the same thickness. Weight does not determine warmth — trapped air does. Clothes do not generate body heat. And conducting heat outward faster would cool you, not warm you, so that reverses the effect.

**58. (D)** Puffed feathers trap more still air, and trapped air is a poor conductor, so the bird loses heat more slowly and stays warm. Conducting heat away faster would cool the bird, not warm it. Fluffing rearranges feathers; it does not make the body produce extra heat. And the warmth comes from trapped insulating air, not from reflecting sunlight (which would not work at all on a cloudy freezing morning).

**59. (A)** Wool traps air, a poor conductor, so it slows the loss of the body's own heat — the person stays warm because their heat is kept in, not because the blanket is a heat source. Wool does not generate heat, which is why it is not hot on its own. 'Conducting cold away' is not how warmth works — there is no flow of cold, only heat moving from warm to cool. And the blanket works by slowing heat loss, not by reflecting cold air.

**60. (B)** Down holds many pockets of still trapped air, and trapped air conducts heat very poorly, so a down jacket insulates better than solid rubber of the same thickness. The jacket does not produce heat from sweat. Conducting body heat outward faster would cool the hiker, the opposite of staying warm. And weight does not determine warmth; the trapped air does — down is actually lighter than rubber yet warmer.
