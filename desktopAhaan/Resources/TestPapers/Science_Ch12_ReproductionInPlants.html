Worked Solutions

1. (A) Asexual reproduction needs only one parent and involves no fusion of gametes (no fertilisation); the offspring are clones. Two parents and gamete fusion belong to sexual reproduction, and seeds are not always produced (e.g. a stem cutting).

2. (A) Growing a whole new plant from a piece of stem of one parent — with no flower, pollen or seed — is vegetative propagation, a kind of asexual reproduction.

3. (B) Bryophyllum is the classic example: tiny buds form along the notches at the margins of its leaves, and each can grow into a new plant.

4. (C) The 'eyes' of a potato are buds on the swollen underground stem (tuber); planting a piece with an eye grows a new plant. They are not seeds or roots.

5. (A) In yeast, a small outgrowth (bud) forms on the parent, grows, and detaches to become a new yeast cell — this is budding (also seen in Hydra).

6. (A) Spirogyra breaks (fragments) into pieces, and each piece grows into a new filament. This mode is fragmentation.

7. (D) Both bread mould (Rhizopus) and ferns reproduce by spore formation — tiny spores that spread and germinate into new individuals.

8. (A) Spores have a tough protective coat, so they can survive unfavourable conditions, and being light they are carried far and wide by wind — both are why spores are so successful for fungi and ferns.

9. (A) Potato (tuber buds), ginger (underground stem), sugarcane (stem cuttings) and money plant (stem cuttings) are all everyday examples of vegetative propagation. The other lists mix in seed-bearing plants or budding/fragmenting organisms.

10. (C) In asexual reproduction there is a single parent and no mixing of gametes, so the offspring are genetically identical — clones. Hence the statement is correct, and cuttings do not first make seeds.

11. (C) The stamen is the male reproductive part of the flower (it makes pollen). The pistil/carpel, with its ovary and stigma, is the female part.

12. (D) A stamen has two parts: the anther (which makes pollen) sitting on top of a stalk called the filament.

13. (B) Pollen grains, which carry the male gametes, are produced in the anther of the stamen.

14. (A) The pistil (carpel) is the female part: from top to bottom it is the stigma, then the style, then the ovary (which holds the ovules).

15. (B) The female gametes (eggs) are inside the ovules, which lie within the ovary of the pistil.

16. (A) A flower that carries both stamens (male) and a pistil (female) is bisexual. If it had only one of them it would be unisexual.

17. (C) Separate male and female flowers means each flower has only one kind of reproductive part, so these flowers are unisexual (as in papaya).

18. (D) Pollination is, by definition, the transfer of pollen from the anther (where it is made) to the stigma (where it lands).

19. (B) The stigma, the sticky and often feathery top of the pistil, is where pollen lands. The anther produces pollen and the filament holds the anther up.

20. (A) Pollen reaching the stigma of the SAME plant — even a different flower on that plant — is still self-pollination. Cross-pollination needs a different plant of the same kind.

21. (B) Cross-pollination is when pollen reaches the stigma of a flower on a DIFFERENT plant of the same kind. Same flower or same plant would be self-pollination.

22. (B) Insects are drawn by sight, smell and food, so insect-pollinated flowers tend to be brightly coloured and sweetly scented, with nectar as a reward.

23. (D) Wind cannot aim, so wind-pollinated flowers make pollen that is light and in large amounts, raising the chance that some lands on a stigma. Sticky/heavy or showy pollen suits insects.

24. (C) Small, green, scentless flowers offer no attraction to insects; producing huge amounts of light, dry pollen is the give-away that the agent is wind.

25. (D) Fertilisation is the fusion of the male gamete (from the pollen) with the female gamete (egg) inside the ovule, forming a zygote. Pollen transfer and fruit ripening are separate steps.

26. (D) This is the classic trap: pollination is only the TRANSFER of pollen to the stigma; fertilisation is the later FUSION of the male and female gametes. They are different events, pollination first.

27. (A) After landing on the stigma, the pollen grain grows a pollen tube down through the style to carry the male gamete to the ovule.

28. (B) Fertilisation needs the male gamete (carried by pollen) to reach the egg; that gamete cannot get there until pollen has first been transferred to the stigma. So pollination must come before fertilisation.

29. (D) The fusion of the male and female gametes forms a single cell called the zygote, which later develops into the embryo of the seed.

30. (C) After fertilisation, the ovary ripens and develops into the fruit (which encloses the seeds).

31. (B) After fertilisation, each ovule inside the ovary develops into a seed. (The ovary itself becomes the fruit.)

32. (D) Each seed comes from one ovule, so a fruit with many seeds must have formed from an ovary that contained many ovules.

33. (D) Once fertilisation is done, the parts that are no longer needed — the petals, stamens and stigma — usually wither and fall off, while the ovary grows into the fruit.

34. (A) The correct order is: pollination (pollen reaches stigma) → fertilisation (gametes fuse) → fruit and seeds form. The other orders reverse or misplace these steps.

35. (D) The fruit forms from the ovary, while the seed (the mango stone) forms from the ovule inside that ovary.

36. (A) Dispersal carries seeds away from the parent, which prevents overcrowding and lets the plant colonise new places where there is room, light, water and minerals.

37. (C) If seeds all grew right under the parent, the seedlings would crowd together and fight one another (and the parent) for light, water, minerals and space — exactly what dispersal avoids.

38. (C) A coconut floats because of its light, fibrous, spongy and buoyant husk, so it is well suited to dispersal by water (it can even cross the sea).

39. (B) Wings (maple) and hairs (drumstick) let seeds catch the air and be carried far, so these are adaptations for dispersal by wind.

40. (D) Tiny hooks or spines catch on the fur of passing animals, so the seeds are carried away stuck to the animal — dispersal by animals.

41. (D) Castor and balsam fruits dry, build up tension, and suddenly burst open, flinging seeds away from the parent — dispersal by explosion (sudden bursting).

42. (A) Madar (Calotropis) seeds are very light and bear silky hairs that act like parachutes, so they are carried by wind.

43. (B) When an animal eats a juicy fruit, the tough seeds pass out unharmed in its droppings far from the parent — dispersal by animals.

44. (C) Wind dispersal relies on seeds being light, often with hairs or wings to catch the air. A seed that is heavy with a thick stony coat is the least suited to being blown about.

45. (C) A still-water plant with light, spongy floating fruits is adapted for dispersal by water, which carries the floating fruit to new spots.

46. (A) Seeds form from the fusion of gametes from (usually) two parents, so the offspring can vary and differ from the parents. Vegetative propagation, in contrast, makes identical clones from one parent.

47. (D) To get plants identical to one prized parent, the farmer should use vegetative propagation — stem cuttings — because asexual offspring are clones. Seeds or cross-pollination would introduce variation.

48. (C) Hydra reproduces by budding (a bulge grows and separates). Among the choices, yeast also reproduces by budding.

49. (A) Bread mould (Rhizopus) reproduces by spore formation — the correct pairing. Spirogyra fragments, yeast buds, and the potato propagates vegetatively, so the other pairings are wrong.

50. (C) A money-plant cutting is a piece of STEM that grows roots and becomes a new plant, showing vegetative propagation from a stem.

51. (C) Pollen carries the male gamete and is produced in the anther. It is not made in the ovary, does not become the seed, and lands on the stigma (not the filament).

52. (D) A bisexual flower has both the pollen-bearing anther and the stigma in the same flower, so pollen can reach its own stigma — self-pollination is possible.

53. (B) Different seed structures match different agents: a drumstick's papery wings suit wind, while a coconut's spongy husk suits water. So they are adapted to different dispersal agents.

54. (C) Cultivated bananas are seedless, yet they keep being grown because new banana plants are raised by vegetative propagation from the parent (its stem/sucker), not from seeds.

55. (B) Pollination (pollen on the stigma) does not guarantee fertilisation. If the male gamete never fuses with the egg in the ovule, there is no zygote, so no seed and no fruit form.

56. (B) The life-cycle order is: the flower is pollinated (ii), fertilisation occurs (iv), a fruit with seeds forms (iii), and a seed then germinates into a new plant (i). So (ii) → (iv) → (iii) → (i).

57. (D) Sexual reproduction makes seeds (from fertilisation) inside fruits, so a plant grown from a seed formed in a fruit shows sexual reproduction. Tubers, leaf-buds and cuttings are all vegetative (asexual).

58. (B) The bee carries pollen from one flower to another's stigma, so it is acting as an agent of pollination. (Fertilisation is the later fusion of gametes, which the bee does not perform.)

59. (B) Deliberately cross-pollinating two different varieties to combine their good traits in the offspring is called hybridisation.

60. (C) New plants growing straight from the underground stem, with no flower, seed or pollen, is vegetative propagation — a form of asexual reproduction.
