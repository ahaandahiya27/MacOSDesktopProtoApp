## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** Physical infrastructure covers transport, utilities, communication and energy. The fibre-optic backbone is physical (communication) and the sewage-treatment plant is physical (utility) — two items. The children's park and the magistrate's court are people-serving social infrastructure (recreation and justice), so the count is two, not three. The 'only the sewage plant' answer wrongly demotes the buried network for being invisible, but the split is by function, not visibility, so a communication network still counts. 'All four' confuses funding source with category — sharing a budget does not make a court or a park physical.

**2. (C)** Infra ('below') + structura ('a structure') give the figurative sense of the foundational base supporting everything built on top — it is not a literal demand that the thing sit under the soil. Highways (above ground) and schools (people-serving) are still infrastructure. The 'only physically below ground' reading takes the metaphor literally and wrongly excludes roads. 'Shared between distant places' misdefines infra as 'between' (that is the tele/inter idea, not infra). 'Tallest structures' inverts the meaning entirely, since infra is 'below', not 'above'.

**3. (C)** Classification follows what a facility does: a hospital serves people's health (social), and a power grid supplies energy (physical). Dependence does not transfer category — the hospital draws on energy infrastructure yet remains social itself. Saying the hospital 'becomes' energy infrastructure confuses using power with generating it. Saying the grid 'becomes social' inverts the same error in the other direction. Treating only roads as infrastructure ignores both arms — social services and energy systems are both basic facilities a country needs.

**4. (A)** An expressway moves people and goods, so it is physical transport; a hospital serves health, so it is social; a piped-water main supplies a basic service to homes, so it is a physical utility. The order is therefore physical transport, social, physical utility. The option that ends 'utility, social' swaps the hospital and the water main. The 'physical energy' label misnames water supply as energy. 'Physical communication' misnames water as a communication network — water is a utility, not a signal carrier.

**5. (A)** Development needs all arms working together: utilities (power, water) underpin transport, industry and social services, and social infrastructure (schools, clinics) builds the capable people development depends on. With both failing, good transport cannot compensate — that is the complete reason. Removing the highways and port is absurd; they are assets, not harms. 'Only roads matter' ignores the very gaps causing the lag. 'Develop fastest of all' contradicts the chapter's point that physical infrastructure alone does not guarantee progress.

**6. (C)** Each region must fix the arm it lacks: P already has power but lacks people-serving services, so it needs social infrastructure; Q has services but unreliable power, so it needs energy infrastructure. The reversed answer tells each region to add more of what it already has, which leaves the real gap open. Pouring everything into highways addresses neither gap. 'Neither needs to act' contradicts the chapter's claim that both physical and social arms are needed for full development.

**7. (D)** Better transport shortens supply and delivery times, and reliable power prevents costly stoppages, so production and distribution both get cheaper — that is the chapter's reasoning. The 'dismiss workers' answer invents a layoff mechanism the chapter never claims. 'Raised his costs' contradicts the reported fall and wrongly bills public works to the factory. 'No difference' denies the very effect the owner observed and ignores how infrastructure shapes production costs.

**8. (D)** Physical systems carry goods, power and information; social services build people's health and skills — that pairing is correct. The first wrong option swaps the two roles. 'Both only move goods' erases the human-development role of social infrastructure entirely. The 'cities versus villages' split is an invented geographic divide the chapter never draws; both arms matter everywhere.

**9. (C)** The split is by function, not price: a cheap footpath and a costly expressway both move people, so both are transport infrastructure — cost is irrelevant. 'Only the priciest works count' restates the very error being refuted. 'Everything costs the same' is plainly false and is not how the chapter divides infrastructure. Filing by cheap-versus-costly invents a cost-based split that the chapter explicitly does not use.

**10. (C)** A wind farm generates power (energy); a water pipeline supplies a basic service (utility); a court delivers justice to people (social); a mobile-tower network carries signals (communication). Only the first set matches all four. The second wrongly calls the water pipeline social — supplying water is a utility, not a people-serving service. The third miscategorises the wind farm as social and the court as communication. The fourth swaps the energy and communication labels onto the wrong items.

**11. (B)** The chapter gives two distinct facts — second-largest road network and roughly 20 million rail passengers daily — and never links the two as the same ranking, so the claim is unsupported. 'Always identical' invents a rule the chapter does not state. 'No railways at all' contradicts the 20-million figure. 'Smallest roads' flatly reverses the stated second-largest network.

**12. (B)** The name comes from its four-cornered shape linking four big cities into a four-sided loop, not from a lane count. The 'four lanes' guess confuses geometry of the route with the number of lanes. 'Four small villages' is wrong — it links major metros, not villages. 'Four foreign capitals' moves the network outside India entirely, which is false.

**13. (C)** The Golden Quadrilateral directly connects Delhi and Chennai (two of its four corner cities) by fast highway, ideal for rushing road cargo between them. A metro is an intra-city commuter rail, not an inter-state freight road. NH44 is a major long national highway, not a short Delhi ring road. The Golden Quadrilateral is a road network, not a sea route, so the coastal-lane reasoning is false.

**14. (D)** Both NH44 and expressways are high-speed national roads under the central government, with NH44 noted as among India's longest highways — so they share a category and differ only in specifics. Calling NH44 a metro line misnames a national highway as urban rail. Calling it a sea port and the expressway a power line places them in wrong categories entirely. Labelling NH44 social and the expressway energy ignores that both are physical transport.

**15. (D)** The 1853 railways served colonial extraction, trade and control first; their role as a mass Indian lifeline came later — that is the repurposing the chapter teaches. 'Lifeline from day one' is exactly the misconception being corrected. 'Still serve only the British' ignores the 20-million-a-day Indian ridership today. 'Built by Indians for free travel' is historically false; the British built them for their own purposes.

**16. (B)** The railway principle is that infrastructure built for one purpose can be repurposed for a different, often wider, need — exactly mirrored by an old defensive wall reused as a public park. 'Torn up when the builder leaves' contradicts the principle, since the railways were kept, not destroyed. 'Useless within a few years' denies durability and repurposing. 'Only ever one cargo' is the opposite of the flexibility the principle describes.

**17. (D)** Twenty million is twenty thousand multiplied by one thousand, so reading it as twenty thousand understates the figure roughly one-thousand-fold. 'One hundred times' would turn twenty million into two hundred thousand, not twenty thousand. 'Ten times' gives two million, far too large. 'One million times' would shrink it to twenty, which is the different misread the earlier papers used; here the misread is to twenty thousand, a factor of about a thousand.

**18. (A)** A metro is a high-capacity urban rail system; by carrying crowds on rails it relieves road congestion — the chapter's exact point. Exporting raw materials is a port-and-rail freight role, not a metro's. Generating electricity is the work of energy infrastructure, not a metro. Carrying containers between coasts describes ships and ports, not an urban commuter rail.

**19. (C)** A metro is intra-city rail; an expressway is inter-state high-speed road — different modes and different reach, though both are physical transport. 'Cargo ships' and 'aircraft' belong to ports and aviation, not these two. Labelling a metro social and an expressway energy mistypes both; both are physical transport. 'Both are inter-state roads' is wrong because a metro is rail confined to one city.

**20. (B)** Avis ('bird') points to flight, so aviation infrastructure is airports and aircraft moving people and goods by air. Harbours and ships are sea transport, a different mode. Power stations are energy infrastructure. Pipelines are utilities. Only the air-transport answer follows the bird root.

**21. (C)** The 159 figure is for airports; sea ports are distinct coastal facilities with no matching number stated, so the equality is invented. 'Always equal' is a fabricated rule. 'No airports at all' contradicts the 159 figure itself. 'All 159 are sea ports' relabels airports as ports, which is false.

**22. (D)** TEU counts standard twenty-foot-equivalent containers. Over two days Port A handled 5,000 + 9,000 = 14,000 and Port B handled 8,500 + 8,500 = 17,000, so Port B handled more in total — the larger total, not the single best day, settles 'more containers'. Comparing only best days (9,000 vs 8,500) ignores the full two-day totals and gives the wrong winner. TEU is a container count, not crane speed, so the 'cannot be made' answer misdefines the unit. TEU has nothing to do with a port's height, so the '3,000 metres taller' reading is nonsense.

**23. (A)** A shared unit like the TEU lets unlike ports be ranked on one fair scale of container throughput. Crane height, airport ownership and metro speed are unrelated quantities that a container-handling unit does not and could not measure.

**24. (D)** Two coastlines mean sea routes opening in two directions, widening trade access — the chapter's point. 'Useless coastal ports' is self-contradictory, since ports exist precisely to connect sea and land. The Himalayas are landlocked mountains, the worst place for a sea port. Coastal ports have no bearing on airport counts, so the 'double the airports' answer is unrelated.

**25. (B)** Bose worked on wireless communication around 1895; the 1853 railways were built by the colonial British, so the claim is doubly off — wrong man, wrong field. Changing the date to 1947 keeps the false claim that he built the railway. 'Built by Indians for free travel' is historically false. 'Designed the Golden Quadrilateral' assigns him a modern road project he had nothing to do with.

**26. (A)** Numerically 1853 precedes 1895, which precedes 1947, so railways come first, Bose's wireless work next, and independence last. Putting 1895 before 1853 reverses the first two. Putting 1947 first ignores that independence is the latest of the three. Placing 1895 after 1947 wrongly makes the wireless work the most recent event.

**27. (A)** A coherer was a receiver-side device that picked up and decoded incoming radio-wave signals, which is why Bose's improvement mattered to communication. Generating railway electricity is an energy-infrastructure function. Pumping water is a utility's job. Carrying passengers on rails is a metro's role. None of these is what a coherer did.

**28. (B)** Declining patents suggests a willingness to let science be freely used; and since a patent grants exclusivity, patenting everything would have legally reserved making and selling to him alone for a set period. 'Worthless inventions' contradicts a respected scientist's pioneering work, and patents do not make things work. 'Legally barred' is unsupported, and patents restrict rather than open up copying. Declining patents is not the same as secrecy, and a patent is not a publishing mechanism.

**29. (D)** Tele + communicare means sharing information across a distance, which a long-distance phone call does exactly. Driving goods is transport, not information-sharing. Loading containers is sea freight. Generating wind power is energy production. Only the phone call sends information far, fitting the roots.

**30. (A)** The chapter splits them by what each carries: communication carries information, transport carries people and goods. The first wrong option swaps the two payloads. 'Both only move electricity' is false for both. The 'abroad versus inside India' divide is invented; both operate everywhere.

**31. (A)** Hydro, solar and wind all draw on renewable natural sources — water, sun and wind respectively. 'All burn coal' is false; none of these three is a coal plant. Calling solar parks and wind farms 'social' mistypes energy works as people-serving services. 'Free natural energy means not infrastructure' ignores that the dams, panels and turbines are built facilities that qualify as infrastructure.

**32. (A)** Swapping coal for solar shifts to a clean, renewable source, making the energy infrastructure more sustainable. 'More polluting' is backwards; solar burns no fuel. The switch does not change category — generating power stays physical energy infrastructure regardless of who uses it. 'Useless' contradicts the chapter's positive treatment of solar parks as real energy infrastructure.

**33. (C)** Power and water are inputs everything else depends on — trains, signals, hospitals all need them — so energy and utilities underpin the whole system. 'Only inside private homes' ignores their use across all infrastructure. 'Replace roads and schools' overstates their role; they support rather than substitute. 'Completely unrelated' is the opposite of the dependency the chapter describes.

**34. (A)** Utilis ('useful') fits everyday essential services like water, power and gas delivered to homes and workplaces. Beauty is irrelevant to the name. Fast roads are transport, not utilities. Devices that decode wireless signals are coherers, a communication item, not a utility.

**35. (D)** Sanitation safeguards health, a central goal of social infrastructure, so it is rightly grouped there. It has nothing to do with aircraft speed, port container counts or highway length — those three answers attach sanitation to unrelated physical facilities.

**36. (A)** Social infrastructure builds capable, healthy, educated people who then drive growth — the full chain. Speeding cargo trains is a transport effect, not a social-infrastructure one. 'Replaces physical infrastructure' is false; both arms are needed. 'Benefits only its builders' ignores that schools and hospitals serve the whole population.

**37. (D)** The chapter frames upkeep as a shared duty of citizens and government, so Student 2 is right. 'Citizens have no role' contradicts that shared duty. 'Only foreigners maintain it' is baseless. 'Needs no care' denies the very premise that infrastructure must be looked after to keep serving everyone.

**38. (D)** Proper use, avoiding damage and reporting faults are exactly the citizen behaviours the chapter calls collective responsibility. Breaking taps is the opposite. Ignoring a leak as 'not my job' rejects the shared duty. Waiting on a foreign agency hands off a duty that belongs to citizens and government together.

**39. (B)** Treating shared facilities as everyone's to maintain is the chapter's collective responsibility. Private ownership would assign each to one family, the opposite of shared. 'Government monopoly' barring citizens contradicts the shared duty. 'Foreign maintenance' offloads the duty abroad, which the chapter rejects.

**40. (B)** A living root bridge is grown from tree roots, showing infrastructure can be natural, sustainable and nature-friendly. 'Only concrete and steel' is refuted by the bridge itself. 'Always harmful to the environment' is the opposite of what this eco-friendly example shows. 'Impossible without machines' is disproved by a bridge grown by hand over years.

**41. (A)** Sustainable infrastructure deliberately uses clean energy and eco-friendly materials to limit environmental and biodiversity harm. Choosing the most polluting materials is the opposite goal. Ignoring the environment for speed defeats sustainability. Insisting only on ancient hand tools confuses 'sustainable' with 'primitive' — the aim is low harm, not avoiding modern methods.

**42. (A)** The Arthashastra reference shows India thought about public works long ago, making infrastructure an ancient concern. 'Unknown until the British' is contradicted by this very ancient text. 'Only Western thinkers' ignores Kautilya, an Indian thinker. The Arthashastra is ancient, so claiming it came after a modern highway is chronologically impossible.

**43. (D)** Each claim is false: infrastructure also includes invisible utilities and social services (not only visible roads and buildings); the British built the 1853 railways for their own colonial ends, not to help Indians; and care is a shared duty, not the government's alone. So all three are wrong, not one, two or none.

**44. (B)** The correct option states both arms, includes invisible and natural works, and names shared care — all accurate. The first wrong option falsely limits infrastructure to physical and misstates the colonial railways. The second falsely narrows it to invisible systems and the government alone. The third correctly mentions social services but then wrongly credits Bose with the 1853 railway, which was a British project, not his.

**45. (B)** Social infrastructure serves people's learning, justice, health and recreation: the library (learning) and the court (justice) qualify — two items. The road is transport, the electricity line is energy, the water tank is a utility, and the broadband router is communication; all four are physical. Counting the water tank as social mistakes a utility for a service. Adding the broadband router compounds that error. Counting only the library forgets the court.

**46. (D)** Physical infrastructure spans energy, transport, utilities and communication: the dam (energy), expressway (transport), sewage-treatment plant (utility) and mobile-tower network (communication) make four. The hospital (health) and sports park (recreation) are social. Counting three omits the communication network. Counting five wrongly adds the sports park to physical. Counting two omits both the utility and the communication network.

**47. (A)** The error is importing one infrastructure fact (road size) into an unrelated measure (rail passengers) with no stated link — the chapter never equates the two rankings. The reporter does not deny railways exist; he over-extends a road fact. Airports are not part of his claim, so confusing roads with airports is not the flaw. Ownership is irrelevant to the ranking error he actually makes.

**48. (C)** Road congestion is a transport problem a metro relieves; school completion is a social-infrastructure aim met by building schools — so the pairing is metro then schools. A solar park (energy) and sea port (freight) address neither problem. Swapping schools and the metro misassigns each to the wrong problem. A water pipeline and wind farm are utility and energy works that do not target congestion or schooling.

**49. (A)** Visibility never decides type; function does, so these split into energy, utility and communication — three distinct physical sub-types, not one. Calling all three social ignores that they move and supply things rather than build human capability. 'Must be visible to count' contradicts the chapter's inclusion of buried utilities. 'Visibility is how the chapter sorts' is exactly the false premise being corrected; the chapter sorts by function.

**50. (B)** Dependence does not merge categories: the metro depot stays transport and the solar park stays energy, each typed by its own function. The park does not 'become' transport just because it powers one; nor does the depot 'become' energy by consuming power. Neither turns social merely because commuters benefit — they are both physical infrastructure of different sub-types.

**51. (C)** The chain runs from physical infrastructure (roads, ports) cutting costs and lifting activity, into social infrastructure (the school) educating the next generation — both arms working together, the chapter's core message. 'Only physical matters' ignores the school's role at the chain's end. 'Social alone cut the costs' is backwards; the cost cut came from roads and ports. 'No effect' denies the very development the chain traces.

**52. (C)** The railway principle is repurposing: a tool built for colonial extraction became India's lifeline, so destroying it would have wasted a usable asset. 'Always tear down colonial works' contradicts that very principle. Keeping it only for British-style export ignores its transformation into mass passenger service. 'Useless and faded away' is refuted by the 20-million-a-day figure today.

**53. (B)** Bose's real contribution was wireless research and improving the coherer around 1895, plus declining most patents — that is the accurate description. He built no national highway in 1853, and patenting contradicts his actual choice. He did not design the Golden Quadrilateral or invent the TEU container. He did not invent the railway or found a sea port; those mix him up with British colonial projects.

**54. (C)** Airports and sea ports are both physical transport infrastructure, differing only in mode (air versus sea). Calling them social mistakes who uses them for what they do — both move goods and people, a transport function. Labelling the airport communication and the port energy mistypes both. 'Only roads are transport' wrongly narrows transport to one mode, excluding air and sea.

**55. (D)** The unifying rule is 'basic facility a country needs', divided by function into physical and social — admitting both the buried pipeline (physical utility) and the hospital (social). 'Only visible things' wrongly drops the buried pipeline. 'Only direct people-services' also wrongly drops the pipeline. 'Only the most expensive' invents a cost test the chapter never uses; both qualify.

**56. (B)** Energy underpins everything — trains, signals, hospitals all need power and water — so it is foundational, not least important; that is the rebuttal. 'Only true infrastructure' overcorrects and denies the other arms. 'Trains and hospitals run with no power' is plainly false. 'Energy is purely social' misclassifies physical energy infrastructure and does not rebut the motion.

**57. (C)** An eco-friendly build embodies sustainable infrastructure, and inviting citizens to report faults embodies collective responsibility — two chapter ideas in one project. Railway origins and aviation's meaning are unrelated to a streetlight project. The road-network ranking and Bose's dates do not bear on it either. TEU and the Golden Quadrilateral concern ports and highways, not streetlights or civic care.

**58. (B)** Social infrastructure (a university and a vaccination clinic) builds people's skills and health most directly, while a power line and a container port are physical infrastructure that help only indirectly — so the two social facilities top the capability measure. Cost does not measure capability-building, so ranking by price is wrong. The four are not equal on this measure; social ones are more direct. A port moves containers, not knowledge, so it does not top a capability ranking.

**59. (B)** Only the first claim is true: the 1853 railways did serve British extraction and control. The second is false — Bose chose NOT to patent much of his work, the opposite of patenting all for profit. The third is false — care is a shared duty of citizens and government. So exactly one is correct, not all three, not two, and not none.

**60. (D)** The correct statement classes highways, metros, airports and railways as physical transport and accurately traces the railways from 1853 colonial origins to a 20-million-a-day lifeline. The first wrong option falsely calls highways and airports social and dates the lifeline role to 1853. The second swaps the roles of metros and long-distance railways and mislabels them all energy. The third puts the Golden Quadrilateral abroad and shrinks ridership to 20 people a day.
