## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** The Arabs had already reached Sindh centuries earlier, so the Turkic wave was not the 'first' arrival. The Turkic invasions are the turning point precisely because they penetrated much deeper and finally produced lasting rule (the Ghurids and the Delhi Sultanate), whereas the Arab raids stayed near Sindh. The idea that it was peaceful trade is false - these were invasions; and the Guptas were long gone and were not restored.

**2. (A)** Both claims are true and together they show that what changed was how FAR the invaders reached - the Turkic wave went deep while the Arabs stayed near Sindh - making depth the key to the 'turning point.' Neither wave conquered the whole subcontinent (the south stayed free), both did cross into India, and the Cholas were a southern Indian power, not invaders.

**3. (B)** Resilience means bouncing back and continuing to flourish, not just barely surviving. The strongest evidence is the continued flowering of scholarship (al-Biruni, Bhaskaracharya), devotional thought (Ramanuja, Basava) and temple art DURING the invasions. The other options are either false (kingdoms were not all destroyed; invaders did return repeatedly) or describe a pause in learning, which is the opposite of resilience.

**4. (D)** The claim of cultural collapse is refuted by what was actually CREATED during the period: al-Biruni's and Bhaskaracharya's scholarship, Ramanuja's and Basava's philosophies, and temples from Khajuraho to Brihadishvara. The distractors assert collapse (halted trade, vanished kingdoms, total Ghaznavid rule) - none of which happened; the south flourished and Mahmud only raided.

**5. (C)** The Khyber and similar passes through the Hindu Kush were established overland trade-and-invasion routes, so the north-west became the recurring entry point. India certainly had a long coastline (the Cholas were a sea power); the passes were mountain routes, not Chola naval bases; and the south did have powerful kingdoms - it simply was not reached by these overland invaders.

**6. (D)** The Hindu Shahis defended the north-western frontier - the very region the passes opened up - against the early Turkic invaders. They did not rule the Tamil south (the Cholas did, and built Brihadishvara), did not found the Sultanate (Aibak did, later), and did not sail to Srivijaya (Rajendra Chola did).

**7. (C)** Since the passes were the gateway for overland invaders, sealing them would have made repeated entry far harder - this follows directly from the chapter's emphasis on the north-west as the route in. The Chola navy was a southern sea matter unaffected by northern passes; Mahmud only raided regardless; and al-Biruni's Sanskrit study did not depend on the passes being open.

**8. (C)** A 'gateway' that invaders kept using is a point of vulnerability, the opposite of a 'safe' frontier - hence it is not described as the safest. The north-west was a land frontier, not sea-sealed; it was in the north, not a southern port; and far from protecting India best, it was the very route invaders exploited.

**9. (A)** Ruling requires staying to govern; Mahmud instead seized booty and returned to Ghazni after each of his roughly 17 raids, so he raided but did not rule. He did reach India (repeatedly) and was not defeated every time; he ruled neither the south nor the north; and the Delhi Sultanate was founded later by Aibak, a Ghurid general.

**10. (D)** The famous temple sacked in Mahmud's raids was Somnath; Brihadishvara was the great Chola temple of Rajaraja I in the far south, beyond Mahmud's reach. Angkor Wat is in Cambodia and was not attacked by Mahmud; the original statement is therefore wrong; and Nalanda was a Buddhist university (later destroyed by Bakhtiyar Khilji), not another name for any temple.

**11. (B)** Plunder is loot - valuables grabbed by force, with the raider then leaving - which matches Mahmud's pattern exactly, unlike 'conquest' which implies keeping and governing land. A treaty is an agreement, not loot; and the tower over a temple's sanctum is a vimana, an unrelated term.

**12. (B)** The core distinction is leaving versus staying-to-rule: raiders (like Mahmud) carry off loot and withdraw, while conquerors (like the Ghurids) keep and govern the land. A raider does NOT build an empire (that is what distinguishes him); the words are not synonyms; and the last option simply scrambles the roles.

**13. (C)** Great wealth could tempt one to assume rule, but plunder is collected by raiding and leaving - holding territory is not needed to loot - so the wealth alone does not prove rule (and he did not rule). He clearly entered India repeatedly; he sacked rather than built temples; and he had nothing to do with the Chola navy.

**14. (C)** Mahmud's Ghaznavid raids came first (early 11th century); the Ghurids later defeated Prithviraj (Tarain, 1192) and stayed to rule; and Aibak, a Ghurid general, then founded the Delhi Sultanate. The other orders place Aibak or the Ghurids before Mahmud, or put Aibak before the Ghurid victory that made his Sultanate possible - all out of sequence.

**15. (A)** 'From plunder to rule' marks the move from Mahmud's loot-and-leave raiding to the Ghurids' staying-to-govern - the very transition the chapter highlights. It is not a shift from ruling to raiding; the Cholas remained a sea power; and the Hindu Shahis defended the frontier rather than plundering Ghazni.

**16. (D)** Lasting rule began with the Ghurids because they stayed and governed (Aibak's Sultanate followed), unlike Mahmud who repeatedly withdrew. Both reached India; Mahmud did not govern (he raided), so the roles are not reversed; and the Ghurids came through the north-west, not from the south, and did not absorb the Cholas.

**17. (D)** Al-Biruni mastering Sanskrit to study Indian sciences, religion and customs shows that learning continued and was two-way - foreigners studied India even as Indian ideas spread out. This directly contradicts the claims that foreigners learned nothing, that Indian learning stopped, or that no books were written.

**18. (D)** Two-way learning needs flow in BOTH directions: a foreigner (al-Biruni) studying India, AND Indian knowledge (sine tables) spreading abroad - exactly the pairing given. The other options describe destruction, one-way borrowing, or no exchange at all, none of which is 'two-way.'

**19. (C)** Sine tables travelling outward proves India exported knowledge - it gave as well as received - which is the point of the example. India clearly knew mathematics (Bhaskaracharya, the sine tables themselves); it did contribute, not merely borrow; and its knowledge plainly did leave the subcontinent.

**20. (D)** The counter is that al-Biruni did the genuine scholarly work of learning Sanskrit and carefully studying India - the mark of real inquiry, not propaganda. He certainly did write a study (so 'never wrote anything' is false), he did not refuse to study India, and the destruction of Nalanda and Vikramashila was the work of Bakhtiyar Khilji, not al-Biruni.

**21. (B)** A foreign scholar studying India (al-Biruni) and an Indian advancing mathematics (Bhaskaracharya) together prove scholarship continued, refuting the idea that learning ended. They do not bear on whether India had mathematics (it clearly did), nor on the south's freedom or the Chola navy, which are separate, true facts.

**22. (C)** 'Al-jabr' means 'reunion of broken parts,' from al-Khwarizmi's book, and gives us the word 'algebra.' The cosmic mountain is Mount Meru; a fleet of ships is a navy; and a short Kannada devotional poem is a vachana - each an unrelated term from elsewhere in the chapter.

**23. (B)** Trigonometry deals with triangles and angles, the very tools astronomers needed to chart the heavens - hence its importance. Gateways (toranas), warships (a navy) and Kannada poems (vachanas) are unrelated terms drawn from other parts of the chapter.

**24. (B)** An Arabic-named technique (algebra) and an Indian export (sine tables) appearing side by side show mathematics crossing cultures - an international enterprise. It was not purely Indian and isolated, was not the work of generals, and certainly was not abandoned - Bhaskaracharya was advancing it.

**25. (C)** 'Al-jabr' (reunion of broken parts) gives 'algebra'; a 'vimana' is the tall tower over a South Indian temple's shrine - the student had swapped them. Mount Meru is the cosmic mountain (linked to Angkor Wat), a vachana is the Kannada poem and a navy is the fleet of ships, so the other options just mislabel everything.

**26. (B)** A great Indian mathematician-astronomer flourishing during the invasions shows Indian science endured and advanced - the chapter's resilience theme. His work was Indian, not a foreign invention; mathematics did not vanish; and science was not confined to raided areas - the free south was a major centre of learning too.

**27. (B)** A torana is an ornamental gateway, the kind the Kakatiyas built at Warangal - evidence of southern art flourishing. The tower over a shrine is a vimana; a village assembly is a sabha; and a subordinate ruler is a samanta - distinct terms students may confuse.

**28. (D)** Active southern dynasties building monuments prove the south stayed free and creative even as the north was invaded. This refutes the claims that all India fell, that the Deccan was Ghazni-ruled (Mahmud only raided the north-west and withdrew), or that the south lacked kingdoms - the Kakatiyas and Hoysalas were exactly such kingdoms.

**29. (C)** The Kakatiyas, Hoysalas and Cholas were all Indian powers of the south and Deccan that thrived during the period - the common thread. None were Turkic invaders, none founded the Delhi Sultanate (Aibak did), and none were Central Asian raiders allied with Mahmud.

**30. (C)** Rajaraja built powerful Chola sea power and the Brihadishvara temple, and Rajendra extended Chola reach by sea to Srivijaya - together they show a flourishing southern dynasty. Neither raided Somnath (Mahmud did), neither fought at Tarain (Prithviraj and Ghuri did), and neither founded Nalanda.

**31. (A)** Sailing all the way to Srivijaya in Southeast Asia shows the Cholas were a powerful maritime force reaching far overseas - southern strength independent of the north. They were not landlocked, not Ghurid vassals, and clearly aware of distant lands they could reach by sea.

**32. (D)** A towering Chola temple shows grand architecture thriving in the independent south during the period. The Cholas built it well before the Sultanate (Aibak's Sultanate came later in the north); Mahmud, who raided temples, did not sponsor Chola building; and far from abandoning temples, the south was producing some of its greatest ones.

**33. (A)** A navy is the war-fleet (the Cholas had a strong one); a vimana is the tall tower over a South Indian temple's main shrine (as at Brihadishvara) - the option states both correctly. The others swap the two, conflate them with a torana (gateway), or mislabel them as a vachana (poem) or sabha (assembly).

**34. (A)** The contrast shows two different theatres: a strong maritime south (Cholas) and a north hit by overland raiders - power was split, not unified under any single force. It was not concentrated in Ghazni (Mahmud withdrew there but did not rule India), not wholly maritime (there were many land kingdoms), and not limited to the Hindu Shahis.

**35. (A)** Bhoja of the Paramaras was the scholar-king who united ruling with scholarship. Prithviraj Chauhan was the king defeated at Tarain, Muhammad Ghuri led the Ghurid invaders, and Rajendra Chola was the southern naval king - none described as the Paramara scholar-king.

**36. (B)** The label 'scholar-king' stresses that Bhoja paired ruling with a genuine devotion to scholarship. He did rule (so 'never held a throne' is wrong), he was an Indian Paramara king rather than a foreign invader, and he had no connection to the Chola navy.

**37. (A)** Mahmud's raids fall in the early 11th century; the First Battle of Tarain was 1191 and the Second was 1192. So the order is raids, then First, then Second. The other sequences either put a Tarain battle before Mahmud's raids or reverse the two battles (1192 cannot precede 1191).

**38. (A)** Ghuri LOST the First Battle of Tarain (1191) to Prithviraj, then returned to win the Second (1192) - the classic trap is to reverse them. He did not win in 1191 or conquer all India then, was not killed (he came back the next year), and the Cholas, a distant southern sea power, had no part in these northern land battles.

**39. (C)** Ghuri's win in 1192 broke Prithviraj's resistance and led to lasting Ghurid rule, soon the Delhi Sultanate under Aibak - hence a hinge. He won, not lost, in 1192; the Chola navy was untouched by a northern land battle; and Mahmud's raids came long BEFORE 1192, not after.

**40. (A)** Prithviraj won the First (1191) but lost the Second (1192) - so the 'both' claim is half right and must be corrected to one win and one loss. He did not lose both (he won the first), the original 'won both' is wrong, and these were land battles in the north, not sea battles against the Cholas.

**41. (B)** Reversing the battles would make it seem Prithviraj failed first and then beat back the Ghurids - obscuring how the 1192 defeat actually opened the way to Ghurid rule. The reversal has nothing to do with Mahmud (a Ghaznavid, not a Tarain combatant), the Cholas, or the much earlier Arab arrival in Sindh.

**42. (B)** Aibak, Muhammad Ghuri's general, established the Delhi Sultanate, turning conquest into lasting rule. The Srivijaya voyage was Rajendra Chola's, the careful study of India was al-Biruni's, and the Lingayat movement was Basavanna's - none of which were Aibak's deeds.

**43. (D)** A sultanate is the realm or rule of a sultan, the form of governance Aibak set up at Delhi. A decorative gateway is a torana; an ideas-hall is the Anubhava mandapa; and a short Kannada poem is a vachana - all unrelated terms.

**44. (C)** Bakhtiyar Khilji destroyed Nalanda and Vikramashila, a heavy blow to centres of learning. He did not found the Chola navy (a southern Indian achievement), did not write the Rajatarangini, and did not build Brihadishvara (Rajaraja Chola did).

**45. (D)** Nalanda and Vikramashila were renowned Buddhist universities, so destroying them was a blow to learning - the chapter's point about the cost of the invasions. They were not naval bases, frontier forts or trading ports.

**46. (B)** Aibak founding the Sultanate and Bakhtiyar Khilji destroying the universities together show the conquest both created new rule and destroyed old learning. It did not leave things unchanged, it did go beyond mere raiding to governance (unlike Mahmud), and it had nothing to do with serving the Cholas.

**47. (C)** Vishishtadvaita holds that the world and souls are real and that loving devotion (bhakti) and surrender lead to liberation. It does not declare the world unreal, does not make ritual sacrifice the sole path, and does not deny the gods.

**48. (D)** Basavanna's Anubhava mandapa was a hall in which people of every background could meet and share ideas, embodying his rejection of caste. The Delhi Sultanate was Aibak's political creation, a torana is a gateway, and the Chola navy is a war-fleet - none of them Basava's institution.

**49. (A)** A vachana is a short Kannada poem; Basavanna used vachanas to share his message of devotion and equality. A temple's tower is a vimana, a fleet is a navy, and a gateway is a torana - other terms students may mix up.

**50. (D)** Ramanuja and Basava together demonstrate vibrant new religious thought during the period - part of the resilience theme. This refutes the claims that there was no religious thought, that the age was only war, or that spiritual life was simply replaced by foreign rule.

**51. (A)** Welcoming people of every background to share ideas, while rejecting caste, marks equality and open dialogue as the movement's core values. It did not uphold caste hierarchy, did not promote rule by a sultan, and was a spiritual-social movement, not a campaign of conquest.

**52. (C)** Two great temple complexes from different regions show the period's flourishing temple art and architecture. This contradicts the ideas that there was only warfare and no building, that India was Ghazni-ruled (Mahmud only raided), or that art had ceased.

**53. (B)** Angkor Wat's towers evoking Mount Meru, the cosmic mountain, show Indian ideas reaching Cambodia through cultural influence, not conquest. India did not conquer Cambodia, Cambodia did not rule India, and India had many great temples of its own (Khajuraho, Brihadishvara).

**54. (D)** Mount Meru is the cosmic mountain of Indian cosmology, symbolised by Angkor Wat's five towers - evidence of India's cultural reach. It is not a Ghaznavid fortress, a Chola warship, or a university like Nalanda.

**55. (A)** Magnificent home temples plus influence abroad (Angkor Wat) show India as a flourishing, outward-reaching cultural centre. It was neither barren nor isolated, was not ruled everywhere by invaders (the south stayed free), and certainly did not forbid temple-building.

**56. (D)** A vimana is the tall tower above a temple's main shrine (as at Brihadishvara); a torana is an ornamental gateway (as at Warangal) - the option states both correctly. The others swap them, call them warships, or mislabel them as a vachana (poem) and a sultanate (a sultan's realm).

**57. (A)** The claim is false because large parts of the north and all of the south remained free, with thriving southern dynasties - so not every kingdom fell. There certainly were invaders (so 'no invaders' is wrong), they did not conquer every region, and it was the north, not only the south, that mainly faced them.

**58. (C)** Mahmud raided and withdrew, the Ghurids (Muhammad Ghuri) came to govern, and Aibak founded the Delhi Sultanate - the only fully correct chain. The other options scramble deeds: Mahmud did not found the Sultanate or rule the south, Aibak did not sail to Srivijaya (Rajendra Chola did) or write on algebra, Ghuri did not build Angkor Wat, Nalanda was destroyed by Bakhtiyar Khilji, and the Khyber was guarded by the Hindu Shahis.

**59. (A)** The age's scholars, saints and temples - all flourishing during the invasions - directly disprove 'nothing but ruin.' Invasions did occur (so denying them is wrong), the south was not conquered (it flourished), and learning did not cease - al-Biruni, Bhaskaracharya and others were active throughout.

**60. (B)** All three claims are false: Mahmud merely raided (lasting rule came later under the Ghurids), much of the north and all the south stayed free, and the period was rich in scholarship, devotion and temple art. The other options accept one or more of the three misconceptions - including the false notion that Mahmud ruled India - and so are wrong.
