## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** The water first turns to vapour rising from the warm base (evaporation), the vapour cools and forms droplets on the lid (condensation), and the cold night turns those droplets to solid ice (freezing). The sequence must begin with vapour leaving the liquid; transpiration and infiltration belong to plants and soil, not a sealed jar.

**2. (A)** Both are the single substance water. True water vapour is invisible; what we call steam above a kettle is already tiny condensed droplets. Neither is a different substance or a newly created gas — water simply shifts between states without becoming something else.

**3. (B)** Different micro-conditions let solid, liquid and vapour coexist together. Water is not restricted to one state region-wide, it does not become a different substance when it changes state, and ice can plainly sit on liquid water far from the poles.

**4. (D)** The cycle recirculates a fixed pool of water; only its form and location change while the total stays nearly constant. Water is neither created by rain, destroyed by the Sun, nor lost to space and topped up — those are misreadings of a closed cycle.

**5. (C)** A cloud appears when vapour is cooled enough to condense into droplets — cooling, not heating, is the trigger. Freezing on the surface and infiltration into soil are unrelated to cloud formation aloft.

**6. (A)** Trees pull up water and let it escape as vapour from their leaves — transpiration — so a forest adds far more moisture than bare rock. Condensation removes vapour rather than adding it, and infiltration and precipitation do not push water up from leaves.

**7. (C)** The parcel must first leave the sea as vapour, then condense into a cloud, then fall as rain on land, then return seaward in a river. Any order that puts cloud or rain before evaporation breaks the physical sequence.

**8. (B)** Hail, drizzle and snow are all forms of water falling from the air to the surface, which is precipitation. They are not evaporation (ground to air), nor condensation (cloud formation), nor transpiration (release from leaves).

**9. (C)** Surface coverage is not the same as usable supply. Roughly 97% of all water is salty ocean and most of the rest is frozen or deep, so the fresh usable share is tiny. Coverage cannot be read as either a fresh-water percentage or proof of scarcity of water overall.

**10. (D)** Since roughly 97% of all water is in the oceans, about 970 of the 1000 jugs would be salty. The 71% figure is surface coverage, not a share of the water itself, and the small remainder is the fresh fraction, not the ocean share.

**11. (B)** Glaciers store the largest share of fresh water, but it is frozen and not readily tapped, so everyday use draws on groundwater and surface water. The claim is only half right — the storage fact is correct, the daily-source part is not — and oceans are salty, not a drinking source.

**12. (C)** Oceans hold by far the most water; the frozen ice-caps and glaciers are the largest fresh store next; the water in rivers at any instant is the smallest of the three. Putting ice or rivers ahead of the oceans, or rivers ahead of glaciers, reverses the true scale.

**13. (D)** Equal rain does not guarantee equal availability, because storage, withdrawal and recharge differ between places — fresh water is unevenly distributed. It is not fixed by rainfall alone, not tied to population size, and certainly not spread evenly everywhere.

**14. (B)** A large share of people drawing on a much smaller share of water means each person has less than the world average — clear water stress. The numbers cannot mean surplus, an equal share, or a slice that conveniently expands with population.

**15. (B)** The upper level at which the underground pores are fully saturated is the water table. The aquifer is the water-bearing rock layer itself, infiltration is the seeping process, and a spring is where groundwater emerges at the surface — none names that saturation level.

**16. (D)** An aquifer is a porous layer that stores water and readily gives it up, which fits the gushing sand. Solid granite holds little and yields little, clay traps water without releasing it, and not every underground layer qualifies as an aquifer.

**17. (C)** Water seeping downward through the soil to reach groundwater is infiltration. Evaporation and transpiration move water upward into the air, and condensation forms droplets rather than carrying rain underground.

**18. (C)** A perennial spring keeps flowing because the water table stays above its mouth, whereas a well goes dry when the table drops below its base. Springs do not manufacture water, the failing well is the shallower one, and seasonal rain does not selectively skip a single well.

**19. (D)** The water table generally mirrors the surface, lying closer to the top in hollows and deeper beneath rises. It is not a uniform flat sheet, it is not deepest under the lowest ground, and it does not rise selectively under high mounds.

**20. (A)** A deeper well only reaches lower water; it does not lift the table, and pumping hard from it tends to lower the table further. It cannot push water up, create new aquifer water, or change salty water to fresh.

**21. (A)** With withdrawal now at 120 against recharge of 60, the deficit drains stored water and the table falls year on year. Pumping does not double recharge, does not pull extra rain underground, and the imbalance does not self-correct.

**22. (C)** When recharge of 40 exceeds withdrawal of 25, stored water builds up and the table climbs. The presence of some pumping does not force a fall, and a surplus does not have to reverse itself to 'balance' — it simply accumulates.

**23. (B)** A steady table needs long-run balance: outflow about equal to inflow. Letting withdrawal exceed recharge would steadily deplete it, zero recharge would starve it, and blocking rainfall removes the very source of recharge.

**24. (A)** P loses 20 units a year and Q gains 20, so P's table drops and Q's climbs despite the mirrored figures. Mirror-image numbers do not cancel between separate towns, larger recharge raises rather than lowers a table, and over-pumping lowers rather than raises P's.

**25. (C)** Adding wells while recharge is fixed increases withdrawal, so the deficit grows and the table sinks faster each year. More wells do not narrow the gap, keep it constant, or somehow create extra recharge.

**26. (A)** Falling stores that drop by larger amounts each year (8, then 12, then 16) mean withdrawal exceeded recharge by a widening margin. Equal or smaller withdrawal would have held or raised the store, and zero withdrawal could not drain it.

**27. (A)** More people and industry pump more water out while paving reduces how much rain seeps in, so withdrawal climbs as recharge drops and the table falls. Roads do not attract rain underground, demand is not auto-matched by rainfall, and the issue here is depth, not salinity.

**28. (D)** What matters for recharge is how much rain can infiltrate, and paving sends it running off instead of into the ground. Sealed surfaces reduce, not increase, infiltration; they do not salt the rain; and recharge depends on infiltration, not merely on how much rain falls.

**29. (D)** Without trees to slow and hold the rain, more water runs off and less infiltrates, cutting recharge and lowering the valley table. Deforestation does not freeze soil, halt rainfall, or salt the rain.

**30. (B)** Little inflow with soaring withdrawal and almost no infiltration is the worst case and drains the table quickest. The other three combine more recharge with less waste, which protects or raises the table.

**31. (B)** Open soil lets rain soak in and recharge the table, while tiling plus a drain ships the water away as runoff. Tiles do not store rain underground, the two surfaces do not recharge equally, and a street drain removes water rather than returning it to the aquifer.

**32. (C)** When recharge shrinks but withdrawal stays the same, the balance tips and the table drops faster — the paving, not extra pumping or salinity or assumed less rain, is the cause.

**33. (B)** A long, gradual fall from sustained over-pumping eventually drops the table beneath the well, drying it despite a normal monsoon. A single normal monsoon does not wreck an aquifer, water does not evaporate up through rock, and salinity would not make a well refuse to hold water.

**34. (A)** A falling table forces wells deeper and makes lifting water harder and costlier while shallow wells fail. It does not cause wells to overflow, rainfall to self-adjust, or soil to freeze.

**35. (B)** Dry wells and pricier pumping are signs that the table has dropped — effects, not the root cause. Treating the symptoms by digging deeper ignores the real driver, they are not coincidental, and they certainly do not mean a rising table.

**36. (C)** Over-pumping drops the fresh water level so salty water encroaches into the same ground — the chapter's account of coastal over-extraction. Pumping does not change water's chemistry, rain is not salty, and concrete does not make salt.

**37. (B)** The cause is withdrawal exceeding recharge, so cutting extraction and raising recharge is the real fix. Bigger pumps, deeper wells and imported water only cope with the falling table without addressing why it falls.

**38. (D)** A recharge pit holds the rain so it soaks down into the aquifer, while a drain sends it away. The pit does not change water chemically, it does not pump anything up, and warming is not what drives the seepage.

**39. (A)** These structures gather and store rain or help recharge groundwater so water lasts into dry months. They were not power plants, desalination works, or rain gauges.

**40. (B)** A johad ponds the runoff so it lingers and infiltrates, recharging the aquifer. It does not pump water up, seal off seepage, or speed evaporation — those would not raise the table.

**41. (A)** Drip delivers water straight to the roots with little loss, cutting use and easing groundwater demand. It does not use more water, eliminate watering altogether, or salinise groundwater.

**42. (D)** Drip wets only the crop's roots, leaving the gaps dry where weeds cannot thrive. There is no weedkiller in the water, drip does not flood, and it does not freeze the soil.

**43. (D)** Drip targets each pot's roots with minimal loss, the most water-thrifty choice. Flooding, all-day sprinkling and overhead hosing all spill and evaporate far more water.

**44. (A)** Hard urban surfaces send vast runoff to drains, so capturing it for recharge matters most in cities. Cities do not inherently get more rain, their groundwater is not limitless, and harvesting is not restricted to soil-free sites.

**45. (D)** Mandating recharge structures directly returns rain to the aquifer, slowing the decline. More bore wells increase withdrawal, tiling cuts recharge, and rushing storm water away wastes the rain that could have recharged the ground.

**46. (D)** Irrigation at 62% dominates the demand, so switching it to efficient drip yields the biggest saving. Drinking is the smallest slice, industry at 8% is not the largest, and uses can certainly be reduced.

**47. (C)** Small leaks combine into a sizeable daily waste, so fixing them quickly is the right move. Dismissing the drips ignores the total, opening taps fully wastes far more, and catching drips in buckets only stores the loss rather than stopping it.

**48. (A)** Bucket baths, shutting taps and mending leaks each cut waste, so all three conserve water. The other sets each list habits that increase use or runoff rather than save water.

**49. (B)** A steady decline despite normal rain points to extra tube wells drawing more than the rains replace, depleting storage. Recharge has not outpaced withdrawal, rainfall did not fail (it was normal), and water did not vapourise away.

**50. (C)** A recovering, shallower water table that revives dead wells is the direct evidence of successful recharge. Ocean salinity, rainfall amount and river flow are not measures of what a recharge programme does to groundwater.

**51. (B)** Sealing the floodplain blocks infiltration, so water that once soaked in now pools as floods and the aquifer gains less. It cannot both flood more and recharge more, nor reduce flooding while paved, nor leave recharge unchanged.

**52. (D)** With inflow exceeding outflow, stored water grows and the table climbs. Two water-saving measures do not drain an aquifer, recharge and withdrawal are not forced to match, and removing the measures would end the gain, not cause it.

**53. (C)** With soil and rainfall held equal, the only difference is method, so drip's lower use explains B's longer-lasting well. Flooding does not conserve more, method clearly does matter, and equal rainfall rules out the 'more rain' explanation.

**54. (A)** More tube wells just draw harder on a finite, slowly-recharged aquifer, so faster pumping depletes it and wells fail. Wells make no new water, the aquifer is not unlimited, and tube wells do not produce rain.

**55. (B)** Together these add recharge and cut waste, so inflow gains on outflow and the table rises. The measures save rather than spend water, they do not salinise it, and they certainly do change the table.

**56. (C)** Equal rain can give unequal tables when pumping and infiltration differ between the villages. Equal rain does not force equal tables, concrete lowers rather than raises a table, and well depth does not change the table's level.

**57. (D)** World Water Day is marked on 22 March. 5 June and 22 April are days set aside for the wider environment and the Earth, and 14 November is unrelated to water.

**58. (D)** Trees slow and hold rain so more infiltrates, aiding the recharge that sustained the well; removing them cuts that recharge. Trees do not pump water up, manufacture water, or stop rain from reaching the ground.

**59. (A)** Plants depend on water for growth and yield, so a shortage makes them wilt and produce less. Scarcity does not speed growth, plants cannot conjure vapour to thrive, and the result is stunting, not salinity.

**60. (A)** The chapter's core point is that fresh usable water is limited and must be managed with care. It is not unlimited, conservation is everyone's job not just farmers', and the salty sea is not a drinking supply.
