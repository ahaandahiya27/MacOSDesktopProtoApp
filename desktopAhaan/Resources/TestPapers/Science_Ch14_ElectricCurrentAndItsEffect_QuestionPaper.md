# Olympiad Test Paper — Science (Class 7)
## Chapter 14: Electric Current and its Effect

| | |
|---|---|
| **Subject** | Science (NCERT Class 7) |

| **Total Questions** | 60 (Multiple Choice, single correct) |
| **Maximum Marks** | 240 |
| **Suggested Time** | 90 minutes |

**Marking scheme:** **+4** for each correct answer · **−1** for each wrong answer · **0** if unattempted.

**Instructions:** Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Recall the circuit symbols (cell = one long thin line + one short thick line; battery = two or more such cells in a row; bulb = a circle with a cross inside; closed switch = a closed gap, open switch = a raised arm leaving a gap; wire = a plain line). A bulb glows only when the circuit is a complete (closed) loop. No calculator is needed. Most questions need two to four reasoning steps; watch for traps. Do not write on this paper — mark answers on your answer sheet.

1. In the symbol for a single electric cell, the **longer thin line** and the **shorter thick line** stand for, respectively:
   (A) the positive terminal and the negative terminal
   (B) the negative terminal and the positive terminal
   (C) the wire and the switch
   (D) two equal terminals with no fixed sign

2. A battery is correctly built from three identical cells by joining them so that:
   (A) the positive terminal of one cell touches the positive terminal of the next
   (B) all three negative terminals are joined at one point
   (C) the positive terminal of one cell joins the negative terminal of the next, in a row
   (D) the cells are stacked with a gap of air between each pair

3. Two identical cells are placed in a torch the **wrong way** — positive terminal facing positive terminal in the middle. The bulb will:
   (A) glow twice as bright
   (B) glow at normal brightness
   (C) not glow, because the cells oppose each other
   (D) glow, but only half as bright

4. A circuit has one cell and one bulb, and the bulb glows dimly. A student replaces the single cell with a battery of two cells joined end-to-end (+ to −) the right way. The bulb now:
   (A) glows more brightly
   (B) glows more dimly
   (C) goes out completely
   (D) glows exactly as before

5. The symbol drawn as a **small circle with a cross (×) inside it** represents a:
   (A) cell
   (B) switch
   (C) bulb
   (D) fuse

6. Which circuit symbol is drawn simply as a **plain straight line**?
   (A) a cell
   (B) a connecting wire
   (C) a bulb
   (D) an open switch

7. In a circuit diagram an electrician draws a small gap in the line with a tilted arm lifted away from one contact. This symbol means the switch is:
   (A) closed, and current flows
   (B) open, and current does not flow
   (C) a fuse that has melted
   (D) a battery being charged

8. A simple circuit is wired as: cell → wire → bulb → wire → **open switch** → back to cell. With the switch in this state the bulb is:
   (A) glowing brightly
   (B) glowing dimly
   (C) not glowing
   (D) glowing and then slowly fading

9. A torch bulb does **not** light when its switch is pressed. Which of these is **not** a possible reason?
   (A) one of the connecting wires is broken
   (B) the cell is dead
   (C) the circuit is a complete closed loop
   (D) the bulb's filament is fused

10. For a bulb to glow in a circuit, the essential condition is that:
    (A) the circuit must be open at one point
    (B) the circuit must form a complete closed loop
    (C) there must be at least three cells
    (D) the switch must be open

11. A battery is made of four identical 1.5 V cells joined correctly in a row (+ to −). The total voltage pushing current round the circuit is about:
    (A) 1.5 V
    (B) 0.375 V
    (C) 6 V
    (D) 4 V

12. Two pieces of wire, P and Q, are each connected in turn across the same cell with the same bulb. With P the bulb is bright; with Q the bulb is dim. The most likely explanation is that:
    (A) wire Q has a higher resistance than wire P
    (B) wire Q is a perfect insulator
    (C) wire P is broken inside
    (D) wire Q has no resistance at all

13. Which of the following is the best **conductor** of electric current, and would let a bulb glow if used as the connecting link?
    (A) a dry wooden stick
    (B) a rubber band
    (C) a copper wire
    (D) a plastic ruler

14. A student joins a bulb to a cell using a strip of **dry rubber** in place of one wire. The bulb does not glow because rubber is:
    (A) a good conductor
    (B) a low-melting-point metal
    (C) magnetic
    (D) an insulator, so the loop is not completed electrically

15. Electric current passing through a wire always makes the wire:
    (A) cooler than the surroundings
    (B) glow with light, whatever the wire
    (C) warmer, because of the heating effect of current
    (D) lose all of its resistance

16. Two wires of the **same length and material** carry the same current. Wire X is thin and has high resistance; wire Y is thick and has low resistance. Compared with Y, wire X gets:
    (A) cooler
    (B) exactly as hot
    (C) hotter
    (D) magnetised but not heated

17. The coil inside an electric room heater is made of **nichrome** rather than copper because nichrome:
    (A) has a very high resistance and a high melting point, so it heats up and glows without melting
    (B) is a much better conductor than copper
    (C) is magnetic
    (D) is cheaper than any other metal

18. As the current through a given heating wire is increased, the amount of heat produced in the wire each second:
    (A) decreases
    (B) becomes zero
    (C) stays exactly the same
    (D) increases

19. The filament of an electric bulb glows white-hot when current flows. It is made of **tungsten** mainly because tungsten:
    (A) is a magnetic metal
    (B) has an extremely high melting point, so it can get white-hot without melting
    (C) is an insulator
    (D) melts very easily at a low temperature

20. Which set of appliances **all** work mainly on the **heating effect** of electric current?
    (A) electric iron, toaster, immersion water heater
    (B) electric bell, crane magnet, loudspeaker
    (C) ceiling fan, mixer, water pump
    (D) compass, bar magnet, torch

21. An electric toaster and a ceiling fan are both running. The toaster mainly uses the ____ effect of current, while the fan mainly uses the ____ effect.
    (A) heating; magnetic
    (B) magnetic; heating
    (C) heating; heating
    (D) magnetic; magnetic

22. An electric fuse is best described as a short piece of wire that:
    (A) is a very good conductor with a high melting point
    (B) increases the current in the circuit
    (C) stores electric charge for later use
    (D) has a low melting point and melts to break the circuit when the current is too high

23. The fuse "protects" the appliances in a circuit. It does this by:
    (A) staying solid and carrying any current, however large
    (B) glowing to warn the user without breaking the circuit
    (C) melting and **breaking** the circuit when the current rises above a safe value
    (D) making the current flow faster

24. A house circuit is rated to carry safely up to 5 A. Which fuse should be fitted in it?
    (A) a 5 A fuse, so it melts only if the current goes above the safe limit
    (B) a 50 A fuse, so it never melts
    (C) a 0.5 A fuse, so it melts during normal use
    (D) no fuse, to avoid interruptions

25. Too many high-power appliances are switched on at the same socket. The current grows very large and the fuse melts. This situation is an example of:
    (A) a short circuit caused by bare wires touching
    (B) overloading the circuit
    (C) the magnetic effect of current
    (D) the cell being connected the wrong way

26. After a fuse has melted ("blown"), the correct action is to:
    (A) replace it with a thicker copper wire so it can never blow again
    (B) find and remove the fault, then fit a new fuse of the correct rating
    (C) join the two melted ends together with tape and reuse it
    (D) replace it with a fuse of a much higher rating to avoid trouble

27. A modern device that does the fuse's job but can simply be **switched back on** after it trips, instead of being replaced, is the:
    (A) tungsten filament
    (B) electromagnet
    (C) electric cell
    (D) MCB (miniature circuit breaker)

28. Someone replaces a blown 5 A fuse with a thick copper wire that will never melt. The danger of doing this is that:
    (A) the bulb will glow more dimly
    (B) the circuit can now overheat and catch fire without anything breaking the current
    (C) the cells will be charged the wrong way
    (D) the magnetic effect will become too strong

29. In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted noticed that a magnetic compass needle placed near a wire **moved** when:
    (A) the wire was simply lying on the table with no current
    (B) the wire was painted a different colour
    (C) an electric current was switched on in the wire
    (D) the wire was made shorter

30. Oersted's experiment showed that an electric current flowing in a wire produces:
    (A) only heat and never any magnetism
    (B) light only
    (C) sound but no magnetism
    (D) a magnetic field around the wire

31. A compass needle lies still below a straight wire. When the current in the wire is switched on, the needle swings aside. If the current is then switched **off**, the needle:
    (A) keeps pointing in the deflected direction for ever
    (B) melts
    (C) starts spinning continuously
    (D) swings back to its original (north–south) direction

32. A current-carrying wire is wound into a coil around a soft-iron nail to make an **electromagnet**. Compared with the straight wire alone, the coil-and-iron arrangement gives:
    (A) a much stronger magnetic effect
    (B) no magnetic effect at all
    (C) a weaker magnetic effect
    (D) only a heating effect, never magnetism

33. Three things are tried on the same electromagnet, one at a time: (i) more turns of wire, (ii) more current, (iii) keeping an iron core inside the coil. Each of these, on its own, makes the electromagnet:
    (A) weaker
    (B) stronger
    (C) lose its magnetism
    (D) reverse into an insulator

34. The biggest reason an electromagnet is more useful than an ordinary permanent bar magnet for lifting and dropping iron scrap is that an electromagnet:
    (A) is always magnetic and can never be switched off
    (B) works without any electric current
    (C) is made of plastic
    (D) can be switched on to lift and switched off to drop the load

35. A crane at a scrap yard uses a large electromagnet. As the operator drives the crane over a mixed heap, the electromagnet will pick up:
    (A) nothing at all, because electromagnets cannot lift things
    (B) every object in the heap, whatever it is made of
    (C) only the plastic and rubber objects
    (D) iron and steel objects, but not the aluminium, copper or plastic

36. In a working electromagnet the soft-iron core is essential because the iron:
    (A) blocks the current completely
    (B) keeps the magnetism for ever, even with no current
    (C) strongly increases the magnetic effect while current flows, then loses it when current stops
    (D) cools the coil down

37. An electromagnet stops working (loses its magnetism) the moment the:
    (A) iron core is painted
    (B) electric current is switched off
    (C) number of turns is increased
    (D) wire is made of copper

38. In an electric bell, the part that becomes a magnet and pulls the iron arm (armature) towards it when current flows is the:
    (A) gong
    (B) electromagnet (coil on the iron core)
    (C) spring
    (D) hammer head

39. In an electric bell, the moment the electromagnet pulls the armature across and the hammer strikes the gong, the contact at the screw is **broken**. The immediate result is that:
    (A) the current stops, the electromagnet loses its magnetism, and the spring pulls the armature back
    (B) the current grows larger and the bell melts
    (C) the electromagnet becomes a permanent magnet
    (D) the bell rings just once and then stops for good

40. An electric bell rings continuously (a buzzing sound) rather than giving a single strike because:
    (A) the contact keeps making and breaking, so the cycle repeats again and again
    (B) the battery voltage keeps rising
    (C) the electromagnet never switches off once started
    (D) the gong is itself a magnet

41. Which single sequence correctly describes one cycle of an electric bell, starting from the switch being pressed?
    (A) hammer hits gong → current stops → electromagnet pulls armature → spring breaks
    (B) current flows → electromagnet pulls armature → hammer hits gong → contact breaks → current stops → spring returns armature → contact remakes

42. A current-carrying coil with **no** iron core is wound with the same wire and carries the same current as an identical coil **with** a soft-iron core inside it. The coil with the iron core is:
    (A) the stronger magnet
    (B) the weaker magnet
    (C) exactly equal in strength
    (D) not a magnet at all

43. A student wants to make her electromagnet **stronger** without changing the battery. The most sensible single change is to:
    (A) wind many more turns of wire on the same iron core
    (B) remove the iron core
    (C) use fewer turns of wire
    (D) replace the iron core with a wooden stick

44. Which statement about the heating and magnetic effects is correct?
    (A) a current produces only a heating effect, never a magnetic one
    (B) a current produces only a magnetic effect, never a heating one
    (C) a current passing through a wire produces both a heating effect and a magnetic effect at the same time

45. Which device makes use of the **magnetic effect** of electric current?
    (A) an electric toaster
    (B) an electric bell
    (C) an immersion water heater

46. A bulb is joined to a single cell and glows. A second identical cell is now added in the **same direction** (+ to −), making a two-cell battery. Compared with one cell, the bulb is now:
    (A) brighter, because the two cells together push a larger current
    (B) dimmer, because two cells share the work
    (C) off, because two cells cancel out

47. Two cells are joined positive-to-positive and negative-to-negative (facing against each other) before being put in a circuit with a bulb. The bulb:
    (A) glows extra brightly
    (B) glows with twice the current
    (C) glows normally
    (D) does not glow, because the cells push against each other and cancel out

48. A circuit is drawn as: cell → closed switch → bulb → back to cell. The bulb glows. A student then opens the switch only. The bulb goes out because:
    (A) the cell has been used up
    (B) the switch turned into a fuse

    (A) nichrome has a much higher resistance than copper, so far more heat is produced in it

    (A) magnetic; heating

    (A) shorter thick line (negative terminal) round to the longer line

53. A child claims: "Adding a second cell the right way always makes a bulb dimmer." This claim is:
    (A) correct, because more cells slow the current
    (B) wrong; correctly adding a cell increases the push and makes the bulb brighter

54. The reason a thin fuse wire melts before the thick household wiring overheats is that, for the same large current, the thin fuse wire:
    (A) carries no current at all

    (A) correct — that is exactly how a fuse works

56. Which observation would best convince you that a current is flowing in a hidden wire, **without** cutting the wire open?
    (A) a nearby compass needle deflects when the supply is switched on
    (B) the wire changes colour

    (D) the wire becomes longer

57. A coil electromagnet lifts at most 5 paper clips with one cell. With **two cells** joined correctly (more current) and **more turns** added, the number of paper clips it can lift will most likely:
    (A) increase
