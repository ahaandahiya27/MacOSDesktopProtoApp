Worked Solutions

1. (D) Exactly one half of the Moon is sunlit at all times; what changes is how much of that lit half points toward us, and that depends entirely on the Sun-Earth-Moon angle as the Moon orbits. The 'fraction the Sun lights' never changes from one half, so that choice is false. Earth's shadow only touches the Moon during the rare lunar eclipse, not during the ordinary monthly cycle, so the shadow option is the classic misconception. The Earth-Moon distance varies only slightly and is not tied to phase, so it cannot drive the lit-fraction list.

2. (B) A right-angle Moon lit on the right is a first quarter; it is waxing and lies a quarter of the orbit past new Moon. One week before first quarter the Moon was near new and just emerging as a thin waxing crescent, lit on the right and visible briefly in the west after sunset. A left-lit waning crescent belongs to the days before the next new Moon, not before first quarter. A gibbous comes after first quarter, not before. Full Moon is half an orbit past new, far later than a week before first quarter.

3. (A) We see a full Moon when the entire sunlit hemisphere faces Earth, which happens when the Moon is opposite the Sun, on the far side of Earth. Placing it between Earth and Sun gives new Moon, because the lit side then faces away from us. A right-angle position gives a half-lit quarter, not full. Phase depends on orbital position relative to the Sun, never on season.

4. (C) The numbers rise each night, so the Moon is waxing; having just crossed 0.50 it becomes a gibbous moving toward full, so the next reading should be larger, around 0.80, a waxing gibbous. A falling fraction would be waning, which contradicts the rising data. A quarter is a single instant at 0.50, not a value the Moon lingers on. A right-lit Moon at 0.50 is a first quarter, not a last quarter, and the figures are already past 0.50 anyway.

5. (B) A sphere in sunlight always has one half lit, but our view of that lit half changes with the Sun-Earth-Moon angle; near new Moon the lit half points away, so only a sliver shows as a crescent. The Sun is not lighting just a strip; it lights a whole hemisphere always. Earth's shadow is not involved in ordinary phases. The Moon's rock is dull but it still reflects enough to be seen; the crescent shape is geometric, not about absorption.

6. (C) First quarter is at one-quarter of the orbit (a right angle), and full Moon is halfway round. An eighth of the way is partway from new toward first quarter, so less than half is lit and growing: a waxing crescent. Gibbous and full come later, past the quarter and half marks respectively, so they are wrong for an eighth of an orbit.

7. (B) Phase is set by the Sun-Earth-Moon angle, which is essentially the same for everyone on Earth at one instant, so both see a half-lit Moon. They may see it tilted differently or upside-down relative to each other, but the lit fraction is the same. Earth's hemispheres do not change which half of the Moon is sunlit. Both night-side observers can see whatever phase is up; the lit fraction does not drop to zero by location.

8. (A) Phases arise purely from how much of the sunlit half faces us as the Sun-Earth-Moon angle changes; the tilt of the orbit does not remove that angle, so phases continue normally. They do not require any particular plane. Waxing always accompanies the lit half turning toward us as the Moon moves away from the Sun direction, so nothing reverses. Seasons are an Earth-tilt effect, unrelated to lunar phase cause.

9. (B) More than half lit means gibbous; lit on the right (a fattening right side) means waxing, so it lies between first quarter and full: a waxing gibbous. A waning gibbous would be lit on the left as it shrinks toward last quarter. A first quarter is exactly half, not more. A waning crescent is a thin left-lit sliver, far less than half.

10. (D) Half an orbit past full Moon is new Moon, which sits roughly in the Sun's direction and is therefore up during the day, invisible at midnight. It would not be full again until a whole cycle (about a month) passes. First quarter is a quarter of an orbit away, not half. A waning gibbous comes only a few days after full, not two weeks later.

11. (D) Growing lit fraction (0 toward 1) is waxing. Waning is the opposite, shrinking phase, which is the second half. Gibbous names only the more-than-half part of the cycle, not the whole growing half. Eclipsing involves Earth's shadow and the rare eclipse, not the routine waxing of phases.

12. (D) Left-lit and shrinking is a waning Moon heading toward new; it continues to thin into a waning crescent and then new Moon. It will not grow toward full, since that is waxing and would be right-lit in the Northern Hemisphere. The lit fraction changes a little every night, so it does not hold steady for a week. The lit side does not abruptly flip; only after passing new Moon does the next cycle begin lighting the right side.

13. (B) The two crescents differ in which limb is lit because they sit on opposite sides of the new-Moon position: waxing is right-lit, waning is left-lit (Northern Hemisphere). They can be equally bright and equally thin, so brightness and size do not separate them. Colour is not a phase property; both are the same pale reflected white.

14. (B) Being opposite the Sun, the full Moon crosses the meridian (its highest point) at midnight, the moment opposite the Sun's noon. It rose at sunset, so by midnight it is high, not still rising. It does not set until around sunrise, so it is not setting at midnight. The Sun does not pull the Moon below the horizon; that idea has no physical basis here.

15. (A) A thin waxing crescent is angularly near the Sun, so it trails the Sun across the sky and sets shortly after sunset, giving a brief window. Being opposite the Sun describes a full Moon, which is up all night, the opposite case. It is low in the west, not overhead, at sunset. Earth's shadow is irrelevant to ordinary crescent visibility.

16. (B) A last-quarter Moon lags the Sun by about six hours behind in rising, so it comes up near midnight and is highest near dawn. A first quarter leads opposite that, rising near noon and highest at sunset. A full Moon rises at sunset and peaks at midnight. A new Moon rises and sets with the Sun, visible to no one at night.

17. (A) The Moon orbits eastward, so each night it rises about 50 minutes later; meanwhile, moving away from the new-Moon direction turns more of its right-lit half toward us, so it waxes. Waning with a westward drift would mean rising earlier and shrinking, the opposite of both clues. The Moon does not stay fixed; it shifts eastward against the stars. Moving away from the Sun's direction is what makes it grow, so 'approaching the Sun' contradicts the growing data.

18. (D) Because the Moon glows only where sunlight strikes it, the lit limb faces the Sun, even when the Sun is just below the horizon. Pointing away from the Sun would mean the lit side is in shadow, which is impossible. The lit edge can point in any direction depending on the Sun's position, not fixed toward the horizon. Earth's shadow plays no role in lighting a crescent.

19. (C) After new Moon the Moon swings away from the Sun's direction, increasing the Sun-Earth-Moon angle, so more of its lit half faces us and the crescent fattens. The total sunlight it receives does not change with this swing; one half is always lit. Earth's shadow is not involved. The Moon's spin rate is constant and does not change how much of its lit half we see.

20. (A) First quarter trails a quarter orbit one way and last quarter the other way from new Moon, so one leads the Sun across the sky and the other lags, putting their high points at sunset versus sunrise. They are not the same point seen differently; they are genuinely opposite quarter positions. Neither is at full or new. Season does not set the daily high-point timing of a phase.

21. (D) In the Northern Hemisphere the right-lit growing Moon is waxing, so a right-bulging gibbous is a waxing gibbous, not waning. A gibbous is more-than-half lit, which a 'D' bulge represents, so it is not a crescent. Gibbous Moons can be lit on either side depending on waxing or waning; they are not 'always left.' A more-than-half disc is gibbous by definition, not a quarter.

22. (A) Full Moon means the sunlit hemisphere is exactly the hemisphere facing us, so we see a complete disc. The Sun never lights the whole sphere, only ever a half. Earthshine is faint and cannot brighten the far half into view. Distance to the Sun barely changes and is unrelated to the full phase, which is an alignment effect.

23. (C) The same-face fact comes from one spin per orbit (synchronous rotation): the Moon turns just enough each part of its orbit to keep the same side toward Earth. If it did not rotate at all, it would show every side over one orbit. A once-a-day spin would not match its month-long orbit and would not lock the face. Two spins per orbit would let us see different sides, not one face.

24. (D) Without spin, a Moon dragged around its orbit keeps the same fixed orientation in space, so the side facing Earth changes continuously and we would see all of it over one orbit. The same-face view actually requires synchronous spin, so stopping the spin breaks it. Reflection does not depend on spin, so the Moon would not vanish. The change is which side faces us, not an upside-down version of one face.

25. (C) The far side receives sunlight on the same monthly schedule as the near side; it is simply turned away from Earth, so 'far' is right but 'dark' is wrong. It is not permanently dark. Sunlight, not starlight, lights it during its day. Its lighting depends on the Sun's position relative to it, not on the phase Earth observers happen to see.

26. (B) Synchronous rotation keeps one hemisphere toward Earth at all times, so the far side is geometrically hidden from any earthbound viewer while a spacecraft can fly around to it. The far side is sunlit in turn, not in permanent shadow. Orbital speed does not blur a hemisphere out of view. The far side does receive sunlight; it is just never turned toward us.

27. (C) Because the Moon keeps one face toward Earth, an observer on that face always looks toward Earth in nearly the same sky direction, so Earth barely moves for them. Atmosphere does not pin Earth in the sky. The Moon rotates slowly (once a month), not fast. Distance does not freeze Earth's apparent position; the synchronous spin does.

28. (D) Synchronous rotation keeps essentially one hemisphere toward us; small wobbles let us peek a little past the edges, but the face is basically constant, not different nightly. The Moon does not tumble freely. Gravity caused the lock long ago but does not rigidly glue a frozen face; the Moon does rotate, just in step with its orbit. Eclipses do not reveal new faces.

29. (D) One face stays toward us only when spin equals orbit (one-to-one). Three spins per orbit breaks the match, so the side facing Earth keeps changing and the whole surface parades past. Orbit time alone does not fix the face; the spin-to-orbit ratio does. The view would cycle through all longitudes, not just two halves. Phase frequency (full Moons) is set by the Sun-Earth-Moon orbit, not by how fast the Moon spins on its axis.

30. (D) One slow spin per orbit means the same side faces Earth and also means the Sun takes about a month to come back to the same point in the lunar sky, so both facts share the one-spin-per-orbit cause. Lack of atmosphere explains temperature swings, not the spin rate. Small size does not by itself set the spin rate, and the face-locking is not coincidence but synchronous rotation. Earth's position has nothing to do with the length of the lunar day.

31. (B) Synchronous rotation keeps the near side toward Earth, so the Moon's solid body always blocks a straight line from the far side to Earth, requiring a relay. The Moon has essentially no atmosphere, and radio is not blocked by darkness anyway. Earth never comes into the far side's sky because the same hemisphere of the Moon always faces us, not because of Earth's daily spin.

32. (C) Earth's daily axial spin turns us into and out of sunlight, giving day and night, whereas the Moon's monthly orbit changes the Sun-Earth-Moon angle that produces phases. Earth's yearly orbit gives the year, not the day. The Moon's orbit does not cause Earth's day. The Sun does not orbit Earth; that is the old, incorrect picture.

33. (B) At any instant the Sun lights one hemisphere of the spherical Earth; the spin carries different places into and out of that lit half, so two opposite places are in day and night at once. The Sun does not switch off. Day and night come from the daily spin, not from seasonal tilt, which affects day length and warmth, not the basic fact that opposite sides differ. The Moon is not blocking sunlight from a whole country.

34. (A) Earth's west-to-east spin makes all sky objects appear to move east to west together; the daily motion is a reflection of our rotation. The objects do not really circle Earth daily. The yearly orbit causes a slow seasonal shift of constellations, not the daily east-to-west sweep. The Moon's gravity does not drag the sky.

35. (D) The Moon's single slow spin per orbit means the Sun crosses the lunar sky about once a month, making each lunar day roughly a month long. It does rotate, so the Sun does not hang fixed. It does not spin once per 24 hours; that is Earth. The Moon orbits Earth, not the Sun directly, and it is the spin rate that sets day length.

36. (C) The length of the day is set by how fast Earth spins, so doubling the spin halves the day to about twelve hours. The year depends on the orbit, not the spin, so it is unchanged. The Moon's phase cycle depends on its orbit, not Earth's spin. Seasons follow from the axial tilt, which is not altered by spin speed.

37. (B) Twilight on Earth is scattered sunlight in the air; with no atmosphere the Moon has nothing to scatter light, so darkness arrives the instant the Sun sets. The slow spin actually makes lunar sunsets take a long time, but the sky still darkens abruptly because there is no air glow. Horizon distance does not create or remove twilight. Distance from the Sun does not produce twilight; an atmosphere does.

38. (D) Earth turns west to east, so places farther east are carried under any sky object first; the eastern observer sees moonrise earlier by the clock. Eastern places are ahead, not behind, in the daily cycle. Being full at one instant does not make moonrise happen at one clock time everywhere; rise time depends on longitude. Moonrise for a full Moon is near sunset locally, not fixed at midnight.

39. (A) The Moon is not a light source; it only reflects the Sun's light, so cutting off sunlight makes it dark and invisible. Stored heat does not make rock glow with visible light. The Moon has no self-light to fall back on. The red colour in a lunar eclipse comes from sunlight bent through Earth's atmosphere, which is exactly the light being removed here, so no red would appear.

40. (C) Stars generate light internally by nuclear reactions; the Moon merely reflects sunlight, so it is not a star. Earthshine is a faint secondary effect, not the main reason the Moon is bright. The Moon is not hot enough to glow visibly. The Moon is visible at many phases, but the point is the source of light, not how long it appears.

41. (A) Lunar noon means the Sun is highest at that location, and both near and far sides take their turn in sunlight over a month, so each at their own noon sees the Sun. The far side is not perpetually dark. Earth does not regularly shade the near side; that would be an eclipse, a rare event. The Moon has real day and night driven by sunlight, since it shines only by reflection.

42. (A) Precisely, the Moon reflects the Sun's light to us; it does not generate light. There are no self-glowing minerals producing moonlight. Earth's atmosphere does not light the Moon. There is no burning of gases; the Moon has essentially no atmosphere and no combustion.

43. (B) At new Moon the Moon is between us and the Sun, so the lit half points away and the dark half faces us; the Moon reflects light, but not toward Earth. The Sun keeps lighting one half always. Earth's shadow is on the opposite side at new Moon and is not involved. Sunlight does reach the Moon; it is the orientation, not a lack of light, that makes the near side dark.

44. (D) A blue sky is sunlight scattered by air molecules; without an atmosphere the Moon's sky has nothing to scatter sunlight, so it stays black even with the Sun shining. Distance does not strip blue from sunlight. The colour of reflected ground light does not set the sky colour. Surface rock absorption does not explain the black sky; the missing atmosphere does.

45. (C) No atmosphere means no wind and no rain, so nothing erases the prints; that lack of air is the root cause. The Moon's gravity is weak, not strong, and gravity does not preserve prints. A slow spin, not a fast one, characterises the Moon, and spin does not pack dust. The Moon has no clouds, again because it has no atmosphere.

46. (A) With no atmosphere there is no blanket to retain heat, so sunlit ground roasts and shaded ground plunges in temperature. The Moon's distance from the Sun does not lurch between day and night. Its spin is slow, not fast, and the swing is about the missing air, not spin. The Moon has no oceans; it has no liquid water at all.

47. (B) Sound travels as vibrations through a medium; with no atmosphere there is nothing to carry the sound, so it is silent. Gravity strength does not govern whether sound can propagate. Hard rock would conduct sound, but the air gap to the listener carries none. Magnetic fields do not cancel sound.

48. (A) Shooting stars glow from air friction in Earth's atmosphere; the airless Moon offers no such friction, so the rock reaches the ground intact and cratered. The Moon does not have a thicker atmosphere; it has essentially none. Gravity is weak, and burning is about friction, not fall speed. There is no magnetic shield doing this.

49. (C) On Earth air resistance slows a feather; the airless Moon removes that, so both fall together. Weak gravity slows the fall but does not make their weights equal, and equal weight is not why they land together. The feather's mass does not change on the Moon. The hammer does not slow down to match; rather, the feather stops being held back by air.

50. (A) Erosion by wind and water on Earth wears craters away; the airless, waterless Moon has no such weathering, so craters persist. Rock hardness is not the key difference; weathering is. More impacts would not by itself preserve old craters. Earth's gravity does not flatten craters; weathering and geology erase them.

51. (C) Lunar gravity is roughly one-sixth of Earth's, so a given leg push carries a jumper much higher. Six times Earth's gravity would make jumping far harder, not easier. Gravity is not the same as Earth's; if it were, the jump height would not change much. Gravity is weak but not zero, so the astronaut comes back down.

52. (D) Apparent (angular) size depends on real size divided by distance; the Sun is both far bigger and far farther, and these nearly cancel, so the two look similar. They are not truly the same size; the Sun is vastly larger. The Moon is much nearer Earth than the Sun, not nearer the Sun. The atmosphere does not magnify the Moon to match the Sun.

53. (B) The Sun is by far the largest, Earth is much smaller, and the Moon is smaller still, giving Sun-Earth-Moon. Earth is not larger than the Sun. The Moon is smaller than Earth, so it cannot rank above Earth. The Moon is the smallest, not the largest, so the reversed order is wrong.

54. (C) The Moon is our closest neighbour, the Sun is much farther, and the next-nearest star is vastly farther than the Sun, giving Moon-Sun-star. The Sun is not closer than the Moon. The next-nearest star is far beyond the Sun, not between Moon and Sun. The star is the most distant, not the nearest.

55. (D) Scale models show large empty gaps relative to body sizes, teaching that space is mostly empty. Thirty Earth-diameters is a wide gap, so the Moon does not nearly touch Earth. The Sun is enormously farther than the Moon, not a little. The three are spread far apart, not packed tightly.

56. (A) Gravity from Earth bends a moving satellite's path into a closed orbit; this applies equally to the Moon and to artificial satellites. The atmosphere is far too thin at orbital heights to hold anything up. Sunlight pressure is far too weak to drive an orbit. A satellite's own spin does not keep it aloft; gravity and orbital motion do.

57. (B) Phases depend on the Sun-Earth-Moon angle, which still cycles as the Moon orbits, so phases continue; only the angular size shrinks with greater distance. Phases do not require a particular distance. Moving farther does not let the Moon catch more sunlight; it would look fainter and smaller. Distance does not turn its reflected white light red.

58. (C) Greater distance reduces both the apparent size and the apparent brightness reaching us, so the star looks smaller and dimmer than the present Sun. Equal intrinsic brightness does not keep apparent brightness the same once distance doubles. Distance shrinks, not magnifies, the apparent size. Moving a star farther does not change what kind of object it is.

59. (A) Phase and which physical half is lit are the same worldwide at one instant; a Southern observer simply views the sky inverted relative to a Northern one, so the same waxing Moon looks lit on the opposite limb. The Moon does not orbit a different way for the south. Both hemispheres see the same phase at the same time, not different phases. The Moon's lit half is fixed by the Sun's direction, not by which hemisphere is below it.

60. (C) The Sun is a fairly average star; it dominates our sky only because it is by far the closest. Many stars produce their own light. There are stars far larger than the Sun. The Sun makes its own light by nuclear fusion and does not merely reflect other stars' light.
