## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** Climate is the average atmospheric pattern built up over many years, so only (ii), which speaks of what winters are reliably like every year, describes climate. Statement (i) reports one morning's snow and (iii) reports the instantaneous temperature now, both of which are short-term weather. A single snowfall is not automatically climate, an instant thermometer reading is weather not climate, and lumping all three together ignores the time-scale that separates the two ideas.

**2. (D)** Climate is an average over many years and is described by temperature and its seasonal spread as well as rainfall, so matching one year's rain total proves little. The 'no flaw' option treats a single year as climate, the claim that rainfall is irrelevant is wrong because rainfall is a key climate element, and the size of the figure is not the issue, the time-scale and the missing temperature data are.

**3. (B)** Weather is the short-term condition and climate the long-term average, so 'today's weather is sunny' and 'its climate is a cold dry desert' use both words correctly. The other three swap the meanings: calling a thousand-year pattern 'weather' and an afternoon change 'climate', calling yesterday's condition 'climate' and the thirty-year average 'weather', and claiming weather is fixed while climate changes hourly all reverse the correct time-scales.

**4. (B)** Climate is the average pattern over many years, so a single week's data is far too short to establish it; the honest answer is that she cannot yet say. One week is not enough to average a climate, day-to-day variation is ordinary weather rather than a fast-changing climate, and treating one week as the climate ignores the long records that the definition requires.

**5. (C)** A single day's matching weather is just one snapshot, whereas climate is the long-term average, so the two places can still differ greatly across the year. The 'not possible' option wrongly equates one day with the whole pattern, the second option reverses the definitions of weather and climate, and the day of the week has nothing to do with either.

**6. (B)** Latitude controls how much the Sun's angle changes through the year; far from the Equator the angle swings widely between seasons, giving Madrid its hot summers and cold winters, while near-equatorial Quito stays even. Altitude is not the seasonal-swing factor, Earth's tiny change of distance from the Sun is negligible against 150 million km, and Madrid certainly has an atmosphere.

**7. (B)** Because Earth is so distant, the difference in distance is negligible; what matters is the angle. Vertical rays at the Equator concentrate heat on a small area, while slanting polar rays spread it thinly (and pass through more air). The 'millions of kilometres nearer' claim is the classic distance fallacy, the poles do absorb some sunlight rather than reflecting all of it, and equatorial regions actually receive more, not fewer, hours of strong overhead sun.

**8. (D)** Near the Equator the Sun stays nearly overhead in every season, so steep rays keep heating concentrated and temperatures high year-round. The tropics are not permanently tilted toward the Sun (Earth's tilt is fixed and the regions facing the Sun change), clouds are not what keeps them warm, and Earth's axis does not point at the tropics; the steady high sun-angle is the real reason.

**9. (A)** With latitude held equal, the deciding factor is altitude: the high Andean city has thin, cold air and lies far above the ground that heats the air, so it is cool despite the low latitude. Latitude is given as the same, so it cannot be the cause; both cities are effectively the same distance from the Sun; and ray-slant depends on latitude and time of day, not on simply being high up.

**10. (A)** Slanting polar rays cross a longer, thicker slice of atmosphere, so more of their energy is scattered and absorbed before reaching the ground, weakening the heating; this adds to the spreading effect of the slant. Light does not speed up to carry extra heat, bending does not make slanting rays vertical, and passing through cold air would not add heat to the rays.

**11. (C)** Climbing thins the air, which holds heat poorly, and moves you away from the ground, the air's main heat source, so the higher Town P is cooler, not warmer. 'Higher means nearer the Sun' repeats the distance fallacy, sharing a latitude does not cancel a 2,200 m height difference, and altitude, not any change of latitude, is what cools Town P.

**12. (B)** Over a few horizontal kilometres the latitude scarcely changes, so the warm foot and frozen summit differ because of altitude: rising air thins and cools. Latitude does not change meaningfully over such a short distance, climbing does not move you appreciably nearer or farther from the sea, and the summit is not meaningfully nearer the Sun than the foot.

**13. (C)** Snow caps over warm valleys at the same latitude are explained by altitude: the peaks rise into thin, cold air. The milder coastal summers are due to the moderating sea, the dry desert behind a mountain is a rain-shadow (relief) effect, and the year-round equatorial warmth is a latitude effect, so only the peaks-over-valleys case is an altitude story.

**14. (A)** Gaining altitude thins the air and carries you away from the ground that warms it, so the temperature falls fairly steadily as you climb. It does not rise (height does not meaningfully near the Sun), it does not stay constant just because latitude is fixed, and the cooling is a steady trend with height, not a flat rise followed by a sudden inexplicable freeze.

**15. (D)** Away from the moderating sea, land heats and cools rapidly, so interior City K has a wider range, while coastal City J is smoothed by the slow-changing sea. The sea makes coasts milder, not more extreme; matching latitude and altitude still leaves the sea-distance difference; and interiors are known for large ranges, not mild ones.

**16. (C)** Because water changes temperature slowly, the sea stays cooler than the land in summer and warmer in winter, smoothing the temperatures of nearby coasts. Water heats and cools more slowly, not faster, it does not reflect away nearly all sunlight, and its temperature does vary, just gently, so 'never varies at all' is also wrong.

**17. (C)** A small annual range points to the smoothing influence of a nearby sea, which warms and cools slowly. A dry interior and a far-inland high plateau both give large ranges through continentality, and a high latitude gives a strong seasonal swing, so all three of those would widen, not narrow, the range.

**18. (D)** With latitude and altitude matched, the difference must come from distance from the sea: coastal City N is moderated, inland City M swings widely. They share a latitude so neither is nearer the Equator, they share an altitude so height is not the cause, and a coastal city certainly receives summer sunshine, so the sea's moderation is the explanation.

**19. (D)** An interior lacks the sea's smoothing, so land heats fully in summer and loses heat fully in winter, driving the hot and cold extremes apart and widening the range. Interiors do not change their distance to the Sun seasonally, a distant sea cannot heat or chill an interior it does not reach, and a place cannot switch its latitude between seasons.

**20. (D)** These north-eastern stations sit where moist summer winds are funnelled and forced sharply upward; the rising air cools, its vapour condenses, and enormous orographic rain falls. Descending leeward air warms and dries (it does not rain), the dry Thar cannot supply heavy rain, and these are warm moist summer winds, not cold Himalayan winter winds.

**21. (A)** The Ghats lift the moist winds, which rain on the windward seaward slopes; the air crosses the crest already dry, so the leeward Deccan lies in a rain shadow. The Deccan is not nearer the Equator, the coast is not higher than the Ghats, and rivers do not generate rainfall, so relief lifting the winds is the true cause.

**22. (A)** Rising air expands and cools; cooler air holds less vapour, so the excess condenses into rain on the windward slope. Rising air expands and cools rather than being compressed and warmed, it does not dry without cooling, and air that has risen passes over the crest and descends on the far side rather than raining back on the windward face.

**23. (B)** By the time air crosses the crest it has rained out its moisture on the windward slope, so the leeward side lies in a 'shadow' of rain and stays dry. It has nothing to do with literal sunlight shadows or shadiness, and the leeward side faces away from the moist wind, not into it.

**24. (A)** Every contrast pairs a wet windward upslope with a drier leeward area, the signature of relief lifting moist winds. Rainfall does not simply track latitude, height alone does not guarantee rain (it depends on windward exposure), and inland areas are generally drier, not wetter, so relief plus wind direction is the unifying explanation.

**25. (A)** Air forced up the windward slope cools and rains there, then sinks dry on the leeward side, so a short crossing takes you from wet to dry, the rain shadow. A few kilometres cannot change latitude or nearness to the Equator meaningfully, and soil type does not control rainfall, so relief and wind direction are the cause.

**26. (D)** The west coast's deluge comes from the Ghats forcing moist winds up to rain; remove the barrier and the coast loses that lift while the interior is no longer so sheltered. The coast would not rain more (the lifting is what produces the rain), the peninsula would not freeze, and removing a major rain barrier could not leave rainfall everywhere unchanged.

**27. (D)** The loo arrives hot and dry from desert lands while winter winds arrive cold from snowy heights, showing that winds bring the qualities of wherever they have been. Winds cannot change a region's latitude, raise its altitude, or relocate it relative to the sea; they simply transport the heat or cold and moisture they picked up en route.

**28. (B)** The loo crosses scorching, arid land and so arrives blistering and parched. Had it come off the sea it would be moist, coming off the Himalayas it would be cold, and it is an ordinary surface wind rather than air heated by underground springs, so its desert path is what makes it hot and dry.

**29. (B)** Winds carry the character of the land they cross, so air spilling over cold, snowy mountains reaches the plains cold and triggers a cold wave. Air off the warm Bay would be warm and moist, air off the hot Thar would be hot, and warm air rising from the plains would not produce a cold wave, so the snowy mountain path explains the chill.

**30. (D)** A wind crossing a warm sea evaporates water into itself and arrives moist, while a wind off a hot dry desert arrives parched. Seas add moisture rather than removing it, the sea wind is not necessarily colder in every season, and the surface a wind crosses very much affects its temperature and moisture, which is the whole principle being tested.

**31. (B)** The monsoon arises because land changes temperature faster than the sea, creating the pressure differences that drive the seasonal winds. The Moon's gravity governs tides, not monsoon winds; rivers do not power the system; and a fixed equatorial temperature has nothing to do with the seasonal land-sea pressure flip.

**32. (D)** Hot summer land forms low pressure, so cooler, higher-pressure moist sea air flows in from the south-west, giving the rain-bearing summer monsoon. High pressure would push air out (the winter case), low pressure cannot push air outward to the sea, and high pressure does not draw air inward, so only low pressure pulling in moist sea winds is correct.

**33. (A)** Summer south-west winds soak up moisture over warm oceans and rain it out on land, whereas winter north-east winds blow off dry land and stay dry. The first wrong option reverses the source of each wind, the second wrongly ties rain to coldness, and the third imagines rain falling vertically from space rather than being carried in moist sea winds.

**34. (B)** The burst marks the arrival of the moist south-west winds, switching a parched, scorching landscape to days of downpour quite abruptly. The Sun does not stop shining, rivers do not evaporate upward into rain, and the burst is a summer south-west event, not the dry north-east winter monsoon.

**35. (B)** The south-west monsoon enters at the southern tip in early June and progresses northward, blanketing the country by roughly mid-July. It does not march from north to south, it does not move from the land out into the seas, and it does not arrive everywhere simultaneously because it takes weeks to cross India's length.

**36. (C)** A sea breeze forms when sun-warmed land makes low pressure and draws in cooler sea air, exactly the land-sea heating contrast that powers the monsoon on a country-sized scale. The Moon's pull governs tides, rivers do not drive these winds, and melting snow is unrelated to the land-sea pressure difference that creates both the breeze and the monsoon.

**37. (D)** In winter the land cools faster than the sea, becoming high pressure, while the relatively warmer sea is lower pressure, so air flows from land to sea, the dry north-east monsoon. The other options put low pressure or no pressure difference over the cold land or high pressure over the sea, none of which would produce the observed offshore winter winds.

**38. (C)** Offshore winter winds are mostly dry, but those that travel over the Bay of Bengal absorb moisture and shed it on the south-east coast. A desert supplies dryness not moisture, the Himalayas lie to the north and not in this wind's path to the south-east coast, and the Arabian Sea is on the west, not the source of the north-east flow reaching Tamil Nadu.

**39. (A)** In summer hot land is low pressure and pulls sea air in; in winter cold land is high pressure and pushes air out, and this seasonal flip reverses the winds. Earth's tilt is fixed (it does not flip), mountains do not swing around, and the Moon's orbit is irrelevant to the monsoon, so the land-sea pressure flip is the real cause.

**40. (D)** The retreating (autumn) season is when the south-west monsoon weakens and pulls back, linking the rainy season to the dry winter. It is not the burst (the arrival), it follows rather than precedes the rains so it is not the pre-monsoon heat build-up, and it has nothing to do with rivers moving.

**41. (C)** The year runs from cool dry winter, into the hot dry pre-monsoon summer, into the rainy south-west monsoon when the burst arrives, and finally the retreating monsoon as the rains withdraw before winter returns. The other orders break this cycle by placing the rains before summer, the retreat before the rains, or the retreat before the rains arrive at all.

**42. (C)** The monsoon so thoroughly governs Indian weather, farming and life that the rainy season stands out as a distinct season, the familiar 'fifth season.' It was not borrowed from Europe, India does have a winter, and festivals follow the seasons rather than each creating its own.

**43. (A)** The six-ritu scheme reads the monsoon-dominated Indian year in finer detail, recognising seasons the four-season model misses. It is ancient rather than recent, it is built around the monsoon rather than ignoring it, and six categories cannot be a mere renaming of four.

**44. (B)** Seasons recur because Earth completes one revolution around the Sun each year while tilted on its axis, repeating the pattern annually. The Sun's output does not rise and fall seasonally, festival dates follow the seasons rather than commanding the winds, and river floods result from the seasons rather than causing them.

**45. (C)** Ladakh lies in the rain shadow of the Himalayas, so it gets little rain (a desert), and its high altitude makes it freezing (cold), hence 'cold desert.' It is not a hot desert, its cold comes from altitude rather than nearness to the pole, and far from being near the sea, its dryness comes from the mountain barrier cutting off moist winds.

**46. (C)** The flat, open Thar lets hot dry winds sweep across it, while the Himalayan wall blocks cold northern winds from the sheltered lands, both of which are effects of relief. Latitude, sea-distance and the Sun's position do not explain why a mountain barrier shields one region while an open plain is exposed; the lay of the land is the deciding factor.

**47. (C)** Ladakh is cold and dry from altitude plus the Himalayan rain shadow, Mumbai's mild range comes from the moderating sea, Cherrapunji's deluge from relief forcing moist winds up, and Kanniyakumari stays warm because of its low latitude. The other matchings misassign these, for instance crediting Ladakh's cold to low latitude or Mumbai's mildness to altitude, which contradicts each region's true controlling factor.

**48. (B)** Srinagar is both far north, where sunlight is weaker and more slanting, and high in the mountains, where altitude keeps air cold, and these stack up to chill it. It is not at a low latitude, it is nowhere near the Equator, and it is not bathed in moderating sea winds, so the correct pair is high latitude plus altitude.

**49. (B)** A weak monsoon means too little rain, so crops fail, water runs short, harvests shrink and food prices climb. A failed (weak) monsoon brings drought, not floods and bumper harvests; it does not warm winters or grow glaciers; and it cannot erase the seasons, so only the drought chain follows.

**50. (A)** Indian agriculture relies heavily on the monsoon, so when rains fail, crops fail, food grows scarce and dear, and rural incomes fall, straining the whole economy. Festivals are not legally cancelled, the sea's moderating role does not switch off, and the Himalayas do not block trade because of a dry year, so farming's dependence on the rains is the reason.

**51. (C)** When the periodic Pacific warming (El Nino) shifts the winds, the monsoon tends to weaken, so India gets less rain and crops and water are stressed. A weakened monsoon brings shortfall, not record floods; it does not change mountain heights; and it has no bearing on whether the sea moderates the coast.

**52. (A)** El Nino is the periodic warming of Pacific waters that can shift winds and weaken the Indian monsoon. The loo is a hot summer land wind, a rain shadow is the dry leeward side of mountains, and the burst is the sudden onset of monsoon rain, none of which is the Pacific warming described.

**53. (B)** A cyclone is built around an intense low-pressure centre into which surrounding air and moisture spiral, feeding the storm. High pressure would push air out (the opposite of a cyclone), equal pressure produces no winds at all, and cyclones grow over warm seas rather than from winter cold over land.

**54. (C)** The calm, clear eye at a cyclone's centre is deceptive; once it passes, the violent winds of the far eyewall strike from the opposite side, so people must stay sheltered. The lull does not mean the storm is over or harmless, and although the eye feels safe, the dangerous part is the surrounding eyewall, not the eye, so fleeing through it is unsafe.

**55. (D)** The fiercest winds whirl in the eyewall, the thick cloud band ringing the calm eye. The eye itself is deceptively still, the violent winds lie within the storm rather than far outside it, and they certainly do not appear after the cyclone has gone.

**56. (D)** The eye's lull and clear sky make people believe the storm is over, but the violent eyewall sweeps back in once the eye passes, so the calm is dangerously deceptive. The eye is calm rather than the strongest-wind region, the cyclone does not lose its power simply because the eye has arrived, and the eye is not a sign the storm has left the sea for good.

**57. (A)** Greenhouse gases trap heat; adding more thickens the blanket and traps even more, warming the Earth. They do not let heat escape (that would cool it), they do not mainly block incoming sunlight, and they do not remove the atmosphere, so added greenhouse gas means more trapped heat and warming.

**58. (A)** Without the natural greenhouse effect Earth would be far too cold; greenhouse gases trap some of the Sun's heat and keep the planet comfortably warm. They do not block all sunlight or freeze the planet (the opposite of warming), and they do not directly create the monsoon, which arises from land-sea heating, so trapping part of the heat is the right reason.

**59. (C)** Burning coal releases carbon dioxide, which traps extra heat, warming and shifting the climate, which can disrupt the monsoon and make crops fail; that is the correct chain. The alternatives go wrong by cooling the sea, freezing rivers or growing forests, none of which follows from burning coal, which adds greenhouse gas and warms.

**60. (A)** Mitigation tackles the cause by reducing emissions (such as switching to renewables) and slowing warming, while resilience prepares communities to cope with impacts (such as floods), so the matched examples are correct. The first option swaps the two definitions, the second over-narrows both to tree-planting, and the third wrongly claims each ignores half the problem.
