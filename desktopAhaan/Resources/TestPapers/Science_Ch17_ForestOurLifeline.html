Worked Solutions

1. (D) A forest is not just a crowd of tall trees — it is a whole system of trees, shrubs, herbs, climbers, animals, birds, insects and micro-organisms living together and depending on one another. The other options leave out most of the community.

2. (D) The canopy is the topmost branchy "roof" made by the crowns of the tallest trees. The understorey and forest floor are lower layers; humus is decomposed matter in the soil.

3. (A) The crown is the branchy, leafy top of a single tree. The canopy is the combined roof formed by many crowns together — a property of the whole forest, not one tree.

4. (B) Smaller trees and shrubs growing in the shade beneath the tallest trees form the understorey. The canopy and emergent layer are at the top; the crown belongs to one tree.

5. (D) Floor herbs and ferns get only the sunlight that filters down through the canopy and understorey above them. They still make their own food by photosynthesis (so "from soil" is wrong); they certainly do not get more light than the canopy.

6. (C) Plants grow at different heights chiefly because they compete for sunlight — the tallest grab it first, and shorter plants survive on what filters through. Soil and age are not the main driver.

7. (A) A sun-loving herb does best in a bright gap where a big tree has fallen and light reaches the ground. Under a dense canopy it is too shady; it cannot live up in the canopy or inside a trunk.

8. (B) From top to bottom the layers run canopy → understorey → shrubs → forest floor. The other orders jumble these levels.

9. (C) Dead leaves, twigs and droppings on the floor are broken down by decomposers — fungi and micro-organisms. Canopy trees, sunlight alone and grazing deer do not do this recycling work.

10. (C) The dark, nutrient-rich material made when decomposers break down dead matter is humus. Canopy is a layer, sand is mineral grains, and nectar is a flower sugar.

11. (B) This is the decomposer trap. Decomposers are essential, not harmful: by rotting dead matter into humus they recycle nutrients that feed new plants. Without them the forest would choke on dead material.

12. (B) With no decomposers, dead leaves and bodies would pile up and the locked-in nutrients would never return to the soil — the forest would be buried in dead matter and starved of recycled nutrients. (It would not improve, nor change overnight.)

13. (C) Decomposers return nutrients to the soil, where plant roots can absorb them again. This closes the loop; the air, canopy and sunlight do not store these nutrients for roots.

14. (B) The repeating path soil → plants → animals → dead matter → decomposers → soil is the forest's nutrient (recycling) cycle. It involves more than water and more than carbon, and it is not a food chain run backwards.

15. (C) Producers are the green plants — they make food from sunlight. Herbivores (deer), carnivores (tiger) and decomposers (fungi) all depend on that food; they do not produce it.

16. (C) In grass → deer → tiger, the deer eats the plant (so it is a herbivore) and is itself eaten by the tiger (so it is the tiger's prey). It is neither a producer nor a carnivore.

17. (D) A food web is many food chains interlinked, because most animals eat, and are eaten by, more than one kind of organism. It still has producers and animals; it is not fixed at three organisms.

18. (D) Trace Web 1: grasshoppers are eaten by frogs, and the small birds also eat grasshoppers. So the grasshopper is eaten by both frogs and small birds. Tigers eat deer (not grasshoppers); snakes eat frogs.

19. (B) Frogs eat grasshoppers and are eaten by snakes. Remove most frogs and grasshoppers face one fewer predator (numbers tend to rise) while snakes lose a food source (their food falls). Grass would not vanish, and tigers (on the deer chain) are unaffected at first.

20. (A) The hawk eats snakes (grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk) and also eats small birds (grass → grasshopper → small bird → hawk) — prey from two different chains. The deer, grasshopper and frog each sit on a single chain here.

21. (C) Every chain in the web starts with green plants, which capture energy from the Sun. So the ultimate energy source for all organisms is the Sun. The tiger, humus and decomposers are not energy sources for the whole web.

22. (A) Tigers eat deer. Remove the tigers and the deer lose their main predator, so the deer population tends to increase first. Grass would not vanish at once, and the hawk-snake link is on another chain.

23. (B) Forests are the "green lungs" because their plants release oxygen and absorb carbon dioxide during photosynthesis. They do not breathe like animal lungs, and option A reverses the gases.

24. (B) By day, forest plants photosynthesise — taking in CO₂ and giving out O₂ — which helps keep the air's gases in balance. They do not only respire, nor absorb all the oxygen, nor make no gases.

25. (D) Cutting a large forest removes plants that were absorbing CO₂, so the amount of carbon dioxide in the air tends to rise. Oxygen, nitrogen and water vapour are not the gas that increases here.

26. (A) Roots grip the soil and the leaf litter slows the rain so water soaks in rather than rushing off — this is how forests curb erosion and floods. They do not drink rivers dry or push soil downhill.

27. (B) Strip a hillside of forest and there are no roots to hold the soil or slow the rain, so heavy rain causes more soil erosion and a greater flood risk — the opposite of "less erosion."

28. (D) Forest cover and litter let rainwater soak slowly into the ground, recharging the groundwater, instead of running straight off. They do not seal the soil or rush water to the sea.

29. (C) Through transpiration (releasing water vapour) and shade, large forests add moisture to the air and help bring rainfall, cooling and dampening the local climate — they do not dry the area or stop wind.

30. (B) A forest is a "lifeline" because it gives oxygen, clean water, soil protection, climate control and wildlife habitat — all at once. The other options each name just one narrow use.

31. (C) Gum, honey, timber and medicinal herbs are genuine forest products. Plastic, petrol and glass are manufactured from other raw materials, not harvested from a forest.

32. (B) As a fallen tree rots, decomposers return the nutrients in its wood to the soil for new plants to use — they are recycled, not lost, raised to the canopy, or turned into sunlight.

33. (D) Energy flows Sun → grass → deer → tiger: plants capture the Sun's energy, herbivores eat plants, carnivores eat herbivores. The other chains run the arrows backwards or jumble the order.

34. (A) Much less energy is available higher up a food chain (most is used up at each step), so the top carnivores like tigers can only be few, while the deer below them are many. The other options are false.

35. (B) A wheat field is one species, one layer, lasting one season; a forest is a stable, many-layered community of many different species lasting many years — that is the real difference, not the presence of a tiger or its size.

36. (D) The correct statement is that a forest is a community of plants, animals and micro-organisms that depend on one another. "Just trees," "no effect on air," and "decomposers play no role" are all wrong.

37. (D) After a clearing, the patch is recolonised step by step — first herbs, then shrubs, then trees — and the forest regrows. It neither stays bare forever nor springs up overnight nor becomes permanent desert.

38. (A) The fact that a burnt or cleared forest grows back shows a forest is dynamic — it grows and regenerates naturally over time. It is therefore not fixed, not man-made-only, and not the same as a crop field.

39. (A) Sunlight is caught first by the canopy, less reaches the understorey, and least of all reaches the forest floor at the bottom. So the forest floor receives the least sunlight.

40. (C) A mushroom is a fungus — a decomposer that breaks down dead matter. A deer is a herbivore, a mango tree a producer, and a hawk a carnivore.

41. (B) In Web 2 the fox eats rabbits (trees → rabbit → fox) and also eats snakes (trees → insect → small bird → snake → fox) — food from two chains. It does not eat plants, insects directly, or the floor fungi.

42. (D) Foxes eat both rabbits and snakes. Wipe out the rabbits and the fox loses one of its two prey, so it is the animal most directly short of food. Snakes, small birds and insects do not feed on rabbits.

43. (A) When foxes, snakes and trees die, the fungi and bacteria (decomposers) on the floor break them down and return their nutrients to the soil. Rabbits, small birds and insects are not decomposers.

44. (A) Small birds eat insects, so a sharp rise in insects means more food for small birds, whose numbers could then increase first. The trees, foxes and fungi are not the most direct first responders.

45. (C) Follow every chain back to its start: both the rabbit (trees → rabbit) and the insect (trees → insect) feed on the trees and shrubs — the producers — for their energy. Foxes, snakes and decomposers are not the base.

46. (A) The canopy, high among the branches, is the typical home of birds and monkeys. Earthworms, fungi, litter beetles and deep-shade moss all belong to the forest floor, not the canopy.

47. (D) Earthworms, fungi and many beetles live mainly on or in the forest floor, among the leaf litter — not in the canopy, crowns or emergent layer at the top.

48. (C) Forest soil is rich because decomposers constantly turn fallen leaves and dead matter into humus, recharging the soil with nutrients. Blocked rain, animal feet and lack of sunlight do not make soil fertile.

49. (A) When losing the tiger lets the deer multiply and overgraze, it shows that removing one species can disturb many others in a forest. Animals clearly do affect plants, and more deer is not always good.

50. (A) Large-scale deforestation sets off: fewer trees → more soil erosion, less rainfall, lost habitat and more CO₂. The other chains wrongly claim benefits from cutting trees.

51. (D) Without the forest, rainwater now runs off fast instead of soaking in, so the stream floods in the rains and dries up in summer — the slow, steady release is gone. The trees were not "drinking floodwater," and the Sun and stream did not move.

52. (C) Plants are producers because they make their own food using sunlight, water and carbon dioxide. They do not eat animals, break down dead leaves, or depend on being fed.

53. (B) The correct pairing is herbivore — deer. Tigers are carnivores (not producers), deer are not decomposers, and grass is a producer (not a carnivore).

54. (C) Bina is right: a forest provides many life-supporting services — oxygen, soil holding, water recharge and wildlife shelter — not just wood. Aman's "wood only" view misses almost everything a forest does.

55. (A) In leaves → caterpillar → small bird → snake, the snake eats the bird and nothing in this chain eats the snake, so it is a carnivore at the top of this chain — not a producer, herbivore or decomposer.

56. (A) Removing the canopy suddenly exposes shade-adapted plants and floor animals to harsh sun, heat and pounding rain that they are not built for, harming the lower layers. It does not leave them unaffected or make the floor colder.

57. (B) Filtered sunlight matters because it lets shade-loving understorey and floor plants make enough food to survive. The light is helpful, not harmful, and it feeds plants, not just animals.

58. (D) A forest stores carbon mainly in its wood, leaves and soil as the plants take in CO₂ and build it into their tissues and humus. Animal fur, streams and the air between trees are not the main carbon stores.

59. (D) A forest is "our lifeline" because, all together, it supplies oxygen, recycles nutrients, holds soil and water, balances the climate and shelters countless species. The single-use options each miss the bigger picture.

60. (C) Conserving a forest means protecting it and planting trees where forest has been lost (afforestation). Clearing it for one crop, removing decomposers, or cutting the canopy would all damage the forest, not conserve it.

Solutions complete: 60 / 60.
