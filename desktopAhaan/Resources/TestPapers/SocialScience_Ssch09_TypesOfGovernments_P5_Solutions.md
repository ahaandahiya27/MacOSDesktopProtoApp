## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** The constitution had described the legislature, executive and judiciary, and keeping them in separate hands is exactly the separation of powers; collapsing all three into one office removes the mutual checks and lets that office abuse power unchecked. The franchise concerns who may vote, not how the organs are arranged, so it is untouched by merging the organs. A free press is a different safeguard and is not what the three named bodies represent. Judicial review of elections is a narrow court power, not the broad structural division being dismantled here.

**2. (B)** Making law by debate and vote is the legislative function, carrying it out is the executive function, and deciding whether the action was lawful is the judicial function, so the first mapping is exact. Swapping legislative and executive labels misnames the assembly's lawmaking as administration. Calling enforcement judicial and the court ruling executive reverses the carry-out and judge roles. Lumping all three as legislative ignores that only the debate-and-vote step is lawmaking while the others are enforcement and judging.

**3. (B)** Letting one body both legislate and judge removes the independent judicial check, so the assembly could rule in its own favour and abuse power; that is why separation of powers matters. Democracy does not forbid efficiency; it limits how power is concentrated. A legislature making laws is normal and proper, so that is not the objection. The judiciary's job is to judge and the legislature's is to make laws, so the last option reverses their roles.

**4. (B)** An independent court that can rule against the government keeps even rulers bound by law and protects rights, which is why independence is the ideal. Making new laws is the legislature's task, not the court's, so that misstates the judicial role. A court that always backs the government would simply rubber-stamp it, defeating the purpose, and speed is not the democratic value at stake. Advising a monarch describes a nominal role under a king, not the active deciding role of a democratic judiciary.

**5. (C)** Separation of powers is about each organ actually functioning independently, so a head who controls the judges and rewrites laws has destroyed it regardless of what the document lists. Merely naming three organs does not guarantee their independence, so the student's claim fails. Holding executive elections does not restore independence to courts the executive controls. Having a written constitution does not by itself secure separation of powers if the text is ignored in practice.

**6. (A)** A monarch who only signs without ever refusing, while elected ministers answerable to the house govern, holds nominal power, which is the constitutional monarchy that doubles as a parliamentary democracy. Signing bills mechanically is not making law, so the absolute-monarchy reading is wrong. A monarch is not a separately elected president, so it is not a presidential system. Ceremony is not religious rule, so theocracy does not fit.

**7. (C)** Whether a monarchy is a democracy turns on who really governs, so the absolute case is not a democracy while the constitutional case is, making the blanket claim false. A king does not automatically end democracy, so the first option is wrong. Automatic assent signals a nominal monarch, not a secret ruler, so the second misreads it. Neither king is elected, so the last option invents a false fact.

**8. (D)** The contrast is that an absolute monarch truly governs while a constitutional monarch is nominal under an elected parliament, and only the elected-parliament case is a democracy. They do not both reign ceremonially, so country size is not the difference. The UK monarch is not elected; the country is a democracy because parliament is elected, not the monarch. An absolute monarch acting fast is not democratic accountability, so the last option is wrong.

**9. (B)** An elected, non-hereditary head of state is exactly what makes a republic, so that label is secured; democracy needs broad voting rights, free speech and protected rights, which are missing, so it is not yet earned. Abolishing a throne does not by itself create rule by the people. A constitutional monarchy keeps a monarch, but the throne is gone, so that label is impossible. The narrow landowning electorate is not the whole people deciding directly, so it is not a direct democracy.

**10. (C)** Democracy describes the people ruling and a republic adds the specific requirement of an elected, non-hereditary head of state; a constitutional-monarchy democracy keeps a hereditary head so it is a democracy but not a republic, proving the two are not identical. They are not exact synonyms, so the first option fails. A republic requires an elected, not hereditary, head, so the second reverses the meaning. The third swaps the two definitions entirely.

**11. (B)** Both can be democracies, so they share elections, protected rights and parliaments; what separates them is whether the head of state inherits the office or is elected. The constitutional monarchy still holds elections for its parliament, so 'only the republic holds elections' is false. Both protect rights, so that is not the divider. The last option reverses which one has the hereditary head.

**12. (B)** An elected head of state makes it a republic, the PM and Council answerable to Parliament make it parliamentary, and rule by elected representatives makes it a democracy, so all three labels hold together. Having a President does not make a system presidential, and India's President is elected not hereditary, so the first option is wrong on two counts. A President is not a hereditary monarch, so the constitutional-monarchy reading fails. India elects representatives rather than voting on every law, so it is not a direct democracy.

**13. (C)** A head of government who comes from the elected house and falls when it withdraws confidence is the defining mark of a parliamentary system. Being chosen through some election does not make it presidential, since presidential heads are elected separately and serve fixed terms regardless of the legislature. One leader heading a government does not make a monarchy, which requires inherited rule. Withdrawing legislative support is a political act, not a religious one, so theocracy does not fit.

**14. (B)** Loss of confidence forces the PM and Council to resign, which shows the executive depends on the legislature, the hallmark of a parliamentary system. The executive is not separately elected for a fixed term in India, so 'nothing changes' is wrong. The President is the nominal head and is not removed by such a vote. The judiciary neither dissolves itself nor appoints governments, so the last option misstates its role.

**15. (A)** The US President is the real executive elected separately, whereas India's President is nominal and the PM and Council govern answerable to Parliament, so the systems differ sharply. The two Presidents do not have identical powers, so the student is not right. India does have a Prime Minister who really governs, so the second option is false. India's President is not chosen the same way as the US President and does not rule independently, so the last option is wrong.

**16. (B)** A head of government emerging from the legislature and removable by it is parliamentary, while a separately elected head serving a fixed term is presidential, so the order is parliamentary then presidential. Reversing them mislabels each. Neither description turns on a hereditary versus elected head of state, so the monarchy-versus-republic pairing is irrelevant. Both involve elected representatives rather than citizens voting on every issue, so the direct-versus-representative pairing does not apply.

**17. (D)** Residents deciding the well themselves is direct democracy, while an elected assembly passing the budget is representative democracy, so the order is direct then representative. Reversing them mislabels each scene. Neither scene is a national referendum or a village-head election, so that pairing misdescribes both. Neither involves a small ruling clique or a monarch, so oligarchy and constitutional monarchy do not fit.

**18. (C)** A referendum lets citizens settle a specific question directly, unlike an election where they pick a representative to decide for them. Restricting the vote to assembly members would make it an internal assembly vote, not a referendum of citizens. Judges do not cast referendum ballots for citizens. A head of state consulting wealthy families is not a citizen vote at all, so that misdescribes a referendum.

**19. (D)** Everyone voting is direct democracy and electing monitors to decide is representative democracy, so the first class moves direct-to-representative and the second reverses it. The first option mislabels the first move. Electing monitors is not creating a monarch, so the monarchy-republic pairing fails. Three elected, reporting monitors are accountable representatives, not an unaccountable ruling clique, so neither move becomes an oligarchy or dictatorship.

**20. (C)** Both forms keep ultimate power with the people, directly or through chosen representatives, which is why both are democracies. A hereditary monarch holding ultimate power would be a monarchy, not a democracy. Forbidding citizens to vote contradicts the very idea of democracy. Requiring religious approval of every decision describes a theocracy, not either kind of democracy.

**21. (C)** With one party, obedient courts and criminalised criticism, power is unchecked and the people cannot really choose or remove rulers, so it is a dictatorship behind democratic words. A scheduled vote with no real choice does not make a genuine democracy. Being a republic concerns only an elected head of state and does not guarantee the missing pillars. Casting a ballot for the sole party is not citizens deciding issues themselves, so it is not a direct democracy.

**22. (A)** X has the full set of democratic pillars while Y has only the outward vote, so X is more truly a democracy. Elections alone are not the complete test, so Y is not the answer. Holding elections on a schedule does not make the two equal when one lacks every other pillar. **23. (D)** Even an elected majority is bound by rule of law and the constitution, and protected rights cannot be stripped by a vote, which is the correct response. **24. (C)** Democracy is majority rule limited by protected rights, so the assertion is false while the reason is incomplete rather than simply true. **27. (B)** Denying one community the vote and office breaks equality of citizens, the principle that everyone counts the same in choosing the government. **28. (C)** Accountability means the government answers to its electors and can be voted out, a check absent in a dictatorship. Being free from all questioning is the opposite of accountability. **29. (C)** Seizing absolute, unchecked power makes this a dictatorship, and monarchy is wrong because it specifically means inherited family rule, which did not happen. **30. **31. (B)** Rule by a small wealthy group is an oligarchy and rule by one unchecked person is a dictatorship, so the order is oligarchy then dictatorship, distinguished by group versus single ruler.
