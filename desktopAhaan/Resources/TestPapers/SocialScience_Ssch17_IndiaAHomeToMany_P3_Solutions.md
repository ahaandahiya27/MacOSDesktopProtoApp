## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** The chapter's central value, vasudhaiva kutumbakam ('the whole world is one family'), holds that India treats outsiders as kin, the direct opposite of keeping all foreigners out. Scheduled-tribe recognition is about constitutional support for groups already here, not about admitting outsiders. The chapter never says a guest must repay hospitality with trade — atithi devo bhava is unconditional. And the chapter shows newcomers kept their faiths, so demanding they abandon them would contradict, not support, India's actual tradition.

**2. (A)** 'Atithi devo bhava' ('a guest is like God') is specifically about hospitality to the guest, while 'vasudhaiva kutumbakam' ('the whole world is one family') is the wider civilisational ideal that frames all of humanity as kin. Neither is a trade slogan or rule of warfare. Neither is a statement about the number of gods worshipped. And neither is a prayer of the Parsis or Jews — they are India's own guiding values, the ethos of acceptance the chapter is built around.

**3. (A)** The point of the chapter is that India's ethos — its characteristic spirit — has long been acceptance, so it became home to many communities over millennia. It is not a story of every newcomer conquering and ruling India; most came as traders or refugees. India was not closed until modern times — Arab merchants arrived as early as the 7th century and Jews even earlier. And the whole chapter refutes the idea that only one community ever lived here.

**4. (D)** 'Cosmopolitan' means at home with many cultures — 'a citizen of the world' — exactly the openness the chapter describes. It is not selective by wealth: India sheltered refugees and even enslaved-origin communities, not just rich traders. It does not mean erasing newcomers' cultures — the chapter stresses they kept their identities. And it is the opposite of clinging to a single culture; that would be the closed attitude the chapter argues against.

**5. (B)** The chapter explicitly presents vasudhaiva kutumbakam as having universal relevance for the modern world, where diverse peoples increasingly share the planet — modern examples like the Tibetan refugees and the Baha'is prove the ethos continued. It was not abandoned after antiquity. It is not limited to traders; refugees were welcomed too. And 'one family' means kinship and acceptance, not India ruling the world.

**6. (B)** The chapter names two paths: refuge (fleeing persecution or danger) and opportunity (seeking trade or a better life). The first newcomer flees persecution — that is refuge; the second chases trade profit — that is opportunity. The reverse order is wrong. 'Conquest' is not one of the chapter's two paths. And 'asylum and persecution' confuses the shelter (asylum/refuge) with the very thing being fled (persecution).

**7. (D)** The Siddis are the exception: they were brought to India enslaved, not by their own choice for refuge or opportunity. The Parsis chose refuge from persecution, the Armenians chose opportunity through trade, and the Tibetans chose refuge from danger — all three made a choice, even if a hard one. Only the enslaved Siddis fit neither voluntary path, which is exactly why the 'everyone came to trade' claim is false.

**8. (C)** The Parsis, the Tibetans, and the Jews all came seeking refuge (the Parsis and Jews fled persecution; the Tibetans fled danger). The Arab merchants and Armenians came for opportunity (trade), and the Siddis were brought enslaved — so any group containing them is not an all-refugee set. Distinguishing refuge from opportunity, and remembering the enslaved Siddis, is the key skill here.

**9. (C)** The chapter contrasts the refugee — pushed out by danger or persecution, with little choice — against those who come freely for opportunity. Keeping traditions was open to all newcomers, not refugees alone, so that is not the distinguishing feature. Coming freely for trade describes the opportunity-seeker, not the refugee. And refugees did become part of Indian society (Tibetans, Parsis, Jews), so exclusion is false.

**10. (D)** To emigrate is to leave a country and to immigrate is to enter one, so a Tibetan leaving Tibet emigrates from Tibet and, settling in India, immigrates to India — both, viewed from the two countries. Swapping the two reverses the directions. One movement of a person is naturally both an emigration (from the old land) and an immigration (to the new). And a refugee is precisely a kind of migrant, so 'does not migrate' is wrong.

**11. (D)** Arab merchants came for opportunity through trade — the correct match. The Parsis came for refuge from persecution, not trade. The Siddis did not come freely for refuge; they were brought enslaved. The Tibetans came for refuge from danger, not for trade. Each wrong pair swaps a refugee or enslaved community into the 'trade' slot, the chapter's most common trap.

**12. (C)** The Bene Israel and the Cochin Jews are Jewish communities of India who preserved their faith and built synagogues. They are not African-origin enslaved people — that describes the Siddis. They are not the Mappila, the Kerala Muslims formed when Arab merchants married locally. And they are not Zoroastrians — that is the Parsis of Gujarat. The names point specifically to India's Jews.

**13. (B)** A synagogue is the Jewish house of worship and a fire temple is the Zoroastrian (Parsi) one — the correct linkage. Reversing them mismatches both. The Syriac Christians, not the Siddis, are tied to churches, and the mosque is linked to Arab Muslims, not the Tibetans. And it was the Arab merchants who built India's oldest mosque, while the fire temple is Parsi — so the last pairing is doubly wrong.

**14. (A)** Surviving synagogues and an unbroken faith show identity-preserving integration: the Jews became part of Indian society yet kept their religion. This refutes the idea that acceptance required giving up one's religion. It also refutes total separation — they were part of Indian life, not apart from it. And it has nothing to do with newcomers ruling the older inhabitants; the point is coexistence, not domination.

**15. (C)** The Arab merchants came for opportunity through trade, not refuge — the odd one out. The Jews and the Parsis both fled persecution and so came for refuge. Because the Arab merchants are clearly the opportunity-seekers, 'all three came seeking refuge' is false. The question rewards separating the trade newcomers from the refugees.

**16. (B)** The Syriac Christians of Kerala are among the oldest Christian communities anywhere, showing Christianity's roots in India are ancient. They did not first arrive in the 20th century. They were not introduced by the African-origin Siddis. And while the Armenians were also Christians who came to trade, the Syriac Christians are a distinct, much older Kerala community — Christianity in India is not limited to people of Armenian origin.

**17. (A)** Both the Syriac Christians and the Jews are ancient communities that kept their own faith while integrating into Indian society — the model of identity-preserving integration. Neither was brought enslaved (that was the Siddis). Neither came as a conquering army; the chapter's communities mostly came for refuge or trade. And neither abandoned its religion — the very point is that they kept it.

**18. (C)** The chapter groups the Syriac Christians with communities like the Parsis and Jews that settled in India and preserved their faith — identity-preserving integration. They did not conquer Kerala. They did not give up their religion; preserving it is precisely the point. And they were not enslaved — that was the Siddis, and the Arab merchants came freely as traders, so that comparison fails twice over.

**19. (B)** Zoroastrianism is among the world's oldest religions; it worships one God, Ahura Mazda, and emphasises choosing good over evil. It is neither recent nor Indian-founded nor polytheistic. It is not a branch of Judaism — the synagogue is the Jewish house of worship, not the Zoroastrian fire temple. And it is not a healing tradition; that confuses it with Sowa Rigpa, the Tibetan 'Art of Healing'.

**20. (A)** Zarathushtra was the prophet who founded Zoroastrianism; the God worshipped is Ahura Mazda. Swapping the two names — making Zarathushtra the god and Ahura Mazda the prophet — is the exact error to avoid. Zarathushtra was not an Arab merchant who built a mosque, nor the 'Good Maharaja' of the Polish-children story; both are entirely different figures from other parts of the chapter.

**21. (D)** The Parsis fled persecution (refuge) and settled in Gujarat, keeping their fire temples and faith — refuge plus identity-preserving integration, a precise double match. They did not come for trade or rule Gujarat. They were not enslaved or made a scheduled tribe — that describes the Siddis. And they are an ancient arrival, not a 20th-century one like the Tibetan refugees.

**22. (A)** The Parsis settled mainly in Gujarat, and the Jews are associated with their synagogues. Kerala is tied to the Syriac Christians and fire temples are Zoroastrian (Parsi), so that pair mismatches both. Tibet and monasteries belong to the Tibetan refugees, and Armenia and churches to the Armenians — neither pair fits the Parsis or Jews.

**23. (D)** The Parsis came for refuge from persecution, not to trade, which directly breaks the 'everyone came to trade' claim. They did not come to conquer or rule Gujarat. They were not enslaved — that was the Siddis. And far from refusing to settle, they settled permanently and kept their fire temples, becoming a lasting part of Indian society.

**24. (C)** The chapter calls Zoroastrianism one of the world's oldest religions. It is not the newest. The Syriac Christians show Christianity reached Kerala in ancient times, not only recently. And Islam reached India's west coast peacefully through Arab merchants from the 7th century — well before, not after, the later military invasions. Three options misdate the arrival of a faith.

**25. (D)** Islam first reached India peacefully through Arab merchants trading on the west coast from the 7th century — separate from, and earlier than, the later military invasions. It did not arrive only by those invasions; that is the very misconception the chapter corrects. It did not first arrive in the 20th century. And it was not brought by the Siddis, who are an African-origin community with a different history.

**26. (B)** The chapter is careful to separate the peaceful 7th-century arrival via Arab traders from the much later military invasions — two distinct events, the trading arrival being far earlier. They are not one event. The peaceful arrival actually disproves the idea that religions spread only by conquest. And it shows Islam did spread by trade, contradicting any claim that it never spread peacefully.

**27. (C)** India's oldest mosque, built where Arab merchants settled, is concrete proof that Islam came early and peacefully by trade, not only by the later invasions. It was not the product of conquest by Arab merchants. It was not built by the Siddis, who are a separate African-origin community. And the Parsis kept Zoroastrianism — they did not adopt Islam, so that option is simply false.

**28. (D)** The Mappila are the Kerala Muslim community that formed as Arab merchants settled and married locally — opportunity-driven trade leading to a lasting community. The Tibetan-refugee shelter, the enslaved African Siddis, and the Polish-children rescue are all separate stories in the chapter and describe entirely different communities and circumstances.

**29. (D)** The Arab merchants who settled and the Mappila community that grew from them show a community forming peacefully through trade and intermarriage — the opposite of 'by force'. So they disprove the idea that communities spread only by conquest. They also show trade can create lasting communities. And the Arab merchants came freely for opportunity; they were not enslaved like the Siddis.

**30. (C)** The Mappila formed from free Arab traders who settled and married locally, whereas the Siddis were brought to India enslaved — a fundamental difference in choice and circumstance. Reversing the two (Mappila enslaved, Siddis free) is wrong. Neither community began as a conquering army. And neither is described as fleeing religious persecution — that motive fits the Parsis and Jews, not these two.

**31. (B)** The Siddis stand out because they were brought enslaved — not by free choice for refuge or opportunity like the others. Keeping one's original religion was true of many communities (Jews, Parsis, Christians), so that is not unique. Having an ancient faith fits the Zoroastrians and others too. And marrying into local families describes the Arab merchants/Mappila, not the enslaved Siddis.

**32. (C)** The Siddis are of African origin and were brought to India enslaved over a long span from about the 7th to the 19th centuries. They are not Tibetan refugees, not Armenian traders, and not the Polish children sheltered during the war — each of those is a separate community with a different origin and a different reason for being in India.

**33. (A)** A scheduled tribe is a community the Indian Constitution recognises for special support and protection, which is the Siddis' modern status. It is not a ban on settling — the recognition assumes they are citizens deserving help. It is not a foreign trading guild; the Siddis are an Indian community of African descent. And it confers protective support, not ruling power over a state.

**34. (B)** The Siddis began enslaved yet are now a scheduled tribe with constitutional protection — showing India eventually drew even an involuntarily-brought community into the protections of society. This contradicts the idea that only wealthy traders were welcome. It contradicts expelling the involuntarily-arrived. And it contradicts keeping enslaved communities permanently outside — the opposite happened.

**35. (C)** Both the Armenians and the Arab merchants came for opportunity through trade and settled in India — the Armenians even building churches. Neither fled persecution as refugees (that fits the Parsis, Jews and Tibetans). Neither was brought enslaved (the Siddis). And neither arrived as 20th-century war refugees — that describes the Polish children and the Tibetans, not these traders.

**36. (B)** The Armenians traded and settled yet built churches — showing that even opportunity-seekers kept their faith and identity while joining Indian society, the same pattern as the refugee communities. They did not trade away their religion. Building churches shows lasting roots, not the absence of them. And trading and settling is the opposite of conquering the regions they traded in.

**37. (C)** The Baha'is, who also came seeking refuge and acceptance, show India's openness continuing into modern times and reaching diverse faiths. So acceptance did not end with the ancient communities. It was not limited to traders — the Baha'is and Tibetans were not traders. And it was not confined to one religion; the whole chapter spans Jews, Christians, Zoroastrians, Muslims, Baha'is and more.

**38. (A)** The Baha'is and the Tibetan refugees are the modern examples of India's continuing acceptance and refuge. The Jews and Syriac Christians are ancient communities. The Parsis (ancient refuge) and Arab merchants (early trade) are also old arrivals. The Siddis' enslavement spanned earlier centuries and the Armenians were earlier traders — none of these pairs makes the 'modern times' point as clearly as the Baha'is and Tibetans.

**39. (C)** The 'Good Maharaja' sheltered Polish children fleeing the dangers of war — a clear example of giving refuge to those in danger. He did not build India's oldest mosque (that was the Arab merchants). He did not found Zoroastrianism (that was the prophet Zarathushtra, long before). And recognising the Siddis as a scheduled tribe is a modern constitutional act, not the Maharaja's deed.

**40. (C)** The Polish children were refugees fleeing the dangers of war, given shelter by the Maharaja. They were not traders seeking opportunity; they sought safety, not profit. They were not brought enslaved like the Siddis — they were sheltered out of compassion. And they were certainly not original inhabitants; they came from far away during wartime.

**41. (A)** Both the Polish children and the Tibetan refugees are cases of India sheltering people fleeing danger — the same value of refuge. The Arab merchants came for trade, an opportunity case, so the link there fails. The Siddis were enslaved, a different kind of arrival entirely. And the Armenians' church-building is about trade and identity, not about giving refuge.

**42. (A)** The Tibetans fled danger and were sheltered — that is refuge. It is not 'opportunity', which the chapter reserves for those who came freely for trade. It is not 'conquest'; they came as refugees, not an army. And it is not 'enslavement', the involuntary path that describes the Siddis. The Tibetans are a clear refuge case.

**43. (A)** Sowa Rigpa, the Tibetan system of medicine, became part of India's AYUSH programme — showing how a refugee community enriched Indian knowledge. It did not replace India's older medical systems; it was added alongside them. It was not kept secret — being included in AYUSH means it was shared. And it was not abandoned; it was preserved and recognised nationally.

**44. (B)** Sowa Rigpa entering AYUSH proves refugees can enrich the host culture, refuting the idea that they add nothing or weaken it. The chapter affirms — not denies — that India sheltered refugees, so that is not the misconception being corrected. The point is not to clarify whether Sowa Rigpa is a religion (it is a medical system). And the Tibetans came for refuge, not trade, so that is a separate matter.

**45. (C)** The Tibetans were given refuge (fleeing danger) and then contributed Sowa Rigpa to India's medicine — refuge plus cultural enrichment, a two-part match. India did not admit only profitable traders; it sheltered refugees who brought no trade goods. Refugees did not have to surrender their knowledge — they shared it through AYUSH. And the example shows enrichment, not the weakening the last option claims.

**46. (D)** Persecution is hostility and ill-treatment, often for religious or other reasons — exactly what many refugees fled. It is not a welcome; that would be the opposite. It is not a trade agreement. And it is not shelter offered to the endangered — that is refuge or asylum, which is what persecuted people seek, not the persecution itself.

**47. (A)** A refugee flees persecution (ill-treatment) and seeks refuge (shelter) — the correct cause-and-response. Reversing them — fleeing shelter to seek ill-treatment — makes no sense. They are opposites, not synonyms. And persecution is the danger fled, not the shelter given; calling persecution 'the shelter' confuses the two ends of the refugee's journey.

**48. (D)** Pluralism is the peaceful coexistence of many communities, faiths and cultures together — what India displays. It is not the rule of one community over the rest; that would be domination, not pluralism. It is not the removal of minorities; pluralism preserves them. And it is not enforcing one shared religion; pluralism means many faiths living side by side.

**49. (A)** Ethos means the characteristic spirit or guiding values of a people — for India, the spirit of acceptance the chapter describes. It is not a system of medicine (that suggests Sowa Rigpa). It is not a place of worship (synagogue, mosque, fire temple, church). And it is not trade goods. Ethos is about values, not buildings, healing, or commerce.

**50. (B)** Tolerance is the attitude of accepting and respecting beliefs different from one's own, while pluralism is the social condition of many communities actually coexisting together — an attitude versus a state of society. They are not about rejection or domination; both are positive. They have nothing to do with medicine or worship. And leaving/entering a country is emigrate/immigrate, an unrelated pair.

**51. (D)** Cosmopolitan means at home with many cultures ('a citizen of the world'); tolerance means accepting and respecting different beliefs; and ethos means a people's guiding spirit or values. The other orderings shuffle these three definitions onto the wrong words. The last option assigns medicine, worship and trade — none of which matches any of the three social terms.

**52. (D)** Leaving the homeland is emigrating and entering India is immigrating, so the person emigrated from home and immigrated to India. The reversed pairing mislabels both directions. The two actions do not cancel out — a single move is naturally both an emigration and an immigration. And persecution (ill-treatment) is not a synonym for immigration; it may be the reason for leaving, but it is a different concept.

**53. (D)** Building synagogues, fire temples and churches while becoming part of Indian life is exactly identity-preserving integration — keeping their identities did not mean staying apart. It does not show they refused to settle; the buildings prove lasting settlement. It is the opposite of giving up their faiths. And it has nothing to do with ruling the older inhabitants; coexistence, not domination, is the theme.

**54. (B)** The chapter's balanced position is identity-preserving integration: newcomers joined Indian society yet kept their faiths, languages and customs — neither total assimilation nor total separation. Full assimilation (losing all origins) overshoots one way. Complete separation overshoots the other. And taking control of India misreads coexistence as conquest. The middle path is the chapter's actual claim.

**55. (B)** The surviving synagogues, fire temples, churches and languages are the chapter's clearest proof that newcomers kept their identities while living within Indian society. They were not handed separate territories to rule. They did not adopt one common religion and language — that would be losing their identities. And they did not leave whenever questioned; they settled permanently, keeping their distinct ways.

**56. (A)** The chapter argues diversity enriched Indian culture — adding foods, words, crafts, music and faiths — directly refuting the 'weakened' claim. It did not strip away older culture; it added to it. It did not leave culture unchanged; it visibly broadened it. And it did not make communities identical; the whole point is that distinct communities coexisted, keeping their differences while contributing.

**57. (B)** Everyday borrowed foods, words, crafts and music are evidence that mixing communities enriches a culture rather than impoverishing it. It does not destroy the culture — these contributions became part of Indian life. It does not leave culture unchanged — the borrowings are visible changes. And it shows far more than trade goods crossed into India: language, cuisine, crafts and music did too.

**58. (D)** The chapter's overall verdict is that diversity is a source of strength and richness, in keeping with vasudhaiva kutumbakam. It is not a weakness that left India vulnerable — the evidence shows enrichment. It is not a problem to be solved by removing minorities; pluralism preserves them. And it is certainly not unimportant — diversity is the chapter's central, valued theme.

**59. (C)** The Jews and Tibetans fled persecution or danger (refuge), the Arab merchants came freely to trade (opportunity), and the Siddis were brought enslaved — the only fully correct sort. 'All four for trade' ignores the refugees and the enslaved Siddis. Calling the Arab merchants refugees and the Tibetans opportunity-seekers mislabels both. And labelling the Jews and Arab merchants as enslaved is plainly false — that path belongs only to the Siddis.

**60. (B)** All three claims are wrong, so the correct count is none. Not everyone came to trade — Parsis, Jews and Tibetans fled persecution or danger, and the Siddis were brought enslaved. Refugees did not lose their identities — they kept synagogues, fire temples, churches and languages. And diversity did not weaken the culture — it enriched it with foods, words, crafts, music and faiths. Each surviving 'only this one is correct' option still endorses a refuted misconception.
