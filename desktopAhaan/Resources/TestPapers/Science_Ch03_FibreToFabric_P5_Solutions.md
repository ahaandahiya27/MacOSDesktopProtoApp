## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** Wool and silk are both proteins, so they share the burnt-hair smell, the charring (not melting), and reasonable wet behaviour; those three clues cannot tell them apart. Felting and matting come from wool's overlapping surface scales, which silk's smooth filament lacks, so the matting clue is the true discriminator. Silk does not smell papery (that is cellulose), so the burnt-hair smell is shared, not unique. Silk does not lose all strength when wet, so wet strength does not separate them. Silk is a protein that chars, not a synthetic that melts, so charring is also shared.

**2. (C)** A melting bead with no smell is the synthetic signature, so Thread 1 = synthetic is right. The burnt-hair char with wet resistance is the protein/animal signature, so Thread 2 is the animal fibre, not the plant one the student named. The papery flare with grey ash and wet weakness is the cellulose/plant signature, so Thread 3 is the plant fibre. The student called Thread 2 'plant' and Thread 3 'animal', swapping them, so exactly two labels are wrong. Melting indicates synthetic, not plant, and grey ash indicates plant, not animal, so 'all three wrong' rests on inverted signatures. Thread 1's bead is clearly synthetic, not animal, so 'only one wrong' is also false.

**3. (D)** All animal protein fibres contain nitrogen and so give the burnt-hair smell on burning, which is therefore predictable. Felting depends on overlapping scales; wool has them and felts, but silk is a smooth protein that does not felt, so 'animal protein' alone does not predict felting. Hence one behaviour is certain and the other is not. Not every animal protein felts (silk does not), so 'both predictable' is wrong. Animal proteins do not smell papery (that is cellulose), so 'either smell' is false. Protein fibres are nitrogen-rich by definition, so the claim that only some contain nitrogen is incorrect.

**4. (A)** Gloss and smoothness alone do not identify silk; the diagnostic clues are the burn smell and wet strength. A papery flare and loss of wet strength are cotton (cellulose) traits, while silk is a protein that keeps much of its strength wet and smells of burnt hair, so the thread is cotton, not silk. Silk does not flare papery, so calling it silk ignores the burn test. Synthetics melt into a bead rather than flaring with a papery smell and do not necessarily weaken when wet, so 'synthetic' misreads the clues. Wool is a protein that smells of burnt hair, not papery, so the wool label is wrong too.

**5. (B)** Wool and silk do keep much of their strength wet while cotton weakens, so the pattern superficially matches; but wet strength is a consequence of these particular fibres, not the definition of an animal fibre, so an 'if and only if' rule overclaims. The defining feature is protein chemistry, best shown by the burnt-hair burn smell. So the rule is not perfect. It is not backwards: animal wool and silk do keep wet strength while plant cotton loses it, so the directions are right even though the rule overstates. Cotton loses, not keeps, its wet strength, so the final option is factually wrong.

**6. (D)** The burn smell sorts fibres into protein (burnt hair: wool and silk) versus cellulose (papery: cotton). It identifies the cotton but cannot tell wool from silk, since both smell of burnt hair, so the silk cannot be singled out by smell alone. Silk smells of burnt hair, not papery, so it shares its smell with wool. Silk does burn (it is a protein), so 'does not burn at all' is false. Cotton smells papery, not of burnt hair, so the three are not all the same; the cotton can in fact be separated.

**7. (D)** Sorting discards coarse guard hairs and keeps the fine undercoat. Flock A: 40% fine of 5 kg = 2 kg. Flock B: 90% fine of 3 kg = 2.7 kg. So Flock B yields more usable fine fibre despite less total fleece. More total fleece does not guarantee more fine fibre when most of it is coarse, so Flock A's bulk is misleading. The winning reason is the guard-hair proportion and the arithmetic, not the sheep's health. Sorting removes the coarse hairs rather than recovering them, so the two flocks do not end up equal.

**8. (C)** Selective breeding shifts a trait within what the animal can already make; here it can make keratin finer and more uniform, but it cannot make a sheep secrete fibroin, which is a different protein produced by the silkworm larva's silk glands. So refinement yes, conversion to silk no. Keratin does not 'change into' silk protein no matter how fine, so the first distractor is biologically false. Mulberry leaves feed silkworms and cannot give a sheep silk glands, so diet does not unlock fibroin. Breeding clearly does change fleece quality (that is its whole purpose), so 'neither' is wrong.

**9. (A)** Cold prompts thicker, better fleece, so siting wool sheep in cool hill regions (J&K, Himachal) is the logical consequence; both the premise and the inference hold. Fleece insulates against cold rather than shedding heat, so the hot-coast alternative rests on inverted biology. Fleece thickness is exactly why the cool region is chosen, so 'nothing to do with region' is false. The driving reason is the cold-fleece relationship, not grass abundance, so the last option attaches the wrong reason to a correct verdict.

**10. (D)** Felting is caused by the surface scales of wool ratcheting together under heat plus agitation; silk lacks those scales, so it cannot felt, which is the decisive structural difference. The crimp gives wool bulk and traps air for warmth, but it is the scales (not mere waviness) that cause matting, so the crimp is a decoy here. It is the scales, not an abstract keratin-versus-fibroin rule, that drive felting, so the protein-only explanation misses the mechanism. Colour and dye have no bearing on felting, so the dye option is wrong.

**11. (C)** You can only grade fibre reliably after washing, so sorting belongs after scouring; sorting greasy fleece mixes grades, and that uneven, mixed batch then takes dye unevenly and spins into poor yarn, so a misplaced early step degrades a much later one. Dyeing fibre before spinning is normal practice and does not 'ruin scouring' (which is already done), so that option is wrong. Burr-picking before dyeing is the correct order, so it is not misplaced. You cannot scour a fleece while it is still on the sheep, so 'scour before shearing' is impossible; shearing must be first.

**12. (C)** Grease interferes with two different downstream jobs in the same way: it hides the fibre's true texture and length (blocking sorting) and it repels dye unevenly (blocking dyeing). Removing it once, by scouring, unlocks both, which is why scouring gates them together. Scouring cleans; it does not colour or grade, so sorting and dyeing are not repeats of it. Grease repels even dyeing rather than helping dye cling, so 'gates only dyeing... grease helps dye' is wrong. Greasy fleece cannot be graded reliably, so 'sorting best on greasy fleece' is also false.

**13. (B)** Dye soaks evenly only into already-clean fibre; doing both at once means grease is still leaving as colour arrives, giving patchy dye. Also, colouring before sorting fixes coarse and fine hairs together in one dyed lot, defeating the later grading needed for fine yarn, so two separate defects arise. The shortcut therefore is not defect-free. A hot dye bath does not generate a burnt-hair smell (that needs burning), so that 'only defect' is invented. Heat and dye cannot transmute keratin into cellulose, so the fibre-change claim is impossible.

**14. (D)** Burrs are tough bits of plant material physically tangled in the fleece; washing removes greasy, dusty matter but cannot dissolve or rinse out solid burrs, so they survive scouring and must be picked out separately. Hot water does not dissolve woody plant fragments, so 'dissolves the burrs' is false. Burrs are plant matter, not grease, so they are not washed out with the grease. Burr-picking is done on the sheared, scoured fleece, never before shearing (there is no detached fleece yet), so that reason is also wrong even though the verdict 'worker is wrong' is right.

**15. (B)** Sorting (grading the fibre by quality) and picking burrs (removing plant debris) both act on already-scoured clean fibre, and neither one uses the other's output, so their order relative to each other can be swapped with no harm — that swap is the safe one. Swapping scouring and shearing is impossible, since you cannot wash a fleece still on the live sheep. Swapping dyeing and spinning does change the result (colouring loose fibre versus finished yarn is not equivalent), so it is not harmless. Swapping scouring and sorting is the fatal swap: grading greasy, unwashed fleece is unreliable, so that order spoils later steps.

**16. (C)** Clean, even colour proves scouring and dyeing were done. Unevenness from mixed coarse and fine hairs is the signature of skipped sorting, and embedded plant fragments are the signature of skipped burr-picking, so those two were omitted. The evidence (clean, evenly coloured) shows scouring and dyeing were not skipped, so the first distractor inverts the facts. The yarn is described as clean, so scouring was not skipped, and shearing must have happened or there would be no yarn at all. Dyeing clearly happened (even colour), and skipping spinning would leave no yarn to inspect, so the last option is impossible.

**17. (D)** Two constraints fix burr-picking's place: clean (scoured) fibre makes burrs visible, so it follows scouring; and removing burrs after dyeing or spinning would mar the coloured thread and leave debris, so it precedes those. Burrs are not removed more easily from spun yarn (they are locked in), so 'last' is wrong. Burrs do not fall out during spinning; they get bound into the yarn, so 'position does not matter' is false. Doing it before scouring means grease hides the burrs, so that order undermines the whole point.

**18. (C)** Scouring leaves 60% of 5 kg = 3.0 kg. Sorting discards a quarter, leaving three-quarters of 3.0 = 2.25 kg of fine wool. Dyeing/spinning then lose 10%, leaving 90% of 2.25 = 2.025 kg ≈ 2.0 kg. The 2.25 kg figure stops after sorting and forgets the final 10% loss. The 2.5 kg figure mishandles the sorting fraction (using a smaller discard). The 1.5 kg figure over-subtracts, e.g. by taking all percentages off the original 5 kg, which double-counts the losses.

**19. (D)** Dyeing's only job is appearance (colour), satisfying the 'no cleaning, grading, debris-removal or twisting' constraint, so it is the unique answer. Scouring's purpose is cleaning; any brightening is incidental, so it violates the 'without cleaning' rule and the student's reason is wrong on purpose. Scouring does not add colour, so the student's premise is also factually false. Sorting grades fibres rather than changing their look in the colour sense, so it is not the appearance step. Because scouring is disqualified, there are not two correct answers.

**20. (D)** Fineness is a property of the breed and its undercoat, not of the shearing month, and sunlight does not 'thin' fibres, so that technical-sounding reason is the false one. Letting a new fleece regrow before winter is a genuine practical reason. Avoiding winter exposure is a real welfare reason. Removing the fleece in warm months when it is not needed for warmth is also valid. So the only invalid reason is the spring-sunlight fineness claim.

**21. (A)** Scouring's water removes grease and dirt from fleece; reeling's hot water softens the sericin gum so the silk filament releases and unwinds. Neither adds colour, which is a separate dyeing step, so the student's claim is wrong. The second distractor swaps the two purposes (wool has no gum to soften and reeling is not for washing fleece). The twist that makes yarn is a spinning/throwing step, not scouring or reeling, so 'add the final twist' is false.

**22. (D)** Felting needs heat plus agitation; cool washing removes heat, while not wringing, drying flat and avoiding the tumble dryer all remove agitation and rubbing, so the four instructions share one purpose: preventing felting/shrinkage. They are not four unrelated risks; the unifying mechanism is felting, so the 'separate risks' option misses the link. They protect the fibre structure, not merely the dye, so the dye-only option is too narrow. Wool felts; it does not dissolve in hot water, so the 'dissolving' explanation is factually wrong.

**23. (A)** Felting requires heat and agitation acting together; Mitten C alone has both and felts most. Mitten A (heat only) and Mitten B (agitation only) each lack one ingredient, so both felt far less than C. So the rank is C, then A and B much lower. Still hot water does not 'penetrate deepest' to out-felt C, so A-first is wrong. Rubbing alone (cold) felts only weakly because heat is missing, so B-first is wrong. Plain contact with water does not felt wool equally; without both heat and agitation little happens, so 'all equal' is false.

**24. (C)** Both fabrics reach the same room temperature, so they are not at different temperatures; the warm feeling is about the RATE of heat conduction from the hand. Wool's air-trapping structure conducts slowly, so the hand loses heat slowly and feels warm, which corrects the student's temperature error. Wool does not become hotter just by being animal-derived, so the first distractor repeats the error. Wool generates no heat by a chemical reaction, so the second is false. Cotton is a natural plant fibre, not a synthetic, so the third is wrong on both counts.

**25. (A)** Wool insulates by trapping still air; water conducts heat much better than air, so soaking displaces the air and the jumper feels cold and clammy, and drying brings the air pockets back, restoring warmth, which explains all three states with one property. Wetting does not turn keratin into cellulose, so the protein-change option is impossible. Dye does not provide warmth, so the dye explanation is wrong. Water is a far worse insulator than air, so the wet jumper genuinely loses warmth; it is not merely a feeling, so the last option is backwards.

**26. (D)** Felting locks the scales together essentially permanently; neither cold water nor softener unlocks them, so the sweater stays shrunken. Cold water and softener do not make the interlocked scales slide apart, so the first option is wrong. Heat promotes more felting rather than reversing it, so 'hot water un-shrinks' is false. The sweater is still keratin, just matted; it has not become cotton, so the last option's reason is incorrect even though its 'will not return' verdict is right by accident.

**27. (B)** Each observation has its own cause: crimp traps air (warmth), scales interlock (matting), and protein/nitrogen chemistry (burnt-hair smell). Trapped air explains only the warmth, not the matting or the smell, so the student's single cause is wrong. The second distractor scrambles the three assignments. Dye explains none of them, since undyed wool is still warm, still mats and still smells of burnt hair, so the dye option is false.

**28. (A)** Insulation comes from trapped still air; the lofty knit (X) holds more air, while felting (Y) compresses the air out, so X is warmer despite equal mass. Density does not equal warmth; packing fibres tight removes the air pockets, so 'denser is warmer' is wrong. Equal mass does not force equal warmth, because structure (air-trapping) governs insulation, so 'equally warm' is false. The two scarves have equal mass, so 'X weighs less' is factually wrong; weight is not the reason.

**29. (D)** The larva is the heavy feeder, and the same larva spins the cocoon as it transforms into a pupa, so both functions belong to one stage. The pupa rests inside the already-spun cocoon and does not spin it, so 'pupa spins' is wrong. The adult moth emerges from the finished cocoon and lays eggs; it does not spin, so 'moth spins' is wrong. The pupa does not feed (it is the resting stage), so 'pupa feeds' is also false; the feeder is the larva.

**30. (A)** Two things are wrong: the larva (not the moth) spins the cocoon, and an emerged moth has bitten through it, cutting the long filament into short pieces so no unbroken thread can be reeled. Saying the moth spins it (just at a different time) keeps a false claim, so the 'only error is order' option is wrong. The moth does not spin the cocoon at all, so the second distractor also preserves a false claim. An emerged cocoon is broken, so it gives short pieces, not the longest unbroken thread, making 'no error' false.

**31. (B)** The larva eats mulberry leaves to grow to its normal full size; once grown it spins a single cocoon whose filament length is fixed by the larva itself, not by how much it was fed past satiation. So doubling the leaves does not double the thread — extra food beyond what the larva needs is simply wasted, making (B) correct. The silk is built by the larva's silk glands, not made 'directly out of' the leaves, so (A) is wrong. One larva spins one cocoon, never two from surplus food, so (C) is wrong, and a silkworm produces only silk, never a wool-like fibre, so (D) is wrong.

**32. (B)** The unbroken filament exists only between cocoon completion and moth emergence; harvest must occur in that gap, and it is narrow because an emerging moth severs the thread. So the window is bounded by two life-cycle events. Eggs contain no wound silk, so harvesting at egg-laying yields nothing. An emerged moth has broken the cocoon, so waiting for emergence ruins, not improves, the thread. Heavy feeding is before any cocoon exists, so there is no thread to harvest then.

**33. (D)** Fibroin is the silk filament woven into cloth; sericin is the gum coating removed during reeling. Keeping only sericin keeps the gum and discards the thread, so weaving fails. The first distractor reverses their roles (sericin is the gum, not the strong thread). They are not equally good threads; only fibroin is woven, so 'both good' is false. Fibroin is a fibre, not a gum, so 'both are gums, neither weavable' misclassifies fibroin.

**34. (A)** Hot water softens the sericin gum so the filament releases smoothly; without it the gum holds the thread fast and pulling breaks it into pieces, so only fragments come off. Dry, gummed thread does not slide off faster; the gum makes it stick, so 'unwinds faster' is wrong. Hot water in reeling frees the thread, not dyes it, so the dye claim is false. A cocoon is one long fibroin filament under a gum coating, so it does contain thread; 'solid gum, no thread' is incorrect.

**35. (C)** A single silk filament is extremely fine and weak, so combining several yields a usable, stronger thread, making (i) the real reason. Mulberry silk is naturally whitish, so there is no colour-blending need, ruling out (ii). A single filament is too thin to use, not too thick, so combining cannot be to thin it, ruling out (iii). Since two of the three reasons are false, 'all three correct' is also wrong.

**36. (B)** Reeling produces thread by unwinding and combining filaments; weaving makes cloth by interlacing warp and weft on a loom, so weaving is the fabric step and reeling is not. The first distractor swaps the two definitions. Weaving does not merely unwind cocoons, so 'both unwind, neither makes fabric' is wrong since weaving clearly makes fabric. Boiling belongs to reeling (to soften gum), not weaving, and heat does not 'form cloth' by itself, so the last option is nonsense.

**37. (B)** Sericin is a stiff gum that coats the fibroin filament; while it is present it stiffens the thread and its rough coating scatters light, so the thread is dull. Degumming strips that coating away, exposing the smooth fibroin beneath — the bare filament now bends easily (softness) and its smooth surface reflects light evenly (lustre), so one change explains both effects, making (B) correct. The shine comes from the bare fibroin, not from a dye, so (A) is wrong. Degumming removes a coating; it does not transmute fibroin into another protein (C) nor inject air pockets into the filament (D), so both are false.

**38. (C)** The sheep gives wool, the silk-moth larva gives silk, and the Pashmina goat gives a fine wool from its undercoat — each is an animal that yields a fibre directly. The mulberry plant yields no fibre at all; it only provides the leaves that the silk-moth larva eats, so it is the odd one, making (C) correct. The sheep does give fibre (wool), so (A) is wrong. It is the larva, not the adult moth, that spins silk, so (B) is wrong, and the Pashmina goat does give fibre (fine wool), so (D) is wrong.

**39. (B)** Muga's gold is a natural feature of its Assam silkworm; boiling softens gum rather than dyeing, and mulberry silk is naturally whitish, so the colour is intrinsic and not universal to silk. Boiling in dye is not how muga gets its colour, so the guide's claim is false. Leaf colour does not pass through to make golden silk, so the diet explanation is wrong. Most silk is whitish, not golden-until-bleached, so the 'all silk golden' idea is false.

**40. (B)** The larva first feeds, then spins the cocoon; the cocoon is boiled to kill the pupa (and soften the gum) before the moth can emerge and break the thread, then it is reeled into thread, then woven into cloth. So boiling must precede reeling. Putting reeling before boiling lets the pupa live and risks the moth breaking the thread, so that order is wrong. Feeding must come before cocoon spinning (a fed larva spins), so starting with cocoon spinning is wrong. Killing the pupa cannot come before there is even a cocoon or a fed larva, so starting with boiling is impossible.

**41. (C)** Pashmina is a fine goat-undercoat wool; muga is a non-mulberry silk from an Assam silkworm, so the correct card names a wool from a goat and a silk from a silkworm. The first distractor swaps both fibre types and sources. Pashmina is wool, not silk, so 'both silks' is wrong. Muga is silk, not goat wool, so 'both goat wools' is wrong.

**42. (A)** Silk fabric originates as the fibroin filament a silk-moth larva spins into its cocoon, recovered by reeling, so the larva is the maker. A goat's undercoat is Pashmina wool, an animal fibre but not silk, so that origin is wrong. Sericin is the gum removed in reeling, not the woven thread, so the fabric is fibroin, not sericin. Seed hairs from plant bolls are cotton, a plant fibre, not silk.

**43. (C)** Cotton (seed) and jute (stem) are plant cellulose; wool (sheep) and silk (silkworm) are animal protein; nylon is a petroleum-based synthetic, so all three categories are correct. Wool is animal, not plant, so grouping it with cotton/jute is wrong, and nylon is synthetic, not animal protein. Nylon is not an animal protein fibre, so the second distractor miscategorises it. Nylon is man-made, not natural, so 'all five natural' is false.

**44. (B)** A cocoon is built from one filament often hundreds to over a thousand metres long, which explains both the cocoon's value and why one long thread can be reeled. A few centimetres wildly underestimates it. The length is not a fixed one metre; it varies and is far longer. The filament is vastly longer than a hair, so 'shorter than a hair' is the opposite of the truth; combining cocoons is for strength, not because each is tiny.

**45. (C)** The silk-moth larva spins silk and the Pashmina goat gives a fine wool, so both halves are right. Sheep give wool (not silk) and the silk moth gives silk (not wool), so that pairing swaps them. The mulberry plant only feeds the silkworm and sheep give wool not cotton, so that pairing is doubly wrong. Yaks and camels give wool, not silk or cotton, so the last pairing is wrong on both counts.

**46. (A)** Sorter's disease is anthrax contracted by inhaling spores in raw fleece dust; the sorter handles that very material, so the sorter is at highest risk. Scouring later removes the spores, so a finished sweater carries essentially none and body heat does not reactivate them, ruling out the shopper. Silk reeling involves boiled cocoons, not anthrax-laden fleece, so the reeler option is wrong. The recognised route is inhalation of raw-fleece spores, not drinking milk, so the shepherd-via-milk option misstates transmission.

**47. (A)** Sorter's disease comes from spores in raw fleece; scouring removes them, so finished scoured wool is essentially spore-free and an unlikely source, meaning this worker's illness is probably not anthrax from the wool. Not all wool workers get anthrax; the risk is specific to handling raw fleece, so 'all get it regardless' is false. Dye does not breed anthrax spores, so that option is wrong. The claim that scoured wool causes no illness 'at all' overreaches; the point is specifically that it is not the raw-fleece anthrax source, so 'cannot be any illness' is unsupported.

**48. (B)** Sorter's disease is caught by inhaling anthrax spores carried in the dust of raw fleece, so the protection that matches the route is one that filters the air the worker breathes — a face mask (respirator), making (B) correct. The disease is not contracted chiefly through cuts on the hands, so gloves (A) miss the route; it does not enter through the feet, so boots (C) are misdirected; and it is not an eye-surface infection, so goggles (D) are misdirected too. Only the airway protection blocks the inhalation route.

**49. (B)** Wool comes off the sheep as short, separate staple hairs; laid side by side they would simply slide apart, so they must be twisted (spun) so the fibres grip one another into a continuous yarn — that is why wool is spun, making (B) correct. Silk, by contrast, is reeled off the cocoon as one long unbroken filament that already has its length, so it needs little twist to stay together. Weight is not why wool needs twisting, so (A) is wrong; silk is an animal protein, not a sticky plant fibre, so (C) is wrong; and the two fibres plainly do not need equal twisting, so (D) is wrong.

**50. (D)** The wool sorter handles raw, unwashed fleece and inhales its spores, so that line is correct. Silk reelers work with boiled cocoons, and dye fumes do not carry anthrax, so that line is false. A shopper's sweater is scoured and spore-free, so wearing it is not a route. The shepherd's recognised exposure would be to fleece-borne spores, not 'only from milk', so that line misstates the route.

**51. (A)** In this blend the wool supplies warmth through its air-trapping crimp, while polyester supplies durability and shape-retention; so replacing wool with polyester would lose warmth, making the parent's reasoning wrong. Polyester does not trap more air than wool's crimped fibres, so '100% polyester warmest' is false. Polyester is a synthetic, not an animal fibre, so that claim is wrong. The verdict 'wrong' is right, but the burnt-hair reason is irrelevant and false, so that option attaches a wrong reason.

**52. (C)** Softness comes from a fine breed plus careful sorting; safety comes from disinfecting fleece and protecting the airway; shape-retention comes from a synthetic blend like polyester, so this plan uses the correct mechanism for each goal. A coarse breed and skipped sorting hurt softness, unmasked sorters are endangered, and grease does not hold a garment's shape, so that plan fails all three. Mulberry leaves are silkworm food, skipping scouring leaves dirty wool, and bright dye does not hide or kill spores, so that plan fails too. A fleece has no pupa to boil, sorting after dyeing is too late for clean grading, and merely adding wool does not give the wrinkle-resistance a synthetic provides, so the last plan also fails.

**53. (D)** Standard reeling boils the cocoon while the pupa is still inside, killing it so the moth cannot emerge and cut the continuous thread; this is the ethical objection and the reason it is hard to avoid. The killing is of the pupa, not of egg-laying adults, so 'crushed while laying eggs' misplaces the stage. Eggs must hatch into the silk-spinning larvae, so boiling eggs would defeat the process and is not what happens. Larvae are well-fed (not starved) so they spin good cocoons, so the starvation option contradicts practice.

**54. (C)** Scarcity combined with desirability raises price; an animal yielding only a little fine fibre yearly makes that fibre rare, so the trader's two-part logic holds. Pashmina (goat) and vicuña (a llama relative) are animal fibres, not mass-produced plant fibres, so the first distractor mis-sources them. Animal fibres vary widely in price by quality and quantity, so 'all priced the same' is false. Scarcity of a desirable good raises, not lowers, its price, so the last option inverts basic economics.

**55. (A)** Cocoons meant for silk (Batch S) must be boiled or steamed to kill the pupa before the moth emerges, because a moth that chews its way out breaks the single long filament that reeling depends on; boiling also softens the sericin so the thread can be unwound. Cocoons kept for breeding (Batch B) must be left unboiled so the moths can emerge, mate and lay the eggs for next season — boiling them would kill the breeding stock. So the two batches cannot be treated alike, making (A) correct. Boiling both (B), boiling Batch B harder (C), or never boiling at all (D) each contradicts one half of this trade-off.
