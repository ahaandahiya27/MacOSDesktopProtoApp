## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** A market is any arrangement - physical or online - that brings buyers, sellers and a price together for an exchange. Riya compared sellers, saw a price and bought, so all three ingredients were present. The building is incidental, not essential. Online sites clearly display real prices, and there is no rule that buyer and seller must be face to face; the location of delivery does not decide where the market 'is' - the exchange of money for goods at an agreed price is the market itself.

**2. (C)** A market needs buyers, sellers AND an agreed price. The haat and the app both have all three. The free toy swap has buyers and a seller of sorts, but no price is paid, so it is not a market in the chapter's sense. Not every exchange is a market - the price element is missing in a free swap. Markets are not limited to physical gatherings (the app counts), nor must they always run on an app (the haat counts), so the single-option answers are too narrow.

**3. (C)** A market is complete only when buyers, sellers and an AGREED price all come together. Here buyers, a seller and goods are all present, but no mutually acceptable price was reached, so no exchange occurred - the missing ingredient is the agreed price. Buyers are clearly present (they are eager), goods are present (the mangoes), and a market can certainly run outdoors like a haat, so those explanations are false.

**4. (D)** Markets arise because each person can produce only a few things but needs and wants many; trading lets a wheat grower obtain cloth, tools and more. There is no government order forcing purchases, and people are perfectly free to make some things at home - they simply cannot make everything. Tax collection is one government activity but it is not the reason markets exist; the root cause is the human inability to be self-sufficient.

**5. (D)** The defining feature common to a cart, a kirana, a mall and a website is that each brings buyers, sellers and a price together. A roof and walls are absent from the cart and the website, an inspector is not always present, and markets routinely sell many kinds of goods - so none of those is the must-have feature. The buyer-seller-price triad is what unifies all four.

**6. (C)** Although water may be a basic need, packaged or supplied water is bought and sold at a price, so it is a market good. The idea that needs are always free is false - food and water are needs yet are routinely paid for. Markets sell both needs and wants, not wants alone, and water is certainly a real good people purchase. The presence of buyers, sellers and a price makes water trading a market.

**7. (C)** An online store does have buyers, sellers and prices, so it is a market - the assertion is true. The reason states exactly the right definition (the triad matters, not a building), so it correctly explains why the online store qualifies. The assertion is not false (online stores are markets), and online stores plainly show prices, so the alternatives that deny these facts are wrong.

**8. (A)** What makes each a market is the buyer-seller-price exchange, which both share despite very different settings. They need not charge the same price - prices often differ between a mall and a haat. A haat is periodic, not open every day, so 'open daily' is false. And neither is required to be government-run. The common, defining feature is the exchange itself, not the comfort or schedule of the place.

**9. (D)** A price below cost wiped out her profit, so she stopped selling - showing the price must cover the seller's costs for supply to continue. The cheapest possible number is therefore not 'best'; it can drive sellers away. The price is reached by both sides, not the buyer alone, and an extremely high price would scare buyers off instead. The 'just right' price sits where the seller can still profit and the buyer is still willing to pay.

**10. (B)** The just-right price is where demand equals supply - here 300 = 300 at 20 rupees, so the market clears. At 10 rupees buyers want far more than sellers will provide (a shortage), and at 30 rupees sellers offer far more than buyers will take (a surplus). The numbers do match exactly at 20 rupees, so a working price plainly exists. Neither the lowest nor the highest price balances the two sides.

**11. (B)** A price below the seller's 80-rupee cost would mean a loss, so she would refuse; the agreed price must rise above 80 yet stay below the 110 she asked, leaving both sides better off - about 95 rupees fits. The buyer's first low offer is not automatically fair, and 60 (even lower than cost) would simply make her stop selling. Sellers are under no obligation to accept any offer, so the 'must accept' option is wrong.

**12. (B)** Setting the cheapest possible price can push it below cost, wiping out profit and driving the seller to stop, which removes the goods from the market - so the assertion is true and the reason explains exactly why. 'Cheaper is always better' is the classic trap and is false. Price clearly does affect supply (too low a price reduces it), so the option denying any link is wrong, and the reason is directly related, not unrelated.

**13. (C)** More buyers competing for an unchanged supply pushes the price up - greater demand with fixed supply raises price. The idea that 'more buyers means cheaper' is backwards. Although supply did not change, demand did, so the price does not stay fixed. High demand never makes goods free - if anything it makes them costlier, the opposite of free.

**14. (B)** A flood of tomatoes is a jump in supply; with demand unchanged, sellers must lower prices to clear the extra stock, so the price falls. More supply does not raise the price - that confuses supply with demand. Demand does not automatically rise just because supply did. And in an ordinary market price is set by demand and supply meeting, not solely by the government, so the price does respond.

**15. (D)** Both sides grew, but demand grew much more than supply, so buyers compete for relatively scarce ice cream and the price rises. More sellers alone would push price down, but here the surge in buyers outweighs that. Because the two sides did NOT grow equally, the price does not stay the same. Any increase in supply does not send price to zero - that ignores the much larger rise in demand.

**16. (C)** Adding lots of supply while demand stays put relieves the shortage, so the price tends to fall back. More supply pushes price down, not up, so a further rise is wrong. Prices are not locked permanently; they respond to the new balance of demand and supply. Increasing supply does not make a good free - it simply reduces the upward pressure on its price.

**17. (A)** Rising demand combined with falling supply squeezes the good from both directions, sending price up the most. Falling demand with rising supply does the opposite, pushing price down. If both are unchanged the price has no reason to move. Rising supply against steady demand also lowers price. Only the simultaneous demand-up, supply-down case maximises the upward pressure.

**18. (B)** Low demand in the dry season means a high price will scare off the few buyers, so most umbrellas stay unsold and earnings drop - the price must reflect what buyers will actually pay. There is no law restricting umbrella sales to one season. The 'highest price means highest earnings' idea ignores that buyers walk away from overpriced goods. And the government does not set umbrella prices each season. The just-right price balances seller's profit with buyer's willingness.

**19. (D)** The just-right (market-clearing) price is exactly where the amount buyers want matches the amount sellers offer, so nothing is left over and no one is left wanting. If buyers want far more than is supplied, there is a shortage and price rises; if sellers offer far more than buyers want, there is a surplus and price falls - neither is the balance point. A price where nobody wants the good is no market at all.

**20. (A)** Heavy demand chasing a tiny supply lets the seller raise the price, because buyers compete for scarce units. The high price is not because the phone is costless to make - production costs exist. High prices do not by themselves increase demand; here demand was already high before the price rose. And no government order forces the high price - it emerges from the demand-supply gap.

**21. (D)** A shrunken supply meeting unchanged demand creates scarcity, pushing the onion price up. Being a daily need does not make scarce onions cheaper - if anything the steady need keeps demand firm while supply falls. Needs are very much priced (people pay for onions every day). And a shortage does not trigger free distribution by sellers; it raises, not removes, the price.

**22. (B)** Pricing far above the balance point pushes buyers toward the seller offering the just-right price, so Seller B sells little. A higher price does not guarantee more profit if buyers refuse to buy. Seller B will not match Seller A's sales while overpriced. And buyers cannot force a price below cost - the seller would simply stop selling; instead buyers just walk away from the overpriced stall.

**23. (D)** Increasing supply to match the heavy demand eases the scarcity and lowers the price toward balance. More advertising would raise demand and push price up further. Making even fewer toys deepens the shortage and raises price. Raising the price does not solve scarcity at the source - the real fix for a shortage is more supply, which moves the market toward its just-right price.

**24. (B)** Supply comes from sellers' willingness to offer; demand comes from buyers' willingness to take - they are the two opposite sides that meet at the just-right price. They do not both mean the buyers' side. The labels are not reversed: supply is the sellers' side, not the buyers'. And neither term means a tax or an MRP; those are unrelated concepts.

**25. (A)** A drought reduces how much wheat is grown, so supply falls; with buyers still needing wheat, the reduced supply pushes the price up. The first blank cannot be 'a rise' because drought destroys, not boosts, output. A fall in supply does not also lower price - scarcity raises it. And supply clearly does change (it falls), so 'no change' is wrong. Less supply, higher price is the correct chain.

**26. (A)** A price the buyer loves may be below the seller's costs; selling at a loss is not sustainable, so the seller stops - this is why the cheapest price is not 'best' for keeping goods available. Sellers do not refuse out of dislike for happy buyers. There is no general law banning low prices. And a low price does not automatically mean poor quality - the real issue is whether the seller can cover costs and still profit.

**27. (C)** A wholesaler deals in large bulk and sells to retailers; the retailer then breaks the bulk into small amounts for final consumers - that is the core contrast. The roles are not reversed (the wholesaler is not the one selling single items to families). Neither makes the goods (that is the producer) nor necessarily exports them. And they do not both sell only to consumers - the wholesaler's customers are mainly retailers.

**28. (A)** A haat appears on fixed days and then disperses, which exactly fits a periodic rural market. A mall is permanent and enclosed, not periodic. A wholesale depot that is always open does not match the 'only certain days' description. An online app is available any time and is not a physical periodic gathering. The defining trait here is the on-certain-days, temporary nature - that is a haat.

**29. (A)** A nearby retail shop is built for small, convenient purchases close to home, perfect for one packet at night. A wholesale market deals in bulk, not single packets, and is not designed for a single late-night buyer. An export house sends goods abroad, not to your kitchen. A haat is only open on certain days, not every hour - so it cannot serve a late-night craving. The retailer is the right fit.

**30. (C)** Combining many sellers' offers in one place for buyers to compare is the job of an aggregator. The app does not make the goods, so it is not the producer. It does not use up the goods, so it is not the consumer. It does not necessarily buy the goods in bulk to own and resell - it simply lists others' offers, which is aggregation, not wholesaling.

**31. (B)** Selling in bulk spreads costs over many metres, so the wholesale per-metre price is lower; the retailer adds the cost and convenience of breaking bulk into small saleable amounts, raising the per-metre price. The lower wholesale price does not mean worse cloth - it is the same cloth in larger lots. The retailer is not the producer. And the lower wholesale price is a natural result of bulk, not a government order.

**32. (B)** A retail shop sells the smallest amounts (a packet or two), a wholesale market sells larger bulk lots, and a producer's factory turns out the largest volumes - so retail to wholesale to producer is smallest to largest. Putting wholesale before retail reverses two steps. Putting the producer first as the smallest is backwards. And they plainly do not all sell the same quantity - the whole chain exists because quantities differ at each stage.

**33. (C)** The shawl moving from a remote weaver to a Delhi buyer shows markets bridge great distances, linking producers and consumers who would never meet otherwise. Markets clearly are not limited to the same town - this very example crosses regions. They are not only for factory goods - a handwoven craft qualifies. And a market always needs a seller; the weaver and the online seller fill that role. Distance-bridging is the key idea.

**34. (C)** The chain exists because each trader adds a real service - buying in bulk, transporting, storing and breaking into small lots - so the mango reaches the city buyer in usable form. There is no law fixing the number of owners. The traders are not useless; remove them and city buyers could not easily get a single mango from a distant orchard. And it is impractical for the orchard owner to deliver personally to every city buyer - that is precisely why the chain of traders exists.

**35. (D)** Biscuits are first made by the producer, sold in bulk to a wholesaler, passed to a retailer, and finally bought by the consumer - producer to wholesaler to retailer to consumer. The other orders scramble these roles: the consumer cannot come first because nothing has been made yet, and the producer cannot come after the retailer or wholesaler because the goods must exist before they can be traded. Only maker-first, user-last is correct.

**36. (A)** Each step adds real services - moving, storing, splitting bulk - plus a fair margin, so the price builds up from 200 to 320 along the chain. It is not pure cheating; the buyer at the end gets a shirt conveniently nearby, which the bare factory price did not include. The rise is not a single government tax. And the retailer is not the producer in disguise - they are different links, each adding value, which is why the price grows step by step.

**37. (D)** The consumer is the final link who actually uses the good. The wholesaler buys to resell to retailers, the retailer buys to resell to consumers, and the producer makes the good to sell onward - none of them is the final user. Only the consumer ends the chain by consuming rather than reselling.

**38. (B)** The bakery makes the bread (producer) and sells single loaves to neighbours (retailer), so it fills both roles in one short chain. It is not a wholesaler-consumer: it does not buy bread in bulk to resell, and it sells rather than eats the bread. It is not a distributor-exporter shipping abroad. Buying flour does not make it 'only a consumer' - its defining market roles toward buyers are producer and retailer.

**39. (D)** A retailer turns a bulk lot into single apples a buyer can grab nearby, adding storage, display and a margin, so the retail price tops the wholesale price. No law forces a doubling of prices. The apple does not change into another fruit. Wholesale prices are real prices actually paid by the retailer. The mark-up reflects genuine value added in moving from bulk to a single convenient unit.

**40. (D)** The cloth reaches a distant small-town shirt through a chain of wholesalers, distributors and retailers who transport it and split it into usable amounts. Buyers do not all travel to Surat - the chain saves them that trouble. Goods do not move without traders; intermediaries do the work. And the government does not personally deliver each town's cloth. The supply chain bridging distance is the correct explanation.

**41. (A)** The distributor carries goods from manufacturers and wholesalers out to scattered retailers, especially across distances. The consumer is the final user, not a mover of goods. The producer makes the goods and typically does not distribute them to every retailer personally. And a certification mark is a quality label, not a link that transports goods. Distribution across distance is the distributor's role.

**42. (A)** The retailer breaks the farmer's 500 kg into 2 kg lots a household can buy - this bulk-breaking is exactly the mismatch's solution. The producer growing more does not solve the quantity mismatch. The exporter just moves potatoes abroad, not into small home-sized lots. And forcing the consumer to buy all 500 kg is the problem, not the fix. The retailer's bulk-splitting bridges the gap between large supply and small need.

**43. (B)** Without intermediaries, a buyer wanting one item would have to approach producers directly and likely buy in bulk - expensive and impractical for daily needs, which is why the chain exists. Goods would not become free; someone still has to make and move them. Removing the convenient links would make buying harder, not easier and cheaper. And producers cannot realistically hand-deliver single free items to every home - that is precisely the job intermediaries take on.

**44. (C)** Middlemen perform real services - moving, storing and breaking bulk so soap is available near you in a single bar - and the price reflects that value, not waste. Saying they 'do nothing' is the trap the question refutes. Middlemen are not the producers; they are separate links. And they do not sell below factory cost - they add to it because they add value. The convenience the buyer enjoys is what the extra price pays for.

**45. (C)** Cold-storing mangoes so they are available beyond the brief harvest is a clear value-adding service - the buyer gains access to mangoes out of season. A trader who neither moves nor stores the crate adds little. A shop charging more for nothing extra is not adding value. Hiding the price is unfair conduct, not value-adding. Genuine intermediation involves real services like storage that benefit buyers.

**46. (A)** When a farmer can handle selling directly, skipping some intermediaries trims the added margins, often lowering the price and raising the farmer's share. Short chains are not illegal - direct farm markets are common. Removing intermediaries does not make goods free; the farmer still charges a price. And chain length is unrelated to quality. The point is that intermediaries add value but also cost, so sometimes a shorter chain benefits both ends.

**47. (B)** The distributor's job is carrying goods across distance to scattered retailers; the retailer's job is selling small amounts to final buyers - the two roles are complementary links. Neither uses up or makes the goods (those are consumer and producer roles). The roles are not reversed - the distributor is not the one selling single items to homes. And printing MRP or setting taxes are not their defining functions. Distance-moving versus small-quantity selling is the right contrast.

**48. (D)** The app lets a small artisan reach a buyer in another state, showing markets connect distant parties and widen a small producer's reach. Markets are not limited to one village - this example spans states. Markets always involve a price - it is not removed. And far from stopping small producers, such markets help them sell to many more buyers. The distance-bridging, reach-widening benefit is the point.

**49. (A)** A supply chain of transporters, storers and distributors carries distant rice into the city in saleable amounts at a workable price - that is how distance is bridged affordably. The city buyer does not harvest the rice. Rice does not appear without transport and traders; moving it is real work. And the government does not grow all rice inside cities. The organised chain connecting distant producer and city consumer is the answer.

**50. (A)** Certification marks, clear label details and honest buyer reviews give real evidence of safety and quality, making them the soundest things to rely on. A flashy advertisement is persuasion, not proof. The lowest price says nothing about quality and may even hint at a problem. The seller's own promise is self-interested and unverified. Consumer awareness means checking independent signals like marks, labels and reviews.

**51. (B)** Fair trade seeks a reasonable, fair return to the producers - here, the artisans should get a fair price for their craft. Paying them even less is the opposite of fair. Fair trade does not abolish prices or make goods free. And it certainly does not ban artisans from selling - it protects their right to a fair share. The aim is fairness to the producer at the start of the chain.

**52. (B)** MRP is the maximum a consumer may be charged, taxes included, so charging above it is not allowed - high demand does not override the printed ceiling. There is no law requiring an above-MRP charge in summer. Charging extra is the opposite of a subsidy, which would lower the price. Consumer protection means the printed MRP caps what the shop may collect, regardless of season.

**53. (B)** The FSSAI mark indicates the food product meets recognised safety standards, making it the more trustworthy pick. A lower price does not make an uncertified snack 'better' - cheapness is not proof of safety. Certification marks do convey real safety information. And FSSAI is about food safety, not energy efficiency (that is the BEE Star label). The aware consumer trusts the recognised food-safety mark.

**54. (C)** Energy use is shown by the BEE Star label, and food safety by the FSSAI mark - that pairing is correct. FSSAI is for food, not energy, and AGMARK is for graded farm produce, not food safety in that slot. AGMARK does not signal energy use, nor ISI food safety. ISI is for industrial standards, not energy, and BEE Star is energy, not food. Matching each mark to its proper domain gives BEE Star then FSSAI.

**55. (A)** A street lamp is open to everyone and one user does not exhaust it for others - the hallmarks of a public good, usually provided by the government. It is not a private good sold to one buyer, since many share it freely. It is not a wholesale lot for resale, nor an export item. The shared, non-using-up nature marks it clearly as a public good.

**56. (A)** This fixes both errors: a rock-bottom price can drive the seller to stop selling, so cheapest is not always best; and a website with buyers, sellers and a price is genuinely a market. Repeating that the cheapest is best and websites are not markets keeps both mistakes. Claiming only physical shops are markets and price is irrelevant is doubly wrong. And a market certainly does need buyers, sellers and a price - those are its defining ingredients.

**57. (D)** A subsidy is government money used to lower a price or support producers, so a gas-cylinder subsidy makes cylinders cheaper for buyers. It does not raise the price - that is the opposite of its purpose. It is not a tax (a tax adds to the price; a subsidy reduces it). And it does not ban sales; it encourages affordable access. The intended effect is a lower, more affordable price.

**58. (D)** Standards and marks protect buyers by certifying safety and quality that an ordinary consumer cannot test alone - that is their purpose. They are not meant to maximise prices. They do not aim to halt private trade; markets continue, just more safely. And they are not designed to exclude poorer buyers. Consumer protection through verified quality is the government's goal here.

**59. (C)** Beyond buying and selling, a market square can bring diverse communities together to talk and share culture - markets can be social and cultural centres. They do not keep communities apart; they mix them. They are not just tax-collection points. And they do not forbid social contact - quite the reverse, they encourage it. The example highlights the market's role as a melting pot of people and culture.

**60. (C)** If buyers chase only the lowest price, prices can fall below sellers' costs; loss-making sellers eventually quit, so fewer goods remain - showing why the cheapest price is not 'best' for the market as a whole. Goods do not get endlessly cheaper without a downside; the downside is vanishing supply. Sellers will not sell at a loss forever - they stop. And the government does not compel sellers to trade below cost. The just-right price, not the rock-bottom one, keeps goods flowing.
