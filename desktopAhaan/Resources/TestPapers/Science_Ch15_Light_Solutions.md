## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** A torch beam appears as a straight line of light because light travels in a straight line (rectilinear propagation). Light does not bend around corners, and the straight beam is not evidence about colour or speed.

**2. (D)** A patch of light passing through a tiny hole and a sharp-edged shadow both arise because light moves in straight lines: rays that are blocked make the shadow, rays that pass straight through the hole make the bright patch. It is not reflection or refraction here.

**3. (C)** Moving the opaque ball closer to the small lamp (and farther from the screen) makes it block a wider cone of the diverging rays, so the shadow on the screen grows **larger**. (Moving it closer to the screen would shrink the shadow — the trap.)

**4. (A)** A shadow is simply the dark region where light is blocked; it shows only the **outline** of the object and has no colour of its own. It is not necessarily larger, and it is never coloured like the object.

**5. (D)** You see the lamp only when light reaches your eye, which (since light goes in straight lines) requires the three holes, your eye and the lamp to all lie on one straight line. Equal size, colour or a curved arrangement do not matter.

**6. (C)** A polished mirror reflects rays in one regular direction (regular reflection), giving a clear image. Rough white paper reflects light in many scattered directions — **diffuse (irregular) reflection** — so no image forms, though plenty of light still bounces off.

**7. (C)** A still water surface acts like a plane mirror (regular reflection) and forms a clear image. Wind makes ripples — an uneven surface — so the reflection becomes scattered, i.e. **diffuse (irregular) reflection**, and the image breaks up.

**8. (B)** The angle of incidence is measured from the **normal**, not from the surface. If the ray makes 30° with the mirror surface, it makes 90° − 30° = 60° with the normal. By the law of reflection the angle of reflection also equals **60°**. (30° is the trap of measuring from the surface.)

**9. (D)** The fundamental law of reflection is that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection — true for every reflecting surface, shiny or dull, and for light of any colour. Reflection is not limited to metals or to white light, and light is not destroyed.

**10. (B)** Printed paper does not glow on its own; we read it because room light strikes it and undergoes **diffuse reflection**, scattering from every point of the page into our eyes. Regular reflection would give a mirror image, not readable text.

**11. (C)** A plane mirror always forms a **virtual, erect** image that is the **same size** as the object and as far behind the mirror as the object is in front. It is never real, magnified or diminished.

**12. (A)** A plane-mirror image is virtual, so it cannot be **caught on a screen**. It is always erect, the same size, and laterally inverted — all of which a plane-mirror image genuinely is. So the one it can never be is "caught on a screen."

**13. (B)** The image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front, i.e. 1.5 m behind. The distance from you to your image is therefore 1.5 + 1.5 = **3 m**. (1.5 m forgets to add the image's distance behind.)

**14. (D)** A plane mirror always forms an image the same size as the object, regardless of how close you stand. Walking towards the mirror does not change the image's size — it **stays the same size as you**. (It may *appear* to loom, but its true size is unchanged.)

**15. (A)** The image is always as far behind as the object is in front. At 2 m the image is 2 m behind; at 5 m it is 5 m behind. So the image moves 5 − 2 = **3 m** farther back. (6 m is the trap of doubling the object's shift.)

**16. (C)** A plane mirror swaps **left and right** (lateral inversion); it does not turn the image upside down or swap near and far. Your right hand appears as the image's left hand.

**17. (A)** In a following driver's rear-view mirror the writing is laterally inverted; printing AMBULANCE already reversed means the mirror reverses it back, so the **driver ahead reads it correctly** and pulls over. It is not for looks, brightness or darkness.

**18. (D)** Lateral inversion swaps left and right. Letters that are **symmetric about a vertical line** (H, I, O, X) look identical after this swap, while R is not vertically symmetric, so it appears reversed. Brightness or colour is irrelevant.

**19. (C)** A clock seen in a mirror reads "12-hour minus the real time." For 3:00 the mirror image reads 12:00 − 3:00 = **9:00**. (The hands at the 3 position appear at the 9 position.)

**20. (B)** Lateral inversion reverses the left-right order and mirror-flips each digit. The digits 1 and 8 are each symmetric, and reversing the order of 1-8-8-1 gives 1-8-8-1 again, so the reflection still reads **1881** (it is a mirror palindrome here).

**21. (B)** The image lies 4 cm behind the mirror, so object-to-image distance = 4 + 4 = **8 cm**. (4 cm ignores the image distance; 16 cm wrongly squares or doubles twice.)

**22. (C)** A plane mirror forms a **virtual** image, which is only an apparent point where rays seem to come from — no light actually meets there, so it **cannot be caught on any screen**, whether in front of or behind the mirror.

**23. (A)** In a plane mirror the image is always as far behind as the object is in front, so the image moves exactly in step with the object. If the man approaches at 1 m/s, his image approaches the mirror at **1 m/s** too. (2 m/s is the rate at which he and his image close on each other, not the image's speed toward the mirror.)

**24. (D)** A surface that curves **inwards** (the hollow side, like the bowl of a spoon) is a **concave mirror**. The outward-bulging side would be convex.

**25. (B)** A concave mirror is called converging because incoming parallel rays are reflected so that they **meet (converge) at a point** (the focus). Spreading rays apart describes a convex mirror; passing light through describes a lens.

**26. (C)** Only a **concave mirror** can form a real, inverted image (of a distant object, the rays actually meet in front of the mirror). A plane mirror and a convex mirror only ever form virtual, erect images.

**27. (D)** When the object is **very close** to a concave mirror (nearer than its focus), the image is **virtual, erect and magnified** — which is exactly why it is used as a shaving/make-up mirror. Far objects would give a real inverted image, but "very close" gives the enlarged erect one.

**28. (A)** A dentist holds a concave mirror close to a tooth so it acts like a magnifier, giving an **enlarged, erect image of the tooth** for a better look. A wider view is what a convex mirror gives, not a concave one.

**29. (D)** A bulb placed at the focus of a **concave** (converging) reflector sends out a strong, nearly parallel beam — ideal for a torch or headlight. A plane or convex mirror would scatter the light instead of focusing it into a beam.

**30. (A)** "Always bigger" is **wrong**: a concave mirror magnifies only when the object is close (nearer than the focus). For a **distant object** it gives a small, **inverted** image. So the image depends on object distance.

**31. (C)** A surface that bulges **outwards** towards you (the back of a spoon) is a **convex mirror**. The inward, hollow side is concave.

**32. (B)** Whatever the object's position, a convex mirror always forms a **virtual, erect and diminished** image. It can never form a real or magnified image — a key trap.

**33. (A)** A convex mirror always makes a diminished image, so it packs a **wider field of view** into the glass — letting a driver see more of the road behind. It does not magnify or show true distance; objects look smaller and farther than they really are.

**34. (C)** "Magnifies thieves" is **wrong**: a convex (security) mirror always gives a **diminished** image. Its value is the wide field of view, letting one mirror cover a large area, not enlargement.

**35. (D)** A convex mirror always forms a **diminished** image, so a nearby car looks small and far. (This is why such mirrors carry the warning "objects are closer than they appear.")

**36. (B)** A plane mirror and a convex mirror both form **virtual, erect** images, and neither magnifies. A concave mirror used for a distant object forms a real, inverted image instead. So the shared property is "they form a virtual, erect image."

**37. (D)** A solar concentrator uses a large **concave** mirror to converge (focus) sunlight onto one spot to produce heat. A convex mirror or a lens would not focus the light to a hot point the way a concave reflector does.

**38. (B)** A blind-junction safety mirror must show a **wide stretch** of road, so a **convex** mirror is used — it gives a wide field of view. A concave mirror would magnify but show only a narrow region.

**39. (B)** (i) wide view = convex; (ii) torch reflector = concave; (iii) same-size image = plane; (iv) can form a real image = concave. The two **concave** ones are **(ii) and (iv)**.

**40. (A)** Wait — re-reading: a single mirror that lets a camera watch the **whole** shop floor needs the widest possible field of view, so a **convex mirror** is the right choice. A concave or plane mirror would not capture the whole floor in one view.

**41. (A)** A lens that is **thicker in the middle** than at its edges is a **convex lens** (a converging lens). The concave lens is the one thinner in the middle.

**42. (D)** A magnifying glass is a **convex lens**: held close to small print it gives an enlarged, erect (virtual) image. A concave lens would shrink the print; mirrors are not used as hand magnifiers here.

**43. (B)** A convex lens is called converging because rays passing through it are **brought together towards a point** (the focus). Spreading rays apart is what a concave lens does.

**44. (C)** A **real** image can be caught on a screen. Of the four, only the **image of a distant tree formed by a convex lens** is real (the rays actually meet and can fall on a screen). Plane-mirror, convex-mirror and close-up magnifier images are all virtual.

**45. (C)** Focusing sunlight to a tiny hot spot shows the convex lens **converges light to a point** (its focus). A concave lens would spread the light out instead, and the effect works with ordinary white sunlight.

**46. (D)** A lens that is **thinner in the middle** than at its edges is a **concave lens** (a diverging lens). The convex lens is thicker in the middle.

**47. (B)** Whatever the object's position, a concave (diverging) lens always forms a **virtual, erect and diminished** image. It can never form a real or magnified image — the lens analogue of the convex-mirror rule.

**48. (A)** A **concave lens** is the diverging element: it spreads parallel rays apart. A convex lens and a concave mirror both converge light, and a magnifying glass is a convex lens.

**49. (C)** A concave lens always gives a virtual, erect, **diminished** image, so the print looks **smaller and upright**. (A convex lens would make it larger — that is the trap.)

**50. (B)** White sunlight is actually a mixture of **seven colours** (the VIBGYOR spectrum). It is not a single pure colour or merely three colours.

**51. (A)** VIBGYOR names the colours in order: **violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red**. The other options either end in "brown," start with "white," or are deliberately garbled.

**52. (B)** The splitting of white light into its component colours by a prism is called **dispersion**. Reflection is bouncing back; lateral inversion is the left-right swap in a mirror; absorption is light being taken up.

**53. (B)** A rainbow forms when sunlight enters countless **tiny water droplets**, each acting like a small prism that disperses the light into its colours. Clouds, the ground, or a sky-mirror do not produce a rainbow.

**54. (C)** A prism splits white light into seven colours; conversely, a fast-spinning seven-colour (Newton's) disc recombines them, so the eye blends the colours and the disc looks **whitish / white**. This is the reverse of dispersion.

**55. (D)** A prism separating white light into seven colours, plus a spinning seven-colour disc looking white, together prove that **white light is made of the seven colours combined**. The prism reveals the colours; it does not create them.

**56. (C)** A prism's two refracting faces are **slanted (not parallel)**, so the colours bent by the first face keep spreading and stay separated. A flat sheet of window glass has parallel faces, so any spreading is undone and no colour band appears.

**57. (C)** A single pure colour like **red** cannot be split further, so a prism only **bends a band of red light slightly without splitting it** into other colours. Only white (mixed) light gives the full spectrum.

**58. (A)** In dispersion, violet (shortest path-bending the most) is bent most and **red** is bent the least. So the colour bending the least is at the **red end** of the spectrum.

**59. (D)** (i) a still pond imaging a tree = **reflection**; (ii) a prism making a colour band = **dispersion**; (iii) a magnifying glass enlarging print = the action of a **convex lens**. In order: reflection, dispersion, action of a convex lens.

**60. (A)** Correct pairing: a plane mirror gives a **virtual, same-size** image; a convex mirror gives a **virtual, diminished** image; a concave lens gives a **virtual, diminished** image. The other options wrongly claim real/magnified/inverted images for these devices.
