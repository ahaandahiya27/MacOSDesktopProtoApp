Worked Solutions

1. (C) Climate is the average condition over a long span, typically decades. The whole point of averaging across many years is that no single year, however unusual, defines the climate; the odd years are data points that get smoothed into the long-run pattern, so they should be kept in the average. Discarding them as 'errors' is wrong, because a genuinely recorded extreme year is real weather data, not a mistake. Splitting the record into two separate climates confuses individual years with climate, which is a single long-term summary. Picking one pleasant year as 'the climate' abandons averaging entirely and substitutes preference for evidence.

2. (C) Climate is the long-term average, so the record that spans decades (40 years of totals) is exactly the kind of evidence that can establish a climate label like 'arid'. The week-long record covers only current weather, so freshness does not make it a climate record. The claim that rainfall data 'of any length' shows climate ignores the time-scale requirement; a single week cannot. The claim that rainfall never reveals climate is false, since long-run rainfall averages are a core part of how climate is defined.

3. (D) Weather is the condition at a moment or over a short span; climate is the long-term average. 'This morning... sunny' is a momentary condition, so it is weather, and 'hot and humid' as a year-round descriptor is the climate, so the student has attached each label to the wrong idea. The words are not interchangeable; their defining difference is the time-scale. Whether Chennai is in fact hot and humid is beside the point, since the structural mistake is the swapped labels. Every place has both weather and climate, so coastal location does not remove either.

4. (D) Two places can share identical annual averages yet differ in climate because climate includes not just totals but how temperature and rainfall are distributed across the seasons; one may spread its 900 mm evenly while the other crams it into a wet season and bakes the rest of the year. So equal averages do not force identical climates. Distance from the Sun barely varies between two cities at the same latitude and is never the controlling factor. Thirty years is a perfectly adequate climate record; the issue is distribution within the year, not the length of the record.

5. (C) A day-to-day forecast is weather; a comparison of one decade with another is climate, and the one thing that distinguishes them is the time-scale, not the quantity measured. Temperature appears in both kinds of statement, so mentioning it does not make a statement weather. Comparing two periods does not by itself make a statement climate, since a forecaster also compares today with tomorrow. Predicting the future is not the same as describing climate, so calling the forecast 'climate' inverts the definitions.

6. (D) Climate is built from long-term averages and typical patterns, so evidence about climate must span many years. A single afternoon storm is one short event, which is weather, not climate. The fifty-year average July temperature, the decades-long month-by-month rainfall pattern, and the usual hot-to-cold spread observed over many years are all multi-year summaries, and each is exactly the kind of evidence that does describe climate.

7. (D) A near-vertical beam at the Equator lands on a small patch, so the energy per square metre is high; the same beam striking at a slant near 60 degrees is smeared across a larger patch, so each square metre gets less. The difference is the angle, not distance: the few-thousand-kilometre gap between the patches is negligible against the Sun's 150-million-kilometre distance. The Sun does not aim stronger radiation at the Equator; it radiates in all directions. The poles do not reflect every ray, and even bare polar ground would still receive less because of the slant.

8. (A) Because the Earth's tilt swings the Sun's overhead point only within the tropics, an equatorial place keeps the Sun high all year, so the angle and intensity of heating hardly change between months; that steadiness gives Quito a tiny seasonal range. Altitude does not produce steadiness, and Quito in fact sits high in the Andes, so the low-altitude claim is factually wrong as well as irrelevant. Distance from the Sun barely differs between places on Earth, so it cannot explain the contrast. The Equator certainly has an atmosphere, so that option is simply false.

9. (D) If closeness to the Sun caused summer, both hemispheres would be hot together, but India's summer is the southern hemisphere's winter; the two hemispheres have opposite seasons because each is tilted toward or away from the Sun in turn, which changes the ray-angle. So seasons come from tilt, not orbital distance. Endorsing the pupil is therefore wrong. The orbit is slightly elliptical rather than a perfect circle, but the key flaw is the simultaneity argument, not the orbit's shape. The monsoon reversal is itself a consequence of seasonal heating, not the cause of the seasons.

10. (C) The lower-latitude place (P, 10 degrees N) keeps the Sun high all year, so it is warmer with a small seasonal swing; the higher-latitude place (Q, 50 degrees N) gets a low slanting winter Sun and a higher summer Sun, so it is cooler overall with a wider swing. Higher latitudes do not gain net warmth from long summer days, because those are paid back by long dark winters and weak slanting light. Matching coast and altitude controls those factors but not latitude, so the two are not identical. Equatorial places have the smallest, not the largest, seasonal swing.

11. (A) The Earth's axis keeps a fixed direction in space, so the tropics are not 'permanently tilted toward the Sun'; over the year each hemisphere tilts toward and then away. What keeps the tropics warm is that the Sun stays high overhead there every month, so rays land steeply in all seasons, which is Student 2's reasoning. Student 1 misdescribes the geometry. The two statements are not equivalent, since one names a false cause and the other the true cause. Nearness to the sea moderates ranges but does not explain why inland tropical places are also warm year round.

12. (D) Two effects weaken a slanting polar ray: the same bundle of light is smeared over a wider patch of ground, lowering energy per square metre, and a low-angle ray cuts through a longer slice of atmosphere, so more of its energy is scattered and absorbed before reaching the surface. Light does not change speed in a way that 'loses heat', and a slanting ray still reaches the ground. There is no inherently 'colder light' from the same Sun, and the ground does absorb sunlight. The Sun is not dimmer over the poles; it is the geometry and atmospheric path that matter.

13. (A) Air is warmed mainly from the ground up, and it thins with height; both effects make a high place cooler, so Town X at 2,000 m is colder than Town Y. The 'nearer the Sun' idea fails because 2,000 m is nothing against 150 million kilometres. Shared latitude controls one factor but not altitude, so the two towns are not equal. Climbing a mountain does not move a place toward the poles, so that reasoning is incoherent even though it reaches the right direction by accident.

14. (A) The base and summit share essentially the same latitude and the same horizontal distance from the sea, so neither can be the cause; what changes is height, and temperature falls steadily as you climb, letting jungle and snow coexist on one mountain. Latitude does not change meaningfully over a few horizontal kilometres. The summit's distance to the sea barely differs from the base's. The 'nearest the Sun is coldest' option is self-contradictory, since being nearer the Sun could only warm a place; the summit is cold because of altitude.

15. (B) Applying 6.5 degrees per 1,000 m to 1,800 m gives a fall of about 11.7 degrees, so 32 minus roughly 12 leaves about 20 degrees, and the station is cooler because air is heated from the ground and thins with height. The 'warmer because nearer the Sun' option gets both the direction wrong and uses a negligible distance change. Altitude clearly affects temperature, so 'unchanged' is false. Climbing does not move a place toward the poles, and the correct lapse gives about 20, not 12, so that option errs in both cause and value.

16. (B) Water heats and cools slowly, so a coast (J) is moderated to a narrow range, while an interior (K) far from that buffer lets land heat fiercely in summer and shed heat fast in winter, widening the range at both ends; that is continentality. Latitude is stated equal, so it cannot be the hidden cause. Altitude is also stated equal, and interiors are not always higher than coasts. The sea moderates J by changing slowly, not by 'pumping' heat in and cold in opposite seasons, so that mechanism is misstated.

17. (D) Land responds quickly to gains and losses of heat, so an interior with no nearby sea to steady it warms intensely in summer and cools intensely in winter, driving both the high and the low further apart. The interior does not move toward or away from the Sun seasonally. A distant sea cannot reach inland to heat then chill a place; its moderating effect is felt only near the coast. A place's latitude is fixed and does not change between seasons.

18. (B) With latitude and altitude matched, a remaining climate factor is how well the moderating sea air actually reaches each place; a city open to onshore winds is steadied more than one shielded by terrain, so this could explain a small difference in range. Closeness to the Sun does not differ meaningfully between neighbouring coastal cities. Population is not the chapter's factor for the broad annual range. The Moon governs tides, not the seasonal temperature range, so it is irrelevant.

19. (C) The sea warms slowly, so Mumbai's coast is held cooler in summer, while inland Nagpur, lacking that buffer, heats rapidly and swelters; the pairing of cause to city is correct here. The sea moderates rather than reflecting extra heat onto the coast, so the 'fiercer Mumbai' option misstates the mechanism. Shared latitude controls only the latitude factor, leaving continentality free to differ, so the summers are not identical. Being inland does not change a city's latitude, so Nagpur is not 'nearer the Equator'.

20. (B) The sea changes temperature slowly, so a place near it is steadied and shows a small annual range; that nearness is the best explanation for a narrow gap. A dry continental interior is the opposite case, producing a large range. A high exposed plateau far from the coast also tends to swing widely. A high-latitude place has strongly changing sun-angles between summer and winter, which widens rather than narrows the range.

21. (A) The loo is hot and dry because it has crossed hot dry land, and the winter cold waves are cold because their air came over cold snowy high ground; both show that a wind delivers the character of the surface it has travelled across. Winds do not move a region to a new latitude, do not change the altitude of the ground beneath them, and cannot transport a place toward or away from the sea; the region stays put while the air, carrying its acquired qualities, moves over it.

22. (A) Air takes on the qualities of the surface it crosses, so a wind off a warm sea gains moisture while a wind off a desert stays dry; thus Wind A is the moister. Seas add moisture to passing air rather than stripping it, so 'drier' is backwards. A warm sea does not make a wind icy, and the reliable contrast asked for is moisture, not a guaranteed cold. The surface most certainly leaves its mark, which is the whole point of this chapter's wind discussion.

23. (D) One wind crossed cold snowy highlands and arrives chilly and dry; the other crossed a warm sea and arrives mild and damp; the contrast is set by the surfaces each travelled over. Latitude is not the active cause here, since the lesson is about what the winds picked up en route. 'Heavier and therefore colder' confuses a vague notion of weight with the real cause, the source region. The two are different air masses shaped by different paths, not one fickle wind 'changing its mind'.

24. (D) Flat openness lets a wind sweep across the plain unhindered, while a high ridge blocks or deflects it from the valley; whether a place is exposed or sheltered is set by the lie of the land, which is relief. Latitude is stated the same, so it cannot be the difference. The shelter comes from the ridge, not from differing nearness to the ocean. The Sun's position is identical for both at the same latitude, so it cannot explain why one is sheltered and the other exposed.

25. (C) Because a wind takes on the character of its path, the loo could only become cool and moist if it had crossed a cool sea that handed it moisture and lowered its heat. Crossing a bigger, hotter desert would make it even hotter and drier. Crossing hot summer plains would add heat, again the opposite. A surface wind cannot travel through empty space, and even if it could it would gain no moisture; the only path that produces cool and moist is over a cool sea.

26. (C) As air climbs it moves into lower pressure, expands, and cools; cooler air can hold less water vapour, so the surplus condenses into cloud and rain on the windward slope. Rising air expands and cools rather than being compressed and warmed, so the second option reverses the physics. Rising air does not 'dry out instantly without cooling'; the cooling is exactly what causes condensation. Air that has crossed the crest descends the far side, so it does not loop back to rain twice on the windward slope.

27. (B) The correct chain is: moist air is forced up the western slopes, drops its rain there, then spills over the crest as descending, drying air, so the Deccan sits in a rain shadow. The Deccan is not 'nearer the Equator', and heat does not 'repel' clouds. The coastal plain is lower than the Ghats, not higher. Rivers do not manufacture rain; the dryness comes from the descending leeward air, not from a lack of rivers.

28. (A) Ladakh sits in the rain shadow of the Himalayas, which strip the winds of moisture, making it dry, and its great altitude makes it bitterly cold, so both halves of 'cold desert' are explained. There is no nearby sea pulling moisture away, and Ladakh is far from the North Pole. It is genuinely cold, not merely nicknamed so, unlike the hot Thar. Low latitude would tend to warm, not cool, and rivers evaporating does not cause the dryness; the rain shadow does.

29. (C) The winds reaching these hills are heavy with Bay of Bengal moisture, and the funnel-shaped terrain forces them sharply upward, cooling them so the vapour condenses into extraordinary rain. Dry Thar winds carry little moisture and cannot deliver record rain. Descending leeward air warms and dries, which suppresses rather than causes rain. The rain is a summer monsoon phenomenon, not cold winter Himalayan winds dropping snow.

30. (B) Each contrast pairs a place where moist air is forced up (wet) with a sheltered or lower place behind it (drier); relief lifting the monsoon winds explains the soaked windward slopes and the dry rain-shadow areas alike. Latitude cannot explain why two nearby places at the same latitude differ so sharply. Altitude alone fails, since the dry Deccan is not always lower than the wet coast. Distance from the sea alone fails, since the inland Deccan is drier, not wetter, than the coast.

31. (D) The Ghats cause the coast's heavy rain by forcing moist air up; remove them and the air is no longer lifted, so coastal rain falls and some moisture drifts inland to the formerly shadowed Deccan. The coast would rain less, not more, because the lifting that causes its rain is gone. Removing a mountain range does not freeze a tropical peninsula. Rainfall would clearly change, since the very mechanism creating the present pattern would be removed.

32. (A) Moist sea air rises on the windward slope, cools and rains, then crosses the crest and sinks dry and warming into the leeward valley, producing a wet side and a parched side within a few kilometres; this is the rain shadow. Latitude does not jump meaningfully over a few kilometres. The far valley is not closer to the Equator; the two sides share essentially the same latitude. Soil does not control rainfall; the difference is set by which side of the ridge the wind has crossed.

33. (A) The monsoon is driven by the difference in how fast land and sea heat and cool, which creates the seasonal high- and low-pressure zones the winds flow between, so removing it would kill the monsoon. The Moon's gravity governs tides, not the seasonal wind reversal. Rivers are far too small to generate a continent-sized pressure system. A fixed equatorial temperature is neither true nor the engine of the monsoon, which runs on the land-sea contrast.

34. (C) Intense summer heating makes the air rise over the land, creating low pressure, and air flows from the higher-pressure sea into that low, arriving from the south-west laden with moisture. Hot rising air gives low pressure, not high, so the two 'high pressure' options misname the system. Winds flow from high to low pressure, so they blow inland into the low, not outward to sea, which rules out the 'dry winds blow outward' option.

35. (B) The south-west monsoon picks up moisture over warm seas before reaching land, so it rains heavily, whereas the winter north-east monsoon starts over dry land and heads seaward, so it is mostly dry. The first wrong option reverses the geography, since the south-west winds come off the sea, not the Thar. Cold winds do not 'always carry more rain'; moisture, not coldness, brings rain. Monsoon winds blow horizontally from sea to land, not vertically down from the upper atmosphere.

36. (A) Although the winter monsoon generally blows off dry land, on its way to the Tamil Nadu coast it crosses the Bay of Bengal, gaining the moisture it then drops as rain. The Thar is a dry desert and supplies no rain-making moisture. Winds blowing down the Himalayas arrive cold and dry. The Arabian Sea is on the west; the moisture for this south-eastern coastal rain comes from the Bay of Bengal to the east.

37. (B) In summer the hot land is low pressure and draws in sea winds; in winter the cold land becomes high pressure and pushes winds out to sea; this seasonal flip of the land-sea pressure pattern reverses the winds. The Earth's axial tilt keeps a fixed direction in space and does not flip twice a year. The Western Ghats are stationary and cannot swing with the seasons. The Moon's orbit does not switch direction and does not drive the monsoon.

38. (D) The year runs from the dry winter into the building hot dry summer, then the rains burst as the south-west monsoon, and finally the monsoon retreats before winter returns; this is the correct order. The rains do not precede the summer heat, so 'rainy then hot' is out of sequence. The retreating phase comes after, not before, the rains, ruling out the option that places the retreat first and the option that places the retreat before the rains.

39. (B) A daytime sea breeze arises because sun-warmed land forms low pressure and draws cooler sea air ashore; the summer monsoon does the same on a continental scale, which is why the analogy works. The Moon drives tides, not the pressure-driven inflow of sea air. Rivers are far too small to set up a country-sized wind system. Snowmelt does not power the monsoon, which is driven by the land-sea heating contrast just like a sea breeze.

40. (A) The south-west monsoon arrives first at the southern tip in early June and then spreads northward, blanketing the country by about mid-July. It does not begin in the north and creep south to reach Kerala last. It moves inland from the seas over the land, not from the coast out to sea. 41. (C) After a long, hot, dry build-up, the moisture-laden south-west winds sweep in and pour down heavy rain, and the sharp contrast with the preceding dryness makes the onset feel like a sudden 'burst'. The Sun does not switch off. Rivers do not lift their water into the sky to make rain; rain comes from the moist incoming winds. 42. (B) The retreating monsoon is the transition when the south-west monsoon loses strength and pulls back, linking the rainy season to the coming dry winter. 43. (C) The monsoon is so powerful in shaping farming and daily life in India that the rains are recognised as a season in their own right, giving the idea of a 'fifth season'. Festivals follow the seasons rather than each demanding a separate one, so they do not create the fifth season.

44. (C) The six ritus split the year into finer slices that match India's monsoon-shaped rhythm, capturing transitions the four-season model blurs. They are an ancient classification, not a recent tourist invention. Far from ignoring the monsoon, they are organised around the rains and the seasons it produces. They are not the four European seasons renamed; there are six, attuned to local conditions.

45. (A) These harvest and seasonal festivals fall when the climate-driven farming cycle reaches key moments, showing that climate shapes agriculture and so the rhythm of cultural life. Festivals are responses to the seasons; they do not command the rains to arrive. Climate clearly does influence culture here, so 'no effect' is contradicted by the very examples given. The festivals follow the farming and monsoon calendar, not latitude alone.

46. (B) Because the tilted Earth orbits the Sun, each part of the year brings the same angle of sunlight as the year before, so the seasons recur annually. River floods follow the seasons; they do not cause them.

47. (B) Srinagar is cold because two factors reinforce each other: a high latitude gives it a low, slanting Sun, and its high mountain altitude lowers temperatures further. 55. (B) The fiercest winds spin in the eyewall, the ring of cloud wrapped around the calm centre, so that is where danger is greatest. The eye itself is calm, not strong.
