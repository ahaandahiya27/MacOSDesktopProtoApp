## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** The globe is sealed and rigid, so the quantity of air inside is fixed and its total weight cannot change — the balance reads the same. The popular phrase 'warm air is lighter' refers to density: heating makes the air expand, so the same amount of air occupies more space and each cubic-metre weighs less, which is why a warmed parcel floats up through cooler surroundings. It does not mean heat erases weight (the first wrong choice), nor that heat is a kind of mass that adds to the reading (the second), nor that air trapped inside a closed globe can push its own container's weight off a balance (the third). The lesson is that 'lighter' here means lower density, not less total weight.

**2. (B)** When you fill a fixed volume with cold air, the air is denser, so more air fits into that volume than when you fill it warm. Balloon P was filled cold, so it holds more air than Q which was filled warm to the same size. After both reach room temperature, P — having more air sealed in — is the more tightly filled and heavier one. The choice claiming Q holds more confuses expansion with quantity (warm air takes more space, so you need less of it to fill a given size, not more). 'Same size means same amount' ignores that density depends on temperature at filling. And cold air does not leave empty gaps; it simply packs more densely. So denser cold filling means more air, making P the answer.

**3. (C)** Cooling plain air only makes it contract a little, so its pressure drops only slightly and the outside air barely wins — a small dent. But steam, on cooling, condenses into a minute volume of liquid water, removing almost all the gas inside; the inside pressure plummets, and the much higher outside air pressure crushes the can. The crush is entirely about the inside-versus-outside pressure imbalance. The student's 'steam is heavier' idea is the flaw: weight of the gas inside is irrelevant; condensation, not weight, empties the can. Boiling does not weaken the metal (the can crushes because of the pressure gap, not soft walls), and ordinary air can certainly be cooled by ice water — it just doesn't vanish the way condensing steam does.

**4. (B)** Wind speed depends on the pressure gradient — how big a drop falls over how short a distance — not on which station is highest or on the total end-to-end difference. Taking a full-width gap as one unit: first-to-second drops 6 over a full gap (6 per unit); second-to-third drops 4 over a full gap (4 per unit); third-to-fourth drops 6 over a half-width gap, which is 12 per unit — by far the steepest. So the fastest wind is between the third and fourth stations. The 'highest pressure makes fastest wind' choice confuses pressure value with gradient. 'Closest to the centre' has nothing to do with speed. And the end-to-end first-to-fourth difference, though largest in total, is spread over the longest distance, giving a gentle gradient. The steep drop over the narrow third-to-fourth gap wins.

**5. (A)** Land heats faster than water, so by noon the air over the field is warmer. That warm air expands, drops in density, and rises; as it leaves, less air presses on the field's surface, so the surface pressure there falls. Wind blows from higher pressure (over the cooler pond) toward lower pressure (over the field), so the surface breeze moves from pond to field. The choice that says water heats faster reverses the well-known land-versus-water rule. The 'warm air is heavier and sinks' choice is the classic density error — warm air is less dense and rises. And the two surfaces do not heat equally; land's faster heating is exactly what sets up the pressure difference that drives the breeze.

**6. (C)** Where the card narrows the gap, the air is forced to move faster, and faster-moving air has lower pressure. With lower pressure on the card side and ordinary higher still-air pressure on the open side, the net push moves the ball toward the card — the same Bernoulli effect behind balls being drawn together by a stream. The choice that says the card raises pressure on its side reverses the rule (faster air means lower, not higher, pressure). Air does not 'stick and drag' the ball toward the nearest object — that is a vague non-mechanism. And a card beside the ball adds no weight to the ball. The pressure difference set up by the faster channelled air is what moves it.

**7. (B)** The card is held up because the outside air pressure pushing up on it exceeds the weight of the water pushing down. On a mountain peak there is less air above, so the outside air pressure is lower and pushes up less. The water's weight is the same as before, so the margin holding the card shrinks and the card is more likely to fall. 'Thin air clings tighter' invents a non-existent stickiness. 'Pressure is the same everywhere' is simply false — it falls with altitude. 'Water cannot fall upside down' ignores that only the air pressure holds it; remove enough of that push and it falls. So lower peak pressure makes the card more likely to drop.

**8. (A)** A cyclone feeds on warm moist air rising from a warm sea; condensation of that vapour releases heat that deepens the central low and pulls in faster wind. The half over land loses that supply of warm moist air, so part of the engine is starved and the storm weakens overall — this is why cyclones fade after landfall. Land does not act as a surface to 'spin against' for extra speed. A cyclone is not self-sufficient; cut off its moisture and it dies, so it certainly still needs the sea. And partial overlap with land does not cleanly split it into two equal storms. Starving half the moisture supply means net weakening.

**9. (B)** Reading the sizes: full cyclone 500 km, eye 30 km, thunderstorm cell 10 km, tornado 0.3 km. From largest to smallest that is cyclone (500), eye (30), cell (10), tornado (0.3). The choice that puts the tornado first commits the classic error of ranking the narrow, violent tornado as the biggest — it is in fact the smallest. The third option swaps the eye and the cell (30 km vs 10 km, so the eye is bigger). The last option puts the eye ahead of the whole cyclone, which is impossible since the eye is just the small core of the cyclone. Careful reading of the figures gives cyclone, eye, cell, tornado.

**10. (C)** Light reaches us almost instantly, so the delay before the thunder is essentially the time the sound takes to travel from the storm to the listener. A steady gap means the distance held constant; a sudden drop to a shorter gap means the sound now has less distance to cover — the storm has come closer. The 'moved away' choice reverses the relationship (farther storms give longer, not shorter, gaps). Brightness of the lightning does not change the travel time of the sound. And the speed of sound does not jump in the middle of a storm; the change in the gap reflects the changing distance, so the storm moved closer.

**11. (B)** The trapped air is a fixed amount throughout. Warming makes it expand, so some pushes out of the tube as bubbles; cooling makes it contract, lowering its pressure so the higher outside pressure drives water up the tube to take the freed space. No air is created or destroyed — the student's conclusion is the misconception the demo is designed to defeat. The water does not boil at hand-warming temperatures (and the bubbles are escaping air, not steam). The glass flask's tiny expansion is negligible and is not what moves the water; it is the air's expansion and contraction that does. So the same air simply expands and contracts.

**12. (B)** Wind speed tracks the pressure gradient: pressure difference divided by distance. The first region drops 20 units over 400 km, about 0.05 units per km. The second drops 12 units over 200 km, about 0.06 units per km — steeper. So the second region, despite a smaller total difference, has the stronger gradient and the faster wind. The choice favouring the first region looks only at the total difference and ignores the distance over which it falls. Wind does not depend on the low value alone; it depends on the difference and the spacing. And distances of a few hundred km are entirely normal for wind to blow across, so 'no wind' is wrong.

**13. (D)** We live at the bottom of an ocean of air, and pressure rises as you go deeper into that ocean because more air lies above you — exactly the diver's experience that deeper water presses harder. Descending into a deep mine puts even more air column above you, so the air pressure there is higher than at the surface, not lower. The student stated the conclusion backwards. Air pressure certainly does change with position in the air column (it falls on mountains, rises in deep places). And the analogy does hold: both fluids press harder the deeper you go, so they follow the same rule, not opposite ones.

**14. (C)** The candle warmed the left bag's air, expanding it to a less dense, lighter state, so it rose. The ice does the reverse: it cools the air in the left bag, which contracts to a denser, heavier state than its surroundings, so that end is pulled down below level. The first wrong choice repeats the density error — cold air is denser and heavier, not lighter. Temperature certainly affects the balance, as the candle already showed, so 'returns to level and stays' is wrong. And ice does not emit a buoyant 'cold vapour' that lifts things. Cooling makes the air denser and the bag heavier, so it sinks.

**15. (A)** Water forced through the narrow gap between the hulls speeds up, and faster-moving fluid has lower pressure. The higher pressure of the slower water on the ships' outer sides then pushes them inward toward each other — the same principle as the two balls drawn together by a stream of air. The student's flaw is exactly the Bernoulli reversal: fast fluid is at lower, not higher, pressure, so it does not push outward. The ships do not remove 'all' the water to make a vacuum; water still flows, just faster and at lower pressure. There is no magnetism involved. And the drawing-together is a sideways pressure effect, not a backward wake pull. Lower pressure in the fast gap is the cause.

**16. (C)** The sea breeze blows because the strongly heated land develops lower pressure than the cooler sea, drawing sea air inland. During the eclipse the land stops being heated, so it stops out-heating the sea; the pressure difference shrinks and the breeze weakens. It does not reverse within seconds — the land cannot drop below the sea's temperature in a few minutes; the land merely stops getting hotter, it does not instantly become colder than the warm sea. Darkness itself does not increase wind. And the breeze is not self-sustaining; it depends moment-to-moment on the temperature difference, so removing the heating weakens it.

**17. (C)** The monsoon's sea-to-land summer wind depends on the land being hotter and therefore lower in pressure than the sea, drawing sea air in. If instead the sea became hotter than the land, the lower pressure would form over the sea; surface air would tend to flow from the cooler higher-pressure land toward the warmer lower-pressure sea — a reversal — and the moist sea-to-land rains would fail. So the wind doesn't merely stop; the driving difference flips, so the tendency is to reverse. A hotter sea pushing 'more moisture onto land' ignores that the wind would now blow the other way. And the monsoon is driven by the land-sea temperature difference, not fixed by the calendar regardless of temperature.

**18. (C)** A thunderstorm needs warm air that rises rapidly and plenty of moisture to condense into towering clouds. Swapping in cold, dry air defeats two requirements together: cold air does not rise quickly, and dry air supplies little vapour to condense — so the storm is starved most reliably. Adding more vapour or making the air warmer would only feed the storm more, not prevent it. Clearing more height overhead also helps the storm grow, not stop. Removing the warm moist rising air is the one change that disables the storm's engine and fuel at once.

**19. (B)** The deeper the central low, the larger the pressure difference between the calm centre and the surroundings, and the faster the surrounding air rushes inward as wind. X at 950 and still falling has a deeper, deepening low than Y steady at 985, so X pulls in faster eye-wall winds and is the greater threat. The Y choice reverses the physics — a higher central pressure means a weaker inward pull, and cyclones draw air inward, not throw it outward. Two cyclones over the same sea need not be identical; their central pressures differ. And central pressure is tightly linked to wind speed, so 'neither is dangerous' is wrong. X's deeper low wins.

**20. (C)** Air has weight; a balloon full of air contains more air than a deflated one, so on a sensitive balance the inflated balloon reads slightly heavier. This same weight of the air column above us is what produces atmospheric pressure. The 'weightless' conclusion is the misconception the experiment refutes. The 'weighs less because buoyant' choice confuses density-driven floating with total weight — the air inside still has mass and adds to the reading. And the extra weight is the air itself, not merely the rubber, which is the same balloon in both states. So the inflated balloon is heavier because air has weight.

**21. (C)** Fast wind across the top lowers the pressure above the roof, while the still air inside the house remains at higher pressure, so the roof is pushed up — the lifting danger. Small vents let the trapped inside air bleed toward the lowered outside pressure, shrinking the inside-outside pressure difference and so reducing the upward lift. The 'blow it up like a sail' choice imagines the wind getting under and pushing up, but the small vents equalise pressure rather than catch wind. Wind over a roof lowers pressure and lifts it (it does not press it down). And vents do not add weight. Bleeding the pressure difference is what helps.

**22. (A)** Fast air across the chimney's open top lowers the pressure there. With lower pressure above and ordinary pressure below, the upward draught through the chimney strengthens — warm air is drawn out faster and fresh air is pulled in at the base, feeding the flame, so it burns brighter. The 'raises pressure and pushes down' choice reverses Bernoulli; fast air lowers pressure. The effect does reach the flame: it works precisely by strengthening the draught through the whole chimney. And blowing across the top does not block it — air streaming over an opening is not a cap. The lowered pressure at the top boosts the draught and the flame.

**23. (A)** A cyclone has a calm, clear low-pressure eye surrounded by the violent eye wall. Passing through the centre, a ship meets the leading eye wall (fierce wind), then the calm clear eye with breaks of sky, then the trailing eye wall, whose winds blow from the opposite direction because the storm's circulation is on the other side of the centre now. This is one storm, not two passing in succession. Cyclones do not pause and restart. And the reversal is not random — it is exactly what the eye-and-eye-wall structure predicts. One cyclone with a calm eye between two eye walls explains the whole log.

**24. (D)** Wind blows from high to low pressure, so surface air moves from the cool high-pressure side to the warm low-pressure side, while the warm air, being less dense, rises. If the warm region were cooled to match the cool one, both would be at the same pressure, the difference driving the wind would disappear, and the surface wind would die. The first wrong choice reverses the wind direction and has warm air sinking. The 'no air moves' choice ignores the existing pressure difference. And warm air does not sink because it is 'heavier' — it is less dense and rises; removing the temperature difference is exactly what stops the wind, so 'no effect' is wrong.

**25. (D)** The decisive modern change is early warning: satellites and radar detect and track cyclones a day or two ahead, letting authorities evacuate people to shelters before landfall. Cyclones of similar strength are not inherently gentler now — the storms are as strong; what changed is the warning and response. Storm surges still occur during cyclones; they have not stopped. And coastal districts remain populated, so mass permanent abandonment is not the reason. Accurate advance warning saving lives is the main cause.

**26. (B)** The gap measures how long the sound takes to travel from the storm, so it tracks distance. The gap shrinking from 9 to 6 seconds means the storm came closer; the gap then growing back to 9 means it moved away again — approach then retreat. 'Steadily closer the whole time' ignores that the gap grew again at the end. The gap depends on distance, not on the lightning's brightness, so 'stayed in one spot' is wrong. And the speed of sound does not speed up and slow down mid-storm; the changing gap reflects the storm's changing distance. So it approached, then receded.

**27. (C)** Two things happen. The thin mountain air outside presses in less than indoor air did, which by itself would let the bottle swell. But the cooling contracts the trapped air and lowers its inside pressure sharply. The cooling effect dominates here: the inside pressure drops below even the reduced outside pressure, so the outside air still wins and the bottle dents inward. The 'swollen because thin air lets it expand' choice forgets the cooling that shrinks the inside air. The two effects do not exactly cancel — the strong cooling outweighs the modest pressure drop outside. And cooling contracts trapped air, it does not expand it. So the bottle dents inward.

**28. (A)** A storm surge is sea water heaped up by the cyclone: the very low central pressure lets the sea surface rise, and the fierce winds pile up and drive that water onto and far across the low coast — much farther and higher than an ordinary tide. It is not slow rainwater collecting (it is a sudden wind-and-pressure-driven mound of sea water). The sea level rises in a surge, it does not drop. And it is sea water driven inland, not fresh river water flowing backward. Low pressure plus fierce wind heaping up the sea is what makes the surge so destructive.

**29. (C)** Head-on light concentrates more heat at the equator; grazing light spreads thin heat at the poles, so the equator is far warmer. Warm equatorial air rises and cold polar air sinks, and air flows between them, setting up the planet-scale wind circulation that redistributes heat. The 'poles warmer' choice reverses the heating. The slant does not warm the globe equally — that uneven heating is the whole point. And the wind belts arise from rising warm and sinking cold air, not from air 'losing its weight.' The equator-warmer-than-poles chain is correct.

**30. (A)** The loop starts with the Sun warming the sea so warm moist air rises (iii). As that vapour condenses higher up, it releases heat that deepens the central low (ii). The deepened low pulls surrounding air inward faster, spiralling toward the centre (i). That deeper low and the rising air then draw still more moist air upward (iv), feeding the cycle onward. So the build-up is iii, ii, i, iv. The other orderings start mid-cycle — with wind already spiralling, or with condensation before any rising air, or with the feedback step before the initial warming — none of which can be the starting cause. The Sun-warmed rising air must come first.

**31. (A)** With the tumbler inverted, the water is at the bottom against the card and the trapped air bubble is above it. That air pushes down on the water with its own pressure, adding to the water's weight on the card. The upward air pressure on the card is unchanged, so the total downward push can now exceed it and the card falls. The 'less water is heavier' choice is self-contradictory. Air pressure does not magically only work on completely full containers — it acts the same, but the added downward push from the bubble tips the balance. And the bubble does not 'glue and drag' the card; it presses down through its pressure. The added downward pressure of the trapped air is the reason.

**32. (D)** The anemometer measures wind speed (faster spin = stronger wind) and the barometer measures pressure (falling = dropping pressure). Strengthening wind plus falling pressure is the textbook approach of a low-pressure system or storm; the near-steady thermometer shows this is not just a temperature change. 'Settled fair weather' contradicts falling pressure with rising wind. Heating does not spin the anemometer — wind does, and the thermometer is steady anyway. And a barometer measures pressure, not present rainfall. Falling pressure with rising wind signals an approaching storm.

**33. (D)** Fast-moving air has lower pressure. When she blows across the top, the lower pressure is above and the higher still-air pressure below pushes the strip up. When she blows across the bottom, the lower pressure is now below and the higher still-air pressure above presses the strip down. So flipping which side gets the fast air flips the direction of the push. 'Blowing always raises paper' ignores that lift came from low pressure being on the upper side. The side certainly matters — it determines where the low pressure sits. And blowing underneath gives a downward push, not extra lift. So the free end is pushed down.

**34. (A)** The true driver of wind is the pressure difference: air moves from higher to lower pressure. Hot regions tend to develop low pressure because their warmed air expands and rises, so cooler air at higher pressure does flow toward them — which is why the direction the student named is often correct. But there is no 'attraction to heat'; pressure does the pushing. Air is not magnetically drawn to warmth. The direction is not always hot-to-cold — it depends on where the low pressure forms, which is usually over the hot region. And wind is closely tied to temperature through pressure, so 'nothing to do with either' is wrong. Pressure, set up by heating, is the real cause.

**35. (D)** A thunderstorm's violence comes from the vigorous up-and-down churning: warm moist air rushing upward and water drops and ice falling back down through it, releasing energy as vapour condenses. Quiet, dry sinking air with no moisture is the opposite of what powers a storm. Lightning is a result of the charge separation produced by this churning, not a first cause that drags the air up. And the ground heats the surface air from below to start the rising, but the cloud's engine is the rising moist air and falling precipitation inside it, not direct contact heating of the cloud. The rising warm moist air with falling water and ice is the engine.

**36. (A)** The crush comes from a pressure difference: when the steam condenses, the sealed can's inside pressure falls far below the outside, and the outside air crushes it. With a wide hole left open, outside air simply flows in to replace the condensing steam, so the inside pressure stays equal to the outside and no crushing difference forms — the can keeps its shape. Letting steam 'rush out faster' does not pull walls in; the open hole equalises pressure rather than creating a vacuum. The crush, when it happens, is all-around from uniform outside pressure, not one-sided at a hole. And cooling does not melt metal. An open hole equalises the pressure, so no crush.

**37. (D)** The sea breeze depends on the land heating more than the sea, forming lower pressure over land that draws in sea air. Mirrors that reflect away sunlight keep the land from heating much, so the land-sea pressure difference shrinks and the breeze weakens. Mirrors reflect light, not 'wind,' so they add no wind. The breeze certainly depends on land heating — that is its cause. And reflecting sunlight away cools the land relative to normal, it does not make the land hotter than the sea, so no reversal to a strong land breeze. Less land heating means a weaker sea breeze.

**38. (D)** In winter the land cools faster and more than the sea, so the cooler land develops higher pressure; wind blows from that higher pressure over the land toward the lower pressure over the relatively warmer sea — hence land to sea. Because this air originates over dry land and has not travelled across the sea collecting moisture, it is dry, so it brings little rain. The choice saying the sea cools faster reverses the land-sea cooling rates. Winter winds are roughly horizontal land-to-sea winds, not straight-up snow-bringers. And cold air does not 'always carry more water'; these land winds are dry. Cooler land giving higher pressure and dry origin explains both direction and dryness.

**39. (A)** A cyclone's centre, the eye, is calm and clear; the fiercest winds and heaviest rain form the eye wall, the ring just around the eye. So the most violent zone is not the geometric centre but the eye-wall ring, and the eye itself is comparatively safe — a position at the centre does not equal maximum violence. The 'eye is most violent' choice is the very misconception being corrected. The calm eye and violent eye wall are not equally dangerous — they differ sharply. And the outer cloud bands are milder than the eye wall, so calling the eye wall calmest is exactly backwards. The eye wall around the calm eye is the deadliest part.

**40. (D)** Cyclone, hurricane and typhoon are different regional names for the very same type of large tropical storm — the name depends on where in the world it occurs, not on size. So treating them as three distinct storms of fixed, ranked sizes is the error; none is permanently the largest. Re-ranking which is biggest still wrongly assumes fixed different sizes. They are not genuinely different storms. And a typhoon is not a tornado — a tornado is a separate, much smaller phenomenon. The real error is calling regional names of one storm three different fixed-size storms.

**41. (B)** Over the heated patch the warmed air expands, becomes less dense and rises away, so less air remains pressing on the surface and the surface pressure falls. The shaded patch is not heated this way, so its air stays put and its surface pressure stays comparatively high — this very difference drives a breeze toward the heated patch. The 'pressure rises because warm air is heavier' choice repeats the density error; warm air is lighter and rises. Heating one patch lowers its pressure, not both patches equally. And the pressure falls modestly as air rises away, it does not drop to zero — the air is not removed entirely. So the heated patch's surface pressure falls.

**42. (C)** Inside both jars the trapped air pushes out with the same pressure. At the valley the outside air pushes in about as hard, so the strains nearly balance. On the peak the outside air is thinner and pushes in less, while the inside push is unchanged, so the net effect is an outward strain on the walls. The 'inward squeeze' choice wrongly says mountains have stronger pressure — they have weaker pressure. The two jars do not feel the same strain, because the outside pressure differs with altitude. And air does not lose all its pressure on a mountain; it is merely thinner. Weaker outside push with unchanged inside push gives a net outward strain.

**43. (A)** Storing clean drinking water ahead of a cyclone is sound because floodwater can contaminate sources and the storm can cut off normal supply. Throwing open all doors and windows lets the wind and rain into the house and can help tear the roof off — it is unsafe. Going to the seashore during a cyclone warning exposes you to surge and violent waves — dangerous. And sheltering under a lone tall tree is hazardous, as it can be blown down or struck. Storing clean water in advance is the safe, correctly justified action.

**44. (B)** From the centre out, a cyclone has the calm, clear, low-pressure eye; then the eye wall, the ring of fiercest winds and heaviest rain; then the outer spiralling cloud bands. The first wrong choice mislabels the eye as violent and high-pressure (it is calm and low-pressure) and the eye wall as calm. The second again calls the eye high-pressure and puts the fiercest winds at the outer rim instead of the eye wall. And a cyclone certainly has a violent zone — the eye wall — so 'calm everywhere' is wrong. Calm low-pressure eye, violent eye wall, outer spiralling bands is correct.

**45. (C)** The cyclone's intensification runs on warm sea water supplying warm moist air that condenses and releases heat to deepen the central low. Take away the warm water — move it over cool water — and little warm moist air rises and little condensation heat is released, so the low stops deepening and the storm stalls. Adding more vapour, deepening the low further, or supplying more warm moist air all feed the cycle and would intensify, not stop, the storm. Removing the warm sea water is the change that starves the engine.

**46. (D)** In hot water the air expanded and swelled the balloon. Moving to ice water cools the air, which contracts; its pressure falls below the outside air pressure, so the higher outside pressure pushes the balloon inward and it collapses. Cold air contracts — it does not expand — so 'swells even further' is wrong. Cooling certainly does affect it (it reverses the swelling), so 'stays swollen' is wrong. And the collapse is driven by the inside-versus-outside pressure difference, not by the rubber pulling itself in. Contracting air with pressure below outside, pushed in by outside air, is the answer.

**47. (D)** The sea breeze needs the land warmer than the sea, giving lower pressure over land that draws sea air in. On the overcast day the land heats only a little, so the difference shrinks and the breeze weakens. On the cold-spell day the land is colder than the sea, so the pressure pattern flips and the surface wind tends to reverse into a land breeze. Cloud and cold do not by themselves increase wind. The breeze does depend on land temperature, so 'exactly the same' is wrong. And cold land air rushing out toward the warm sea is a land breeze, not a stronger sea breeze. So: weaker, then reversed.

**48. (B)** Falling pressure (barometer dropping fast) combined with strengthening wind at sea is the classic sign of an approaching low-pressure system or storm, so the responsible advisory is to warn of it and bring the boats in. Falling pressure does not promise settled fair weather — the opposite. The combined readings are far from meaningless; they are a clear warning, so telling the fishermen to keep fishing far out would be dangerous. And a fast-falling barometer is not a forecast of a freeze; it is about an approaching storm. Warning of an approaching storm and recalling the boats is the safe call.

**49. (D)** The fast-moving air in the stream is at lower pressure than the slower surrounding air, so whenever the ball drifts to the edge, the higher outside pressure pushes it back into the stream — and this works along whatever direction the stream points, including a 45-degree tilt. The ball is not weightless; it has weight that the stream supports while the pressure difference centres it. The air is not 'sticky glue' holding it on a path — that is a vague non-mechanism. And there is no magnetism. The lower pressure inside the fast stream, with higher pressure around it pushing inward, keeps the ball in the slanted stream.

**50. (A)** First the Sun warms the ground and the surface air on a hot humid day (iv). That warm moist air rises and cools as it goes up (ii). On cooling, its water vapour condenses into towering tall clouds (iii). As the cloud droplets merge and grow too heavy to stay aloft, they fall as rain (i). So the order is iv, ii, iii, i. Starting with rain falling (i first) puts the result before its cause. Starting with clouds already formed (iii first) skips the warming and rising that make them. And starting with air already rising (ii first) leaves out the initial warming that lifts it. Warming, rising, condensing, raining is the true sequence.

**51. (B)** A narrow whirling column only a few hundred metres wide, reaching from a cloud to the ground, short-lived and cutting a narrow destructive track, is a tornado. A tropical cyclone is hundreds of kilometres across and lasts far longer — far bigger than this. A sea breeze is a gentle horizontal coastal wind, not a violent vertical column. And a high-pressure region is a broad calm system, the opposite of an intense narrow whirl. These features identify a tornado.

**52. (C)** The single underlying fact is that land heats and cools faster than water. By day the land heats quickly, becomes warmer than the sea, develops lower pressure, and draws cool sea air inland — a sea breeze. By night the land cools quickly, becomes cooler than the sea, so now the warmer sea air rises and air flows out from the land — a land breeze. The Sun does not 'blow' wind directly; it heats surfaces unevenly. The sea does not change temperature faster — it is the slower one, which is the whole point. And the reversal is not a fixed clockwork; it is driven by the changing land-sea temperature difference. Land's faster temperature change underlies both breezes.

**53. (A)** Wind speed in a cyclone is driven by the pressure difference between the surroundings and the centre. A deeper central low means a bigger difference, so the surrounding higher-pressure air rushes inward faster — stronger winds. A deep low does not still the air; it accelerates it inward. Cyclones draw air inward, not push it outward. And the central pressure is closely tied to wind speed (sea temperature matters by feeding the low, but the immediate driver of wind is the pressure difference). A deeper low gives a bigger difference and faster inrushing wind.

**54. (B)** Fast wind over a roof lowers the pressure above it while the still air inside stays at higher pressure, so the roof is pushed up; the best defence is a heavy, well-anchored, low-profile roof, which is firmly fixed down and presents less surface to the lift. A light, loosely laid roof is the easiest to lift, not the safest. A tall, broad, unanchored roof offers a large surface for the lift and is not held down by size alone. And fast wind over a roof lowers the pressure to lift it, not press it down. A heavy, anchored, low roof resists the lift best.

**55. (B)** The flame stays bright because the chimney sets up a draught: warm air rises out the top and fresh air is drawn in at the bottom, supplying the flame. Sealing the top stops the warm air escaping, so fresh air no longer flows in, the draught fails, and the flame dims or goes out. Trapping the warm air does not feed the flame — it suffocates it. The flame does depend on the through-flow of air, so 'unaffected' is wrong. And blocking the top stops the draught rather than speeding it. Sealing the top kills the draught, so the flame dies.

**56. (D)** The cover is held up only when the upward air pressure on it exceeds the total downward force, which is the water's weight plus the cover's own weight. A light card adds almost nothing, so it holds. A heavy metal plate adds a large downward weight, so the total downward force may now exceed the upward air pressure and the plate falls. 'Heavier covers gripped harder' invents a non-existent grip. The cover's weight does matter — it adds to the downward side. And air pressure pushes up on the cover but does not lift heavy metal away upward. The plate's extra weight can overcome the air pressure, so it may fall.

**57. (C)** A cyclone is fed by warm moist air rising off a warm sea; the condensation of that vapour releases heat that deepens its low and powers its winds. Once over land, it loses that supply of warm moist air, so the cycle is starved and the storm weakens within a day. The storm does not 'freeze solid' over land. Buildings do not form a wall that blocks all the wind across a storm hundreds of kilometres wide. And the eye does not snap shut on contact with soil. Being cut off from warm, moist sea air is the correct reason for the weakening.

**58. (D)** Faster eye-wall winds go with a deeper, lower central pressure, since the bigger pressure difference between the surroundings and the centre drives faster inrushing wind. M's winds (200 km/h) are faster than N's (120 km/h), so M most likely has the lower (deeper) central pressure. The choice giving N the lower pressure inverts the relationship. Forming over the same sea on the same day does not force identical central pressures — their wind speeds already differ. And higher central pressure does not 'fling winds outward faster'; cyclonic winds rush inward, driven by a deeper low. So M has the deeper low.

**59. (A)** Both seasonal reversals trace to one fact: land heats and cools faster than the sea. In summer the land becomes much hotter than the sea, forming lower pressure that pulls moist sea air inland (the wet monsoon). In winter the land cools faster and becomes cooler than the sea, forming higher pressure that pushes dry air out to sea. The Moon's gravity governs tides, not these seasonal winds. The sea does not change temperature faster — it is the slower one, which is exactly why the land's swings drive the reversal. And the monsoon is not a calendar reflex divorced from heating; it is driven by the land-sea temperature difference. Land's faster temperature change underlies both.

**60. (D)** As a cyclone's centre passes over a town, the leading eye wall arrives first with fierce wind from one direction (ii). Then the calm, clear eye passes over (i). Then the trailing eye wall brings fierce wind from the opposite direction (iii). Finally the outer bands move off and the wind and rain ease (iv). So the order is ii, i, iii, iv. Starting with the calm (i first) puts the eye before the leading eye wall. Putting both fierce winds back to back with no calm between (ii then iii) skips the eye passing over. And the easing-off (iv) is the end, not the start. Leading eye wall, calm eye, trailing eye wall, then easing is the true sequence.
