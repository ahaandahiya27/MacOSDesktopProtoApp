Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

Which of the following pairs are **both** animal fibres?

  • Acotton and silk
  • Bjute and wool
  • Cflax and cotton
  • Dwool and silk✓

Working

✓

Animal fibres come from animals: wool (sheep, goat, yak, camel) and silk (silkworm). Cotton, jute and flax are plant fibres. Only "wool and silk" has two animal fibres.

Q2

Wool and silk feel different from cotton mainly because:

  • Aall three are made of the same material
  • Bcotton is made of protein and wool of cellulose
  • Csilk is a synthetic fibre and cotton is natural
  • Dwool and silk are made of protein, while cotton is made of cellulose✓

Working

✓

Animal fibres (wool, silk) are made of protein; plant fibres (cotton) are made of cellulose. The different chemistry gives the different feel. (Option C reverses the two.)

Q3

A burning sample gives a smell like burning hair. The fibre is most likely:

  • Acotton
  • Bjute
  • Cwool✓
  • Dflax

Working

✓

Protein fibres smell like burning hair when burnt; cellulose fibres (cotton, jute, flax) smell like burning paper. Of the choices, only wool is a protein fibre, so the smell of burning hair points to wool.

Q4

Which of these is **not** a wool-yielding animal?

  • Ayak
  • Bcamel
  • Cgoat
  • Dsilk moth✓

Working

✓

The silk moth gives silk, not wool. Yak, camel and goat all yield wool, so the odd one out is the silk moth.

Q5

The thick coat of hair on a sheep, from which wool is obtained, is called the:

  • Afleece✓
  • Bcocoon
  • Csericin
  • Dburr

Working

✓

The thick coat of hair on a sheep is the fleece. A cocoon belongs to a silkworm, sericin is silk gum, and a burr is a bit of plant debris.

Q6

The main reason a sheep grows a thick fleece is to:

  • Aprovide wool for humans
  • Bkeep the animal cool in summer
  • Cprotect it from insect bites
  • Dtrap a layer of air that keeps the animal warm✓

Working

✓

The fleece insulates the sheep by trapping a layer of air, keeping it warm in cold weather. (Providing wool for humans is a use we make of it, not the animal's reason for growing it.)

Q7

Pashmina, one of the softest and warmest wools, comes from a kind of:

  • Asheep
  • Bsilkworm
  • Cgoat✓
  • Dcamel

Working

✓

Pashmina is the very fine, warm wool obtained from a special kind of goat (the pashmina/changthangi goat), not from sheep, silkworm or camel.

Q8

Wool keeps you warmer than a smooth plant fibre of the same thickness mainly because wool fibres:

  • Atrap more air between and within the fibres✓
  • Bare heavier
  • Care coloured
  • Dare made of cellulose

Working

✓

Wool fibres are crimped and trap a lot of still air both between and within the fibres; trapped air is a poor conductor, so it keeps you warm. Smooth plant fibres trap far less air. (Colour and weight have nothing to do with warmth.)

Q9

In India, sheep reared for wool are found in the highlands of states such as:

  • AKerala and Tamil Nadu
  • BWest Bengal and Odisha
  • CJammu & Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh✓
  • DGoa and Andhra Pradesh

Working

✓

In India, wool sheep are reared in the cold highlands such as Jammu & Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh (also Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Arunachal). Warm coastal states like Kerala are not wool regions.

Q10

The correct order of the **first three** steps in processing wool is:

  • Ascouring → shearing → sorting
  • Bshearing → scouring → sorting✓
  • Csorting → shearing → scouring
  • Dshearing → sorting → scouring

Working

✓

The processing order begins shearing → scouring → sorting. You must first cut the fleece (shear), then wash it (scour), then grade it (sort). The other orders put washing or sorting before the fleece is even removed.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

Shearing is best done in spring or early summer rather than winter because:

  • Awool grows faster in summer
  • Bremoving the fleece in cold winter would leave the sheep without protection from the cold✓
  • Cthe dye sticks better in summer
  • Dshears work only in warm weather

Working

✓

If the fleece were sheared off in winter, the sheep would lose its insulation in the cold and could fall ill. Shearing in spring/summer leaves time for new wool to grow before the next winter.

Q12

Scouring is the step in which the sheared fleece is:

  • Awashed to remove grease, dust and dirt✓
  • Bcut from the sheep's body
  • Cdyed in different colours
  • Dtwisted into yarn

Working

✓

Scouring is the thorough washing of sheared wool in warm water to remove grease, dust and dirt. Cutting it off is shearing; colouring is dyeing; twisting into yarn is rolling/spinning.

Q13

If the scouring step were skipped, the most direct problem would be that the wool:

  • Awould be too short
  • Bcould not be dyed at all
  • Cwould catch fire easily
  • Dwould stay full of grease and dirt, spoiling later steps✓

Working

✓

Scouring removes grease and dirt. Skip it and the wool stays greasy and dirty, which spoils sorting, dyeing and spinning later. (It can still be dyed, just badly; it does not become short or flammable because of skipping.)

Q14

Sorting separates the cleaned wool into different grades on the basis of features such as:

  • Atexture, length and colour✓
  • Bthe price of the sheep
  • Cthe season it was sheared
  • Dthe country of origin only

Working

✓

Sorting grades the cleaned wool by physical features of the fibre — texture, length and colour. The price of the sheep or the country alone are not sorting criteria.

Q15

"Picking out the burrs" in wool processing means removing:

  • Athe grease from the fibre
  • Bthe dye from the wool
  • Csmall bits of plant material caught in the fleece✓
  • Dthe skin from the fleece

Working

✓

Burrs are small bits of plant material (seeds, leaf fragments) caught in the fleece; picking them out removes this plant debris. Grease is removed by scouring, not burr-picking.

Q16

In wool processing, dyeing comes **after** sorting and picking burrs but **before** rolling into yarn. Natural fleece before dyeing is usually:

  • Acreamy white✓
  • Bbright red
  • Cjet black
  • Ddeep blue

Working

✓

Raw, undyed fleece is naturally a creamy white, which is why it can then be dyed into many colours. It is not naturally red, black or blue.

Q17

The very last of the six wool steps turns the cleaned, coloured fibres into:

  • Aa fleece
  • Byarn ready for weaving✓
  • Craw cocoons
  • Dsericin

Working

✓

The final (sixth) step rolls/combs the cleaned, dyed fibres into yarn ready to be woven into fabric. A fleece is the raw start; cocoons and sericin belong to silk.

Q18

A factory removes burrs **before** scouring the fleece. The most likely result is that:

  • Athe wool will be cleaner than usual
  • Bthe grease and dirt make it hard to spot and remove the burrs, so burrs are missed✓
  • Cnothing changes, since order does not matter
  • Dthe wool will be dyed automatically

Working

✓

Burrs are easiest to see and remove on clean wool. Doing burr-picking before scouring means the grease and dirt hide the burrs, so many are missed — order matters here.

Q19

The serious occupational disease that wool **sorters** are at risk of is:

  • Athe common cold✓
  • Bsorter's disease (anthrax)
  • Cmalaria
  • Dtooth decay

Working

✓

Wool sorters who breathe in spores from raw fleece are at risk of sorter's disease (a form of anthrax). The other illnesses listed are unrelated to wool sorting.

Q20

Sorter's disease is caused by:

  • Abacterial spores (Bacillus anthracis) present in raw fleece✓
  • Ba virus in the dye
  • Ceating wool
  • Dcold weather alone

Working

✓

Sorter's disease is caused by spores of the bacterium Bacillus anthracis that can be present in raw fleece — it is bacterial, not viral, and not caused by cold weather or by eating wool.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

A wool sorter is most likely to catch sorter's disease by:

  • Adrinking warm water used in scouring
  • Binhaling dust and spores from the raw fleece✓
  • Ctouching dyed yarn
  • Dwearing a finished sweater

Working

✓

A sorter catches the disease mainly by inhaling dust and spores from the raw fleece while handling it. Dyed yarn and finished sweaters have already been cleaned, and scouring water is not the route.

Q22

Which step in wool processing carries the **highest** risk of sorter's disease?

  • Arolling the finished yarn
  • Bwearing a woollen scarf
  • Chandling raw, unwashed fleece during sorting✓
  • Dbuying wool from a shop

Working

✓

The risk is highest when handling raw, unwashed fleece (the sorting stage), because that is when the spores are still present. Finished/processed wool and shop-bought sweaters carry little risk.

Q23

The buyer of a finished, scoured and dyed woollen sweater is at very low risk of sorter's disease because:

  • Asweaters are made of cotton
  • Bthe disease only affects sheep
  • Cthe fleece has been cleaned and processed, removing the spores before the wool reaches the shop✓
  • Dthe dye kills all bacteria in the body

Working

✓

By the time wool reaches a shop it has been scoured and processed, removing the spores, so the buyer of a finished sweater is at very low risk. (Sweaters here are wool, not cotton, and the disease is not "sheep-only.")

Q24

A sensible safety measure in a modern wool factory is to:

  • Amake sorters work without masks to save cost
  • Bskip the scouring step
  • Cshear the sheep in winter
  • Dprovide masks, gloves and good ventilation, and disinfect fleece before sorting✓

Working

✓

Sensible safety means protecting the worker: masks, gloves, good ventilation and disinfecting the fleece before sorting. Working without masks or skipping cleaning steps would increase risk.

Q25

Two sheep breeds are compared. Merino has very few coarse "guard" hairs; Lincoln has many. Which breed gives the **finer, softer** wool?

  • AMerino✓
  • BLincoln
  • Cboth give equally soft wool
  • Dneither gives soft wool

Working

✓

Fine, soft wool comes from breeds with very few coarse guard hairs. Merino has very few guard hairs, so Merino gives the finer, softer wool; Lincoln (many guard hairs) is coarser.

Q26

A sheep's fleece contains two kinds of hair. The coarse outer hairs are the **guard hairs**, and the fine inner hairs form the:

  • Acocoon
  • Bburrs
  • Cundercoat✓
  • Dsericin

Working

✓

A fleece has coarse outer guard hairs and a fine inner undercoat. The fine inner hairs form the undercoat. (Cocoon and sericin belong to silk; burrs are plant debris.)

Q27

The reason sorting is needed before spinning fine yarn is that:

  • Aall the fibres in a fleece are exactly the same
  • Bsorting adds colour to the wool
  • Csorting removes the grease
  • Da fleece mixes coarse and fine hairs of different lengths, which must be separated for the right quality✓

Working

✓

A single fleece mixes coarse and fine hairs of different lengths and colours. To spin fine, even yarn you must first sort and separate these — that is why sorting precedes spinning. (Sorting does not add colour or remove grease.)

Q28

Why should a woollen sweater be washed in **cool** water with a mild detergent rather than hot water?

  • Ahot water has no effect on wool
  • Bhot water and rough washing make the fibres lock together and the sweater shrinks✓
  • Ccool water dyes the wool
  • Dwool dissolves in cold water

Working

✓

Hot water plus rough agitation makes wool fibres' tiny scales interlock, so the garment felts and shrinks permanently. Cool water and gentle handling prevent this. (Wool does not dissolve in cold water.)

Q29

A child washes a pure-wool sweater in very hot water and scrubs it hard. The most likely result is that the sweater:

  • Agrows larger
  • Bchanges into cotton
  • Cstays exactly the same size
  • Dshrinks and becomes matted✓

Working

✓

Very hot water and hard scrubbing felt the fibres together, so the sweater shrinks and becomes matted — it does not grow, turn into cotton, or stay unchanged.

Q30

Sericulture is the term for the:

  • Arearing of silkworms for silk✓
  • Brearing of sheep for wool
  • Cweaving of cotton
  • Ddyeing of wool

Working

✓

Sericulture is the rearing of silkworms for silk. Rearing sheep is for wool; weaving cotton and dyeing wool are different activities.

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

The silkworm is actually the **larva** (caterpillar) of the:

  • Ahoneybee
  • Bbutterfly
  • Csilk moth✓
  • Dhousefly

Working

✓

The silkworm is the larva (caterpillar) of the silk moth — not of a bee, butterfly or housefly.

Q32

The correct order of the silk moth's life cycle is:

  • Aegg → pupa → larva → adult moth
  • Blarva → egg → adult moth → pupa
  • Cegg → larva → pupa → adult moth✓
  • Dadult moth → pupa → larva → egg

Working

✓

The silk moth's life cycle is egg → larva (silkworm) → pupa (in the cocoon) → adult moth. The other orders place the stages out of sequence.

Q33

The stage at which the insect spins silk around itself is when it changes from a:

  • Aegg into a larva
  • Blarva into a pupa, forming the cocoon✓
  • Cpupa into an adult moth
  • Dadult moth into an egg

Working

✓

The insect spins silk while changing from larva into pupa, forming the cocoon around itself. That larva-to-pupa transition is the cocoon-spinning stage; the other transitions do not produce silk.

Q34

The favourite food of the common (mulberry) silkworm is:

  • Aoak leaves
  • Brose petals
  • Cmulberry leaves✓
  • Dwheat grains

Working

✓

The common mulberry silkworm feeds mainly on mulberry leaves — that is why it is called the mulberry silkworm. Oak leaves feed tasar worms; roses and wheat are not its food.

Q35

The cocoon is best described as:

  • Aa single continuous silk thread wound around the pupa✓
  • Bmany short threads glued together randomly
  • Ca layer of wool
  • Dthe egg of the moth

Working

✓

A cocoon is a single continuous silk thread spun around the pupa — it is this one long thread that makes silk valuable, not many short glued pieces.

Q36

In commercial silk-making the cocoon is treated with steam or boiling water **before** the moth comes out. The main reason is that:

  • Ait makes the moth grow faster
  • Bit dyes the silk
  • Cif the moth bit its way out, it would break the single long thread into many short pieces✓
  • Dit feeds the pupa

Working

✓

The cocoon is steamed/boiled before the moth emerges because an emerging moth would bite through the cocoon and break the single long thread into many short, less useful pieces. (It is not done to feed or grow the insect.)

Q37

Reeling is the step in which silk thread is:

  • Acut from a sheep
  • Bunwound from the softened cocoon✓
  • Cdyed black
  • Dwoven into a saree

Working

✓

Reeling is unwinding the silk thread from the softened cocoon. Cutting from a sheep is shearing (wool); dyeing and weaving are later, separate steps.

Q38

Cocoons are placed in hot water before reeling. The hot water mainly:

  • Acolours the silk
  • Bsoftens the sericin (gum) so the thread can be unwound✓
  • Chatches the eggs
  • Dfeeds the silkworm

Working

✓

Hot water softens the sericin, the gum holding the silk thread together, so the thread can be found and unwound. It does not colour the silk, hatch eggs or feed the worm.

Q39

Several cocoons' threads are often combined during reeling because a single fibre is:

  • Atoo thick to use
  • Bthe wrong colour
  • Ctoo thin and weak to use on its own✓
  • Dmade of cotton

Working

✓

A single silk fibre is extremely thin and weak, so during reeling several cocoons' threads are combined into one stronger strand. (It is not too thick, nor the wrong colour, nor cotton.)

Q40

After reeling, silk is made into cloth by **weaving**, in which:

  • Athreads are washed in warm water
  • Bthe cocoon is boiled
  • Cthe moth lays eggs
  • Dlengthwise (warp) and crosswise (weft) threads are interlaced on a loom✓

Working

✓

Weaving interlaces lengthwise warp threads with crosswise weft threads on a loom to make fabric. Washing, boiling and egg-laying are not weaving.

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

Which sequence shows the correct journey from silkworm to silk fabric?

  • Areeling → cocoon → fabric → weaving
  • Bweaving → cocoon → reeling → fabric
  • Cfabric → weaving → reeling → cocoon
  • Dcocoon → reeling → weaving → fabric✓

Working

✓

The journey is: the worm spins a cocoon → reeling unwinds the thread → weaving makes cloth → fabric. So cocoon → reeling → weaving → fabric is correct.

Q42

The white silk most widely produced in the world comes from the **mulberry** silkworm. India is also well known for non-mulberry silks such as:

  • Anylon and polyester
  • Bcotton and jute
  • Ctasar, eri and muga✓
  • Dwool and pashmina

Working

✓

Besides mulberry (white) silk, India is famous for the non-mulberry silks tasar, eri and muga. Nylon/polyester are synthetic; cotton/jute are plant fibres; wool/pashmina are not silk.

Q43

Muga silk, prized for its natural golden colour, is mainly associated with which region of India?

  • APunjab
  • BKerala
  • CGujarat
  • DAssam✓

Working

✓

Muga silk, with its natural golden sheen, is the speciality of Assam. It is not associated with Punjab, Kerala or Gujarat.

Q44

Some people object to traditional silk-making on ethical grounds because:

  • Athe pupae inside the cocoons are killed when the cocoons are boiled✓
  • Bsilkworms are fed too much
  • Csilk is too cheap
  • Dthe moths are kept as pets

Working

✓

The ethical objection is that the pupae inside the cocoons are killed when the cocoons are boiled to keep the thread whole. The other options are not the reason for the objection.

Q45

Which statement correctly matches a fibre to its source?

  • Asilk — from a plant's seed pods
  • Bsilk — from a silkworm's cocoon; wool — from an animal's fleece✓
  • Cwool — from a silkworm's cocoon
  • Dcotton — from a sheep's fleece

Working

✓

The correct match is: silk comes from a silkworm's cocoon and wool from an animal's fleece. The other options swap or misassign the sources.

Q46

Compared with natural fibres, synthetic fibres such as polyester and nylon are generally:

  • Amade from plants and animals
  • Balways warmer than wool
  • Cspun by silkworms
  • Dman-made from petroleum, durable and wrinkle-resistant, but not biodegradable✓

Working

✓

Synthetic fibres like polyester and nylon are man-made from petroleum: durable and wrinkle-resistant but not biodegradable. They are not from plants/animals, not warmer than wool, and not spun by silkworms.

Q47

A "80% wool + 20% polyester" blend is used in sweaters because the polyester:

  • Amakes the sweater smell of hair
  • Bturns the wool into cotton
  • Cadds strength and helps the wool keep its shape✓
  • Dis cheaper than air

Working

✓

A little polyester added to wool adds strength and helps the garment keep its shape (resist stretching). It does not turn wool into cotton or make it smell of hair.

Q48

Both wool and silk are animal fibres, yet wool is bulky and warm while silk is smooth and glossy. The best explanation is that:

  • Asilk is actually a plant fibre
  • Bwool is man-made
  • Cthey are chemically identical
  • Dthey are made of different proteins arranged differently (crimped keratin vs smooth fibroin)✓

Working

✓

Both are proteins, but different ones arranged differently — crimped, scaly keratin makes wool bulky and warm, while smooth fibroin makes silk glossy. Silk is not a plant fibre and wool is not man-made, so they are not identical.

Q49

A single mulberry cocoon can yield one continuous silk thread that is roughly:

  • Ahundreds to over a thousand metres long✓
  • Ba few centimetres long
  • Cexactly one metre long
  • Dshorter than a human hair

Working

✓

A single mulberry cocoon's thread is remarkably long — roughly hundreds to over a thousand metres — which is what makes one cocoon so useful. The other lengths are far too short.

Q50

Which everyday observation best shows that wool is an **animal** fibre rather than a plant fibre?

  • Ait is sheared from the body of a living sheep✓
  • Bit is woven into cloth
  • Cit can be dyed different colours
  • Dit keeps you warm

Working

✓

The clearest sign that wool is an animal fibre is that it is sheared from the body of a living sheep. Being woven, dyed or warm are true of plant fibres too, so they do not prove animal origin.

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

A label reads "100% silk." Tracing it back to its true biological origin, this fabric began as:

  • Athe seed hair of a plant
  • Bthe cocoon spun by the larva of the silk moth✓
  • Cthe fleece of a goat
  • Dthe stem of a jute plant

Working

✓

Tracing "100% silk" back to its origin, the fabric began as the cocoon spun by the larva (silkworm) of the silk moth — not seed hair, goat fleece or jute stems.

Q52

In wool processing, which single step **both** improves the look of the wool **and** does **not** clean or align it?

  • Ascouring
  • Bdyeing✓
  • Cshearing
  • Dsorting

Working

✓

Among the steps, dyeing improves the look (adds colour) but does not clean the wool (scouring does) or align/spin it (rolling does). So dyeing is the step that fits.

Q53

Why is sericin removed during reeling rather than left on the thread?

  • Ait is a stiff gum that sticks the thread to the cocoon and to itself, so the thread cannot be unwound or woven smoothly while it is on✓
  • Bit is poisonous to wear
  • Cit is the wrong colour for dyeing
  • Dit makes the silk too thin

Working

✓

Sericin is a stiff gum that glues the thread to itself and to the cocoon; while it is on, the thread cannot be unwound or woven smoothly, so it is softened/removed during reeling. (It is not removed because it is poisonous or the wrong colour.)

Q54

A trader claims his "vicuña wool" is rare and costly because the vicuña gives very little wool per year. This reasoning is:

  • Awrong, because vicuña is a plant
  • Breasonable, because a fibre that an animal produces only in tiny amounts is naturally scarce and expensive✓
  • Cwrong, because all wools cost the same
  • Dwrong, because vicuña wool is synthetic

Working

✓

The reasoning is sound: a fibre that an animal makes only in tiny amounts each year is naturally scarce, which raises its price. Vicuña is an animal (a llama relative), its wool is natural, and wools certainly do not all cost the same.

Q55

A factory wants to produce the **finest, softest** woollen yarn. Which combination of choices makes the most sense?

  • Aa fine-wool breed (few guard hairs) plus careful sorting of the fleece✓
  • Ba coarse breed, no sorting
  • Cskipping scouring to save the fibres
  • Dusing a silkworm instead of a sheep

Working

✓

To get the finest, softest yarn you want a fine-wool breed (few guard hairs) and careful sorting of the fleece. A coarse breed with no sorting gives rough yarn, skipping scouring spoils it, and a silkworm gives silk not wool.

Q56

Which of the following correctly pairs an animal with the fibre it provides?

  • Asheep → silk
  • Bsilk moth → wool
  • Cmulberry plant → silk
  • Dyak → wool✓

Working

✓

Correct pairing: yak → wool. Sheep give wool (not silk), the silk moth gives silk (not wool), and a mulberry plant is the silkworm's food, not a silk source.

Q57

A student says, "Boiling the cocoon is only done to clean it." The most accurate correction is:

  • Athe student is fully right
  • Bboiling dyes the silk
  • Cboiling chiefly softens the sericin for reeling and kills the pupa so the long thread stays whole — cleaning is not its main purpose✓
  • Dthe cocoon is never boiled

Working

✓

The student is wrong: boiling mainly softens the sericin for reeling and kills the pupa so the long thread is not broken — cleaning is not its main purpose. (The cocoon is indeed boiled, and boiling does not dye it.)

Q58

Putting the wool steps in order, which is the **fourth** step (after shearing, scouring and sorting)?

  • Ashearing again
  • Bpicking out the burrs✓
  • Creeling
  • Dweaving the saree

Working

✓

The six steps run shearing, scouring, sorting, picking burrs, dyeing, rolling. After shearing, scouring and sorting, the fourth step is picking out the burrs. (Reeling and weaving belong to silk.)

Q59

Which chain correctly traces wool from animal to wearable yarn?

  • Adyeing → shearing → rolling → scouring → sorting → picking burrs
  • Bscouring → sorting → shearing → dyeing → rolling → picking burrs
  • Crolling → dyeing → picking burrs → sorting → scouring → shearing
  • Dshearing → scouring → sorting → picking burrs → dyeing → rolling into yarn✓

Working

✓

The correct wool chain is shearing → scouring → sorting → picking burrs → dyeing → rolling into yarn. The other chains scramble this order.

Q60

A health officer wants to cut down sorter's disease in a wool unit. The single most effective measure is to:

  • Adye the wool in brighter colours
  • Bdisinfect the raw fleece and give sorters masks, gloves and good ventilation✓
  • Cshear the sheep more often
  • Dsell only finished sweaters

Working

✓
