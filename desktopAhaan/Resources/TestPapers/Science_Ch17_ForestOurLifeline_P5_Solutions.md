## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** The fern's stated optimum is 10 to 15 units and it is harmed above 50. Only the shrub-layer reading of 12 units lies inside that window. The 100-unit canopy top far exceeds the scorch threshold, so 'more light is always better' fails for a shade-adapted plant. The 3-unit herb floor is below the fern's productive range, so it would make too little food, defeating the idea that darker is automatically safer. The 40-unit understorey is tolerable but below optimum and chosen only by a vague 'middle suits middle' rule rather than by matching the numbers.

**2. (A)** Each step here loses about ninety percent (60000 to 6000 to 600), so the tiers track the producer base. Tripling the base would push the supportable predator mass towards about three times as much, but because the real fraction passed on varies, the answer is an estimate, not a guaranteed exact 1800. The 'fixed by instinct' option ignores that predator numbers are limited by the energy available below them. The 'cut off from the plant supply' option forgets that a carnivore's energy traces back through its prey to the producers. The 'gain rather than lose' option inverts the rule that energy is lost as heat at every transfer.

**3. (B)** From grass to hawk there are four transfers (grass to grasshopper, to frog, to snake, to hawk). Passing on a tenth four times gives a tenth of a tenth of a tenth of a tenth, which is one part in ten thousand. 'About half' ignores the heavy loss at each step. 'Almost all' inverts the rule: the top animal receives the least, not the most. 'None at all' is wrong because energy does flow up the chain, it is just heavily reduced, not stopped.

**4. (C)** The fox was the rabbit's sole predator but only one of the vole's two (the owl remains). So rabbits are released more fully and tend to climb more steeply than voles, and their grazing presses the shared clover. 'Exactly the same amount' ignores that the vole still has the owl. The clover would be grazed more, not less, so 'clover increases' fails; and the owl eats voles, not clover. The owl and fox were not predator and prey of each other, so the fox's loss does not starve the owl.

**5. (D)** Nutrients are recycled: dead matter and dung are broken down by decomposers into the soil, where roots take them up again, so that arrow is a loop. Energy is different: it enters as sunlight, is captured by producers, and is lost as heat at every transfer, never returning, so it is a one-way arrow needing constant resupply by the Sun. The option claiming both loop overlooks the heat loss. The option swapping them reverses the truth. The 'nothing moves' option contradicts the very idea of a food chain.

**6. (B)** Fertility depends on nutrients being released from dead matter into the soil. Plot P's living decomposers do this, keeping litter thin and soil fed; plot Q lost its decomposers, so litter accumulates with its nutrients still locked away, starving the soil. The thick mat is therefore a sign of broken recycling, not richness, refuting 'thicker means richer.' The wind explanation is invented and ignores the stated decomposer kill. Both plots get identical leaf fall, so 'plot Q stopped dropping leaves' contradicts the setup.

**7. (A)** Forest plants respire day and night, so the night carbon-dioxide reading is real. But by day photosynthesis takes in much more carbon dioxide and releases much more oxygen than respiration uses, so over a full day the forest is a net oxygen supplier. The reporter mistakes one night-time slice for the whole picture. Plants do take in oxygen for respiration, so 'never' is wrong. Photosynthesis needs light and so does not run at night, so it cannot cancel the reading at the moment it occurs.

**8. (A)** Spiders ate caterpillars and were eaten by warblers, so losing spiders both releases caterpillars (one fewer predator) and removes one of the warbler's foods, pushing the warbler onto caterpillars. The warbler also eats caterpillars, so it does not starve. The spider is a predator of caterpillars, not a protector of leaves, so the leaves are not directly defended by them. The spider was warbler prey, not a competitor of the hawk for warblers, so the hawk does not gain that way.

**9. (A)** A producer makes its own food from sunlight; the fungus instead lives on the dead log, breaking it down, which makes it a decomposer regardless of its position in this chain. 'Starts the chain therefore producer' confuses position with feeding method. A herbivore eats living plants; the fungus consumes dead, not living, plant matter and is not a plant-eater in that sense. The beetle eating the fungus does not make the fungus a carnivore, that label would describe an animal eating other animals.

**10. (D)** Roots and leaf litter slow rain so it infiltrates; remove them and rain runs off fast, eroding soil (muddy floods), failing to recharge the spring (it floods then dries), and reducing both transpiration and shading (hotter, drier air). One lost mechanism, infiltration plus tree cover, drives every effect. Trees do not 'drink floodwater and breathe out a spring.' Baking bare soil hardens it and reduces, not increases, water holding. Drying wood releasing steam is trivial and cannot explain erosion, the spring or the heat.

**11. (C)** Decomposers return nutrients from dead matter to the soil; remove them and dead matter accumulates while its nutrients stay locked away, so the soil is steadily drained and plant growth falls, not rises. Plants need soil minerals (sunlight supplies energy, not minerals), so 'never need the soil' is false. Decomposers do far more than tidy: they are the recycling step, so 'no part in feeding plants' is wrong. Plants take nutrients up, they do not manufacture and add minerals, so they cannot replace the decomposers.

**12. (A)** The sixty-year sequence of herbs, then shrubs, then trees, with soil and animal life building up, is exactly why a forest cannot be made quickly: it is a slow, staged development. 'A single season' contradicts the decades observed. The forest was not finished at the herb stage; the canopy and humus came much later. The succession happened naturally without people planting, so 'only deliberate planting' is wrong.

**13. (B)** Pollination (by the moth) continues, so fruit and seeds still form; what is lost is dispersal, so seeds mostly fall under the parent and few new saplings establish elsewhere, weakening the species' spread and renewal. Spread does depend on dispersal, so 'unaffected' is wrong. Seeds crowded under the parent compete and are easily found by pests, so the species does not spread faster. Losing the bird has no effect on the moth, so pollination is not stopped and the tree does not die at once.

**14. (B)** Losing half the rabbits pushes the fox onto its other prey, the voles; heavier fox predation on voles means the owl, which shares that prey, faces more competition and may take fewer. The fox eats voles too, so it does not starve. A fox short of one food hunts the other harder, so voles are pressed down, not freed to increase. Rabbits eat clover and voles eat grass, so rabbits do not 'keep voles in check', and clover would be grazed less, not wiped out, with fewer rabbits.

**15. (C)** Cutting the forest for timber gives a one-off product but destroys the continuing services (oxygen, soil, water, climate, habitat) that only a living forest provides, so the standing forest is worth much more than its wood. Clean air and steady water are clearly valuable to people, so the logger's premise fails. Products and services are different categories, so timber does not 'include' the services. Limiting the value to animal shelter ignores the human-supporting services the chapter stresses.

**16. (B)** Both the owl and the fox eat mice and sparrows; halving sparrows pushes both predators onto mice, so mice are hunted harder and tend to decline, and with fewer mice the seeds are eaten slightly less. Mice also eat seeds, so sparrows were not the seeds' only consumer. Each predator still has mice, so neither dies out. Mice eat seeds, not sparrows, so losing sparrows does not remove the mice's food.

**17. (C)** Burning releases the trees' stored carbon as carbon dioxide, and the lost trees no longer remove carbon dioxide by photosynthesis, so the gas rises from both the release and the lost uptake. Oxygen does not rise; burning consumes oxygen, and the claim ignores the carbon released. The chapter's point is about carbon dioxide, not nitrogen. Carbon does not 'disappear' on burning, it becomes carbon dioxide, so the air is not unchanged.

**18. (D)** Being self-sustaining with little outside input means the same nutrients keep cycling: decomposers return them from dead matter to the soil for roots to reuse, the nutrient cycle of the forest. Plants use sunlight for energy, not to create minerals, so they cannot manufacture nutrients. The premise states little is imported, so 'rain imports all of it' contradicts the setup. Animals do not ferry sea minerals in daily; the recycling is internal.

**19. (B)** Losing the fish starves the kingfisher (decline) and leaves phytoplankton ungrazed (may build up), while the single decomposer community keeps recycling dead matter from both chains into one soil, which is exactly what links the two chains. The deer and leopard eat grass-based food, not fish, so they do not collapse. The shared decomposers show the chains are connected, refuting 'entirely separate.' Because the decomposer community is shared, the chains do not have private, isolated decomposers.

**20. (D)** Forest fertility comes from two things working together: decomposers recycling dead matter into humus, and roots plus litter binding the soil so the rich layer is not washed away, both lost when land is ploughed bare. A canopy does not keep out all rain, and dryness does not create fertility. Darkness does not 'create nutrients'; shade alone is not a nutrient source. Trampling is incidental and is not the source of the soil's richness.

**21. (B)** The bats are unharmed, so pollination and fruiting continue; the loss is the beetle's decomposing role, so fallen fruit rots more slowly and nutrient recycling near the tree is delayed. Killing the beetle does not affect the bats, so pollination is not stopped. Whole, unrotted fruit does not feed the roots; nutrients must be released by decomposers first, so growth does not speed up. Decay matters because it returns nutrients, so the tree is not unaffected.

**22. (B)** The services (oxygen, soil, water, climate, habitat) flow from the whole living, layered community, so protecting it and restoring lost forest keeps them running. Removing decomposers breaks the nutrient cycle and starves the soil. Clearing the canopy exposes and harms the lower layers and floor life. Converting to one crop replaces the many-species forest with a single-season monoculture, destroying the very community that provides the services.

**23. (D)** Removing snakes cuts the eagle's food (decline) and releases frogs (rise); more frogs eat more grasshoppers (decline), so grass is grazed less and recovers. Snakes eat frogs, not grasshoppers, so they do not directly protect the grass, and the grass would gain, not vanish. The snake was eagle prey, not a competitor for frogs, so the eagle does not increase. A mid-chain removal clearly ripples both ways, so 'nothing changes' is wrong.

**24. (C)** The soil stays rich only because dead leaves and wood are recycled into humus by decomposers; removing that dead matter cuts off the nutrient return, so the soil is gradually drained. Plants need soil minerals, so sunlight does not free them from the soil. Rotting matter enriches rather than poisons the soil, so removing it does not help. Rain does not deliver a daily mineral supply from the sky, so the soil can indeed lose nutrients.

**25. (D)** The ants shielded aphids from ladybirds; remove the ants and ladybirds prey on aphids freely, so aphids fall and ladybirds (now better fed) give the spiders more prey. The ants protected aphids, they were not the aphids' food, so aphids do not rise from losing a food source. The ants did not graze leaves, so the leaves are not stripped. The ants were not spider food, so the spiders do not starve.

**26. (D)** A forest's defining nature is being a diverse, layered, self-renewing community lasting many years, unlike a monoculture grown and harvested in a single season. Size is not the essential difference; a small patch can be a forest. A forest need not contain a particular large predator to count as one. A forest is full of animals and insects, so 'free of animals' is the reverse of the truth.

**27. (B)** Broad, thin leaves present a large surface to gather the little filtered light at the floor, letting the shade fern make enough food in dim conditions. The floor is shaded and damp, not the harshest dry sun, so water storage is not the point. Plants cannot photosynthesise without light or draw energy from humus, so 'no light at all' is wrong. The canopy blocks most light, so the floor is dim, not flooded with sun to be shed.

**28. (A)** The many services arise because the forest is an interlinked community in which each part supports the others, exactly what the decaying log illustrates. Calling it 'a stock of timber' misses the living interactions. The trees are not unrelated; they are linked through soil, food webs and shared decomposers. A forest renews itself naturally and is not rebuilt by people each year.

**29. (C)** The balance is kept by recycling: decomposers break down dead matter and dung, returning nutrients to the soil to roughly match what plants remove. Plants use sunlight for energy, not to manufacture minerals, so they do not add new nutrients from light. Plants do take nutrients up into their tissue, so 'never leave the soil, only borrowed for a moment' is wrong. Rain does not deliver daily mineral supplies from the sky.

**30. (A)** Losing weevils pushes the thrush onto caterpillars, pressing caterpillar numbers down; the leaves lose one grazer (the weevil) but gain pressure on the other (more caterpillars eaten), a mixed effect. The thrush still eats caterpillars, so it does not starve. Caterpillars do eat leaves, so the leaves are not left to a single grazer and are not stripped bare. The hawk eats thrushes, not weevils, so losing weevils does not directly cut the hawk's food.

**31. (A)** Shade-adapted plants are tuned to the weak filtered light and use it to photosynthesise enough to live, so the dim light matters. It is not harmful and should not be blocked; without it the lower plants could not make food. It does feed the plants, not merely warm animals. Photosynthesis uses light but plants still need water and soil nutrients, so the light does not free them from those needs.

**32. (D)** Forests influence local climate through two real processes: transpiration adds moisture that favours rainfall, and shade plus cover keep the ground cooler, so removing them brings heat and less reliable rain. Trunks do not drag clouds down. Living trees do not burn and seed clouds with smoke. Shade reduces heating but does not absorb every bit of heat so the land 'never warms', which is an overstatement.

**33. (C)** Losing beetles pushes the lizard onto grasshoppers, pressing grasshopper numbers; the lizard still has a food source, so the snake above it is only mildly affected. The snake does not die out, since the lizard persists. Beetles and grasshoppers both eat clover but beetles do not 'hold grasshoppers in check', so the clover is not consumed entirely. The lizard is a carnivore and does not eat clover, so it does not double from extra clover.

**34. (C)** The forest resists erosion because roots hold the soil and litter slows the rain so it infiltrates rather than running off; the bare slope lacks both, so rain sheds fast and carries soil away. Trees do not stop soil 'floating off' by drinking rain. A canopy does not bake soil hard; it shelters it. Cooling the rain is not how forests prevent erosion, and 'only cool rain is gentle' is invented.

**35. (C)** 'Cycle' fits because the same nutrients are returned to the soil and reused repeatedly, whereas the food chain carries energy one way and loses it as heat, so it does not loop. The cycle need not be a perfect equal-sized circle each year, that exactness is invented. The difference is the return path, not a limit on the number of organisms. The loop reuses the same nutrients, it does not destroy them and make new ones, that option reverses the truth.

**36. (D)** All the contrasts (steady stream, mild summers, fertile soil versus floods, dry summers, heat, thin soil) trace to the forest's combined services: water regulation, climate moderation and soil holding, lost together when cleared. These are real, linked forest services, so it is not chance. Climate and soil fertility are linked to forest cover, so it is not 'only flooding.' The cleared hill is plainly worse off, so the villagers did not confuse the outcomes.

**37. (A)** Removing bees works both ways: birds lose prey (decline), snakes lose prey one step later, and the plants lose their pollinator so they set fewer seeds and fruit. Pollination depends on the bees, so 'nothing to do with the bees' is wrong. Plants pollinated less set fewer seeds, not more, so they do not grow faster. Fewer bees means less food up the chain, not 'cleaner flowers and healthier prey', so the snakes do not increase.

**38. (A)** Forest litter and root channels let rain infiltrate slowly, recharging the groundwater that feeds the well; bare land sheds rain fast, so recharge fails and the well dries. Rain is not turned into oxygen, and oxygen does not condense into well water. The forest loosens, not seals, the soil; sealing would prevent infiltration. Sending rain to the sea would empty, not top up, the well, so that option contradicts itself.

**39. (D)** Without the tiger, deer multiply unchecked and overgraze young plants, so the forest cannot renew itself, a clear example of one species' removal rippling widely. Extra dung does not 'instantly' create forest; the overgrazing harm dominates. Forest species are interdependent, so 'nothing changes' is wrong. Deer are herbivores and cannot hunt tigers, so they cannot balance their own numbers that way.

**40. (A)** Light feeds producers, but the floor's abundance of dead matter and damp shelter supports huge numbers of decomposers and ground feeders, so the darkest layer can hold the most life. 'Least light means fewest organisms' assumes all life depends directly on light, which decomposers do not. The floor is not actually the brightest layer; the canopy intercepts the light. Floor organisms do not glow to make their own food; they live on dead matter.

**41. (B)** Killing algae starves the tadpoles (decline), but the fish still has the litter-fed larvae, so the fish and the heron are squeezed, not eliminated. The heron does not die out at once, since the litter chain still supplies the fish. The fish is partly affected (its tadpole supply drops), so 'unaffected in every way' is wrong. Leaf litter comes from fallen leaves, not from algae, so it does not disappear with the algae.

**42. (B)** A forest is a built-up, layered community whose soil and life develop in stages over many years, so it cannot be regrown complete in months. Scattering seeds does not yield a grown forest in one season. A burnt patch can recover slowly, so 'never grows back' is wrong. Regrowth follows succession, not the introduction of a predator, so releasing a top predator does not make it regrow in months.

**43. (C)** Grass is the producer that feeds the zebra, which feeds the lion and hyena, which also scavenge those kills, so destroying the grass cuts the energy base for the whole web and every animal suffers. The carnivores depend on the grass through their prey, so 'only the zebra' is wrong. The lion is not the only one hit; the hyena and zebra suffer too. Animals cannot make their own food, so 'nothing is affected' is impossible.

**44. (C)** All animal energy traces back to producers, so destroying them removes the base that feeds herbivores and, through them, carnivores, so every level suffers. Meat-eaters depend on prey that depend on plants, so 'only plant-eaters' is wrong. Every level, not just the top predator, loses its energy source. Animals cannot make their own food, so 'nothing serious happens' is false.

**45. (B)** Honey, gum, medicinal herbs and timber all come directly from forest plants and animals and are genuine forest products. Plastic and petrol are manufactured or fossil products, not obtained from a living forest, so any group containing them is wrong. Plastic is not a natural forest substance, so the 'sticky or natural-seeming' grouping fails. Petrol is not dug from forest land as a forest product, so that group is also wrong.

**46. (B)** Two key roles are lost together: without the predator, herbivores multiply and overgraze; without decomposers, dead matter accumulates and the soil is no longer fed, so the forest is harmed on both fronts. More herbivores overgrazing and a starved soil is harm, not improvement. Decomposers are essential to the nutrient cycle, so their loss does matter. The two losses both damage the forest, so they do not cancel out.

**47. (D)** Of 100 units, 80 are taken by the canopy, leaving 20; the understorey takes 15 of those, leaving 5; the shrub layer takes 4, leaving 1 for the herb layer. So about 1 unit reaches the floor. '20 units' ignores the understorey and shrub absorption. '50 units' invents an equal split that the figures contradict. '0 units' is wrong because some light does filter through, just very little, which is why shade plants survive.

**48. (C)** Light is captured highest, where leaves and fruit and the animals feeding on them live; dead matter falls and collects on the dim floor, feeding the decomposers there, so the two resources, and the life depending on each, sit in different layers. The ground is the darkest, not the brightest, layer. Dead matter falls down to the floor, it is not carried up to the canopy. Light and dead matter are not spread evenly, so the layers are not identical.

**49. (B)** Removing shrews helps warblers (no egg loss) and caterpillars (one fewer predator), and pushes the owl onto warblers; that is the fullest first picture. The owl also eats warblers, so it does not starve. Shrews ate caterpillars and warbler eggs, not leaves, so the leaves are not stripped. Shrews preyed on caterpillars, so caterpillars do not depend on shrews for survival and do not vanish.

**50. (A)** A forest's layered, varied habitats house and feed a great diversity of species, so protecting the forest protects all of them, not just the trees. A forest contains many kinds of organisms, not one, so that option is false. The variety of life depends on the forest's habitats, not merely on soil minerals. Forest species depend on the forest, so they could not survive equally well on bare land.

**51. (C)** With deer halved, the leopard turns to its other prey (hares and birds), pressing them harder, while the deer's share of grazing eases. The leopard has three prey, so it does not starve. Deer do not control hares (both eat grass), so halving deer does not unleash hares to wipe out the grass; grass is grazed slightly less. Hares and birds eat grass and insects, not deer, so they do not halve from losing the deer.

**52. (A)** Trees take in carbon dioxide and build the carbon into wood, leaves and, via litter, soil humus, which is where the bulk of forest carbon is stored. It is not held loose in the air between trees, that would not be 'stored.' Streams carry little of the forest's carbon compared with the biomass and soil. Animal fur and feathers hold only a tiny fraction, not most of it.

**53. (D)** The interlocking leafy roof of many trees is the canopy; the top of one single tree is its crown, so Meera names the canopy and Vikram the crown. The first option swaps the two terms. **54. (C)** Removing foxes releases the rabbits (their only predator), so rabbits rise, and frees voles from one of two predators, so the owl faces less competition and may take more voles. **55. (A)** A forest is defined by a diverse, layered, self-renewing community over time, so observing that is the strongest evidence. **56. (D)** A mushroom is a fungus that breaks down dead matter into humus, the very definition of a decomposer, so that pairing is correct. A tiger is a carnivore, not a producer; producers are green plants. Grass is a producer, not a carnivore. **57. (B)** A forest influences temperature, moisture and rainfall, soil stability, flood and drought patterns and carbon dioxide together, so losing it changes all of these, not just temperature. Shade is only one of many roles, so 'only temperature' is too narrow. Forests affect water, soil and climate as well as gases, so 'only carbon dioxide' is too narrow. Forests have measurable effects, so 'nothing at all' is wrong.

**58. (D)** Energy falls at each transfer, so the organism furthest from the producer, the top hawk, traces back to the smallest share. **59. (C)** Killing grasshoppers starves the frogs (decline), pushing the snake onto its other prey, the mice; the hawk still has snakes, so it is only mildly affected. The snake also eats mice, so it does not starve. Mice eat grass, not grasshoppers, so losing grasshoppers does not remove the mice's food. **60. (A)** Decomposers are the step that breaks dung and dead bodies into humus, returning nutrients to the soil; cut them and the nutrients stay locked in undecayed matter, so this break most directly stops the return. Grazing animals pass nutrients on but do not make humus, so removing them is not the key break.
