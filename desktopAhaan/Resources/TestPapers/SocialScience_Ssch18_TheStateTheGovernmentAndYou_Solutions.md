## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** A state is a political organisation governing a defined territory and population, with four parts. It is not just buildings, the current government, or one leader.

**2. (D)** A state's four parts are population, territory, government and sovereignty. The others list organs, tiers, or government roles, not the parts of a state.

**3. (B)** The government is just one of the state's four parts. It is not the whole state, the population, or the territory.

**4. (D)** The government is one changing part; the state endures across elections - like teachers changing while the school stays. The reversal and 'state is only police' options are wrong.

**5. (D)** The lasting school is the state; the changing teachers are the government. The other pairings reverse or confuse the analogy.

**6. (A)** A government makes, enforces and interprets laws. It does not own all land, dictate religion, or rewrite the Constitution yearly.

**7. (C)** Governments change after elections, while the state endures. A government does run the country and is not the territory.

**8. (D)** An election changes the government while the state continues. The reversal and the 'both vanish / neither changes' options are wrong.

**9. (D)** The government, through its legislature, executive and judiciary, makes, enforces and interprets laws. The population, territory and sovereignty are other parts of the state.

**10. (B)** Besides the government, the state includes its population, territory and sovereignty. It is not only ministers, only courts, or just the government.

**11. (B)** Sovereignty is a state's full power to make and enforce its own laws free of outside control. It is not a minister's whim, the vote, or the territory.

**12. (A)** A sovereign state makes and enforces its own laws without outside control. It is not foreign-controlled, lawless, or unable to enforce law.

**13. (C)** The police and army are part of the state's machinery directed by the government, not the government itself. They are not the whole government, the population, or foreign.

**14. (C)** The police and armed forces continue serving the state across changes of government. They are not all dismissed, made the government, or ended.

**15. (A)** The police and army are permanent state machinery, unlike the government elected for a fixed term. They are not elected, party-changing, or lawmakers.

**16. (B)** India is a democratic republic. It is not a monarchy, theocracy, or dictatorship.

**17. (D)** Democracy is rule by the people through free and fair elections. King-rule, religious rule and unelected-officer rule are other forms.

**18. (C)** In a republic the head of state is elected, not hereditary, with powers limited by a constitution. Inheritance, no constitution, and no voting describe other systems.

**19. (B)** The UK, Canada and Sweden are democracies with monarchs, so not republics - a democracy need not be a republic. The other options are false.

**20. (A)** The UK is a democracy (elected parliament) but not a republic (it has a hereditary monarch). The other options misclassify it.

**21. (A)** Democracy is about the people ruling; a republic is about an elected (non-hereditary) head of state. The first option captures this; the others reverse or confuse it.

**22. (B)** Guaranteed rights mean the majority cannot trample minorities - the rule of law over mere numbers. It is not majority-can-do-anything, majority-only, or government-only rights.

**23. (A)** The rule of law and constitutional rights protect minorities, so the majority cannot do anything it likes. The majority does have power, does not lose to minorities, and rights do exist.

**24. (C)** Guaranteed rights make the rule of law stronger than the majority's wishes. The majority does not always override the law, minorities are protected, and the Constitution is not ignored.

**25. (A)** The rule of law means everyone, even the government, must follow the law. It is not government-above-law, citizens-only, or minority-only.

**26. (C)** Matsya nyaya ('law of the fishes') means that without law, the strong devour the weak - why we need a government. The other options are wrong.

**27. (D)** Government and the rule of law maintain order, preventing matsya nyaya where the strong prey on the weak. It is not unlimited strong-rule, removing laws, or rule-free life.

**28. (C)** Without law and order, society slides into matsya nyaya, the strong preying on the weak. It would not yield automatic equality, the rule of law, or a working constitution.

**29. (A)** The rule of law binds even the powerful, protecting the weak - the opposite of matsya nyaya. It does not free the powerful, target only the weak, or abolish government.

**30. (B)** The legislature makes laws (Latin lex 'law' + latio 'proposing'). Enforcing is the executive, interpreting is the judiciary, and commanding the army is not its defining role.

**31. (C)** Legislature is from lex/legis ('law') + latio ('proposing'). 'To carry out' gives executive, 'judge' gives judiciary, and 'treaty' gives federalism.

**32. (D)** Passing laws is the legislature's work. The executive enforces, the judiciary interprets, and the bureaucracy is the permanent executive.

**33. (D)** The executive carries out and enforces laws (Latin exsequi, 'to carry out'). Making laws is the legislature and interpreting them the judiciary.

**34. (C)** The executive's two parts are the political executive (ministers) and the permanent executive (bureaucracy). The other terms are invented.

**35. (C)** The political executive - ministers - sets direction and changes with elections. Staying on regardless and exam-selection describe the permanent executive.

**36. (B)** The permanent executive - civil servants - stays on and implements decisions across governments, giving stability. It does not change each election, make laws, or serve as head of state.

**37. (A)** The day-to-day work is largely done by the permanent bureaucracy, not ministers alone. There is a bureaucracy, and the courts do not run daily administration.

**38. (B)** The bureaucracy is the permanent, exam-selected civil service. Elected ministers are the political executive, judges the judiciary, and voters the electorate.

**39. (D)** The judiciary interprets laws and settles disputes (Latin judex, 'judge'). Making laws is the legislature, enforcing the executive.

**40. (C)** As an independent watchdog, the judiciary checks laws and actions against the Constitution. It does not obey the ruling party, make laws, or command the police.

**41. (D)** Judicial review lets courts strike down laws and actions that breach the Constitution. It is not lawmaking, electing ministers, or tax collection.

**42. (A)** Through judicial review the judiciary can strike down an unconstitutional law - a key check. It is not powerless, a lawmaker, or able to arrest voters.

**43. (D)** An independent judiciary can check even the government fairly, upholding the Constitution. It is not party-controlled, minister-obeying, or check-removing.

**44. (C)** The government's triple role is protector, provider and regulator. The others list organs, tiers, or parts of a state.

**45. (D)** As protector the government keeps citizens safe and defends the country. Providing services is the provider role and setting rules the regulator role.

**46. (B)** As provider the government supplies services such as schools, hospitals and roads. Defence is the protector role and rule-setting the regulator role.

**47. (B)** As regulator (Latin regula, 'rule') the government sets and enforces fair rules. Fighting wars is protection and building hospitals is provision.

**48. (A)** Setting and enforcing safety rules is the regulator role. Border defence is protection, road-building is provision, and voting is a citizen's act.

**49. (A)** RTI lets citizens request information about government actions, increasing transparency and accountability. It is not double-voting, law-ignoring, or judge-appointing.

**50. (B)** Citizens hold government accountable through grievance redressal, RTI, the media, civil society and voting. Ignoring elections, one-minister rule and abolishing the Constitution do the opposite.

**51. (B)** Voting out a government is a key way citizens hold it accountable. It is not foreign sovereignty, the regulator role, or judicial review.

**52. (B)** A free media exposes failures and keeps the government accountable. It does not make laws, replace courts, or run the bureaucracy.

**53. (D)** Grievance redressal lets citizens raise and resolve complaints about services - a form of accountability. It is not foreign voting, appointing ministers, or striking down laws.

**54. (C)** The three tiers are union, state and local. The others list the organs, the triple role, or the parts of a state.

**55. (D)** Decentralisation spreads power so local matters are decided locally. It is not concentration, abolishing local bodies, or foreign control.

**56. (A)** Three tiers reflect decentralisation, with decisions at the appropriate level. It is not central concentration, one-person rule, or abolishing states.

**57. (A)** Federalism (Latin foedus, 'treaty') shares power between central and state/local governments. It is not all-central, no-centre, or king-rule.

**58. (C)** Letting a local body decide local roads is decentralisation. It is the opposite of centralising, abolishing the local tier, or foreign control.

**59. (C)** The state has four parts and endures; the government is one part that changes with elections. The other options reverse or conflate them.

**60. (A)** The government is one part of the state, the rule of law and rights shield minorities, and democracies like the UK are not republics - correcting all three errors. The other options keep one or more misconceptions.
