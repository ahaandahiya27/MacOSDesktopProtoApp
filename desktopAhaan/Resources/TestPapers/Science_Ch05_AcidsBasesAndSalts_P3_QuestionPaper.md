**Marking scheme:** **+4** for each correct answer · **−1** for each wrong answer · **0** if unattempted.

**Instructions:** Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. Paper 3 (first hard tier). Traps: confusing the colour an indicator shows with the family it names; assuming "no litmus change" proves a substance is water; treating neutralisation as making a mixture acidic or basic rather than neutral; mixing up which member of a neutralisation pair to add to a sting, soil or waste; reading a single indicator result as conclusive when a second indicator contradicts it; thinking strength of taste equals chemical family. Reason in 2-4 steps; no recall gimmes. Do not write on this paper — mark answers on your answer sheet.

1. A juice tastes sharply sour. Before any test is done, the most reliable prediction you can make about how it will behave with litmus is that it will:
   (A) turn red litmus blue, because sour things are bases
   (B) leave both litmus papers unchanged, because taste tells nothing about litmus
   (C) turn blue litmus red, because sourness is a property of acids
   (D) turn turmeric paper red, because sour things make turmeric red

2. Two unlabelled bottles are tested. Bottle 1 turns red litmus blue; bottle 2 turns blue litmus red. If you accidentally mix roughly equal amounts of the two, the litmus result of the mixture is most likely:
   (A) no change in either litmus, because an acid and a base tend to neutralise each other
   (B) turns blue litmus red, because the acid is always stronger
   (C) turns red litmus blue, because the base is always stronger
   (D) turns both litmus papers green, because mixing produces a new colour

3. A student says: "Milk of magnesia must be an acid because people take it for a stomach problem." The correct correction is:
   (A) it is a base; it is taken precisely to neutralise the excess acid causing acidity
   (B) it is an acid; that is why it cures the acidic stomach
   (C) it is neutral, so it simply dilutes the stomach acid
   (D) it is a salt that washes the acid away without reacting

4. Phenolphthalein is added to four colourless liquids: lemon juice, distilled water, sugar water, and lime water. The number of these in which it turns pink is:
   (A) 2
   (B) 3
   (C) 0
   (D) 1

5. A drop of china rose (hibiscus) indicator is added to vinegar. Knowing china rose turns dark pink (magenta) in acids and green in bases, the colour you should expect is:
   (A) green, because vinegar is acidic
   (B) dark pink (magenta), because vinegar is acidic
   (C) green, because vinegar is basic
   (D) colourless, because vinegar is neutral

6. When dilute hydrochloric acid is exactly neutralised by sodium hydroxide, then the water is evaporated away, what is left behind is:
   (A) sodium hydroxide left over from the base
   (B) hydrochloric acid that did not react
   (C) common salt (sodium chloride)
   (D) a fresh acid stronger than the first

7. An ant's sting causes a burning pain because it injects formic acid. A friend suggests rubbing lemon juice on it. The best judgement is:
   (A) yes, lemon juice is a base and will neutralise the acid sting
   (B) yes, adding more acid cancels the first acid
   (C) no, only water can ever relieve a sting

8. A liquid leaves both red and blue litmus unchanged. A classmate concludes "it must be pure water." The most accurate response is:
   (A) not necessarily; any neutral substance, such as salt water or sugar water, also leaves litmus unchanged
   (B) correct, only pure water leaves both litmus papers unchanged
   (C) wrong, because water actually turns blue litmus red
   (D) wrong, because all neutral liquids turn red litmus blue

9. A factory's waste water turns blue litmus red and must be treated before it enters a river. The correct treatment is to add:
   (A) more acid, to balance the waste

17. Baking soda and vinegar are mixed inside a sealed sandwich bag, which quickly swells tight and could burst. The swelling is best explained by:
