Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

The 11th and 12th centuries are called a 'turning point' because:

  • AIndia was completely peaceful with no invasions
  • BTurkic invasions penetrated far deeper into India than the earlier Arab raids✓
  • Cthe Guptas rose to power again
  • Dthe Arabs first reached India

Working

✓

The period saw Turkic invasions reaching much deeper than the earlier Arab raids, a major turning point. India was not peaceful, the Guptas had long gone, and the Arabs had arrived centuries earlier.

Q2

Compared with the earlier Arab raids, the Turkic invasions of this period:

  • Areached much further into the subcontinent✓
  • Bnever crossed into India
  • Cstayed only in Sindh like the Arabs
  • Dwere purely peaceful trade missions

Working

✓

Turkic invasions penetrated far deeper than the Arab raids that had been confined near Sindh. They did cross into India, went beyond Sindh, and were not peaceful trade.

Q3

Despite the invasions, the chapter stresses that this period's lesson is one of:

  • Atotal and permanent defeat
  • Bresilience and the enduring power of knowledge✓
  • Cthe end of all Indian culture
  • Dcomplete foreign rule over all India

Working

✓

The period's lesson is resilience and the lasting power of knowledge, with scholars, saints and temples flourishing. It was not total defeat, the end of culture, or all-India foreign rule.

Q4

The 'Khyber Pass' was important because it:

  • Awas a sea route to Sri Lanka
  • Blay deep in the Tamil south
  • Cwas a Chola naval base
  • Dwas a major pass through the mountains, long used as a trade and invasion route into India✓

Working

✓

The Khyber Pass through the Hindu Kush served as both trade and invasion route into India. It was not a sea route, southern, or a Chola base.

Q5

The Hindu Shahis are remembered in this period as a power that:

  • Aruled the Tamil far south
  • Bbuilt Angkor Wat in Cambodia
  • Cguarded the north-western approaches against the early Turkic invaders✓
  • Dfounded the Delhi Sultanate

Working

✓

The Hindu Shahis defended the north-western frontier against early Turkic invaders. They did not rule the south, build Angkor Wat, or found the Sultanate.

Q6

Invaders repeatedly entering India through the north-west shows the region was:

  • Acompletely sealed off by the sea
  • BIndia's safest frontier
  • Ca southern coastal port
  • Da recurring gateway for invasions, like the Khyber Pass✓

Working

✓

The north-west, with passes like the Khyber, was again a gateway for invaders. It was not sea-sealed, the safest frontier, or a southern port.

Q7

Mahmud of Ghazni is remembered for leading about:

  • Aa single peaceful trade mission
  • Bthe building of Nalanda
  • C17 plundering raids into India✓
  • Dthe founding of the Chola navy

Working

✓

Mahmud of Ghazni led about 17 plundering raids. He did not make a peaceful trade mission, build Nalanda, or found the Chola navy.

Q8

A famous temple destroyed during Mahmud of Ghazni's raids was:

  • AAngkor Wat
  • BBrihadishvara
  • CSomnath✓
  • Dthe Anubhava mandapa

Working

✓

Mahmud's raids destroyed the Somnath temple. Angkor Wat (Cambodia), Brihadishvara (Chola) and the Anubhava mandapa (Basava's hall) were not destroyed by him.

Q9

The claim 'Mahmud of Ghazni conquered and ruled India' is wrong because he:

  • Aonly raided India for plunder and withdrew each time✓
  • Bfounded the Delhi Sultanate and ruled for life
  • Cnever actually reached India
  • Druled all of south India

Working

✓

Mahmud raided about 17 times for plunder and withdrew each time; he did not rule India. The Sultanate came later under others, he did reach India, and he did not rule the south.

Q10

'Booty' or 'plunder', which Mahmud's raids carried off, means:

  • Aa peace treaty between kings
  • Ba school of philosophy
  • Ca kind of temple tower
  • Dvaluable goods seized by force during a raid or war✓

Working

✓

Booty or plunder is goods seized by force in war. It is not a treaty, a philosophy, or a temple tower (a vimana).

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

Mahmud's repeated raids, followed each time by withdrawal, are best described as:

  • Abuilding a lasting empire in India
  • Braiding for treasure rather than conquering to govern✓
  • Cpeaceful pilgrimages
  • Dnaval expeditions overseas

Working

✓

Mahmud raided for treasure and returned home, not building a lasting empire. It was not empire-building, pilgrimage, or naval expedition.

Q12

The key difference between Mahmud of Ghazni and the later Ghurids was that Mahmud:

  • Acame to rule, while the Ghurids only raided
  • Braided for loot and left, while the Ghurids came to stay and rule✓
  • Cbuilt temples, while the Ghurids destroyed them by accident
  • Dnever reached India, while the Ghurids did

Working

✓

Mahmud raided and withdrew; the Ghurids came to stay and govern, founding lasting rule. The reversal, the temple claim and the 'never reached India' option are wrong.

Q13

'Raiding for loot' and 'conquering to govern' are different because:

  • Athey mean exactly the same thing
  • Ba raider always builds an empire
  • Ca raider seizes treasure and leaves, while a conqueror stays to rule the land✓
  • Da conqueror never keeps the land

Working

✓

A raider takes loot and leaves; a conqueror stays to rule - very different. They are not the same, raiders need not build empires, and conquerors do keep land.

Q14

Lasting foreign rule in north India began with:

  • AMahmud of Ghazni's first raid
  • Bthe Chola navy
  • Cthe Ghurids and their generals, not Mahmud of Ghazni✓
  • Dthe scholar al-Biruni

Working

✓

Lasting foreign rule began with the Ghurids and generals like Aibak, not Mahmud's raids. The Chola navy and al-Biruni were not foreign rulers of north India.

Q15

Al-Biruni is best described as a:

  • Ascholar who learned Sanskrit and studied India deeply✓
  • BTurkic general who destroyed Nalanda
  • CChola admiral
  • DTamil poet-saint

Working

✓

Al-Biruni was a scholar who learned Sanskrit and studied India's sciences, religion and customs. He was not a general, an admiral, or a poet-saint.

Q16

The claim 'foreign scholars like al-Biruni came only with the armies and learned nothing from India' is wrong because al-Biruni:

  • Arefused to learn Sanskrit
  • Bdestroyed all Indian books
  • Cnever wrote anything
  • Dstudied India's mathematics, astronomy, religion and customs and wrote a careful book about the country✓

Working

✓

Al-Biruni learned Sanskrit and wrote a careful study of India, showing deep two-way learning. He did not refuse Sanskrit, destroy books, or write nothing.

Q17

The chapter calls this 'an age of two-way learning' because:

  • Aonly India learned, never sharing anything
  • Bno learning happened at all
  • Clearning flowed only into India
  • Dforeign scholars studied India while Indian science (like its sine tables) also travelled outward✓

Working

✓

Knowledge flowed both ways: scholars studied India, and Indian science such as sine tables spread outward. It was not one-way or absent.

Q18

That al-Biruni wrote a careful book about India shows that, even amid invasions:

  • Aall learning had stopped
  • BIndians knew nothing worth studying
  • Cbooks were never written
  • Dserious scholarship and curiosity about India continued✓

Working

✓

Al-Biruni's careful study shows scholarship continued amid the storms. Learning had not stopped, India had much worth studying, and books were written.

Q19

Indian 'sine tables' travelling outward to shape mathematics elsewhere is an example of:

  • AIndia only ever borrowing from others
  • Bmathematics being unknown in India
  • CIndia contributing knowledge to the wider world✓
  • Dlearning that never left India

Working

✓

Indian sine tables spreading abroad show India contributing knowledge to the world. It is not pure borrowing, mathematical ignorance, or knowledge that stayed put.

Q20

Bhaskaracharya is remembered as a great Indian:

  • ATurkic invader
  • BChola admiral
  • Ctemple-builder only
  • Dmathematician and astronomer✓

Working

✓

Bhaskaracharya was a great mathematician and astronomer, showing the endurance of Indian science. He was not an invader, an admiral, or merely a builder.

The survival of scholars like Bhaskaracharya during this period shows that Indian science:

  • Acompletely disappeared
  • Bendured and kept advancing despite the invasions✓
  • Cwas invented only by foreigners
  • Dnever existed at all

Working

✓

Q22

'Algebra' takes its name from the Arabic 'al-jabr', meaning:

  • A'the cosmic mountain'
  • B'a fighting fleet of ships'
  • C'reunion of broken parts', the title of al-Khwarizmi's book✓
  • D'a short Kannada poem'

Working

✓

Algebra is from Arabic al-jabr ('reunion of broken parts'), al-Khwarizmi's book title. The cosmic mountain is Mount Meru, a fleet is a navy, and a short Kannada poem is a vachana.

Q23

'Trigonometry', vital to astronomy, is the mathematics of:

  • Atemple towers
  • Btriangles and angles✓
  • Cfighting ships
  • DKannada poetry

Working

✓

Trigonometry (Greek trigonon 'triangle' + metron 'measure') is the maths of triangles and angles. Temple towers are vimanas, ships make a navy, and Kannada poems are vachanas.

Q24

The Kakatiyas, who flourished in the Deccan, are linked to the decorative stone gateway called a:

  • Avimana over a shrine
  • Btorana, as built at Warangal✓
  • Csabha assembly
  • Dsamanta vassal

Working

✓

The Kakatiyas built the torana (decorative gateway) at Warangal. A vimana is a temple tower, a sabha a village assembly, and a samanta a subordinate ruler.

Q25

The Hoysalas, like the Kakatiyas, were a power of the:

  • Afar north-west, founding the Sultanate
  • BCentral Asian steppe
  • CDeccan and the south, which flourished during this period✓
  • DArab lands of Sindh

Working

✓

The Hoysalas were a flourishing southern and Deccan power. They were not north-western Sultanate founders, steppe nomads, or Arabs.

Q26

That the Kakatiyas and Hoysalas built great works in the south shows that, while the north faced invasions:

  • Aall of India had fallen to invaders
  • Bthe south remained free and flourishing✓
  • Cthe south was empty of kingdoms
  • Dthe south was ruled from Ghazni

Working

✓

The thriving Kakatiyas and Hoysalas show the south stayed free and flourishing while the north faced invasions. India had not all fallen, the south had kingdoms, and it was not Ghazni-ruled.

Q27

Rajaraja Chola I is remembered as a:

  • ATurkic raider of Somnath
  • Bmaster of the seas who built Chola naval power✓
  • CGhurid general
  • DKannada poet-saint

✓

Rajaraja Chola I built powerful Chola sea power. He was not a Turkic raider, a Ghurid general, or a poet-saint.

Q28

A 'navy', which the Cholas built powerfully, is:

  • Aa decorative stone gateway
  • Ba hall for exchanging ideas
  • Ca cosmic mountain
  • Da country's fighting ships and the sailors who crew them✓

Working

✓

A navy (Latin navis, 'ship') is a country's fighting ships and crews. A gateway is a torana, an ideas-hall the Anubhava mandapa, and the cosmic mountain Mount Meru.

Q29

The great Chola temple linked to Rajaraja I, with a soaring tower (vimana), is the:

  • ASomnath temple
  • BNalanda university
  • CBrihadishvara temple✓
  • DAngkor Wat

Working

✓

Rajaraja I built the Brihadishvara temple with its towering vimana. Somnath was raided by Mahmud, Nalanda was a university, and Angkor Wat is in Cambodia.

Q30

Rajendra Chola I is famous for a naval expedition to:

  • Athe Khyber Pass
  • BGhazni
  • CSrivijaya, in Southeast Asia✓
  • DKannauj

Working

✓

Rajendra Chola I led a naval expedition to Srivijaya in Southeast Asia. The Khyber Pass, Ghazni and Kannauj are northern or overland, not naval targets.

  • BPrithviraj Chauhan

Q34

Calling Bhoja a 'scholar-king' means he:

  • Cwas a foreign invader
  • Dled the Chola navy

Q35

  • BMahmud of Ghazni
  • CRajaraja Chola I
  • Dal-Biruni

Working

✓

The Ghurids were led by Muhammad Ghuri. Mahmud of Ghazni led the earlier Ghaznavid raids; Rajaraja was a Chola and al-Biruni a scholar.

Q36

At the First Battle of Tarain (1191), Muhammad Ghuri:

  • Adefeated Prithviraj Chauhan
  • Bwas defeated by Prithviraj Chauhan✓
  • Cconquered all of India
  • Ddestroyed Nalanda

Working

✓

Ghuri lost the First Battle of Tarain (1191) to Prithviraj Chauhan. He won the Second (1192); he did not conquer all India at Tarain, and Nalanda was destroyed by Bakhtiyar Khilji.

Q37

At the Second Battle of Tarain (1192), Muhammad Ghuri:

  • Bwas again defeated and went home for good
  • Cwas helped by the Cholas
  • Ddestroyed Somnath

Working

✓

Ghuri won the Second Battle of Tarain (1192), defeating Prithviraj Chauhan and opening the way to lasting rule. He was not defeated, Chola-helped, or the destroyer of Somnath (that was Mahmud).

Q38

The Indian king defeated at the Second Battle of Tarain was:

  • APrithviraj Chauhan✓
  • BRajaraja Chola I
  • CBhoja
  • DHarshavardhana

Working

✓

Prithviraj Chauhan was defeated at the Second Battle of Tarain (1192). Rajaraja Chola, Bhoja and Harshavardhana were other rulers.

Q39

Which sequence of the Tarain battles is correct?

  • AGhuri won the First (1191), then lost the Second (1192)
  • Bboth battles were won by Prithviraj
  • CGhuri lost the First Battle (1191), then won the Second (1192)✓
  • Dboth battles were fought at sea

✓

Ghuri lost the First Battle of Tarain (1191) and won the Second (1192). The reversal, the 'Prithviraj won both' and the 'at sea' options are wrong.

Q40

The shift 'from plunder to rule' in this period means invaders moved from:

  • Araiding for treasure to staying and governing the land✓
  • Bgoverning to merely raiding
  • Ctrading peacefully to fighting
  • Druling the south to ruling Ghazni

✓

'From plunder to rule' marks the shift from raiding for treasure (Mahmud) to staying to govern (the Ghurids). It is not the reverse, a peace-to-war shift, or about Ghazni.

Bakhtiyar Khilji destroyed Nalanda and Vikramashila. He did not found the Chola navy, write the Rajatarangini, or build Brihadishvara.

  • AChola naval bases
  • BTurkic forts
  • Cgreat centres of learning and Buddhist universities✓
  • DTamil trading ports

✓

Nalanda and Vikramashila were great Buddhist universities, so their destruction was a heavy loss to learning. They were not naval bases, forts, or trading ports.

Basavanna founded the Lingayat movement, rejecting caste and ritual and teaching devotion, honest work and equality. It was not a sultanate, a navy, or the Tripartite Struggle.

Q47
