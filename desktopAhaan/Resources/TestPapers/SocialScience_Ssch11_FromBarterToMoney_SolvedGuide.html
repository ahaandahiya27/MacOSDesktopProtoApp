Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

The 'barter system' is:

  • Abuying goods using coins and notes
  • Bpaying for goods with a debit card
  • Cstoring value in a bank account
  • Dexchanging goods or services for other goods or services, with no money involved✓

Working

✓

Barter is the direct swap of goods or services for other goods or services, with no money. Using coins, cards or bank accounts all involve money, so none is barter.

Q2

Which everyday example is true barter?

  • Atrading a pencil directly for an eraser✓
  • Bbuying a pencil for 5 rupees
  • Cpaying for an eraser by UPI
  • Dsaving 5 rupees to buy a pencil later

Working

✓

Swapping a pencil for an eraser, with no money, is barter. Paying rupees, using UPI, or saving money all involve money, so they are not barter.

Q3

The idea that 'any time you buy something it is barter' is wrong because barter:

  • Aspecifically means exchanging goods for other goods, with NO money used✓
  • Balways involves money changing hands
  • Conly ever happens in modern shops
  • Dis the same thing as net banking

Working

✓

Barter means no money is used - goods for goods. The moment money is the medium it is no longer barter, so shopping with money, modern shops and net banking are not barter.

Q4

Before coins existed, which of these served as 'money' in barter-based societies?

  • Adebit cards and QR codes
  • Bcowrie shells, salt, cattle and cloth✓
  • Cprinted bank notes
  • DUPI transfers

Working

✓

Commodities like cowrie shells, salt, cattle and cloth acted as money before coins. Cards, notes and UPI are far later inventions.

Q5

The chapter notes that even giant stone discs were once used as:

  • Awriting tablets for laws
  • Bwheels for carts
  • Cweapons in war
  • Da form of money✓

Working

✓

Giant stone discs served as money in one society, showing how varied early 'money' was. They were not law tablets, cart wheels, or weapons.

Q6

'Commodities' are:

  • Aproducts or goods that can be traded, bought and sold✓
  • Bonly coins made of gold
  • Conly digital payments
  • Dthe study of old coins

Working

✓

Commodities are tradable goods. Gold coins are one kind of money, digital payments are a transfer method, and the study of coins is numismatics.

Q7

The Junbeel Mela is mentioned to show that:

  • Abarter has completely disappeared everywhere
  • Bbarter still survives today in some places, even in the age of money✓
  • Cmoney was never really invented
  • Donly digital money is used in India

Working

✓

The Junbeel Mela shows barter surviving in pockets even now. It does not prove barter is gone, that money was never invented, or that only digital money exists.

Q8

The 'double coincidence of wants' problem in barter means:

  • Atwo coins are needed for every purchase
  • Bboth traders must be related to each other
  • Ca trade works only if each person has exactly what the other wants✓
  • Dgoods must be counted twice over

Working

✓

Barter needs a double coincidence of wants - each must have what the other wants and want what the other has. It is not about two coins, kinship, or double-counting.

Q9

A farmer with wheat wants shoes, but the shoemaker does not want wheat. In barter, this is a problem of:

  • Ainflation
  • Bno double coincidence of wants✓
  • Cportability
  • Dlegal tender

Working

✓

The trade fails because there is no double coincidence of wants - the shoemaker doesn't want wheat. Inflation, portability and legal tender are different issues.

Q10

Money solves the double-coincidence problem because:

  • Amoney makes goods divide themselves
  • Bmoney forces people to want wheat
  • Cmoney removes the need to trade at all
  • Deveryone accepts money, so you need not find someone who wants your exact goods✓

Working

✓

Since everyone accepts money, you can sell to anyone and buy from anyone without matching exact wants. It does not magically divide goods, force wants, or end trade.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

'No common standard measure of value' in barter means it was hard to:

  • Acarry goods from place to place
  • Bkeep goods from rotting
  • Csplit a good into parts
  • Dagree how much one good was worth in terms of another✓

Working

✓

Without a common measure, comparing the worth of an ox and bags of wheat was hard. Carrying, spoiling and splitting are the separate portability, durability and divisibility problems.

Q12

A 'common standard measure of value' is useful because it:

  • Agives an agreed worth so the value of different goods can be compared✓
  • Bmakes all goods weigh the same
  • Cforces everyone to want the same thing
  • Dturns goods into shells

Working

✓

A common measure lets people compare values on one agreed scale. It does not equalise weights, force shared wants, or change goods into shells.

Q13

'Divisibility' of money means it can be:

  • Asplit into smaller parts for small purchases✓
  • Bcarried easily in a pocket
  • Cstored for many years
  • Ddeclared legal tender

Working

✓

Divisibility is being splittable into parts. Easy carrying is portability, lasting is durability, and being legally accepted is legal tender.

Q14

'Portability' of money means it is:

  • Aable to last a long time
  • Beasy to carry from place to place✓
  • Csplittable into halves
  • Dbacked by the government

Working

✓

Portability is being easy to carry. Lasting is durability, splitting is divisibility, and government backing is fiat or legal tender.

Q15

'Durability' of money means it:

  • Alasts a long time and can be stored✓
  • Bcan be split into tiny parts
  • Cis easy to carry
  • Dis always made of gold

Working

✓

Durability is lasting and being storable. Splitting is divisibility, easy carrying is portability, and money need not be gold.

Q16

A live ox is a poor 'money' mainly because it is:

  • Atoo easy to split into change
  • Bhard to divide, carry and store✓
  • Ctoo light to be valuable
  • Daccepted everywhere as legal tender

Working

✓

An ox can't be split for small purchases, is hard to carry, and may sicken or die - failing divisibility, portability and durability. It is not easy to split, weightless, or legal tender.

Q17

'Money' is best defined as:

  • Aany object that is made of metal
  • Bonly notes printed on paper
  • Cthe common tool everyone accepts to make or receive payments for goods and services✓
  • Dgoods swapped directly for other goods

Working

✓

Money is the commonly accepted tool for payments. It need not be metal, is not only paper notes, and direct goods-for-goods swapping is barter, not money.

Q18

Money came into existence mainly to:

  • Amake barter work better without changing it
  • Breplace all trade with gifts
  • Cend the need for any goods
  • Dsolve the problems of the barter system✓

Working

✓

Money was invented to overcome barter's problems (double coincidence, no common measure, poor divisibility, portability and durability). It replaced barter rather than merely improving it, and did not end trade or goods.

Q19

The claim 'money has always existed' is wrong because:

  • Abarter came first, and money was invented later to solve barter's problems✓
  • Bmoney is older than barter
  • Ccoins existed before people did
  • Dmoney and barter began on the very same day

Working

✓

Barter, swapping goods for goods, came first; money was invented afterwards. So money is not older than barter, did not predate people, and did not begin alongside barter.

Q20

Using money to buy bread is money acting as a:

  • Astore of value
  • Bmedium of exchange✓
  • Cstandard of deferred payment
  • Dcommon measure of value only

Working

✓

Spending money to buy goods is its medium-of-exchange function. Saving it is store of value, paying later is deferred payment, and pricing goods is the measure function.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

Saving money to use next year is money acting as a:

  • Amedium of exchange
  • Bcommon measure of value
  • Cstore of value✓
  • Ddouble coincidence of wants

Working

✓

Keeping money for future use is the store-of-value function. Spending it is medium of exchange, pricing is measure of value, and double coincidence is a barter problem.

Q22

Pricing a bicycle at 5,000 rupees so its worth can be compared with other goods uses money as a:

  • Astore of value
  • Bstandard of deferred payment
  • Cmedium of exchange
  • Dcommon measure of value✓

Working

✓

Expressing worth in rupees so goods can be compared is the measure-of-value function. Saving is store of value, paying later is deferred payment, and buying is medium of exchange.

Q23

Agreeing to repay a loan of 1,000 rupees next month uses money as a:

  • Amedium of exchange
  • Bcommon measure of value
  • Cstore of value
  • Dstandard of deferred payment✓

Working

✓

Settling future debts in money is the standard-of-deferred-payment function. Buying now is medium of exchange, pricing is measure of value, and saving is store of value.

Q24

Which list correctly names the four functions of money?

  • Abarter, commodity, coin, note
  • Bcowrie, salt, cattle, cloth
  • Cmedium of exchange, store of value, measure of value, standard of deferred payment✓
  • Ddivisibility, portability, durability, double coincidence

Working

✓

Money's four functions are medium of exchange, store of value, measure of value and standard of deferred payment. The other lists name forms of money, commodities, or barter problems.

Q25

Money is more useful than an ox as a 'store of value' chiefly because money:

  • Adoes not sicken or rot and can be kept and divided easily✓
  • Bcan be eaten in hard times
  • Cgrows larger over the years
  • Dcan pull a plough

Working

✓

Unlike a living ox, money does not die or rot and is easy to store and split, making it a better store of value. It cannot be eaten, does not grow, and cannot plough.

Q26

The ancient Indian coins called 'karshapanas' carried small stamped marks known as:

  • AQR codes
  • Bbank logos
  • Crupa symbols✓
  • Dpaper watermarks

Working

✓

Karshapanas bore stamped 'rupa' symbols. QR codes, bank logos and watermarks are features of much later money.

Q27

The English word 'rupee' comes from the Sanskrit 'rupya', meaning:

  • A'paper note'
  • B'wrought (stamped) silver'✓
  • C'cowrie shell'
  • D'quick response'

Working

✓

Rupee derives from rupya, 'wrought (stamped) silver'. It is not from paper, shells, or 'quick response' (which is QR).

Q28

The very first coins in India were made of:

  • Aprinted paper
  • Bplastic cards
  • Ccoloured shells only
  • Dmetal, such as silver, stamped with symbols✓

Working

✓

Early Indian coins were stamped metal like silver. Paper, plastic cards and shells alone came at other points in the story.

Q29

'Minting' refers to:

  • Aspending money quickly
  • Bthe process of producing coins✓
  • Cthe rise in prices over time
  • Dcarrying coins in a pocket

Working

✓

Minting is making coins (a mint is the facility). Spending is not minting, rising prices are inflation, and carrying is portability.

Q30

Coins are often made of an 'alloy' because an alloy:

  • Acombines metals to make the coin strong and durable✓
  • Bmakes the coin worth its weight in gold
  • Clets the coin be eaten
  • Dturns the coin into paper

Working

✓

An alloy mixes metals to give coins strength and durability. It does not guarantee gold value, make coins edible, or turn them to paper.

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

The two faces of a coin are called the:

  • Aobverse and reverse✓
  • Bmedium and store
  • Cbarter and money
  • Drupa and karshapana

Working

✓

A coin's faces are the obverse ('heads') and reverse ('tails'). The other pairs name money functions, exchange types, or ancient coin terms.

Q32

On a coin, the 'obverse' is the:

  • Aback side, the 'tails'
  • Bedge of the coin
  • Chole in the centre
  • Dmain side, the 'heads'✓

Working

✓

The obverse is the main 'heads' side; the reverse is the 'tails' back. The edge and any central hole are not the obverse.

Q33

'Numismatics' is the:

  • Aprocess of printing notes
  • Bsystem of barter exchange
  • Cstudy of coins, paper money and the history they reveal✓
  • Drise of prices over time

Working

✓

Numismatics (Greek nomisma, 'coin') is the study of coins and currency and their history. Printing notes, barter and inflation are different things.

Q34

A historian who studies old coins to learn about ancient kings is practising:

  • Aminting
  • Bbartering
  • Cinflation control
  • Dnumismatics✓

Working

✓

Studying coins for history is numismatics. Minting is making coins, bartering is swapping goods, and inflation control is economic policy.

Q35

Paper money was first used in:

  • AIndia
  • Bancient Rome
  • CBritain
  • DChina✓

Working

✓

Paper currency was first used in China. It reached India only in the late 18th century; Rome and Britain were not its origin.

Q36

Paper currency was brought to India in the:

  • Aancient Vedic period
  • BGupta era
  • Clate 18th century✓
  • Dyear 1950

Working

✓

Paper money came to India in the late 18th century. It did not exist in Vedic or Gupta times, and 1950 is the year the Constitution took effect.

Q37

Today, paper currency in India is issued only by the:

  • Alocal shopkeepers
  • Beach state separately
  • CUnited Nations
  • DReserve Bank of India✓

Working

✓

Only the Reserve Bank of India issues India's paper currency. Shopkeepers, individual states and the UN do not.

Q38

Why can a 500-rupee note buy goods even though the paper itself is cheap?

  • Abecause the paper is made of precious metal
  • Bbecause the ink is worth 500 rupees
  • Cbecause everyone accepts it and the Reserve Bank of India backs it✓
  • Dbecause the note can be eaten

Working

✓

A note's value rests on universal acceptance and RBI backing - trust, not the material. The paper is not precious, the ink is not worth 500 rupees, and notes are not food.

Q39

The claim 'a note is valuable because the paper is precious' is wrong because modern money rests on:

  • Athe gold content of the paper
  • Btrust and government backing, not the worth of the material✓
  • Cthe weight of the note
  • Dthe colour of the ink

Working

✓

Modern money is valuable because people trust it and the government backs it, not because of the material. Paper has no gold, and weight and colour do not set value.

Q40

'Fiat money' is money that has value because:

  • Athe government declares it legal tender and people trust it✓
  • Bit is made of precious metal
  • Cit can be bartered for cattle
  • Dit is printed on gold leaf

Working

✓

Fiat money (Latin fiat, 'let it be done') is valuable because the government makes it legal tender and people trust it, not because the material is precious.

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

'Legal tender' is money that:

  • Acan be refused by anyone at any time
  • Bis only valid abroad
  • Cmust, by law, be accepted to settle a payment within a country✓
  • Dis made only of gold

Working

✓

Legal tender must legally be accepted for payments in a country. It cannot be freely refused, is not abroad-only, and need not be gold.

Q42

The claim 'to be real money, a coin must be made of gold or silver' is wrong because:

  • Aall modern coins really are solid gold
  • Bmodern coins and notes are fiat money, valuable through trust and legal-tender status✓
  • Cmoney cannot exist without precious metal
  • Donly barter is real

Working

✓

Modern money is fiat - its value comes from trust and legal-tender status, not precious metal. Coins are not solid gold, money needs no precious metal, and barter is not the only 'real' exchange.

Q43

Early coins differed from modern coins mainly in that early coins:

  • Awere fiat money, while modern coins are precious metal
  • Bwere valuable for their precious metal, while modern money is fiat money based on trust✓
  • Cwere digital, while modern coins are physical
  • Dwere never accepted in trade

Working

✓

Early coins held value through their precious metal; modern money is fiat, resting on trust and legal-tender status. The reversal, the digital claim and the 'never accepted' claim are all wrong.

Q44

Fiat money works smoothly only as long as:

  • Ait is made of more and more gold
  • Bbarter is completely banned
  • Cpeople trust it and the government keeps it as legal tender✓
  • Dprices never change at all

Working

✓

Fiat money depends on continued trust and legal-tender status. It does not rely on gold content, a barter ban, or unchanging prices.

Q45

'Inflation' is:

  • Aa general rise in prices over time, so each rupee buys a little less✓
  • Ba fall in prices, so each rupee buys more
  • Cthe making of new coins
  • Dthe study of old money

Working

✓

Inflation (Latin inflare, 'to inflate') is a general rise in prices, reducing each rupee's buying power. Falling prices, minting and numismatics are different.

Q46

If a chocolate that cost 10 rupees last year costs 12 rupees this year, this is an example of:

  • Abarter
  • Bdeflation to zero
  • Cdouble coincidence of wants
  • Dinflation✓

Working

✓

A general rise in price over time is inflation. It is not barter, a fall to zero, or a barter-matching problem.

Q47

Inflation matters because, over time, the same amount of money:

  • Abuys fewer goods than before✓
  • Bbuys more goods than before
  • Cturns into gold
  • Dbecomes illegal tender

Working

✓

Under inflation each rupee buys less, so the same money buys fewer goods. It does not buy more, become gold, or lose legal-tender status.

Q48

'Digital money' is:

  • Acoins made of a new shiny metal
  • Bpaper notes printed in colour
  • Ccowrie shells used online
  • Dmoney in electronic form, transferred between bank accounts without being touched✓

Working

✓

Digital money is intangible, electronic money moved between accounts. It is not a new metal coin, a paper note, or shells.

Q49

A 'QR code' used for payments is a:

  • Acoin stamped with a king's face
  • Bprecious metal disc
  • Cpattern of black and white squares holding the receiver's bank-account information✓
  • Dpaper cheque

Working

✓

A QR ('quick-response') code is a scannable square pattern carrying the receiver's account details. It is not a coin, a metal disc, or a cheque.

Q50

The claim 'UPI is a completely different kind of money from rupees' is wrong because UPI:

  • Acreates a brand-new currency each time
  • Breplaces rupees with gold
  • Cis a form of barter
  • Dsimply moves the same rupees electronically between bank accounts✓

Working

✓

UPI, cards and QR payments move the same rupees electronically - only the transfer method changes, not the money. No new currency is created, no gold replaces rupees, and it is not barter.

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

Paying for vegetables by scanning a shop's QR code with a phone is an example of:

  • Adigital money transferring rupees between accounts✓
  • Bbarter, since no cash is touched
  • Cminting new coins
  • Dprinting new notes

Working

✓

A QR payment moves rupees electronically - digital money. It is not barter (money is used), nor minting or printing.

Q52

Digital payments like UPI and cards are best described as a new:

  • Akind of money replacing the rupee
  • Bform of barter
  • Cmethod of transferring money, not a new kind of money✓
  • Dtype of commodity money

Working

✓

Digital payments change how money moves, not what the money is - the rupees are the same. They are not a new currency, barter, or commodity money.

Q53

Which sequence shows the evolution of money in correct order (earliest first)?

  • Adigital money, then paper, then coins, then barter
  • Bmetal coins, then barter, then digital, then paper
  • Cbarter, then commodity money, then metal coins, then paper currency, then digital money✓
  • Dpaper currency, then barter, then digital, then coins

Working

✓

Money evolved from barter to commodity money to metal coins to paper currency to digital money. The other orders scramble this timeline.

Q54

Commodity money (like cowrie shells) came ____ metal coins.

  • Aafter
  • Bbefore✓
  • Cat the same time as
  • Dinstead of, replacing them forever

Working

✓

Commodity money preceded metal coins in the evolution of money. It did not come after, alongside, or permanently replace coins.

Q55

Compared with barter, the invention of coins made trade easier mainly by providing:

  • Abigger animals to trade
  • Ba way to avoid all transactions
  • Ca portable, divisible, durable and widely accepted measure of value✓
  • Da return to swapping goods for goods

Working

✓

Coins gave a portable, divisible, durable, accepted measure - fixing barter's flaws. They did not provide bigger animals, end transactions, or revive barter.

Q56

The overall story of money is best summed up as a move from:

  • Atrusted money back to pure barter
  • Bgold coins to cowrie shells
  • Cswapping goods directly toward more convenient, widely trusted tools of exchange✓
  • Ddigital payments to stone discs

Working

✓

Money evolved from direct barter toward ever more convenient, trusted means of exchange (coins, paper, digital). The other options reverse the direction of this evolution.

Q57

A 'transaction' is:

  • Athe study of old coins
  • Ba piece of business between people, especially buying or selling✓
  • Ca rise in prices
  • Da metal alloy

Working

✓

A transaction is an act of business such as buying or selling. The study of coins is numismatics, rising prices is inflation, and an alloy is a metal mixture.

Q58

'Currency' and 'denomination' differ in that currency is the ____ while a denomination is a ____.

  • Aspecific unit like 10 rupees ... whole system of money
  • Bsystem of money used in a country ... specific unit like 10 or 100 rupees✓
  • Cstudy of coins ... rise in prices
  • Drise in prices ... study of coins

Working

✓

Currency is a country's money system; a denomination is a specific unit such as 10 or 100 rupees. The other options reverse or confuse these.

Q59

Which pairing of money form and feature is correct?

  • Apaper money was first used in India; 'rupee' is a Greek word for 'coin'
  • Bpaper money was first used in China; 'rupee' is from Sanskrit 'rupya'✓
  • Cdigital money is older than barter
  • Dcoins were invented before barter

Working

✓

Paper money began in China and 'rupee' comes from rupya. Paper money did not start in India, 'rupee' is not Greek, digital money is recent, and coins came after barter.

Q60

A student says, 'Money is valuable only if it is made of gold, and UPI is a brand-new currency.' The best correction is:

Modern money is fiat - valued by trust and legal-tender status, not gold - and UPI merely transfers the same rupees electronically. The other options repeat or compound the misconceptions.
