# Social Science — Chapter 3: Climates of India · Paper 2 (Advanced) Solutions

**1. (D)** The **decades-long pattern** of a place is its **climate**. "Raining right now" is weather (hourly), "rains every June" is a season (yearly), and the long average over decades is climate.

**2. (A)** Climate is measured over **many years — at least several decades**. A single afternoon, a few hours or one week describe weather, not climate.

**3. (B)** Alongside the usual four seasons, India is famous for its **rainy season — the monsoon**, so vital that whole festivals and the farming year revolve around it.

**4. (C)** The traditional Indian calendar divides the year into **six ṛitus** (such as Vasanta, Grīṣma, Varṣhā…), not four.

**5. (D)** An **arid** climate means **very little rainfall** — the defining trait of the Thar Desert. Deserts are about dryness, not necessarily heat.

**6. (A)** Shimla is cool because of its **high altitude**: temperature falls as you climb, so a hill town stays cooler than the plains at the same latitude.

**7. (B)** The fifth climate factor, with latitude, altitude, distance from the sea and winds, is **topography** — the lie of the land (mountains, slopes, coasts).

**8. (C)** Kanniyakumari is warm year-round because of its **low latitude** near the Equator, where the Sun's rays fall almost straight down all year.

**9. (D)** Temperature dropping as you go up shows the effect of **altitude** — the higher you climb in the troposphere, the colder it gets.

**10. (A)** Mumbai's even, mild, never-extreme pattern is a **maritime climate**: the nearby sea, slow to warm and cool, smooths out the temperature.

**11. (B)** Inland Nagpur swings to extremes because **land heats and cools faster than the sea, and there is no nearby sea to moderate it** — the hallmark of a continental climate.

**12. (C)** The monsoon winds are the climate factor of **winds** in action — moving air that carries heat and moisture across regions.

**13. (D)** The Western Ghats forcing air to rise and rain is the factor of **topography** — the shape of the land steering the weather.

**14. (A)** A small pocket whose climate differs from the surrounding region is a **microclimate** — like a shaded forest patch or a warm city centre.

**15. (B)** An urban heat island is warm because **concrete and tar store heat while there is little vegetation to cool the air** — not because of trees, altitude or the sea.

**16. (C)** "Monsoon" comes from the Arabic *mausim*, meaning **season** — fitting, since it names the seasonal reversing winds.

**17. (D)** Most of India's rain arrives with the **southwest monsoon in summer (roughly June–September)**, when moist sea winds sweep inland.

**18. (A)** The **northeast (winter) monsoon** brings rain especially to **the Tamil Nadu (south-east) coast**, watering it while much of India is dry.

**19. (B)** The monsoon's engine is the **unequal heating of land and the ocean** — they warm at different rates, setting up the pressure differences that drive the winds.

**20. (C)** In summer the hot land's air rises and pressure drops, so **moist winds blow from the sea in towards the land, bringing rain**. (In winter it reverses.)

**21. (D)** The slope facing the rain-bearing wind, which gets heavy rain, is the **windward side**.

**22. (A)** The sheltered slope, where the air has already lost its moisture, is the **leeward side** — the dry rain-shadow side.

**23. (B)** The Deccan is dry because **the Ghats squeeze the rain out on their windward west, leaving little for the leeward east** — the classic rain-shadow effect.

**24. (C)** Mawsynram and Cherrapunji are drenched because they **catch monsoon air forced up the Khasi hills, wringing out enormous rain** — windward extremes, not rain-shadow.

**25. (D)** The **windward western** slopes of the Western Ghats are **drenched by the monsoon**; it is the leeward east that turns dry.

**26. (A)** Near the Equator the Sun's rays strike **almost straight down, most directly**, concentrating heat — which is why low-latitude lands are hot all year.

**27. (B)** India's **west coast** has the tropical wet climate — warm with heavy monsoon rain caught by the Ghats.

**28. (C)** The cold, snowy **alpine** climate belongs to the **Himalayas**, where great altitude brings year-round cold.

**29. (D)** The interior Deccan, with moderate rain and dry spells, is broadly **semi-arid** — drier than the coast but not a true desert.

**30. (A)** The hot, dry, dusty north-Indian summer wind is the **loo**, notorious for its scorching afternoons.

**31. (B)** A cyclone's calm, cloudless centre is the **eye**, ringed by the fiercest winds.

**32. (C)** Severe cyclones most often strike India's **eastern (Bay of Bengal) coast**, which faces the storm-breeding warm waters.

**33. (D)** The **natural** greenhouse effect is essential because it **traps just enough of the Sun's heat to keep Earth warm enough for life** — without it the planet would freeze.

**34. (A)** Burning coal, oil and gas **adds greenhouse gases that strengthen the effect and warm the planet further** — the human-driven part of climate change.

**35. (B)** Mitigation means **taking steps that slow warming and cut its causes — planting trees, using clean energy** — acting on the *causes*, not just coping afterward.

**36. (C)** Resilience is the capacity to **withstand and recover quickly after a difficulty like a flood** — bouncing back, not preventing every disaster.

**37. (D)** India's forecasting and warning body is the **India Meteorological Department (IMD)**.

**38. (A)** When a disaster actually strikes, ground rescue and relief are carried out by the **National Disaster Response Force (NDRF)** — IMD warns, NDRF acts.

**39. (B)** A worrying climate-change sign for farmers is a monsoon turned **erratic — sometimes late, sometimes in damaging bursts** — rather than steady.

**40. (C)** Festivals like Onam, Pongal and Baisakhi track the seasons because **farming life follows sowing and harvest, which follow the climate and monsoon**.

**41. (D)** Agriculture leans on the monsoon because **a large share of farmland is rain-fed, so the timing and amount of monsoon rain decide the harvest**.

**42. (A)** A late, weak monsoon most likely brings **drought and crop failure in rain-fed areas** — the rain simply does not arrive when crops need it.

**43. (B)** The coastal town swings less because **the nearby sea warms and cools slowly and moderates the air** — the maritime effect.

**44. (C)** Hill stations stay cool despite the latitude because of **altitude** — height, not nearness to sea or Equator, is doing the cooling.

**45. (D)** With P at sea level and Q 1,500 m up at the same latitude, the best single explanation for Q's cooler 24 °C is **Q's higher altitude makes it cooler**.

**46. (A)** The leeward land is dry because the arriving air **has already dropped most of its moisture climbing the windward side** — a rain shadow.

**47. (B)** The southwest monsoon bursts over **Kerala first, then advances northward across India** over the following weeks.

**48. (C)** The retreating-monsoon season is when **the winds begin to reverse and withdraw, often bringing rain to the south-east coast** (the northeast monsoon).

**49. (D)** Clearing hill forests worsens floods and landslides because **roots and leaf cover normally hold soil and slow runoff; without them, rain rushes down and the slope gives way**.

**50. (A)** Forest fires are most likely in **the hot, dry season**, when leaf litter and undergrowth are parched.

**51. (B)** Landslides strike where heavy rain meets **steep, unstable slopes** — water loosens the soil until it slides.

**52. (C)** A river overflowing its banks after days of rain causes a **flood**.

**53. (D)** The chief greenhouse gas added by burning fossil fuels is **carbon dioxide** (CO₂).

**54. (A)** "Poured this morning but cleared by noon" describes **the weather of that day** — a short-term, changing state, not climate or season.

**55. (B)** A season differs from weather and climate because it **returns in a regular pattern every year** — predictable and recurring.

**56. (C)** A cool, damp forest pocket different from the open land around it is a **microclimate**.

**57. (D)** The monsoon is the economy's "lifeline" because **farming, water supply and hydro-power all depend on its rains** — a poor monsoon ripples through the whole economy.

**58. (A)** On a cyclone warning the wisest move is to **move people to shelters and secure homes and boats before it lands** — preparation beats waiting.

**59. (B)** Matching region to climate: the Himalayas, the Thar and the west coast are respectively **alpine, arid, tropical wet**.

**60. (C)** India's climate is so varied because of **its huge range of latitude and altitude, long coastlines, the Himalayan wall and the monsoon together** — no single factor, but their combination.
