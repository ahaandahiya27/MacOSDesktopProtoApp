## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** The Harappan (First Urbanisation) order is earliest; iron became widespread only much later in the Ganga-plains phase; that iron-fed surplus helped janapadas merge into the larger mahaajanapadas; and the mahaajanapadas finally gave way to the first empires. The option that puts iron before the Harappans reverses the two urbanisations. The option placing empires before iron and the merging puts the last stage in the middle. The option starting with the merging puts the larger states before the Harappans and before the iron that made them possible.

**2. (C)** 'Revival' would wrongly imply continuity of people, place and features; in fact the second wave was a later, independent rise in a new region marked by iron and punch-marked coins. The option about reusing Harappan citadels and script is false: the new cities were elsewhere and did not carry on the Harappan script. The 'only a few decades apart' option is wrong because roughly a thousand non-urban years separated the two. The 'Harappans moved east' option invents a migration the chapter does not describe and contradicts the long gap.

**3. (A)** The claim is that the whole working system, not just walls, was lost; it would be undermined only if trade, writing and administration had actually continued. The 'people went back to village life and trade and writing faded' option supports the claim rather than undermining it. The 'thousand-year gap before cities returned' option also supports a loss of urban order. The 'new cities used iron and coins' option is about the later wave's novelty and says nothing against the loss of the earlier order.

**4. (A)** Two independent lines agreeing is convincing because they are unlikely to share the same mistake, so the converging picture is sturdier. The 'texts record exact spoken words, so texts alone are decisive' option overstates what texts give and dismisses archaeology. The 'texts are always wrong' option falsely discards one whole source. The last option is impossible: the Harappan First-Urbanisation people did not write the later texts, whose script is undeciphered, and they did not excavate later sites.

**5. (A)** 'Where the people set foot' signals a community that settled and tied itself to a fixed land, the very change that made early states possible. The 'army on the march' option contradicts the whole point, which is settling down rather than moving. The 'coin' option confuses a word for a territory with money. The 'sprawling empire' option overshoots the scale: a janapada was a single clan's home territory, not an empire.

**6. (C)** The chapter's criteria are settled population, fixed territory and an organising authority, and janapadas met all three under a rajaa, so size does not disqualify them. The 'must rule many regions' option imposes an empire-sized test the chapter never sets. The 'no leader at all' option is false, since a rajaa led the janapada. The 'iron coins from the start' option is an anachronism, as coins (silver punch-marked) belong to a later stage, not the origin of janapadas.

**7. (B)** The prefix 'maha-' (great) marks scale: mahaajanapadas were the bigger states into which earlier janapadas merged, so the two terms differ in size and stage. The 'coin and route' option misidentifies both words entirely. The 'mahaajanapada came first and broke apart' option reverses the direction of change, which ran from small to large. The 'always a republic vs always a monarchy' option is false, since both monarchies and ganas/sanghas existed among the mahaajanapadas and that is not what the prefix marks.

**8. (A)** The chapter credits fertile soil, available iron and connecting rivers and routes; a cold, dry, riverless plain is the opposite of what it describes, since rivers were a key advantage, so that option is the odd one out. Fertile soil with surpluses is a genuine reason. Access to iron is a genuine reason. Rivers and trade routes linking the region is a genuine reason.

**9. (B)** Sixteen is a useful traditional grouping from the textual tradition, not a precise modern headcount, so a little caution is fair. The 'certainly one hundred' option asserts a precise number the chapter never gives. The 'never existed' option denies real, well-attested states. The 'compiled by the Harappans' option is impossible, since the Harappans vanished long before the mahaajanapadas and their lists.

**10. (C)** The sixteen were many distinct, sizeable states existing around the same time and spread over a broad region. The 'sixteen successive empires' option turns contemporaries into a sequence. The 'small villages in one valley' option shrinks states into a hamlet cluster. The 'names for one kingdom' option collapses many states into a single one, which the wide geographic span (Gandhaara to Anga) contradicts.

**11. (A)** Walls, a water-filled ditch and a pinch-point gate form a coherent defence-and-control package, exactly what a rich state with rivals would build. The 'beautiful for festivals' option ignores that all three are defensive. The 'store rainwater' option is contradicted by the rampart and narrow gate, which are not water tanks. The 'wander in freely' option is directly contradicted by the narrow gate, whose purpose is to restrict movement.

**12. (D)** Narrowing the gate concentrates movement at one controllable point, letting few guards watch, inspect and even collect tolls. The 'rainwater drains faster' option confuses a gateway with a drain. The 'two elephants side by side' option would require a wide gate, the opposite of narrow. The 'look graceful' option treats a security feature as mere decoration.

**13. (B)** Continuous occupation for 2,500 years points to enduring advantages of site, such as fertile surroundings, river access and trade position. The 'nothing has changed' option mistakes continuity for being frozen in time. The 'founded by the Harappans' option is wrong, since these were Second-Urbanisation capitals, not Harappan ones. The 'never fortified' option contradicts the chapter, which describes these capitals as ramparted and moated.

**14. (A)** Punch-marked coins appeared in the later Ganga-plains wave; the Harappans had no coinage at all, so dating a coin to their period is the error. The 'only gold coins' option is false, since the Harappans used no coins of any metal. The 'never used anywhere in ancient India' option is false, since coins were used, just later. The 'made of iron' option misnames the metal, as punch-marked coins were silver.

**15. (A)** A medium of exchange exists to ease buying and selling, so its widespread use signals expanding commerce beyond barter's reach. The 'jewellery' option ignores their role as money. The 'buried with the dead' option reduces a trade tool to a grave good. The 'made barter more popular' option is self-contradictory, since coins mark a move away from barter, not toward it.

**16. (C)** Coins drifting far from home is the natural signature of long-distance trade carrying them along the routes, and one cause explains both finds. The 'made on the spot' option contradicts the coins being identified with distant regions. The 'one identical coin' option is false, since regions usually struck their own coins, which is why these can be traced to Avanti and Anga. The 'forbidden to leave' option would make such finds impossible.

**17. (C)** The double difficulty of matching wants is what makes barter clumsy, and a common coin neatly removes it. The 'only after sunset' option invents a time restriction barter never had. The 'bank in every village' option is anachronistic, since barter predates banks. The 'needs iron tools' option falsely links swapping goods to a particular technology.

**18. (C)** Although each region usually made its own coins, neighbouring coins also circulated and travelled along trade routes, even abroad. The 'never accepted another region's coins' option contradicts the finds of foreign coins. The 'only the Harappans minted' option is impossible, since coins postdate the Harappans entirely. The 'banned outside the capital' option contradicts the cross-regional circulation the chapter describes.

**19. (B)** 'Hereditary' means power is inherited by birth, so the throne usually went to the king's son or kin, the very thing that distinguishes a monarchy from a gana/sangha. The 'assembly voted each time' option describes a republic, not a hereditary monarchy. The 'wealthiest merchant' option ignores birth as the basis of succession. The 'foreign conqueror inherits' option confuses conquest with inheritance.

**20. (D)** A monarch's duties included keeping order, collecting revenue, and maintaining defence; succession was hereditary, not decided by public election, so the election option is the duty that does not belong. Keeping law and order is a genuine duty. Collecting taxes is a genuine duty. Maintaining an army and fortifications is a genuine duty.

**21. (C)** In a monarchy the king held power yet was supposed to govern with the advice of the sabhaa and samiti and his ministers, sharing counsel but not rule. The 'assemblies governed directly, king a figurehead' option describes a republic, not a monarchy. The 'ignored entirely' option erases the advisory role the chapter affirms. The 'fresh king every year by vote' option contradicts hereditary kingship.

**22. (A)** Older Vedic words for assemblies alongside later republican terms point to a continuous, evolving tradition of shared decision-making. The 'Vedas composed after the mahaajanapadas' option reverses the time order the layering implies. The 'no earlier roots' option ignores that the Vedic terms show exactly such roots. The 'gana and sangha mean a hereditary king' option is backwards, since those terms describe republics, the opposite of hereditary kingship.

**23. (C)** In a gana/sangha the assembly chose the ruler by debate and, if necessary, a vote, and that locus of decision is the defining republican trait. The 'eldest son always inherited' option describes a monarchy, not a republic. The 'Harappan priests appointed' option is impossible, since the Harappans were long gone. The 'foreign empire imposed' option misdescribes how these early republics chose their leaders.

**24. (A)** The accurate picture is a majority of monarchies plus at least two republics, Vajji and Malla, so the blanket claim fails. The 'entirely correct, no exceptions' option ignores those two republics. The 'none ever had a king' option swings to the opposite error, since most did have kings. The 'only the southern ones had kings' option adds a geographic qualifier the chapter never makes.

**25. (C)** These republics spread decision-making across many family and clan heads, broader than one king's will yet short of universal suffrage. The 'every adult voted equally' option overstates inclusion into a modern democracy. The 'single richest individual decided' option narrows power to one person, contradicting shared decision-making. The 'foreign rulers decided' option removes self-rule entirely, which is the opposite of a republic.

**26. (D)** The essential contrast is WHO decides: a single inheriting king versus a decision-making assembly that also picks the ruler. The 'farming vs no farming' option is false, since both relied on farming. The 'no army vs large army' option is false, since both maintained armies. The 'iron vs bronze' option is false, since both belonged to the iron age, so only the locus of political power genuinely separates them.

**27. (D)** The line shows the king governed WITH advice, not that he was powerless or replaceable yearly. The 'no real power, assemblies governed' option overshoots into a republic. The 'elected every year' option contradicts hereditary kingship. The 'no army at all' option is unrelated to the point and contradicts the monarch's duty of maintaining an army.

**28. (B)** Taxes funded the costly running of a state: soldiers, walls and officials. The 'replace farming' option is false, since taxes were often levied on farm produce rather than replacing it. The 'discourage trade' option is false, since states benefited from trade. The 'abolish the need for a ruler' option is self-contradictory, since collecting revenue is itself one of the ruler's functions.

**29. (D)** Walls, tax collection and armies require an authority able to mobilise resources, the hallmark of organised states. The 'loose bands of wanderers' option contradicts the fixed, organised authority such features imply. The 'purely religious settlements with no government' option ignores the very governing apparatus described. The 'reconstructions of Harappan cities' option is false, since these were new Ganga-age states, not copies of the long-collapsed Harappan order.

**30. (D)** The First Urbanisation worked copper and bronze; iron became widespread only in the later Ganga-plains wave, which it powered. The 'Harappans used iron, second wave bronze' option reverses the two. The 'both depended on gold tools' option is false for both waves. The 'Harappan iron coins' option is doubly wrong, since the Harappans had no coins and did not work iron.

**31. (D)** Iron's value was practical and agricultural: tougher tools opened heavy, forested land and lifted harvests that supported non-farmers and soldiers. The 'worshipped as sacred' option treats a working metal as a cult object. The 'removed the need for trade routes' option is false, since trade in fact grew. The 'printed the first books' option is anachronistic and unrelated to iron's role.

**32. (A)** An iron plough breaks tougher soil and grows more, generating the surplus that supports town-dwellers, the engine of urban growth. The 'harvests fall' option reverses the expected effect. The 'trade routes close' option is wrong, since more surplus means more, not less, trade. The 'need for rulers and taxes disappears' option is wrong, since a larger settled population needs administration more, not less.

**33. (D)** 'Necessary but not sufficient' means iron was required yet had to act together with soil, routes and rulers to yield cities. The 'iron alone instantly produced cities' option claims sufficiency, the opposite point. The 'never used in the Ganga plains' option denies iron's necessity. The 'cities appeared before iron' option contradicts the chapter's sequence, in which iron came first and enabled the growth.

**34. (D)** Iron weapons made armies more effective, which helped powerful states conquer and merge rivals into larger kingdoms. The 'warfare disappeared' option is the opposite of what stronger armies imply. The 'farming ended' option is false, since farming expanded with iron ploughs. The 'led to writing' option links two unrelated developments.

**35. (A)** Since iron tools enabled the land-clearing and farming behind the surplus, removing iron would have starved that surplus and weakened the urban wave. The 'cities grew even faster' option contradicts iron's enabling role. The 'Harappan civilisation never ended' option is irrelevant, since its collapse happened long before and for other reasons. The 'coins invented sooner' option is unsupported, since the money economy rested on the trade that iron-fed growth made possible.

**36. (B)** The Second Urbanisation itself disproves the claim, since after about a thousand non-urban years city life reappeared in a new region. The 'never abandoned' option is false, since the Harappan cities were indeed lost. The 'no gap at all' option denies the well-attested gap. The 'cities since the Stone Age' option overstates continuity and ignores the documented break.

**37. (C)** A jaati was a community defined by occupation, with skills and customs handed down within families. The 'water-filled ditch' option describes a moat, not a social group. The 'long-distance highway' option describes a patha, a trade route. The 'silver coin' option describes a punch-marked coin, not a community.

**38. (B)** Kshatriyas were the varna associated with protection and warfare. The 'Brahmins defend in battle' option swaps roles, since Brahmins were linked with knowledge and rituals. The 'Vaishyas perform rituals' option is wrong, since Vaishyas were linked with trade and agriculture. The 'Shudras rule as warriors' option is wrong, since Shudras were linked with service occupations.

**39. (D)** The chapter stresses that in this early phase movement between occupations was possible, so it was not fixed from the start. The 'never existed' option denies a system the chapter does describe. The 'only the Harappan cities' option is wrong, since it belongs to the later Ganga-plains age. The 'rigid on the very day it began' option restates the misconception rather than correcting it.

**40. (B)** 'Uttara' means north and 'dakshina' means south, so the names themselves fix the directions: Uttaraapatha north, Dakshinaapatha south. The first distractor reverses the two. The 'only east to west along the coast' option misdescribes routes that ran broadly north and south. The 'inside a single mahaajanapada' option shrinks routes that spanned many regions into one state.

**41. (C)** To pass from the northern plains into the peninsula, the southern route crossed the Vindhyas, the dividing range between north and south India. The Himalayas lie far to the north, the wrong direction for a route heading south. The Aravallis run through the northwest, off the southward path. The Western Ghats edge the western coast and are not the range a route from Kaushambi southward would first cross.

**42. (B)** The routes were arteries for people and ideas as well as merchandise: pilgrims, armies, beliefs and coins all travelled along them. The 'only royal letters' option is far too narrow. The 'only soldiers' option ignores pilgrims, ideas and coins. The 'only iron ore, never people' option contradicts the very mixing of people and ideas that helped unify the regions.

**43. (B)** The southern trio were the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas. Magadha, Kosala and Avanti were powerful northern mahaajanapadas. Vajji, Malla and Anga lay in the north and east. Kuru, Panchaala and Matsya were northern janapadas/mahaajanapadas, so only the Chola-Chera-Pandya set is southern.

**44. (D)** The south's prized resources, gems, gold and spices, pulled it into the wider trade networks. The 'cold climate' option is neither accurate nor a draw for trade. The 'no rivers, easier travel' option is wrong, since the absence of rivers would hinder movement. The 'far from the sea' option is false, since the south's coastal position actually aided maritime trade.

**45. (B)** By this date trade, coins and ideas had linked the regions into a single connected land, with influence spilling beyond India's borders. The 'emptied of people' option contradicts the bustling, connected picture. The 'three separate islands' option is geographically false. The 'ruled by the Harappans again' option is impossible, since the Harappan civilisation had ended roughly a thousand years earlier.

**46. (B)** As the land integrated, the mahaajanapadas gave way to the early empires, the next, larger stage of state formation. The 'just being founded' option puts their beginning where their end belongs. The 'all became republics' option is false, since their era was ending, not transforming wholesale. The 'relocated into the Indus valley' option invents a migration the chapter does not describe.

**47. (A)** Magadha's strength flowed from its fertile Ganga location, rich harvests, river routes and access to resources such as iron, the very ingredients the chapter ties to powerful states. The 'no rivers' option is wrong, since rivers were an asset, not a defensive gap. The 'gana/sangha that elected rulers' option misclassifies Magadha, a monarchy. The 'far to the south near the Pandyas' option misplaces a northern state.

**48. (C)** The second wave took root where its enabling conditions converged: the fertile, iron-rich, route-linked Ganga plains, not the long-collapsed Indus cities. The 'rebuilt the Indus cities' option ignores the new region. The 'no farming' option is false, since the plains were intensely farmed. The 'iron unknown' option is false, since iron was central to the Ganga-plains wave.

**49. (B)** The developments are interlinked: agricultural surplus underpinned cities and states, the resulting trade called for coins, and each fed back into the others as a single web of cause and effect. The 'three different millennia' option separates roughly contemporary events. The 'coins first, then farming' option reverses the order, since surplus farming preceded and enabled coins. The 'separate continent' option is plainly false, since all unfolded in the same land.

**50. (D)** Fertile soil and iron give surplus, but the chapter ties powerful states to connectivity as well; cut off from routes, a region would lose the exchange of goods, coins and ideas. The 'isolation always protects' option treats being cut off as an advantage the chapter does not grant. The 'trade played no role' option contradicts the chapter's emphasis on connectivity. The 'guaranteed largest empire' option overclaims from soil and iron alone.

**51. (B)** Shishupalgarh, in the east with its square fortified plan, was the capital associated with Kalinga. Magadha, in Bihar, had capitals such as Rajagriha, not an eastern square-planned site. Gandhaara lay in the far northwest. Avanti lay in central-western India, none of which matches an eastern, square-planned city.

**52. (D)** The driving sequence runs from iron, to cleared land, to surplus food, to cities and states, each step enabling the next. The 'coins, then barter, then trade stopped' option reverses the relationship, since barter came first and coins helped trade grow. The 'cities grew, forests reappeared, iron vanished' option runs the consequences backwards. The 'empires, then janapadas, then clans settled' option inverts the whole order, since clans settled first and empires came last.

**53. (A)** The clearest support is that Second-Urbanisation cities like Varanasi remain alive and busy after roughly 2,500 years, an unbroken thread. The 'Harappan cities still inhabited' option is false and concerns the First Urbanisation. The 'iron fallen out of use' option is false. The 'barter still the main trade' option is false, so none of these supports the claim.

**54. (C)** Larger states formed as more powerful janapadas, backed by iron arms and surplus wealth, defeated or absorbed weaker ones. The 'Harappans returned' option is impossible, since they had long since vanished. The 'no one wanted land any more' option contradicts clans' strong ties to their territory. The 'foreign empires split states into janapadas' option reverses the direction, which ran from small units to large.

**55. (D)** Walls, taxation and armies together require an authority able to gather and direct resources, which is exactly what marks an organised state. The 'Harappan seals, weights, drainage' option points to the earlier First Urbanisation, not these states. The 'absence of rulers, assemblies or coins' option would argue against statehood, not for it. The 'cowrie exchange with no walls, taxes or army' option lacks every feature that signals an organised state.

**56. (D)** A coin drifting far from its mint is the signature of trade carrying it along routes, while a capital alive after 2,500 years shows the second wave's unbroken continuity, so each find supports its own claim. The first distractor swaps the two matches. The 'Harappans founded the Ganga cities' option is false, since those cities were not Harappan. The 'barter never gave way to coins' option contradicts the rise of coined money the travelled coin itself reflects.

**57. (A)** These early republics shared decisions among many leading-family heads, broader than one king's will yet short of universal suffrage, so 'early' not 'modern' is right. The 'every adult voted equally' option overstates inclusion. The 'rulers inherited by birth' option is false, since the assembly chose them, which is what makes them republics. The 'assemblies took no decisions' option contradicts their defining feature.

**58. (B)** The chapter stresses that a good rajaa governed with the counsel of the assemblies and ministers, not by himself, so 'ruled entirely alone' is wrong. The 'monarchies did not exist' option is false, since most mahaajanapadas were monarchies. The 'assemblies governed directly' option describes a republic, not a monarchy. The 'elected and removed each year' option contradicts hereditary kingship.

**59. (C)** The chapter's logic begins with iron, which let people clear land and work heavy soil, producing surpluses that fed cities and supported trade needing coins, and so cities and states grew. The first distractor puts trade and coins before the iron that ultimately made them possible. The second puts cities before the iron and surplus that built them, reversing the chain. The third places surplus before the iron that produced it, breaking the causal order.

**60. (A)** Iron became widespread only in the second wave; the Harappans worked copper and bronze, so iron marks the later phase, not a shared feature. The 'Harappans used iron, abandoned later' option reverses the truth. The 'both relied on iron equally' option is false, since the First Urbanisation did not use iron. The 'iron only for coins' option is wrong, since coins were silver, not iron, and were a Second-Urbanisation development.
