Worked Solutions

1. (C) The Harappan (First Urbanisation) order is earliest; iron became widespread only much later in the Ganga-plains phase; that iron-fed surplus helped janapadas merge into the larger mahaajanapadas; and the mahaajanapadas finally gave way to the first empires. The option that puts iron before the Harappans reverses the two urbanisations. The option placing empires before iron and the merging puts the last stage in the middle. The option starting with the merging puts the larger states before the Harappans and before the iron that made them possible.

2. (C) 'Revival' would wrongly imply continuity of people, place and features; in fact the second wave was a later, independent rise in a new region marked by iron and punch-marked coins. The option about reusing Harappan citadels and script is false: the new cities were elsewhere and did not carry on the Harappan script. The 'only a few decades apart' option is wrong because roughly a thousand non-urban years separated the two. The 'Harappans moved east' option invents a migration the chapter does not describe and contradicts the long gap.

3. (A) The claim is that the whole working system, not just walls, was lost; it would be undermined only if trade, writing and administration had actually continued. The 'people went back to village life and trade and writing faded' option supports the claim rather than undermining it. The 'thousand-year gap before cities returned' option also supports a loss of urban order. The 'new cities used iron and coins' option is about the later wave's novelty and says nothing against the loss of the earlier order.

4. (A) Two independent lines agreeing is convincing because they are unlikely to share the same mistake, so the converging picture is sturdier. The 'texts record exact spoken words, so texts alone are decisive' option overstates what texts give and dismisses archaeology. The 'texts are always wrong' option falsely discards one whole source. The last option is impossible: the Harappan First-Urbanisation people did not write the later texts, whose script is undeciphered, and they did not excavate later sites.

5. (A) 'Where the people set foot' signals a community that settled and tied itself to a fixed land, the very change that made early states possible. The 'army on the march' option contradicts the whole point, which is settling down rather than moving. The 'coin' option confuses a word for a territory with money. The 'sprawling empire' option overshoots the scale: a janapada was a single clan's home territory, not an empire.

6. (C) The chapter's criteria are settled population, fixed territory and an organising authority, and janapadas met all three under a rajaa, so size does not disqualify them. The 'must rule many regions' option imposes an empire-sized test the chapter never sets. The 'no leader at all' option is false, since a rajaa led the janapada. The 'iron coins from the start' option is an anachronism, as coins (silver punch-marked) belong to a later stage, not the origin of janapadas.

7. (B) The prefix 'maha-' (great) marks scale: mahaajanapadas were the bigger states into which earlier janapadas merged, so the two terms differ in size and stage. The 'coin and route' option misidentifies both words entirely. The 'mahaajanapada came first and broke apart' option reverses the direction of change, which ran from small to large. The 'always a republic vs always a monarchy' option is false, since both monarchies and ganas/sanghas existed among the mahaajanapadas and that is not what the prefix marks.
