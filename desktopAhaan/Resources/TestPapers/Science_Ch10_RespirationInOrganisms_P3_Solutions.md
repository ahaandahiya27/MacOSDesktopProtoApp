## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** The equation produces carbon dioxide and water (both collectable materials) plus energy. Energy is what the cell uses to do work; it is not matter, so it cannot be collected in a jar. Water is genuinely a product, so saying it is not produced is wrong. Carbon dioxide is produced, not used up. Oxygen is on the left (a reactant), not the right, so that option misreads the equation.

**2. (D)** Breathing is the gaseous exchange that brings oxygen to the blood; respiration is the energy release inside cells that consumes that oxygen. When muscles work hard, they respire faster and need more oxygen, so breathing speeds up to supply it — the two are linked, not unrelated. Faster breathing does not slow respiration. Respiration occurs in every cell, not just the lungs.

**3. (D)** In aerobic respiration the glucose is fully broken down to carbon dioxide and water, releasing a large amount of energy. In anaerobic respiration glucose is only partly broken down (to alcohol or lactic acid), so much energy stays locked in those products and far less is released. Anaerobic respiration uses no oxygen, so it cannot 'use extra oxygen.' Alcohol is an anaerobic (yeast) product, not aerobic. Speed is not the reason for the lower yield; incomplete breakdown is.

**4. (A)** Yeast in dough has little oxygen, so it respires anaerobically: glucose → alcohol + carbon dioxide + energy. The carbon dioxide gas forms bubbles that expand the dough and make it rise. Yeast does not photosynthesise (it is not green and there is no light in dough), so it does not give off oxygen. The dough is not hot enough to boil into vapour. Lactic acid is a muscle product, not a yeast product.

**5. (D)** Yeast breaks down sugar without oxygen, producing alcohol (the smell) and carbon dioxide (the bubbles) — the basis of brewing. The plain juice has no yeast, so neither change occurs there, meaning it did not respire faster. Yeast does not run the reaction backwards into sugar and oxygen, and it does not photosynthesise.

**6. (C)** During the sprint the muscle ran short of oxygen and respired anaerobically, building up lactic acid that causes cramp. On resting, breathing continues to deliver oxygen, which clears the lactic acid, so the cramp eases. Rest does not make MORE lactic acid. Muscles make lactic acid, not alcohol (that is yeast). Refilling with carbon dioxide would not relieve a cramp.

**7. (C)** Anaerobic respiration in muscles happens during sudden, heavy exertion when oxygen supply cannot keep up with demand, so lactic acid forms. A sudden heavy lift fits this. Reading, gentle walking, and sleeping are all low-demand activities where oxygen supply easily meets demand, so respiration stays aerobic.

**8. (A)** The lungs carry out breathing — they take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide. Energy is released by respiration, which happens inside every living cell, not in the lungs. So 'lungs make energy' is wrong even during exercise. The nose only lets air in and cleans it. Blood carries gases but does not 'make energy' as it passes the lungs.

**9. (C)** The increase is 28 − 16 = 12 breaths per minute. 28 is the jogging rate itself, not the increase. 44 is the two rates added (28 + 16). 16 is the resting rate. The question asks for the difference, which is 12.

**10. (B)** Half an hour is 30 minutes, so 15 × 30 = 450 breaths. 45 would be only three minutes (15 × 3). 900 would be a full hour (15 × 60). 30 is just the number of minutes, not breaths. The correct product is 450.

**11. (B)** The numbers climb from sleeping (12) up to running (36) as activity increases, showing breathing rate rises with effort because more oxygen is needed and more carbon dioxide must be removed. The data clearly rise, not fall, so 'falls as activity increases' is wrong. They change, so 'stays the same' is wrong. The lowest rate is during sleep (least activity), not the highest, so 'fastest at rest' is wrong.

**12. (A)** A trained body uses oxygen more efficiently, so the athlete can do the same work with fewer breaths — A's lower rate signals better fitness, not worse. A higher rate (B) means the body is working harder to keep up, so B is not the fitter one. A low rate does not mean respiration has stopped, and all living cells need oxygen, so 'A's cells do not need oxygen' is false.

**13. (D)** High up, the air is thinner (less dense), so each breath delivers less oxygen; the body breathes faster to make up the shortfall. The cause is the thin air, not the cold acting on the diaphragm. Mountain air is not 'full of carbon dioxide.' The lungs do not physically shrink with altitude; the oxygen content of each breath is what drops.

**14. (B)** Air enters the nostrils, passes the nasal cavity, goes down the trachea (windpipe), splits into the bronchi, reaches the alveoli, and oxygen crosses into the blood there. The other orders jumble the sequence — putting the nasal cavity after the trachea, or the nostrils after the nasal cavity, or the bronchi before the trachea. Only nostrils → nasal cavity → trachea → bronchi → alveoli → blood is anatomically correct.

**15. (C)** Inhalation: the diaphragm contracts, flattening and moving down, while the ribs swing up and out — together these enlarge the chest cavity so air is drawn in. The other options mix a downward diaphragm with inward ribs, or a domed diaphragm with outward ribs, which contradict each other or describe exhalation. Only flatten-down diaphragm plus up-and-out ribs gives a larger chest.

**16. (B)** When the chest cavity enlarges, the lungs expand and the air pressure inside them drops below atmospheric pressure; air then flows from high to low pressure, into the lungs. The lungs have no muscle to actively 'suck' — they are passive. The diaphragm enlarges the cavity but does not push air up from the stomach. Temperature differences are not what drive the airflow; the pressure difference does.

**17. (C)** On exhaling, the diaphragm relaxes (domes up) and the ribs move down and in, so the chest shrinks; this raises the pressure inside the lungs above the outside air, and air flows out. An expanding chest with falling pressure describes inhalation, not exhalation. The lungs do not manufacture air to push out, and the ribs move down and in (not further up and out) during exhalation.

**18. (D)** The rubber sheet at the bottom acts like the diaphragm. Pulling it down enlarges the space in the bottle, lowering the pressure so the balloon (lung) inflates — exactly what happens in inhalation when the diaphragm flattens downward. Pulling down cannot model exhalation, when the diaphragm domes up. The sheet is the diaphragm, not the ribs; the balloon, not the sheet, is the lung.

**19. (C)** Insects breathe through spiracles (the small openings) that lead into a branching network of tracheae carrying air straight to the tissues. Gills and bronchi belong to fish and humans. Stomata and lenticels are plant structures. Nostrils and alveoli are parts of the human system. Only spiracles and tracheae describe a cockroach.

**20. (D)** Fish gills extract oxygen dissolved in water. Boiling expels the dissolved oxygen, so the gills find little to absorb and the fish struggles — this is a shortage, not an excess, of oxygen. Fish do not breathe carbon dioxide. The trouble is the missing oxygen, not the water's purity or the fish 'swallowing' it.

**21. (A)** A given volume of water contains far less oxygen than the same volume of air, so a fish must move a large amount of water across its gills to get enough oxygen — hence the near-constant pumping. It is not that fish 'respire many times faster' as cells; it is the low oxygen content of water. Gills can take oxygen from still water too (movement just helps); the real limit is how little oxygen water holds.

**22. (D)** Earthworms take in oxygen through their moist skin from air in the soil spaces. After heavy rain, water fills those spaces, leaving too little air, so worms come up where oxygen is available. They do not 'drink water to breathe.' Earthworms have no gills. Waterlogged soil has LESS available air-oxygen, not extra, so they are escaping a shortage, not an excess.

**23. (D)** An adult frog breathes through both its lungs (in air) and its moist skin, and the skin can absorb oxygen dissolved in water, letting it stay submerged. Adult frogs do not have fish-like gills. It does not store oxygen in its stomach. A living frog cannot stop respiring — its cells always need energy — so 'stops respiring completely' is false.

**24. (B)** A fish uses gills, a cockroach uses spiracles, and an earthworm uses its moist skin — all correct. The second set swaps gills and spiracles and gives the earthworm lungs, all wrong. The third gives a fish moist skin and an earthworm gills, both wrong. The fourth gives a fish lungs and an earthworm spiracles, both wrong.

**25. (B)** Plants respire all the time, day and night. In bright daylight they also photosynthesise, usually faster than they respire, so the net exchange is taking in carbon dioxide and giving out oxygen. It is wrong to say a plant does not respire by day — respiration never stops. It clearly does photosynthesise in light, and it is active (not idle) while sealed.

**26. (A)** In darkness there is no photosynthesis, but respiration continues, so the plant takes in oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide all night — the box ends up with more CO2 and less O2. Plants do not photosynthesise without light, so it does not gain oxygen at night. They are not idle; respiration goes on. The day-time net result (more O2) needs light, which is absent here.

**27. (C)** Leaves exchange gases through stomata, woody stems through lenticels, and roots obtain oxygen from air in the spaces between soil particles. The first wrong set swaps stomata and lenticels and adds gills (an animal organ). The others bring in spiracles and alveoli, which are animal structures, not plant ones. Only stomata / lenticels / soil air spaces is correct for a plant.

**28. (C)** Limewater turns milky in proportion to carbon dioxide, so faster milkiness with exhaled air shows exhaled air is richer in CO2 than room air. It does not show exhaled air has zero oxygen — exhaled air still contains plenty of oxygen, just less than fresh air. Room air does contain a small amount of CO2 (it just turns limewater milky slowly). Exhaled air has LESS oxygen, not more.

**29. (A)** A flame needs oxygen. Exhaled air has less oxygen (used up by respiration) and more carbon dioxide, so the candle goes out sooner. More oxygen would help a flame, not snuff it. The result is consistent and not chance, since the gases differ. The extra water vapour is real but the main reason the flame dies is the reduced oxygen, not the moisture.

**30. (A)** Milky limewater shows carbon dioxide was produced and the candle going out shows oxygen was used up — both signs of respiration in the living, germinating seeds. They cannot be photosynthesising to make oxygen in a flask with no light. Living seeds clearly do respire, so 'dead' is wrong. Carbon dioxide (not just water vapour) was released, proven by the limewater.

**31. (D)** Aerobic respiration gives carbon dioxide + water; yeast's anaerobic respiration gives alcohol + carbon dioxide. The common product is carbon dioxide. Lactic acid comes from muscle anaerobic respiration, not yeast or aerobic. Oxygen is a reactant in aerobic respiration, not a product. Water is an aerobic product but is not the 'only' product, and it is not produced by yeast fermentation.

**32. (B)** Respiration is a property of all living cells, plant and animal, because all need energy. Plants both photosynthesise and respire, so 'only animals respire' is false. Respiration never stops while alive — even a sleeping animal's cells keep respiring. And in a human every cell respires, not just lung cells (the lungs only handle gas exchange).

**33. (D)** Cells never stop respiring while an organism is alive, so oxygen must be supplied continuously, even in sleep — hence steady breathing. Cells do not pause respiration to 'store air.' Lungs do not make glucose. Breathing is needed at all times, not only during exercise, because resting cells still need energy.

**34. (D)** A fish breathes through gills; a cockroach takes air in through spiracles into tracheae — both correct. The first wrong option swaps gills and spiracles between fish and cockroach. The second gives the earthworm lungs (it uses skin) and the frog only gills (an adult frog uses skin and lungs). The third gives the frog spiracles and the fish moist skin, both wrong.

**35. (C)** The diaphragm is the dome-shaped muscle below the lungs; its movement changes the chest volume to draw air in and push it out. The larynx (voice box) sits above the windpipe and does not drive breathing. A bronchus is an airway branch, not a pump. An alveolus is a tiny air sac for gas exchange, not a muscle.

**36. (A)** Nasal hair and mucus clean the incoming air by trapping dust and germs before it reaches the delicate lungs. They cannot add oxygen — the air's oxygen comes from outside. They do not release energy; that happens inside cells during respiration. They certainly cannot convert carbon dioxide into oxygen.

**37. (A)** Both a flame and a respiring animal consume oxygen and release carbon dioxide; in a sealed jar the oxygen runs out and CO2 builds up, so the flame dies and the mouse struggles. Neither releases oxygen or uses up CO2 — that is the reverse. Sunlight is irrelevant to a candle and a mouse. Neither produces glucose; cells consume glucose.

**38. (B)** Inhalation begins with the muscles moving (diaphragm flattens, ribs rise), which increases chest volume, which lowers the lung pressure, which finally lets air flow in: iii → ii → iv → i. The other orders place air flowing in first, or pressure dropping before the muscles move, or volume changing before the muscles act — all reverse cause and effect.

**39. (B)** Breathing is a physical/mechanical process of moving air in and out (gas exchange); respiration is the chemical process inside cells that releases energy from glucose. The reversed option swaps the two definitions. They are not the same reaction, and respiration is not confined to the lungs. The last option reverses where each occurs.

**40. (B)** Fermentation (yeast respiring without oxygen) gives off carbon dioxide, which does not support burning, so it puts the splint out. Oxygen would make the splint burn brighter, not go out. Hydrogen would give a 'pop,' not a quiet snuffing, and is not a fermentation product. Yeast does not breathe out nitrogen; the snuffing gas is CO2.

**41. (C)** An adult frog breathes through its moist skin and lungs, not gills only, so that pairing is the incorrect one the question wants. A fish does use gills, a cockroach does use tracheae (via spiracles), and a human does use lungs — all correct pairings. So the false one is the frog with 'gills only.'

**42. (B)** Even while holding the breath, cells keep respiring and producing carbon dioxide, which builds up in the blood and drives the urge to breathe. Oxygen is being used up, not building up. Lungs do not make lactic acid (muscles do, and not from simple breath-holding). Muscles make lactic acid, not alcohol; alcohol is a yeast product.

**43. (A)** The trachea (windpipe) is the main tube carrying air to the bronchi and lungs; if it is damaged, air cannot reach healthy lungs, so breathing is impaired. Energy is released in cells, not in the trachea. The trachea does not 'make' oxygen — air carries it. The trachea is an airway, not a blood pump.

**44. (B)** Oxygen passes into the blood at the alveoli — thin-walled sacs in close contact with blood capillaries. A bronchus is a larger branching airway, not where gas crosses into blood. The trachea is the windpipe leading down to the bronchi. The diaphragm is the breathing muscle, not a gas-exchange surface.

**45. (A)** Yeast respires the sugar anaerobically, releasing carbon dioxide; in the sealed flask P this gas is trapped and inflates the balloon. Yeast does not make oxygen. The solution does not boil at room temperature, so it is not steam. Room air did not leak in — the gas was produced inside by the yeast, which is why the open flask Q does not inflate a balloon.

**46. (C)** Respiration uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and water, so exhaled air has less oxygen, more carbon dioxide, and more water vapour than inhaled air. It is not richer in oxygen. It still contains plenty of oxygen (just less), so 'no oxygen at all' is wrong. And the composition clearly changes, so 'exactly the same' is wrong.

**47. (A)** As the working muscles use up the limited oxygen during the dive, they increasingly switch to anaerobic respiration, forming lactic acid. Muscles cannot photosynthesise to make oxygen. One breath cannot keep hard-working muscles fully aerobic indefinitely. Respiration cannot stop in living muscle — cells always need energy.

**48. (B)** Steady consumption of oxygen shows the animal's cells are respiring continuously, using oxygen to release energy. Using up oxygen is the opposite of photosynthesis (which gives out oxygen), so that is wrong. Breathing supplies oxygen precisely so respiration can use it, so 'no respiration inside' makes no sense. The oxygen is being used, not stored unused.

**49. (D)** Whatever organ takes oxygen in — gills for the fish, lungs for the human — the oxygen is ultimately used by cells to release energy from food through respiration. Neither animal photosynthesises. Glucose comes from food, not from the breathing organs. Energy is released from glucose using oxygen; carbon dioxide is a waste product, not a source of energy.

**50. (B)** Respiration produces carbon dioxide (milky limewater) and uses oxygen (candle goes out); seeds in the dark show this because there is no photosynthesis to mask it — proof that plants respire. Oxygen bubbles in sunlight show photosynthesis, not respiration. Growth and flowers opening do not directly demonstrate gas exchange of respiration.

**51. (C)** In exhalation the ribs drop down and in and the diaphragm relaxes upward (domes up), shrinking the chest and raising the lung pressure so air is pushed out. Ribs up/diaphragm flat/pressure falling describes inhalation. A flattening diaphragm enlarges the chest (inhalation), so any option pairing a flat diaphragm with exhalation is wrong.

**52. (D)** The rate climbs from 16 to 24 with exercise, which shows effort raises breathing rate to supply more oxygen and clear more carbon dioxide. The data rise, so exercise does not lower or fix the rate, and breathing certainly does not stop during effort — it speeds up.

**53. (D)** Oxygen allows the glucose to be broken down completely into carbon dioxide and water, which releases the full amount of energy (aerobic respiration). Oxygen does not multiply glucose. Its role is chemical breakdown, not cooling. Oxygen is a gas used in the reaction, not 'energy itself' — the energy comes from breaking down the glucose.

**54. (B)** While holding and pushing out a long phrase, the player's intake of oxygen is reduced and carbon dioxide accumulates, so the gasp afterward restores oxygen and clears carbon dioxide. Lungs do not make alcohol. The gasp is about gas exchange, not cooling the windpipe. Glucose is not made by breathing — it comes from food.

**55. (C)** A pump and the chest both work by changing a volume to move air — the valid comparison. A pump does not respire or break down glucose. It does not extract oxygen from water (that is a gill's job). A pump does not produce carbon dioxide to turn limewater milky on its own; that comes from respiration.

**56. (C)** Aerobic respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy. The version with carbon dioxide as a reactant and oxygen as a product is photosynthesis-like and wrong. The reversed equation is photosynthesis. Leaving out oxygen on the left describes anaerobic respiration, not aerobic. Only glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy is correct for aerobic respiration.

**57. (A)** All three turn limewater milky, showing carbon dioxide is released by the candle (burning), the grasshopper (animal respiration), and the seeds (plant respiration). The living things release CO2, not absorb it. The seeds respire and give out CO2 (not oxygen) in the jar. CO2 is clearly produced without fire too, by the living respiring organisms.

**58. (A)** During the race the muscles respired anaerobically and built up lactic acid; afterwards the body needs extra oxygen to break that lactic acid down, so deep fast breathing continues for a while. The lungs are not mechanically 'stuck.' Muscles make lactic acid, not alcohol. Breathing continues to REMOVE the lactic acid, not to make more of it.

**59. (C)** Air entering reaches the nasal cavity first, then the trachea, then branches into the bronchi, and finally the alveoli where gas exchange occurs. Putting the trachea before the nasal cavity, or the bronchi before the trachea, breaks the real route. The last option is the reverse (exit) order. Only nasal cavity → trachea → bronchi → alveoli is correct for incoming air.

**60. (D)** Respiration produces water, so exhaled air is rich in water vapour; in cold air this vapour condenses into visible mist. Oxygen does not freeze into a cloud at that temperature. Carbon dioxide is a colourless gas and is not what you see. Nitrogen does not condense into mist in ordinary cold air. The visible mist is condensing water vapour.
