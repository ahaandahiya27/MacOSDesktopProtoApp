## Worked Solutions

**1. (D)** The two samples reach one common temperature. Because there is three times as much cold water as hot, the balance point is pulled strongly toward the cold side, not to the plain midpoint: the 80 °C gap is split so that the cold side, being three parts to one, takes only a quarter of the rise. The hot 100 g falls about 60 degrees (from 90 to 30) while the cold 300 g rises about 20 degrees (from 10 to 30) — equal total heat moved, landing near 30 °C. The plain-midpoint answer ignores the unequal amounts. The 'much nearer the hot value' answer wrongly assumes the hotter water wins regardless of quantity; here the cold share is bigger. Temperatures of mixed water never add together, so 100 °C is impossible — that would be hotter than either sample.

**2. (C)** Equal masses do not mean equal share of the gap here, because water takes much more heat to warm one degree than the metal gives up cooling one degree. So the metal cools through a large range while the water warms only a little; the meeting point lands well below the 60 °C midpoint and near the water's 20 °C. The equal-mass midpoint answer ignores that water and metal respond very differently to the same heat. The 'near 100 °C' answer wrongly lets the hotter object dominate; in fact the water barely shifts. And water certainly does change temperature when heat enters it — it simply rises slowly, so it does not stay exactly at 20 °C.

**3. (A)** Temperature tells you how hot each liquid ended up; it does not by itself tell you how much heat energy each one swallowed. Equal final temperature with equal volumes does not force equal heat absorbed, since different liquids warm by different amounts for the same heat. The 'always equal heat' answer confuses equal temperature with equal heat. Oil is not 'always hotter' — both ended at the same temperature by the question's wording. And heat and temperature are related, not unrelated: heat is the energy that flowed in, temperature is the resulting hotness, so 'no heat absorbed' is plainly wrong since both warmed up.

**4. (D)** Heat flows only from a hotter place to a cooler one; with everything at 25 °C there is no temperature difference, so there is no net flow at all. The tank does hold more total heat than the coin, but total heat content does not drive flow — only a temperature gap does, and there is none. The idea that small objects always lose heat to large ones, or that rooms always warm metal coins, both assume a temperature difference that this question removes. Same temperature everywhere means equilibrium and no net heat transfer.

**5. (B)** The mug started much colder than the tea, so heat flowed from the hotter tea into the cooler mug until they approached the same temperature; warming a cold mug takes a fair amount of heat, which is why the tea drops quickly at first. There is no such thing as 'cold flowing in' — only heat flows, and it flows out of the tea, not cold into it. The warm room cannot cool the tea by adding warmth; if anything the room is cooler than 70 °C tea, so the room also receives heat. And tea does not 'make heat' — it only holds the heat it had and loses it; its temperature falls because heat leaves it, chiefly into the cold mug here.

**6. (B)** For equal amounts of the same liquid, the heat taken in depends on the size of the temperature rise, not the final value. P climbs 15 degrees (10 to 25) while Q climbs only 10 degrees (50 to 60), so P needed more heat. Ending at a higher temperature (Q at 60 °C) does not mean more heat was added — Q started high and rose little. 'Same amount of water' alone is not enough; the rises differ, so the heat differs. And heat certainly entered both samples since both warmed; different starting temperatures do not block heat from entering.

**7. (A)** Heat is conserved: the heat the ball loses equals the heat the water gains. But the two change by different numbers of degrees because water resists temperature change far more strongly than the metal does — so the same heat moves the ball's temperature far more than the water's. Heat does not 'disappear', so the loss does not exceed the gain. The two do not change by equal degrees; only the heat amounts are equal, not the temperature swings. And metal does not 'lose all its temperature instantly' — it cools gradually until it matches the water.

**8. (A)** Only the laboratory thermometer reaches −6 °C, since the clinical scale starts at 35 °C — so the brine must use the lab thermometer. The 38.4 °C bath reading must be carried across the room, which needs the kink that holds the thread: that is the clinical thermometer's job. Using the clinical thermometer for everything fails on the brine, which is far below its range. Using the laboratory thermometer for everything fails the 'carry the reading away' need, because its thread falls back the moment it leaves the warm water. The last option reverses both tools: a clinical thermometer cannot reach −6 °C, and a lab thermometer cannot hold a reading while carried.

**9. (A)** The kink in a clinical thermometer keeps the mercury thread from slipping back, so it stays at the highest value reached until shaken down. Since 40.1 °C is higher than the second child's 38.5 °C, the cooler body cannot push the thread up, and it cannot fall on its own — so she still reads about 40.1 °C. The thermometer does not 'reset' itself; that is exactly why it must be shaken. Readings never add together. And the thread does not drift to 37 °C on its own — without a shake it stays where the kink left it.

**10. (C)** Because the body only ranges over a few degrees, a clinical thermometer can devote its whole scale to 35–42 °C, spreading those degrees out so each tiny division is small and a fever can be read very finely. The liquid's ability to expand is not the limit — the same kind of liquid is used in laboratory thermometers over a far wider range. Cost is not the reason; the design is about precision in the body range. And both thermometers measure temperature, not 'heat versus temperature' — that distinction is not what sets their ranges.

**11. (C)** Tracking water to boiling needs a scale reaching about 100 °C and a thread that follows the temperature freely as it rises — exactly the laboratory thermometer. A clinical thermometer's range stops near 42 °C, so it cannot reach boiling, and its kink would lock the highest reading instead of following the changing temperature. Reading in Celsius does not help if the range is too small. And the two thermometers are not interchangeable: only the laboratory one reaches 110 °C, so 'either' is wrong.

**12. (D)** A laboratory thermometer has no kink, so once it leaves the hot water and cools in air, its liquid contracts and the thread falls — the reading changes. A clinical thermometer's kink would hold the highest value, so it would keep the reading. So the two behave oppositely, not identically — 'both locked' is wrong. Having no kink does not freeze a thread; it does the opposite, letting it move freely. And a cooling thermometer's thread falls, it does not climb, so 'both climb after removal' is wrong.

**13. (C)** Across a 100-degree span, X's 200 divisions each cover 100 ÷ 200 = 0.5 °C, while Y's 40 divisions each cover 100 ÷ 40 = 2.5 °C. The smaller a division, the finer the reading, so X (0.5 °C steps) reads more finely than Y (2.5 °C steps). Fewer divisions over the same range means each one covers MORE temperature, not less, so Y is coarser, not finer. Sharing the same range does not make them equally fine — the division count decides that. The last option swaps the two values, which is arithmetically wrong.

**14. (C)** The gap between numbers is 4 degrees split into 8 divisions, so each division is 4 ÷ 8 = 0.5 °C. Five divisions above 20 is 5 × 0.5 = 2.5, giving 20 + 2.5 = 22.5 °C. Counting '5 divisions = 5 degrees' wrongly treats each division as a whole degree. Treating divisions as automatic tenths gives 20.5 °C, but here each division is half a degree, not a tenth. The 24 °C value is the next numbered mark (8 divisions up), but the thread is only 5 divisions up, not 8.

**15. (B)** Each whole degree is split into 5 divisions, so each division is 1 ÷ 5 = 0.2 °C. Four divisions above 38 is 4 × 0.2 = 0.8, giving 38 + 0.8 = 38.8 °C. Reading the divisions as tenths gives 38.4 °C, but here they are fifths (0.2 °C each), not tenths. Four divisions is not the top of the scale — 42 °C is far higher. And 38.04 °C wrongly treats the divisions as hundredths.

**16. (C)** The readings are 31 °C (4:00), 35 °C (4:02), 39 °C (4:04) — a rise of 4 °C every two minutes, i.e. about 2 °C per minute. Continuing at that rate, by 4:06 (two more minutes) it adds 4 °C to 39, reaching about 43 °C. The scale does not stop at 40; the numbered marks continue above 40, so the thread can pass the 10th division. Heating at a steady rate adds equal amounts, it does not double, so 78 °C is wrong. And a rising thread does not freeze at 39 °C; it keeps climbing as the water keeps heating.

**17. (B)** Two degrees split into 4 divisions makes each division 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5 °C. Three divisions above 26 is 3 × 0.5 = 1.5, so 26 + 1.5 = 27.5 °C. Reading divisions as tenths gives 26.3 °C, but here each is half a degree, not a tenth — that student misread the scale. The two values 26.3 and 27.5 are clearly different numbers, so 'both right' is impossible. And 29 °C would require 3 whole degrees, not 3 half-degree divisions, so 'neither' is wrong too.

**18. (A)** Ten degrees split into 10 divisions makes each division 1 °C. Three divisions below the 0 mark is 3 degrees below zero, i.e. −3 °C. The reading is below zero, so it must be negative, not +3 °C. Each division is 1 °C, not 10 °C, so −30 °C overcounts each step tenfold. And the thread is measured from the 0 mark (3 below it), not from the −10 mark, so −13 °C uses the wrong reference point.

**19. (B)** Five degrees split into 5 divisions makes each division 1 °C. The first reading is 10 + 4 = 14 °C; the second is 20 + 1 = 21 °C. The change is 21 − 14 = 7 °C, a rise. Comparing only the division counts (4 versus 1) misses that the second reading sits above the higher 20 mark, so the temperature rose, not fell. The 10-degree value is just the gap between numbered marks, not the actual change. And 17 °C would be the 2nd division above 15, which is not where either reading sits.

**20. (C)** In a solid rod heat travels by conduction — particle to particle along the metal — and copper passes it on far more readily than wood, so the copper bead melts quickly while the wood's bead is barely warmed. The rods are solids, so radiation is not the main mechanism inside them. Convection needs a fluid that can flow; the particles of a solid rod are fixed, so hot metal cannot 'rise' through it. And the wax bead does not generate heat; it only melts when conducted heat arrives, which happens fast in copper and hardly at all in wood.

**21. (D)** Steel conducts heat away from your warm hand much faster than wood, so your skin cools faster touching the steel and it feels colder — even though a thermometer would read 8 °C on both. The two objects are at the same temperature by the question's wording, so the wood is not actually colder. 'Feel' depends on how fast heat leaves your hand, not on temperature alone, so they do not feel equally cold despite matching temperatures. And the steel does not give heat back to a 32 °C hand — your hand is warmer, so heat flows from hand into steel, making it feel cold.

**22. (D)** In copper the particles are close together and vibrate against their neighbours, passing heat quickly from particle to particle. In air the particles are spread far apart and collide only rarely, so energy is handed on very slowly — air conducts poorly. Air is not empty; it does contain particles, just widely spaced. A material's conducting ability is not decided by being colder — copper is the better conductor regardless of temperature. And air's particles do move; conduction in air is slow, not totally stopped — air still conducts a little, just very poorly.

**23. (A)** A metal base conducts the burner's heat readily into the food, while a wood or plastic handle conducts poorly, blocking heat from the grip so it stays cool — both goals met. A wooden base would block heat from reaching the food, defeating the cooking, and a metal handle would carry heat to the hand and burn it. A metal handle does not 'stay coolest'; metals conduct heat to the hand quickly. And a plastic base would resist passing heat into the food, so the food would not cook well.

**24. (D)** Tile is a better conductor than wood, so it draws heat from the warm foot more quickly; the faster cooling of the skin makes the tile feel cold even though a thermometer reads 24 °C on both. The two are at the same temperature by the question's wording, so the tile is not actually colder. Wood does not make its own heat — it just conducts the foot's heat away slowly, so the foot stays warmer and the wood feels neutral. And the foot judges both surfaces by the same rule — how fast heat leaves it — so the comparison is valid, not meaningless.

**25. (D)** Copper, aluminium, and iron are all metals and all good conductors, so this list has no insulator in it. The 'copper, wood, aluminium' list hides wood, which is a poor conductor (an insulator). The 'iron, air, silver' list hides air, which is one of the best insulators when trapped. The 'aluminium, plastic, copper' list hides plastic, a poor conductor. Only the all-metal list qualifies.

**26. (B)** Heated water expands and becomes less dense, so it rises; cooler, denser water from the sides sinks to take its place, and this rising-and-sinking sets up a convection current. Hot water is lighter than cold water, not 'the heaviest', so it rises rather than sinks. Water heats mainly by this moving convection, not by still conduction straight up — water is a poor conductor. And the water does not instantly boil to steam at the base; well below boiling it simply circulates as warm and cool water trade places.

**27. (A)** The dye shows warm water rising directly above the heated corner and cooler water sinking elsewhere, so the convection loop forms over the actual heat source, wherever that is. The rising point follows the heat, so it is not fixed at the centre. There clearly is a current — the dye traces a loop, not still spreading — so conduction alone cannot explain it. And heating makes water lighter, not heavier, so it rises at the heated corner rather than sinking there.

**28. (B)** Warm air rises, so a low heater sends warmth up through the whole room as a convection current; a ceiling heater's warm air simply stays up high, leaving the lower room cool. Warm air does not 'fall down' — it rises, so a high heater does not heat faster. Air carries room heat mainly by convection currents, not by conduction, and those currents depend on the heater's height, so position matters. And warm air rises rather than sinks, so the floor does not become the hottest part under a ceiling heater.

**29. (C)** Convection requires a fluid — a liquid or gas — whose warmed portions can rise and circulate; in a solid copper bar the particles are locked in place and cannot flow, so heat spreads only by conduction. Painting it black affects radiation absorption, not whether a solid can convect. No material convects merely by being hot — it must be able to flow, which solids cannot. And a solid bar certainly can be heated in the middle; the point is only that the resulting heat spreads by conduction, not convection.

**30. (C)** Convection runs strongly when hot, lighter water sits below cold, denser water: in tube Q the hot bottom water rises and the cold top water sinks, so a vigorous current forms. In tube P the hot water is already on top — the stable, lighter-above-heavier arrangement — so there is no buoyancy push and little circulation. Hot water on top does not sink; it is lighter and stays up, so P does not drive a strong current. A temperature difference alone is not enough; the hot layer must be positioned below to rise. And colouring the water does not stop it from convecting.

**31. (D)** The hot air rises away from you, yet you still feel warmth — and it passes through clear glass — which is the signature of radiation: it travels straight from the fire, needs no moving air, and crosses transparent glass. If it were convection, blocking the air with glass would cut the warmth, but it does not. Conduction through a long air-to-glass-to-face chain is far too slow and weak to explain the felt warmth across a gap. And the fire does not 'push cold air' at you to warm you — that would cool you, not warm you.

**32. (C)** A hotter object emits more radiation each second, so the 500 °C plate radiates much more than the 150 °C one. Cooler objects do not radiate faster 'to catch up' — being cooler means emitting less. Although the plates are identical in colour and size, their temperatures differ, and temperature strongly controls how much they emit, so they do not radiate equally. And all warm objects radiate heat, in the dark as well as in sunlight — radiation does not require sunlight to occur.

**33. (D)** In a vacuum there is no matter, so conduction (which needs touching particles) and convection (which needs a flowing fluid) cannot work; only radiation, which travels without any medium, can carry heat away — so the probe cools by radiation. There is no surrounding fluid in a vacuum for convection to stream into. A vacuum has no particles, so it cannot conduct. And the probe does lose heat — radiation works without matter, so it is not stuck keeping all its heat forever.

**34. (A)** A dull dark surface is a better emitter of radiation than a shiny one, so kettle M radiates its heat away faster and its water cools more. Shiny surfaces are poor emitters, so N loses heat more slowly and stays warmer — it is not the cooler one. Equal starting water does not mean equal cooling, because the surfaces emit at different rates. And radiation happens indoors too; all warm objects radiate heat whether or not sunlight is present.

**35. (A)** A dull dark surface is good at both emitting and absorbing radiation: it emits its own heat fast (so the black teapot cools quickest, and the shiny one keeps tea hot) and it absorbs sunlight fast (so the black object warms fastest in the sun). One property — that dark surfaces are strong at both emitting and absorbing — explains both results. Shiny surfaces are poor at both, the opposite of the claim that they 'absorb and emit better'. Dark surfaces can both absorb and emit; they do not 'absorb but not emit'. And colour affects emission as well as absorption, so the teapot result is fully explained, not unrelated.

**36. (B)** Only radiation crosses a vacuum, because it needs no medium; conduction needs particles in contact and convection needs a moving fluid, and a vacuum has neither — so just claim (3) is correct, one in total. Conduction cannot cross a vacuum, so 'two correct' is wrong. Heat does not always find a way — across a vacuum only radiation works, so 'all three' is wrong. And radiation does cross a vacuum without matter, so 'none correct' is also wrong.

**37. (B)** Conduction and convection both need matter, so removing the matter in the gap (the vacuum) blocks them; radiation needs no matter, so the vacuum cannot stop it — instead the shiny silvered walls, being poor emitters, cut the radiation. So vacuum handles conduction and convection, shiny walls handle radiation. The vacuum does not block radiation (radiation crosses a vacuum freely), so the reversed pairing is wrong. The vacuum cannot block radiation by itself, so the shiny walls are not merely decorative. And the shiny walls do not block conduction or convection — that is the vacuum's job — so 'shiny walls block all three' is wrong.

**38. (C)** Foam is full of trapped air, a very poor conductor, so it slows heat loss best; metal is a good conductor, so it carries heat away fastest and keeps the drink hot worst; ceramic falls in between. Metal does not 'seal heat in' — it conducts heat out quickly, so it is the worst, not the best. The three are made of different materials with very different conducting abilities, so they do not keep heat equally. And solid ceramic walls do not beat foam's air pockets — trapped air is the better insulator, so foam wins, not ceramic.

**39. (A)** Trapped, still air conducts heat poorly, so it insulates well in clothing; but when air can move, it carries heat away by convection, so a windy day strips heat from the body and air no longer insulates. Air is not 'always a perfect insulator' — once it moves, convection defeats it. Wind cools you by carrying heat away in moving currents (convection), not because moving air suddenly conducts better. And air neither destroys nor creates heat; it only slows or speeds heat loss depending on whether it is still or moving.

**40. (D)** A shiny silver surface is a poor emitter and a good reflector of radiation, so the thin foil blanket bounces the hiker's own radiated body heat back instead of letting it escape — that is its main trick, different from wool's trapped air. The silver does not 'generate warmth'; no cloth makes heat. The sheet is thin and traps very little air, so it does not work the way wool does. And conducting the cold surroundings 'away' is not how it warms — the point is to keep the body's radiated heat from leaving by reflecting it back.

**41. (B)** Dark surfaces absorb radiation well, so the black coat soaks up more sunlight and feels warmer by day; but dark surfaces also emit well, so at night the black coat sheds the body's own heat faster — that is the trade-off. White reflects sunlight rather than absorbing it, so the white coat is the cooler one in the sun, not the warmer. Black does not 'make heat'; it only absorbs the Sun's radiation, which helps in daylight but not at night. And colour clearly matters in the sun even at equal thickness, because dark and light surfaces absorb sunlight very differently.

**42. (C)** A dull dark surface both absorbs well (so the black box got hotter in the sun) and emits well (so in the dark room it radiates its heat away fastest and cools quickest). Shiny surfaces are poor emitters, so the white box loses heat slowly — it does not cool fastest. Same size and shape does not make them cool equally, because their surfaces emit at very different rates. And a sealed box does not keep heat forever; both cool by radiation, just at different speeds.

**43. (A)** Dark surfaces absorb radiation strongly, so a dull black plate soaks up the most sunlight and passes that heat to the water in the pipes — that is why it is painted black. Black absorbs sunlight; it does not reflect the most (a white surface reflects more), so 'reflects then heats by reflection' is wrong. White absorbs less, not more, so the claim that white would absorb more is backwards. And the plate's colour matters a great deal — a white plate would reflect much of the Sun's heat away, so the glass alone is not what does the heating.

**44. (C)** By day the land heats faster than the sea, so warm air rises over the land and cooler sea air flows in to replace it — a sea breeze blowing from sea to land. The sea does not heat faster than the land by day; the land does, so 'land breeze, sea heats faster' is doubly wrong. The Sun does not 'drag air' across the water; the breeze comes from the rising warm air over the land. And the land does not stay cooler than the sea in the afternoon — it is the warmer surface, which is what drives the inland sea breeze.

**45. (C)** At night the land loses heat faster than the sea, so the sea becomes the warmer surface; warm air rises over the sea and cooler air flows out from the land — a land breeze from land to sea. The sea does not cool faster than the land at night; the land cools faster, so 'sea cooled faster' is wrong. The land is the cooler, not warmer, surface at night, so 'land stays warmer' is wrong. And the reversal is not 'automatic' — it is caused by the land cooling faster than the sea, changing which surface is warmer.

**46. (B)** Breezes are driven by the temperature difference between land and sea; near dawn and dusk the two are changing over and briefly nearly equal, so there is almost no difference to push air, and the wind goes calm. The difference is smallest, not greatest, at those changeover times, so 'greatest difference' is wrong. The Sun is low, not overhead, at dawn and dusk, and its position does not 'cancel' breezes anyway. And the calm is not a fixed clock rule — it follows the land-sea temperatures, which happen to even out around those times.

**47. (D)** Both breezes are the same convection: warm air rises over the hotter surface and cooler air flows in from the cooler surface. Only which surface is warmer changes — land by day, sea by night — so the same process runs in reverse. The land breeze is not conduction; it is convection of moving air, just reversed. The Moon does not drive the land breeze; both breezes come from differential heating and cooling by the Sun's daily cycle. And neither breeze is radiation — moving air is convection in both cases.

**48. (A)** Sea and land breezes need one surface to be warmer so its air rises and cooler air flows in; with the land and sea always at the same temperature there is no warmer side, so no breeze forms in either direction. The sea is not 'always cooler' here — the two are equal by the question's wording, so no sea breeze. Nights do not 'always' send air seaward; that needs the land to cool below the sea, which cannot happen if they stay equal. And a coast does not always produce a breeze; with no temperature difference, there is no driving cause.

**49. (B)** The Sun's heat crosses empty space and is absorbed by the land — that is radiation (step 1). The warm air over the land then rises and cooler sea air flows in to replace it — that moving air is convection (steps 2–3). The Sun cannot conduct heat to the land across space, because conduction needs touching matter, so step 1 is not conduction. The warm air moving sideways is bulk flow, which is convection, not radiation or conduction. And not all steps are conduction; the Sun-to-land step crosses a vacuum (radiation) and the air movement is convection.

**50. (D)** Touching a hot mug transfers heat by direct contact, which is conduction — correct. Feeling warmth across a room from a heater you do not touch is radiation, since it crosses the gap with no contact and no moving air needed — correct. Warm air spreading from a floor heater is moving fluid, which is convection — correct. All three match, so 'only two', 'only one', and 'none' all undercount the correct pairings.

**51. (A)** A thermos slows but never stops heat loss: the vacuum cuts conduction and convection and the shiny lining cuts radiation, yet the cap, the seams, and a small amount of radiation still let heat escape slowly, so the cocoa eventually cools. A thermos does not generate heat of its own, so it is not 'running out of made heat'. A vacuum slows heat loss; it does not speed it up, so the cocoa does not cool as fast as in an open cup. And 'cold' does not flow in — only heat flows, and it slowly leaks out, which is what cools the cocoa.

**52. (B)** Steel conducts heat away from the foot rapidly, so the skin cools fast and the steel feels much colder — but both objects are at the same 1 °C, so a thermometer reads about 1 °C on each. Foam conducts poorly, so it feels less cold, not more, so 'foam feels colder' is wrong. Feel and temperature do not always match — that is the whole point; the steel feels colder even though both read the same. And the thermometer would not read lower on the steel, because the steel is not genuinely colder — it only removes heat from the foot faster.

**53. (C)** There is three times as much hot water as cold, so the meeting point is pulled strongly toward the hot side: the cold 100 g rises about 30 degrees (from 30 to 60) while the hot 300 g falls only about 10 degrees (from 70 to 60), moving equal total heat and landing near 60 °C. The plain midpoint (50 °C) ignores the unequal amounts. Cold does not 'always pull hardest' — here the hot share is bigger, so the mix lands above the midpoint, not below it. And temperatures never add together, so 100 °C — hotter than either sample — is impossible.

**54. (A)** A clinical thermometer's scale stops near 42 °C, far below the 150 °C sample, so it simply cannot reach that temperature — the reading would run off the top of the scale. Liquids do not 'freeze' when heated; freezing happens when cooled, not at 150 °C. The kink only holds the highest value reached, but the real problem is the scale's small range, not the kink. And temperature is measured in Celsius for both — the unit is not the obstacle; the limited range is.

**55. (B)** A laboratory thermometer has no kink, so once the hand is removed the warmed liquid cools and contracts, and the thread falls back toward the room's 22 °C — showing the liquid expands when heated and contracts when cooled. Only a clinical thermometer's kink keeps the highest reading; a laboratory one does not. Once the hand lets go it adds no more heat, so the thread cannot keep rising. And the thread settles at the room temperature it is sensing (about 22 °C), not at 0 °C — a thermometer reads its surroundings, not zero.

**56. (D)** The dark bare ground absorbs more sunlight and gets hotter, so it warms the air above it more; that warmer air rises faster as a stronger convection current and carries the smoke up. Smoke is not 'naturally lighter' over one kind of ground — what differs is how much the ground heats the air. Conduction does not 'push' smoke upward; the rising is buoyant convection. And radiation does not grab and lift particles — it is the heating of the air, then convection, that lifts the smoke.

**57. (B)** A white surface reflects most of the Sun's radiation, so the whitewashed walls absorb less heat and the rooms stay cooler. Dark colours absorb more heat, warming the house, so they do not 'keep it cool' — the dark house is hotter, not cooler. Equal wall thickness does not make them equally cool, because the surface colours absorb sunlight very differently. And white reflects rather than absorbs sunlight, so the white house is cooler, not hotter.

**58. (D)** Foam with a lid insulates the walls and blocks the open top, so it stays hottest longest. Wool wrapped on a metal cup adds a poor-conducting trapped-air layer, beating a plain foam cup which still loses heat from its open top. The bare uncovered metal cup conducts heat away fastest, so it cools soonest. The reversed order wrongly puts the worst insulator (bare metal) first and the best (lidded foam) last. The four are different in insulation, so they do not cool at the same rate. And a plain foam cup with an open top loses more from the top than the lidded foam or the wool-wrapped metal, so it is not the best.

**59. (D)** Two degrees split into 4 divisions makes each division 0.5 °C. Morning: 18 + (1 × 0.5) = 18.5 °C. Afternoon: 22 + (3 × 0.5) = 23.5 °C. The change is 23.5 − 18.5 = 5 °C. Comparing only the division counts (3 versus 1) ignores that the readings sit above different numbered marks, two degrees apart. The 4-degree gap between the 18 and 22 marks is not the actual change, since the two readings are not on the marks themselves. And treating divisions as tenths gives the wrong readings (18.1 and 23.1); here each division is half a degree, not a tenth, so the values are 18.5 and 23.5.

**60. (A)** Most of the distance between the Sun and Earth is empty space with no matter, and conduction needs touching particles, so it cannot work across a vacuum — the heat must travel by radiation, which needs no medium. There is no continuous air bridge to the Sun, so conduction through air is impossible. Convection needs a flowing fluid, but space is a vacuum with no fluid to flow, so that fails too. And it is not conduction through the upper air either — the vast vacuum between rules out conduction entirely, leaving radiation as the only mode.
