1. A boy in the parable says: 'I counted नव; therefore one of us is मग्नः.' His reasoning fails at exactly one step. Which?

4. The boys recount several times and keep getting नव. What does the repetition prove about their error?

11. Why is the पथिकः, and not the cleverest boy, the one who must reveal the tenth?

Worked Solutions

1. (C) The arithmetic 'nine present, ten started, so one lost' is valid IF the count of nine were true. It is not: each counter skips himself, so the real present total is ten and the count should read दश. The break is in the premise (the miscount), not in the inference or in the meaning of मग्नः. So the chain is sound logic on a false fact. The 'asleep' swap is wrong (मग्नः = drowned); 'all safe' is the true situation but is not where HIS reasoning breaks; and agreeing with him ignores the self-omission the whole story turns on.

2. (D) The error is structural: a counter cannot include himself in his own count, so he always lands one short of the true total, whatever the size. More counters do not cancel it — each repeats it. They are not copying one another; each independently undercounts by exactly one (not two — the पथिकः stands outside the group and is not part of 'the boys' being counted). This generalises the off-by-one self-omission beyond the specific number ten.

3. (A) An outside counter (the पथिकः) includes all ten and gets ten; any boy counting from inside the group excludes himself and gets nine. The total present never changes — boys do not come and go, and none is swept away (no one actually drowned). It is not a skill gap in counting. The variable is the counter's vantage point: a self-excluding insider versus an all-including outsider.

4. (A) Consistency of a wrong answer signals a systematic flaw, not accuracy. Each recount is done the same way — a boy counting the others while skipping himself — so it reproduces the off-by-one error identically. No new boy drowns each time (nobody drowned at all), and rivers do not count. Reliability of a method does not make it valid; here the repeated नव is the fingerprint of the same self-forgetting.

5. (D) The fix targets the exact flaw: include the omitted self by adding one for the counter. Averaging two undercounts of nine still yields nine. Counting only those in the river loses even more boys (those on the bank). Care or speed is irrelevant — even the most careful boy structurally cannot count himself unless the procedure tells him to add himself. Only 'न' + adding oneself repairs the off-by-one.

6. (D) Ten truly crossed (Q on Paper-1 fixes the number at ten) and ten are truly present; the 'missing' one is missing only from each boy's awareness — a blind spot, hence epistemic, not a real (ontic) absence. No rescue occurs because no one is lost. The tenth did exist and did cross. The gap is between knowing and being, which is precisely why a mere shift in viewpoint (the पथिकः) dissolves it.

7. (A) The defect is exclusion of exactly one element — the counting subject himself — from the counted set, every time. Sanskrit numerals plainly go past nine (दश, एकादश...), so it is no linguistic ceiling. They do not double-count anyone, and the पथिकः is outside the group, not wrongly included. Precise wording matters at olympiad level: it is self-exclusion, not over-counting or a vocabulary limit.

8. (A) The whole point is that a fixed external reference (a name list, or the outside पथिकः) counts all ten regardless of who reads it, because the reader's own name is already on it — the self is not omitted. A correctly made list includes everyone by definition. It is not about counting past nine (they can) nor about removing anyone. This mirrors why the पथिकः, an outside reference, succeeds where each insider fails.

9. (B) Surprise (आश्चर्य/विस्मय) is the natural first response when a trusted result (their नव) is overturned by an authority's दश; relief/joy (हर्ष, प्रहृष्टाः) follows once they grasp it. The traveler does count and does communicate the number, in terms they understand. They had NOT found the tenth themselves — that is exactly what they were failing to do. The emotional sequence is surprise → joy, matching विस्मय preceding हर्ष.

10. (B) 'दश' alone gives only a total; 'त्वम्' (you) pins the uncounted one onto the very person who kept missing him — himself. The traveler is not naming himself the tenth (he is outside the group). असि is the verb 'are' (a statement), not a command (that would be भव) nor a question. There is no two-bank choice. The pronoun is the hinge that converts arithmetic into self-recognition — the seed of the Vedantic reading.

11. (D) The flaw is positional, not intellectual: any member counting from inside omits himself no matter how clever. Standing outside, the पथिकः includes every boy. No boy drowned, so 'the cleverest drowned' is false; knowing the road has nothing to do with set-counting; and the boys were plainly counting aloud, not silent. The outsider's vantage — not extra cleverness — is decisive.

12. (C) Arithmetically the line just restores the missing unit: nine seen plus the overlooked self makes ten. The teaching reading takes that overlooked self and identifies it with the very one who was searching — हence तत्त्वमसि. Both readings agree on the number; they differ on what the +1 MEANS. The line names no river, असि is present tense throughout, and the point is recognition, not blame.

13. (A) Saying 'no one drowned' removes the false alarm but leaves the procedural error intact — they would undercount again, because the self-omission is untouched. 'दशमः त्वम् असि' fixes the cause by pointing to the omitted self. It is not stronger or identical: it lacks the crucial 'you'. The weakness is conceptual (it never addresses the blind spot), not just a matter of choosing नवमः vs दशमः.

14. (C) He gives no new boy and performs no rescue (none drowned); he imposes no punishment and orders no census. What he does is dispel a misconception by turning the boys' gaze onto the overlooked self — the classic teacher's act in the Vedantic frame. Hence गुरु-figure, not rescuer, judge, or king.

15. (A) In तत्त्वमसि, तत् is 'That' (the sought reality / Self), त्वम् 'you', असि 'are' — 'That you are.' Mapping onto the parable, 'That (the tenth being sought)' is identified with 'you (the seeker).' The river is the obstacle crossed, not the goal; the traveler is the teacher who reveals, not the sought; counting is the activity, not the object. So तत् ↔ the tenth, and त्वम् ↔ the boy who is it.

16. (B) The tenth is each counter himself — fully present, in fact the most active member (he is counting!). What was missing was his inclusion of himself in his own awareness, not his body. He did not hide and return, no substitute stood in, and no new boy was created. The grief was over an illusion: the loss was only in the counting, never in reality — the heart of the 'Self present all along' reading.

17. (D) On the surface it is a number puzzle resolved by an outside viewpoint; underneath, the 'tenth' the boys hunt for is themselves — the Self is identical with the seeker (तत्त्वमसि). It is not a race, a flood-safety tale, or a market story; those belong to other chapters. The two levels — arithmetic and metaphysical — are bound by the single image of the overlooked self.

18. (C) 'Double-check' is good advice but misses two things: (1) the error is structural self-omission, which more checks of the same kind reproduce, and (2) the chapter's whole point is the Self/seeker identity (तत्त्वमसि). It is not the WHOLE lesson; the river's danger and the village name are incidental and never the teaching. The metaphysical layer is what elevates this above a counting tip.

19. (C) The analogy is exact: the one thing a counter cannot see is the counter — the nearest item of all — mirroring how the Self, being the very subject, is overlooked while one searches outward. It is not about forgetting others' names or miscounting coins, and the parable is explicitly framed (तत्त्वमसि) to carry this metaphysical mapping. Nearness causing oversight is the shared structure.

20. (C) The chapter binds the parable to तत्त्वमसि: the sought tenth is the seeker. Careful arithmetic and outsider-wisdom are surface morals at best (and outsiders aren't always wiser — the पथिकः wins only by vantage, not knowledge). The group-of-ten line is a literal misreading. Only the Self-is-the-seeker statement names the chapter's actual theme.

21. (D) Position in a sequence is ordinal: the last boy is the दशमः ('tenth'). A bare count of how many there are is cardinal: दश ('ten'). The two roles are distinct, so neither word does both jobs, and they are not swapped (the last one is 'tenth', the total is 'ten'). This is the cardinal/ordinal division applied to the parable's own headcount.

22. (D) Ordinals are adjectives and agree with their noun; the masculine दशमः has the feminine दशमी (-ई stem). With बालिका (feminine), agreement gives दशमी बालिका. It does not stay masculine (दशमः), one does not switch to the cardinal दश for females, and बालिका is feminine not neuter, so दशमम् is wrong. Self-verified: ordinal adjectives inflect for gender, हence दशमी.

23. (B) The ordinals 'first, third, fifth, seventh, ninth' are प्रथमः, तृतीयः, पञ्चमः, सप्तमः, नवमः — correctly increasing. The एकम्/त्रीणि/... list is cardinals, not ordinals. The list with द्वितीयः then चतुर्थः then तृतीयः is out of order (fourth before third). The last list is high-to-low, not low-to-high. Only the first is both all-ordinal and ascending.

24. (C) दशमः → दशमी for a feminine noun shows the ordinal agreeing like an adjective; the plain count 'ten' does not track the counted noun's gender the same way. So the contrast is exactly the reverse of 'ordinals never change, cardinals always agree.' They are not both fully agreeing, and Sanskrit words certainly do inflect. The parable's headcount (दश boys) vs ordinal place (दशमः boy) illustrates this.

25. (C) नवमः = ninth, दशमः = tenth — consecutive positions; तु ('but/whereas') simply contrasts the two. They are different boys (ninth ≠ tenth), and they are adjacent, not first-and-last. नवमः ('ninth', ordinal of नव) must not be confused with नव(-ीन) 'new'; this is an ordinal, not an age word. So: neighbours in line.

26. (C) 'दशमः कः?' = 'The tenth — who (is he)?' Both words are masculine nominative singular and refer to the same individual; कः seeks his identity, with असि/अस्ति understood. कः is not accusative (that would be कम्), neither word is accusative here, and दशमः is an ordinal adjective/substantive, not a verb. Agreement of दशमः and कः in nominative is what makes them co-refer.

27. (B) 'For the first time' uses the ordinal प्रथम-, here in the adverbial/neuter form प्रथमम्. एकम् is the cardinal 'one', not 'first'. दशमम् is 'tenth', the wrong rank. And one cannot keep प्रथमः unchanged when an adverbial neuter प्रथमम् is required — ordinals inflect. So प्रथमम् is right; the trap is cardinal-for-ordinal and frozen-form errors.

28. (A) A counter enumerates the others and stops; the unreached position is the one his own self would have occupied — effectively the top of the count, leaving him at nine and the true tenth (himself) unreached. It is not the first (he can see the first) nor the middle, and it is not random — it is systematically the self, the omitted top. This ties the off-by-one directly to the highest ordinal दशमः.

29. (D) तृ ('cross') is transitive; what is crossed — the river — is its कर्म (object), so it takes द्वितीया (accusative): नदीम्. The subject is the boys (who 'went', गताः), not the river. Gerunds do not force dative, and there is no rule making 'river' inherently accusative — it is accusative here only because it is the object of crossing. Self-verified: object of तीर्त्वा → accusative नदीम्.

30. (D) A Sanskrit gerund (ल्यबन्त like तीर्त्वा) shares its subject with the finite verb of the sentence. Here both the crossing and the going are done by the boys (बालकाः, nominative plural). The river is the object crossed, not a doer; the gerund has no independent doer; and पारम् ('to the far bank') is accusative goal, not the subject. So one subject — the boys — performs both actions.

31. (B) Each gerund denotes an action completed before the next; the finite verb (अखादन्, 'they ate') is last in time. So the sequence follows the surface order: तीर्त्वा (crossed) → गत्वा (went) → अखादन् (ate). The finite verb is not first in time; the actions are sequential, not simultaneous; and the order is cross-then-go (river before forest), as written. Gerund chaining = ordered prior actions.

32. (C) तीर्त्वा is an indeclinable gerund pairing with a finite verb: 'crossed the river, (then) went home' — correct. It cannot be the predicate of अस्ति ('is') as if a participle of state. Pairing तीर्त्वा with अतरत् ('crossed') is redundant and ill-formed (the gerund and finite verb name the same crossing). तीर्त्वति is not a word — तीर्त्वा does not conjugate (it is indeclinable). Only the first is well-formed.

33. (B) तीर्त्वा is the gerund of तृ; its लङ्-लकार third-person plural is अतरन् ('they crossed'). अगायन् is from गा ('sing'), अगणयन् from गण् ('count'), and अमज्जन् from मस्ज् ('sink/drown', the verb behind मग्नः). Only अतरन् shares the root तृ with तीर्त्वा. This keeps the four key parable-roots — cross, sing, count, drown — apart.

34. (C) The gerund (अव्यय/ल्यबन्त) तीर्त्वा is fixed in form regardless of who crosses or how many. गताः, a past participle, agrees as masculine nominative plural and would shift (e.g. गता for a feminine subject). तीर्त्वा does not change for tense either (it has no finite tense). गताः is not indeclinable. So तीर्त्वा = unchanging, गताः = agreeing — the defining contrast.

35. (A) तरन् is the present (शतृ) participle 'crossing/while crossing'; it does not convey the completed-before sense that तीर्त्वा ('having crossed') gives. नदीम् is correctly accusative (object crossed), not the subject. गताः is correctly plural for 'they.' The genuine fault is using a simultaneous present participle where a prior-action gerund is meant — exactly the तीर्त्वा vs तरन् distinction.

36. (B) The initial अ- here is the augment (अडागम) prefixed to past-tense (लङ्/लुङ्/लृङ्) forms; in अगणयत् it marks लङ्-लकार, 'he counted.' It is NOT the negating अ- of compounds (that would not carry the past-tense ending -त्). The future would be गणयिष्यति; a command (लोट्) would be गणयतु. So अ- = past augment, giving 'he counted.'

37. (A) लङ्-लकार third-person: singular अगणयत्, plural अगणयन्. The form does change for number, so अगणयत् cannot serve as plural. गणयिष्यन्ति is future ('will count'), not past. अगायन् is from गा ('sing'). Self-verified: 'they counted' = अगणयन्, the plural of अगणयत्.

38. (A) गण् gives present गणयति and past अगणयत् — same root, correct tense shift. गणयति → अगायत् switches to root गा (sing). गायति → अगणयत् likewise mismatches sing-present with count-past. तरति → अमज्जत् switches तृ (cross) to मस्ज् (sink). Only गणयति → अगणयत् keeps one root across the tense change.

39. (B) मग्नः ('drowned/submerged') is the past participle of मस्ज् ('sink'); its लङ्-लकार 'he sank' is अमज्जत्. अगणयत् is from गण् (count), अतरत् from तृ (cross), अगायत् from गा (sing). Only अमज्जत् matches the 'drowned' idea behind मग्नः. The trap is letting the surrounding count/cross/sing roots leak in.

40. (B) अगायत् (गा, sing) and अगणयत् (गण्, count) differ in their root portion: गा-य- versus गण्-अय-. The contrast is in the consonant cluster (य vs णय), reflecting the different roots — exactly the गा-vs-गण् warning of the chapter. Adding अ- does not relate sing to count; changing त्→न् only shifts to plural; and the forms ARE relatable through their distinct roots, not suppletive. Listening for the ण्/count consonant is the chapter's own cue.

41. (A) Cross = तृ (→ तरति, तीर्त्वा), sing = गा (→ गायति), count = गण् (→ गणयति, अगणयत्), drown/sink = मस्ज् (→ मग्नः). The other sets swap cross with count, or count with sing, or substitute ज्ञा (know) / reorder. Only तृ/गा/गण्/मस्ज् pairs each action with its right root — the four roots the chapter deliberately keeps distinct.

42. (D) The present गणयति lacks the अ-augment (which marks past) and ends in -ति; अगणयत् has the augment and the लङ् ending -त्, so it is past, not present. The present does NOT take the augment, so 'अगणयति' is impossible. अगणयन् is the past plural, not the present. So the two markers of pastness — augment अ- and the लङ् ending — both rule out a present reading.

43. (D) Ten is 'many' (more than two), so 'which of many?' = कतमः. कतरः is reserved for a choice between exactly two. कः is the general 'who' but not the precise 'which-of-a-set' word the question calls for. किम् ('what') is for things, not for selecting a boy. The number of options (ten) dictates कतमः.

44. (A) Exactly two paths → 'which of two' = कतर-, here instrumental कतरेण (by which path). कतम- is for many, wrong when there are only two. केन ('by which/what') is the general form and does not encode the two-way restriction the question highlights. कतिभिः asks 'by how many', a different question. So कतरेण पथा is precise for a two-way fork.

45. (B) कति = how many, कदा = when, कतरः = which of two, कतमः = which of many. Matching in the listed order gives 'how many / when / which of two / which of many.' The other orderings misalign at least one pair, and none of the four words means 'who' (कः) or 'what' (किम्). Self-verified term by term.

46. (A) With ten candidates the correct selector is कतमः ('which of many'); कतरः is strictly for a two-way choice, so it is the wrong word for a field of ten. कतरः is not a verb and does not mean 'how many' (that is कति). It does carry the false 'someone drowned' assumption, but the word-level error the question targets is two-vs-many: कतरः vs कतमः.

47. (B) Reduced to two, 'which of two?' = कतरः, with the genitive dual अनयोः ('of these two'). कतमः (which of many) is now too broad. कति asks 'how many', a count not a selection. The 'who...when' form changes the question entirely. So narrowing from many to two flips कतमः → कतरः — the precise contrast the chapter teaches.

48. (C) कतमः/कतरः both ask 'which?' and differ only in how many options exist (many vs two). The other listed pairs differ in CATEGORY of question: कः asks about a person, किम् about a thing; कदा asks time, कुत्र place; कति number, कदा time. None of those is a pure set-size contrast. Hence the correct choice is that NONE of the offered pairs matches — they differ in kind, not in set size.

49. (B) The तद्धित suffix -इक (with -अः for masculine) forms 'one connected with X'; from पथिन् ('road') it yields पथिकः, 'one of the road' = traveller/wayfarer. It is not a diminutive ('small road'), not an action noun ('road-building'), and not a negative ('roadless'). Knowing पथिकः is a common noun built this way (not a proper name) is the chapter's point.

50. (D) 'दशमः कः?' = 'Who is the tenth?'; the elided copula is अस्ति, third-person singular, matching the singular दशमः/कः. असि (2nd person) belongs to the traveller's later direct address 'दशमः त्वम् असि', not to this open question. सन्ति (plural) would need a plural subject; here the subject is one person. The question is in the narrative present, not past अभवत्. Self-verified: understood verb = अस्ति (3 sg).

51. (D) विषण्णाः ('dejected') is a predicate adjective/participle qualifying बालकाः ('boys'), so it agrees as masculine nominative plural. It is not accusative — a verb of being (अभवन्, 'became') takes a nominative complement, not an object. It is plural, not singular (all the boys grieve), and it agrees with the masculine बालकाः, not some abstract 'feminine sadness'. Agreement: nominative plural masculine matching the subject.

52. (A) ततः here is the sequencing adverb 'then/thereafter', marking that प्रहृष्टाः (delighted) comes after विषण्णाः (sad) — the emotional turn once the tenth is found. It is not a causal 'because', and while ततः can mean 'from there' (ablative sense of तद्) the narrative use is temporal here. It carries no negation. So ततः = 'then', ordering sad → (discovery) → glad.

53. (C) विषण्णाः (sad) → प्रहृष्टाः (delighted) is a negative-to-positive shift; दुःखिताः → सन्तुष्टाः (sorrowful → contented) matches that direction. प्रहृष्टाः → विषण्णाः is the reverse (positive → negative). श्रान्ताः → क्लान्ताः stays negative (both 'tired'). विस्मिताः → आश्चर्यचकिताः stays within surprise (no relief). Only sorrow → contentment mirrors the parable's emotional arc.

54. (C) On hearing 'दशमः त्वम् असि,' the boys first feel विस्मय (surprise/wonder that nine was wrong), then हर्ष (joy/relief) as the truth lands — so wonder is intermediate, joy final. विस्मय is not a synonym of हर्ष (one is surprise, the other joy). Their starting state was विषण्णाः (sad/grieving), not wonder. And no one actually drowned, so विस्मय causes no drowning. Sequence: sad → surprised → joyful.

55. (B) Before a consonant, a word-final म् may be written as anusvāra: नदीम् → नदीं (then तीर्त्वा). The dot represents the म् sound, not an added long vowel, not a meaningless mark, and not part of the gerund (तीर्त्वा keeps its own form). So नदीं = नदीम् with the म् shown as anusvāra — a spelling/sandhi convention, not a change of word.

56. (D) All four right: पथिकः = traveller (not the road itself), अगणयत् = he counted (गण्, not गा 'sang'), मग्नः = drowned/submerged (not delighted), प्रहृष्टाः = delighted (not sad, not drowned). Each distractor flips exactly one pair (road / sang / mood-swaps), so only the first set is fully correct. This bundles the chapter's key vocabulary traps.

57. (A) All four correct: तीर्त्वा is an indeclinable gerund; अगणयत् is लङ्-लकार (imperfect past); नदीम् is accusative (object of crossing); दशमः is an ordinal ('tenth'). Each distractor breaks one: calling तीर्त्वा a future verb, or अगणयत् present, or नदीम् nominative/dative, or दशमः a cardinal. Only the first keeps every label right — the chapter's full grammar profile.

58. (C) The summary invents a real drowning and a physical rescue, contradicting the parable: the tenth was present all along (each boy's own uncounted self), and the पथिकः reveals the truth ('दशमः त्वम् असि') rather than saving anyone. The error is not a river/lake or walk/dive detail — it is the fabricated loss-and-rescue, which erases the chapter's whole point. So the root error is treating an epistemic gap as a real drowning.

59. (D) Correct chain: gerund तीर्त्वा (completed crossing, past), then अगणयत् (counted, past) yielding नव through self-omission, resolved by 'दशमः त्वम् असि' — the self was present all along. The distractors break it: 'will cross/sang/drowned' mislabels tense, root, and outcome; 'found दश, no worry' erases the miscount that drives the story; and 'the traveller was the tenth' is false — the tenth is each boy himself, the पथिकः stands outside. Only the first is consistent in grammar and meaning.

60. (B) The forms serve the story: दशमः (ordinal) names the sought one, तीर्त्वा (gerund) orders 'crossed then counted', अगणयत् (past) narrates, and कतमः frames 'which of the many?'. So grammar and parable interlock — not unrelated padding, and the story is not merely a grammar drill (it carries तत्त्वमसि). Nothing in the grammar 'proves' a real drowning — none occurred. Tools and tale are one fabric.
