## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** The name marks how the political map kept being rearranged: after the Mauryas broke up, regions were reshaped into many rival kingdoms that fought and allied for power. There was no single unifying empire in this age, and the reorganisation was of the whole map of kingdoms, not merely of Mauryan provinces.

**2. (D)** The empire broke from WITHIN: around 185 BCE the last Maurya ruler was assassinated by his commander-in-chief, Pushyamitra Shunga. The foreign peoples entered the northwest only later, after the breakup had weakened the frontier; they did not cause the collapse.

**3. (B)** Pushyamitra Shunga, the Mauryan commander-in-chief, assassinated the last emperor and founded the Shunga dynasty. Kharavela ruled Kalinga, Kanishka was a later Kushana, and Gautamiputra was a Satavahana, none of whom ended Mauryan rule.

**4. (A)** Because the breakup is dated before the invasions, it cannot have been caused by them; instead the collapse left the frontier undefended, and the Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Kushanas exploited that opening afterwards. Cause precedes effect here.

**5. (B)** The chapter's big idea is that this was at once an age of fierce war-and-alliance competition AND a time of remarkable cultural confluence: new art schools, busy trade and great Sanskrit texts. War and cultural flowering happened together; one did not cancel the other.

**6. (A)** Kings married their children into other dynasties to seal peace and join lands, a deliberate political tool that often achieved what war could not. These were monarchies, not elective states, and the alliances kept rival identities alive rather than abolishing them.

**7. (A)** A matrimonial alliance was a tool of POLITICS, not just a wedding: it cemented friendship and merged territories between dynasties. It was diplomatic, not a private affair, not a religious rite, and not a tribute payment.

**8. (A)** The ashvamedha was a Vedic ritual asserting supreme kingship: a horse was let loose and all the territory it wandered across unopposed was claimed by the king. It was about declaring paramount power, not elections, marriages or calendars.

**9. (D)** A queen performing this supreme-kingship ritual shows that Satavahana royal women held genuine authority, consistent with kings taking mother-names and queens donating land and commissioning inscriptions. It contradicts the idea that women were mere royal decoration.

**10. (B)** The Shungas of north and central India revived Vedic ritual and are linked to the carved railings of the Bharhut Stupa. Sea trade with Rome was the southern and Satavahana story, the Silk Route the Kushanas', and the Shaka era the Shakas'.

**11. (B)** A Vedic-reviving dynasty still connected with carved railings on a Buddhist stupa shows the age's mixing of traditions; reviving one set of rituals did not stop art for another. It does not make the Shungas Buddhist, nor the stupa a Vedic temple, and Buddhism was clearly still alive.

**12. (B)** A stupa is a dome-shaped Buddhist monument, frequently decorated with carved railings depicting religious stories. The poem anthology is Sangam literature, the river dam is an anicut, and the pillar describes something like the Heliodorus pillar.

**13. (D)** The Shungas were a north-and-central-Indian dynasty. The Tamil south belonged to the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas, the Deccan to the Satavahanas, and Central Asia to peoples like the Kushanas.

**14. (D)** The Satavahanas were the great dynasty of the Deccan. The Tamil south had the three crowned kingdoms, north-central India the Shungas, and the northwest saw the Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Kushanas.

**15. (C)** Ships stamped on Satavahana coins point to active maritime trade, which reached as far as Rome. The coins are read as evidence of commerce, not sea-worship, territorial loss, or naval war with the inland Kushanas.

**16. (D)** 'Gautamiputra' means 'son of Gautami', a mother-name. The Satavahana custom of mother-names reflects the real influence of royal women. It is not a god's name, a city, or a monastic title.

**17. (A)** Gautami Balashri and other Satavahana royal women donated land and had inscriptions carved recording their gifts, which is how historians know of them. They were not army commanders against Rome, Tamil Sangam poets, or Gandhara sculptors.

**18. (D)** All three facts show royal women wielding real authority, directly contradicting the belief that queens were mere decoration. The other statements are accepted facts that this evidence does not challenge.

**19. (C)** Sea routes from the Deccan's western ports linked the Satavahanas to Roman markets. They did not border Rome, were never a Roman province, and the overland Silk Route was the Kushanas' domain, not theirs.

**20. (D)** Kharavela was the Chedi king of Kalinga. He was neither a Satavahana of the Deccan, a Kushana of Gandhara, nor a Chera of the Tamil south.

**21. (C)** Kharavela combined kingship with deep devotion to Jainism, hence 'monk-king'. He did not abdicate, was not a non-ruling Buddhist monk, and did not invent a new religion.

**22. (C)** The Hathigumpha inscription is carved writing recording a ruler's deeds, exactly the kind of inscription historians depend on. It is not Tamil poetry, a Roman record, or a coin portrait.

**23. (D)** An inscription is text cut into a lasting material such as stone or metal, frequently recording deeds, gifts or beliefs. A stamped coin, an oral poem and a wall painting are different kinds of evidence.

**24. (C)** The Tamil south's three crowned kingdoms were the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas. The other sets belong to north/central-Indian dynasties, the northwestern invaders, and the great northern empires respectively.

**25. (B)** Sangam literature, the oldest Tamil poetry on love, heroism and generosity, gives the Sangam Age its name. It is named for a body of poetry, not for battles, a royal election, or a Buddhist council.

**26. (A)** Sangam literature expresses themes of love, heroism and generosity. It is Tamil court-and-life poetry, not a manual of Vedic ritual, a Kushana chronicle, or Silk-Route trade law.

**27. (C)** Roman coins in the south reflect brisk sea trade between the Tamil kingdoms and Rome. They were never Roman subjects, did not mint in Rome, and their kings were crowned at home.

**28. (C)** The Pandyas were a Tamil southern kingdom. The Cholas (also Tamil south) did not rule the Deccan (that was the Satavahanas), Kalinga was the Chedis' (not the Cheras'), and the northwest belonged to the invaders, not the Pandyas.

**29. (D)** The Grand Anicut is an ancient dam across the Kaveri that diverted water across the delta, making it a rich rice region. It is not a stupa, a pillar, or a fort.

**30. (A)** From Tamil anai ('dam') + kattu ('to build'), an anicut is a low dam that lifts river water into canals. It is not a marriage treaty (alliance), a monument (stupa), or a calendar (samvat).

**31. (C)** By spreading the Kaveri's water across the delta, the Anicut created one of India's richest rice lands, feeding the population and armies behind Chola power. It was an irrigation work, not a naval barrier, a fortress, or a boundary marker.

**32. (D)** The Cheras and Pandyas prospered on sea trade, reaching as far as Rome. The Silk Route was the Kushanas' overland network; they received no Kushana tribute and held no Central Asian mines.

**33. (B)** Roman familiarity with Tamil-coast goods points to lively maritime commerce between south India and the Mediterranean: trade, not conquest, colonisation, or Tamil rule in Italy.

**34. (B)** Both the Deccan Satavahanas and the southern Tamil kingdoms traded with Rome by SEA, using their coastal ports. Neither relied on the overland Silk Route, which connected China to the Mediterranean through the Kushana lands.

**35. (A)** The Indo-Greeks descended from the Greek satraps Alexander left in the northwest. They were not Shungas, Chedis, or Kushanas.

**36. (C)** A Greek envoy raising a pillar in devotion to an Indian deity shows cultural blending, with the Indo-Greeks absorbing Indian beliefs. It is the opposite of forcing Greek gods, shunning Indian religion, or destroying temples.

**37. (C)** Much of our knowledge of the Indo-Greek kings comes from their coins, which preserve names and portraits. The coins were real trade currency, not Roman-minted, and carried no Tamil Sangam poetry.

**38. (C)** The Mauryan collapse left the northwest poorly defended, opening the door to the Indo-Greeks. The southern Cholas did not invite them, the Kushana Silk Route came later, and the Shaka era is unrelated to their arrival.

**39. (A)** Though they arrived as conquerors, the Indo-Greeks and Kushanas were deeply influenced by India's culture, producing blends in which Indian themes stayed dominant. They neither erased Indian culture, nor stayed wholly separate, nor unified all India.

**40. (B)** The Shakas are called the Indo-Scythians. The Indo-Greeks were a different group of invaders; 'Indo-Romans' and 'Indo-Cholas' are not terms used in the chapter.

**41. (A)** A calendar era, the Shaka Samvat, was developed in the Shaka period and India's national calendar still uses it. The Julian calendar is Roman, and the other two are not calendars at all.

**42. (C)** From Sanskrit samvatsara ('year'), a samvat is a reckoned era counted from a fixed starting point. The dam is an anicut, the shrine a stupa, and the marriage a matrimonial alliance.

**43. (B)** A living national calendar rooted in the Shaka era is proof the age left enduring legacies. It is evidence against the ideas that nothing survived, that Romans ran it, or that it was cut off from Indian tradition.

**44. (B)** Kanishka is the great Kushana king. Kharavela was a Chedi, Pushyamitra a Shunga, and Gautamiputra a Satavahana.

**45. (A)** The Kushanas commanded stretches of the Silk Route, the great overland trade network between China and the Mediterranean. The Anicut was Chola, the Bharhut railings Shunga, and the Sangam academies Tamil.

**46. (D)** The Silk Route was an overland network through Central Asia and Persia linking China to the Mediterranean. It was not a single Deccan sea lane, a Chola delta road, or a poets' circuit.

**47. (D)** Kanishka's coins record his titles and depict a range of gods he honoured, evidence of his power and the era's religious mixing. They do not show him rejecting religion, ruling the Tamil south, or shunning trade.

**48. (A)** Kushana overland trade plus Satavahana and southern sea trade means India was linked to distant markets by both land and sea at once, not isolated, village-bound, or reliant on one route.

**49. (A)** Gandhara art clothed Buddhist subjects in Greek and Roman forms, such as wavy hair and toga-like robes, a Greco-Buddhist fusion of the northwest. The red-sandstone description is the Mathura school; the others are not art schools at all.

**50. (A)** The Mathura school was an indigenous style centred on Mathura, carving fuller, rounder figures in spotted red sandstone. The Greek robes belong to Gandhara, the love-and-heroism poems to Sangam literature, and the ships to Satavahana coins.

**51. (C)** Gandhara is the Greco-Buddhist, Hellenistic-influenced school of the northwest; Mathura is the home-grown Indian school in red sandstone. Neither is Tamil, neither is purely Greek, and both are sculpture, not poetry.

**52. (C)** A Buddhist figure rendered in Greek forms is textbook cultural confluence, traditions flowing together into something new. It is not destruction of a culture, nor influence-free, nor a Vedic revival.

**53. (B)** Cultural confluence is the flowing-together of cultures to make new shared styles, with a strong local character surviving, exactly what happened in Indian art and coinage of this age. It is neither replacement, nor strict separation, nor unchanging spread.

**54. (D)** The chapter ties the flourishing of Sanskrit literature, including the Mahabharata and Ramayana, to this age. The Sangam anthologies are Tamil, the Arthashastra belongs to the earlier Mauryan period, and Hathigumpha is an inscription, not literature.

**55. (D)** Indian themes staying central in blended coins and art shows India absorbing and reshaping foreign influences while keeping its identity, not expelling them, not being wholly Hellenised, and certainly not halting art.

**56. (C)** Greco-Buddhist Gandhara art, Kanishka's coins honouring many gods, and a surge in Sanskrit literature are the concrete proofs of confluence. There was no single imposed style, foreign elements were clearly present, and Indian religions thrived.

**57. (D)** Coins (ships, titles, gods) and inscriptions (Hathigumpha, Nashik, Naneghat) carry much of what we know, since continuous written chronicles are scarce, hence their value. It is not that no one was literate, that nothing was built, or that Rome wrote its history.

**58. (A)** Coins, cave inscriptions and sculptures are exactly the durable physical sources the chapter relies on. The other lists contain modern items that did not exist in this age.

**59. (B)** The Maurya breakup (c. 185 BCE) came first; the Satavahanas rose in the 2nd century BCE; Kanishka's Kushanas peaked in the 2nd century CE. So breakup, then Satavahanas, then Kanishka is the right order.

**60. (B)** The age shows division and creativity side by side: rival kingdoms warred and allied while Gandhara and Mathura art, far-reaching trade and Sanskrit literature flourished. The age was not unified, art and trade did not stop, and Indian culture was not banned.
