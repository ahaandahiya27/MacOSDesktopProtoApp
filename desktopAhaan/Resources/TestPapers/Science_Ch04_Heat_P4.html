1. (C) Heat flows from the hotter water to the cooler water until both reach one common temperature. With equal amounts of the same liquid, that common value sits exactly halfway between the two starting temperatures: (20 + 80) ÷ 2 = 50 °C. The hotter portion does not simply impose its 80 °C — it cools as it warms the other half. Adding the two temperatures (giving 100) treats temperature like a quantity that piles up, which it does not. And heat does not settle at the lower value of 20 °C; the cool water warms up while the hot water cools down.

2. (D) When the amounts are unequal, the final temperature is pulled toward the temperature of the larger amount. Here there is four times as much hot water (400 g) as cold water (100 g), so the mix ends up well above the midpoint of 45 °C and much closer to 70 °C. The midpoint of 45 °C would only be right if the amounts were equal. 'Cold water always wins' is false — the side with more substance has the bigger say, and here that is the hot water. Plainly averaging the two temperatures and nudging the result also ignores that the larger hot share controls the outcome.

3. (A) The final temperature is dragged toward whichever side has far more substance. The bucket has overwhelmingly more water than one drop, so the bucket barely changes and stays near 25 °C; the drop's small heat is shared out among a huge mass of water. The midpoint of 62.5 °C would only apply if the two amounts were equal, which they are wildly not. Being the hottest object does not let one drop heat a whole bucket — heat content depends on amount as well as temperature. And adding warm water cannot cool the bucket below 25 °C.

4. (B) Heat flows only when there is a temperature difference, always from hotter to colder. Both blocks are at 40 °C, so there is no difference and no net flow — both simply stay at 40 °C. Having more metal gives block X more total heat, but the same temperature, so it does not push heat into Y. 'More metal means higher temperature' confuses amount of heat with temperature; they are different. And although X stores more heat than Y, heat does not flow without a temperature gap.

5. (C) Heat travels from the hotter body to the cooler one. The tea is hotter than the room, so heat moves out of the tea into the room air; the tea cools and the room warms by a tiny, barely noticeable amount, until the tea matches room temperature. The student's claim reverses this — the room does not warm the tea. There is no separate substance called 'cold' that flows; cooling is just the loss of heat. And size does not set the flow direction — temperature difference does.

6. (C) For the same liquid and the same amount, the heat needed depends on how many degrees the temperature rises, not on the starting value. Both rose by 10 °C, so each absorbed about the same heat. Ending at a higher temperature (Q at 80 °C) does not by itself mean more heat was added during this step. Starting cold does not make P need extra heat for the same 10 °C climb. And heat is exactly what raised each temperature, so saying no heat entered is wrong — heat and temperature change are tightly linked here.

7. (C) Task (i) is at −5 °C, far below the clinical scale's lowest mark of 35 °C, so only the wide-range laboratory thermometer can reach it. Task (ii) needs a reading held in place after the thermometer leaves the body and carried to another room — that is exactly what the clinical thermometer's kink does, plus its scale is spread finely over the body range for tenth-of-a-degree fevers. Using the clinical one for both fails on (i) because −5 °C is off its scale. Using the laboratory one for both fails on (ii) because its kink-free thread would slip as it cooled on the walk to the other room. The last pairing assigns the wrong tool to each task.

8. (A) The kink traps the thread at the highest temperature it has reached. After the sick patient it sits at 41.0 °C. The warm water at 38 °C is cooler than 41.0 °C, so the liquid does not expand enough to push the trapped thread any higher — it stays near 41.0 °C, giving a falsely high reading. This is precisely why a clinical thermometer must be shaken down before each use. The thermometer does not reset itself; that is the job of the shake. Readings do not add up across uses. And the kink stops the thread from dropping back, so it does not fall to room temperature on its own.

9. (B) A laboratory thermometer must cover about 120 degrees of range, so each small division is comparatively large (around 1 °C), and it cannot distinguish 39.2 °C from 39.3 °C. A clinical thermometer devotes its whole length to roughly 35–42 °C, so it shows tenths of a degree clearly. The range claim is false: 39.2 °C is inside −10 to 110. Lacking a kink only means the reading is not held while carried away — it can still measure body temperature in contact. And the liquid does not freeze anywhere near body temperature.

10. (C) A clinical thermometer has a kink that traps the thread at the highest temperature reached, so once laid on the cool table it still reads 41 °C. A laboratory thermometer has no kink, so its thread moves freely with temperature; as its liquid cools on the table the thread slides back down, so it does NOT hold 41 °C. It is not true that glass traps heat to lock a reading — only the kink locks it. The kink-free laboratory thermometer is precisely the one that loses the reading, not the one that keeps it. And neither thermometer resets the instant it is removed; the clinical one in particular keeps its trapped reading.

11. (D) Across 50 °C, P's 100 divisions make each one 50 ÷ 100 = 0.5 °C; Q's 25 divisions make each one 50 ÷ 25 = 2 °C. The thermometer whose smallest division is smaller can be read more finely, so P (0.5 °C steps) beats Q (2 °C steps). Fewer divisions over the same range make each division LARGER, not smaller, so Q is coarser. Equal ranges do not guarantee equal fineness — division count decides it. And the computed values are 0.5 °C and 2 °C, not 0.5 °C and 1 °C.

12. (B) Ten equal divisions in one degree make each division 1 ÷ 10 = 0.1 °C. Three divisions above 40 means 40 + 3 × 0.1 = 40.3 °C. Reading 40.03 places the decimal one digit too far, as if each division were 0.01 °C. Counting the 3 divisions as whole degrees (40 + 3 = 43) ignores that they are tenths. And 40.15 would require divisions of 0.05 each, which is not the stated scale.

13. (B) Fifty degrees split into 10 equal divisions makes each division 50 ÷ 10 = 5 °C. Three divisions above 50 means 50 + 3 × 5 = 50 + 15 = 65 °C. Reading 53 wrongly treats each division as 1 °C (50 + 3). Reading 50.3 wrongly treats each division as 0.1 °C. And 100 jumps to the next numbered mark, ignoring that the thread is only 3 of the 10 divisions up.

14. (B) Ten degrees over 5 divisions makes each division 2 °C. Morning: 20 + 2 × 2 = 24 °C. Noon: 30 + 4 × 2 = 38 °C. The change is 38 − 24 = +14 °C, a rise. Reading the divisions as 1 °C each (giving 22 and 26) halves the true values. The 10 °C gap between the numbered marks is not the change in the thread's position. And simply comparing the division counts (2 vs 4) and calling it a 2 °C rise ignores both the 2 °C size of each division and the move from the 20 mark up to the 30 mark.

15. (D) Five degrees over 5 divisions makes each division 1 °C. Three divisions below 0, moving toward colder, gives 0 − 3 = −3 °C. Reading +3 °C ignores that the thread is below zero, in the colder direction. Reading −15 wrongly treats each division as 5 °C. And −8 wrongly starts counting from the −5 mark instead of from 0.

16. (C) Three degrees split into 6 divisions makes each division 3 ÷ 6 = 0.5 °C. Five divisions above 12 means 12 + 5 × 0.5 = 12 + 2.5 = 14.5 °C, so the second student is correct. The first student assumed each division was 0.1 °C, which is only true when there are 10 divisions per degree — not the case here. The two values 12.5 and 14.5 are clearly different numbers. And 17 °C would require each division to be 1 °C with five of them, which contradicts the stated 0.5 °C divisions.

17. (C) Each division is 1 °C, so the readings are 30 + 2 = 32, 30 + 5 = 35, 30 + 8 = 38 °C — a steady rise of 3 °C per minute. Continuing the pattern, 9:03 gives 38 + 3 = 41 °C, which is 1 division above the 40 mark. The thread is not capped at the 9th division above 30; it simply crosses into the next numbered region. The rise is steady (adding 3 each minute), not doubling. And there is no rule forcing the thread to stop on a numbered mark — it lands wherever the temperature is.

18. (D) In a solid, heat travels by conduction: vibrating particles pass energy to their neighbours, so the disturbance reaches near points first and far points later. That is exactly why the butter melts in sequence from the bowl end outward along the handle — a gradual arrival, not chance. If radiation reached every dab equally and instantly, they would melt together, which they do not. Convection needs a fluid that can flow; the solid handle's particles are locked in place and cannot circulate. And the butter does not make its own heat — it merely melts when conducted heat arrives.

19. (C) The faster the wax clears, the faster heat reached that point, so the best conductor loses its wax first. Aluminium clears first, then iron, then glass, while plastic barely melts — so the order is aluminium > iron > glass > plastic. Being light does not improve conduction; plastic is light yet the poorest conductor here. Iron conducts well but less than aluminium in this set, and the order must follow the melt times, not a guess. And an identical heat source does NOT equalise the results — the material decides how quickly heat passes through, which is the whole point of the experiment.

20. (C) Same shape means the only difference is the material. Steel is a good conductor and quickly delivers heat to its far end; bamboo is a poor conductor, so heat creeps along it slowly and the far end needs much more time to reach the same temperature. Identical shape does not make the times equal, because the materials conduct at very different rates. Poor conductors heat their far ends MORE slowly, not faster. And bamboo does conduct heat — just slowly — so it is not 'never.'

21. (B) Both objects are at 18 °C, so neither is actually colder. But the steel knife is a good conductor: it whisks heat away from your warmer palm quickly, cooling the skin fast, so it FEELS colder. Ceramic is a poorer conductor and draws heat more slowly, so it feels less cold. The ceramic is not at a lower temperature — both are 18 °C. They do not feel equally cold despite equal temperature, because conduction rates differ. And the steel, being colder than your hand, takes heat from it rather than giving heat back, so it cannot feel warmer.

22. (D) Conduction is the passing of energy from particle to neighbouring particle. In iron the particles are tightly packed and constantly in contact, so energy is relayed quickly. In the air trapped in wool the particles are widely separated and seldom touch, so the relay is feeble and slow. The trapped air certainly contains particles — they are just spread out. Temperature does not set conduction ability; iron is not 'colder.' And air particles actually move fast; it is their large spacing, not slowness, that makes conduction poor.

23. (A) The body must carry heat from the heating element into the water, which calls for a good conductor — metal. The grip must keep heat from reaching the hand, which calls for a poor conductor (insulator) — plastic. Reversing them (plastic body, metal grip) would leave the water barely warmed while the grip conducts heat and burns the hand. An all-metal kettle would make the grip hot, not cool. And an all-plastic kettle would not heat the water well, since plastic is a poor conductor — defeating the boiling goal.

24. (D) Heated soup expands, so the same mass takes more space, making it less dense (lighter) — it rises. Cooler, denser soup sinks to replace it, and the cycle repeats, forming a convection current that warms the whole pot. Warmed liquid is lighter, not heavier, so it does not sink. Liquids conduct heat poorly, so the heat is not simply conducted straight up through still layers — the bulk movement of soup does most of the carrying. And the soup does not flash to vapour at ordinary heating; it circulates as a liquid first.

25. (D) Convection currents rise wherever the water is heated, because that is where the water expands, lightens, and floats upward; the cooler water elsewhere sinks. Heating the left edge therefore anchors the rising limb at that edge, not the centre. The current does not always rise centrally — it follows the heat source. Conduction through still water is far too slow to trace this loop; the sawdust moves because the water bulk-moves. And heated water becomes lighter, so it rises at the heated edge, not sinks.

26. (D) Warm air rises. A heater mounted high near the ceiling warms air that is already at the top, where it tends to stay, leaving the lower (living) part of the room cold. A floor-level heater sends warm air up and around the whole room as a convection loop, so low placement heats a room better. Warm air does not fall down to fill the room; that reverses convection. Air conducts heat poorly, so heating does not spread by conduction regardless of height. And with a low heater the warm air rises, so the floor does not become the hottest part.

27. (C) Convection works only in fluids (liquids and gases) because the warmed parts must rise and circulate, carrying heat bodily. In a solid the particles are locked in their positions and cannot flow, so no convection current can form — heat spreads through a brick by conduction instead. Paint colour affects radiation, not whether a solid can convect. No amount of heat lets a rigid solid's particles circulate. And solids certainly can be heated in the middle; they just transfer that heat by conduction.

28. (A) Convection is driven by warm (lighter) fluid rising and cold (denser) fluid sinking. Heating at the top leaves the warm water already on top and the cold below — which is already the stable layering — so there is no tendency to overturn and circulate, and heat can only creep downward by slow conduction. Warm water is lighter, not denser, so heating the top does not send it to the bottom. Convection can certainly occur in a beaker when it is heated from below; shape is not the issue. And the cold layer does not 'block' cooling — there is simply no driving force for a current, which is exactly why kettles are heated from below, not above.

Dark surfaces both absorb and emit well, not one without the other. And colour affects emission too, not absorption alone, so the cooling difference is fully explained by surface type.

(A) Conduction needs touching particles and convection needs a flowing fluid, so the thin near-still-air gap between the panes greatly slows both of those paths (and a true vacuum gap would stop them almost entirely). 56. (A) A metal ladle left standing in a boiling pot becomes hot by direct particle contact — conduction (correct). Feeling warm where sunlight comes through a closed window is radiation travelling in straight lines from the Sun, crossing the gap with no moving air needed (correct). Water bubbling and churning as it boils is the bulk movement of heated water rising and cooler water sinking — convection (correct). All three pairings match their definitions, so the count is three. Each of the smaller-count options would require at least one pairing to be wrong, but none of them is.

57. (D) A cold-box drastically reduces all three modes — the thick foam starves conduction and convection of easy paths, and the sealed lid blocks air movement — but it cannot remove every path. Heat still leaks slowly through the foam walls, the lid seams, and every opening, so over hours the ice warms and melts. The box does not make 'cold' of its own to 'run out.' The foam slows heat entry, it does not speed it up. And there is no flow of 'cold' into the box — warming is the slow leak of outside heat into the ice.

58. (C) Step 1: the glowing coals warm the cook's face across a gap without touching — that crossing of a gap, in straight lines and needing no moving air, is radiation. Steps 2–3: the heated air over the coals rises and cooler room air flows in to replace it — bulk movement of a fluid, which is convection. The coals cannot conduct heat across the gap to the face (no continuous touching matter), so step 1 is not conduction. Warm air rising and flowing is not radiation, so steps 2–3 are not radiation. And the moving air is convection, not conduction, since air conducts heat poorly and here it is physically flowing — so 'all conduction' is wrong.

59. (D) A laboratory thermometer has no kink, so its thread moves freely. The warm hands heat the bulb's liquid, which expands and pushes the thread up to about 34 °C; once the hands are removed the liquid cools toward room temperature, contracts, and the thread falls back toward 28 °C. This demonstrates the expand-on-heating, contract-on-cooling behaviour the thermometer relies on. Only a clinical thermometer's kink holds the highest reading — this one does not. The thread cannot keep rising once the hands are gone, since no more heat is being added. And with no hand on it the thermometer reads the room's 28 °C, not 0 °C.

60. (B) Both objects are at 5 °C, so a thermometer reads 5 °C on each — they are equally cold by measurement. But marble (stone) is a good conductor and pulls heat from the warm foot rapidly, chilling the skin fast, so it FEELS far colder; the wood, a poor conductor, draws heat slowly and feels less cold. Wood does not feel colder, and it is not at a lower temperature. Feel and temperature do not always match — that mismatch is the whole point, since the marble feels colder despite equal temperature. And the thermometer would NOT read lower on the marble, because the marble is not genuinely colder — it only conducts heat away faster.
