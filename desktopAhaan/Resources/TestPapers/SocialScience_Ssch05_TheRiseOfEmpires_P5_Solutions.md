## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** Imperium points to a type of power — supreme command over many lands and the rulers within them — which is exactly the layered overlordship that marks an empire off from a single kingdom; that is the intended point. The naming-in-Latin option mistakes the etymology lesson for a claim about official titles, which the chapter never makes. The standing-army option is false because ordinary kingdoms also kept armies; what differs is whom the supreme power reaches over. The treasury option reduces the contrast to wealth, the very surface feature the question says is NOT the deepest distinction.

**2. (B)** An empire is defined by layered sovereignty — subordinate kings remaining on their thrones under one overlord — so Q, not P, is the empire; the student has mistaken internal tightness for the imperial structure. The option calling P the empire because empires allow no lesser kings inverts the definition: tributary kings keeping their thrones are central to how empires work. Calling both empires ignores that P has no subordinate kings at all. The republic option is simply outside the chapter, which describes empires as ruled by emperors, not elected.

**3. (C)** Both a tributary king and a satrap are devices for ruling far-off lands indirectly, sparing the emperor from administering every village himself — that shared purpose is what the question asks for. The no-army option is false; tributaries and satraps coexisted with armies and did not abolish the need for force. The succession option imports a rule about choosing the next emperor that the chapter never attaches to these arrangements. The no-taxes option contradicts the very point of tribute, which is revenue flowing to the centre.

**4. (C)** The chapter frames the six supports as solving distinct problems — communication for distance, currency for unified trade and tax, art and learning for cultural cohesion — none of which raw force or stored wealth performs, so the army-plus-treasury view leaves real gaps; that is the correct refutation. The 'forbidden to operate together' option invents a non-existent rule. Collapsing treasury into army misreads the chapter, which treats a thriving economy and trade, not just plunder, as the wealth source. Ranking art and learning above all is a different (and unsupported) claim; the chapter presents the supports as interlocking, not hierarchical in that way.

**5. (D)** The chapter explicitly makes a thriving economy the support on which the army, officials and roads rest, because the wealth from trade and taxation funds them; that reverses the debater's claim that the army comes first. The army-as-base option confuses winning land with sustaining the realm: an unfunded army cannot be paid or kept. The currency-as-base option mistakes a tool for measuring and moving wealth for the source of wealth itself. The art-and-learning option inverts the dependency the chapter draws — culture is supported by the economy's surplus, not the other way round.

**6. (B)** A single currency and standard measures let distant regions trade smoothly and let the state tax that trade under one system — the merchant's easier commerce and the state's reliable revenue are two sides of the same coin, which is the linked answer required. The tax-relief option is wrong because uniform measures make taxing easier, not abolished. The monopoly option contradicts the point: shared standards open trade widely rather than restricting it to one person. The disbanding-the-army option is unrelated and false, since currency reform has nothing to do with ending military protection.

**7. (C)** Divergent coins and measures raise the friction of trade between the provinces and make the state's tax-gathering uneven, so revenue and the economic cohesion that the empire runs on both decline — the chain the chapter implies. The stronger-army option reverses cause and effect; weaker shared revenue does not strengthen the army. The govern-directly option is a non sequitur, since currency confusion does nothing to let an emperor personally rule far provinces. The more-food-and-iron option confuses monetary standards with agriculture and mining, which different coins would not boost.

**8. (C)** Self-running guilds kept craft and trade flowing, and that prosperity fed the king's treasury, so disrupting them with daily policing would damage the commerce that funded him — the motive and the risk the question wants. The legal-ban option invents a law forbidding kings from touching trade, which the chapter never states. The no-taxes option misreads the relationship: the king benefits precisely because guild prosperity is taxable. The no-power option overstates the case; the king chooses to leave guilds alone for sound reasons, not because they outrank him.

**9. (D)** These overland arteries carried goods, news and troops, so controlling them brought taxable trade, fast communication and military reach together — the multi-support advantage the chapter stresses, which is more than just marching roads. The borders option is wrong: trade routes are not the same as fixed frontiers. The sea-lanes option misidentifies them; Uttarapatha and Dakshinapatha are overland routes, not ocean routes. The edicts-only option absurdly narrows their purpose to one ruler's inscriptions, ignoring their economic and military value.

**10. (B)** Iron ploughs lifted agricultural output (the economy) and iron weapons equipped the army (the military), so one resource advanced two distinct supports — the dual effect the chapter describes. The coins-and-ships option invents iron coinage and a Magadhan navy that the chapter does not describe. The sell-abroad-and-tribute option recasts iron as an export and a gift to rivals, which is not the chapter's account of how iron helped Magadha. The pillars-and-temples option borrows Ashokan edict-imagery and applies it wrongly to Magadha's early iron advantage.

**11. (D)** Magadha's advantages mattered because they reinforced one another — food sustaining people, iron arming and equipping them, elephants adding war-power, rivers moving everything cheaply — which is the interlocking logic the chapter draws, not a flat list. The seaports option is false; Magadha's strength was inland and river-based, not naval. The gold-and-Silk-Road option imports wealth sources the chapter does not credit to Magadha. The mountains-and-desert option not only misdescribes Magadha's geography but absurdly claims it needed no army, contradicting the chapter's stress on military strength.

**12. (D)** Unifying many kingdoms, issuing coins and keeping a huge army are exactly the imperial features the chapter credits to Mahapadma Nanda, refuting the 'ordinary king' claim. The Arthashastra-and-mahamatras option mixes Kautilya's work and an Ashokan office into the Nanda's reign, neither of which belongs to him. The edicts option transplants Ashoka's signature practice onto the Nandas. The Alexander option is chronologically and factually wrong: it was Chandragupta's era, and the Nandas did not defeat Alexander.

**13. (D)** Dhana Nanda's heavy taxation and oppression drained loyalty, and that very loss of support let Chandragupta overthrow him — the correct cause-and-consequence pairing. The giving-away-the-treasury option contradicts the chapter, which stresses his riches and oppression, not generosity. The no-coins option invents a trade collapse and a welcomed Greek satrap that the chapter does not describe. The handed-throne-to-Alexander option is plainly false; the Nandas neither surrendered to Alexander nor did he reach Magadha.

**14. (A)** The Nandas came first and unified Magadha; the Mauryas then replaced them and expanded far further — the correct order and division of roles. Reversing them, so the Mauryas came first and the Nandas overthrew them, scrambles the sequence. The 'side by side as rivals' option denies that one succeeded the other, which the chapter makes clear. The edicts option is the reverse of the truth: it was Ashoka the Maurya who is famous for edicts, not the Nandas.

**15. (D)** Alexander overran the Persian Empire on his way to India, and his exhausted troops' refusal to advance is the immediate cause the chapter gives for his retreat — both correct. The Nanda option is doubly wrong: he did not conquer the Nandas, and Chandragupta did not defeat him. The Maurya option is impossible, since the Maurya empire was founded only after Alexander left. The Roman option is anachronistic and false; Alexander never conquered Rome, which was not yet such a power.

**16. (C)** Porus was the Indian king of the Pauravas defeated by Alexander near the Indus — the chapter's clear description. The Macedonian-general option confuses Porus with a Greek satrap. The Greek-envoy option confuses him with Megasthenes, author of the Indica. The Nanda-founder option confuses him with Mahapadma Nanda; Porus had no part in founding the Nanda dynasty.

**17. (B)** A battlefield victory did not translate into lasting Indian rule; Alexander left weak satraps and opened contacts, so his real influence was indirect — the chapter's point, which answers the student's objection. The fifty-years option invents a long direct rule that never happened. The founded-the-Maurya-dynasty option is false; Chandragupta founded it. The Greek-edicts option fabricates a practice; the famous edicts were Ashoka's, in Prakrit and Brahmi, not Alexander's in Greek.

**18. (A)** Alexander's departure and death left weak Greek satraps whose hold crumbled, giving Chandragupta openings to defeat or absorb them in the northwest — the chapter's explanation. A 'strong, united Greek army' is the opposite of the weakness that actually helped him. The Ashoka-treaty option is anachronistic; Ashoka ruled generations later. The Nanda-loyalty option is false: Chandragupta overthrew the Nandas, so their soldiers were not his lifelong loyalists.

**19. (B)** Chandragupta rose by overthrowing the unpopular Nandas with Kautilya's guidance — the chapter's stated combination. The Alexander-crowning option is false; Alexander did not appoint him. The 'inherited from his grandson Ashoka' option reverses the generations, since Ashoka was Chandragupta's grandson, not the reverse. The Persian-marriage option invents an alliance the chapter does not describe.

**20. (A)** Kautilya was also known as Chanakya or Vishnugupta, and his Arthashastra is the manual of statecraft and economics, separate from the Indica written by the Greek envoy — all correct. The Megasthenes option swaps the author of the Indica into Kautilya's place. The Panini option misnames him and miscasts the Arthashastra as Ashoka's edicts. The Porus option is plainly false in both name and the nature of the work.

**21. (B)** The seven limbs are king, ministers, territory and people, fort, treasury, army and allies, and the body-analogy means a single weak limb endangers the whole — the correct list and the correct reason. The geography list replaces the limbs with physical features the chapter does not name. The military list reduces the state to war-tools and wrongly claims force alone holds it. The last option keeps the wrong (social-class) list and merely swaps 'luck' for 'tradition', so it is still incorrect.

**22. (B)** Listing treasury and army separately reflects their different roles — funding versus defending and enforcing — which holds even though the treasury pays the army, so the separation is purposeful, not an error. The 'really one limb' option misses that distinct functions justify distinct limbs. The paid-in-grain option contradicts the obvious point that the treasury funded the army. The 'never used at the same time' option is plainly false, since a state needs funds and force together.

**23. (B)** Bindusara links the dynasty as Chandragupta's son and Ashoka's father, Megasthenes was the Greek envoy who wrote the Indica, and Kautilya was the mentor and author of the Arthashastra — the only fully correct matching. The other options shuffle these roles, variously making Bindusara the envoy or the founder, Megasthenes the mentor or Chandragupta's son, and Kautilya Ashoka's father or the envoy — each of which misassigns at least one figure.

**24. (C)** The Arthashastra is Kautilya's insider manual of how the state should work, and the Indica is Megasthenes' outsider snapshot — the correct pairing of source to strength. Swapping the authorship of the two works is the classic trap and is false. Calling both religious scriptures misdescribes both texts. The poetry-and-edicts option invents genres for each that neither wrote; the edicts were Ashoka's, not Megasthenes'.

**25. (A)** Bindusara sits between Chandragupta and Ashoka as the founder's son and Ashoka's father — his correct place in the succession. The mentor option confuses him with Kautilya. The envoy option confuses him with Megasthenes. The founder option confuses him with Chandragupta, who actually overthrew the Nandas.

**26. (C)** The Mauryan answer was provinces under governors and officials, with spies added so the centre could monitor distant affairs and keep watch on its own officials — the correct two-layer description. The abolished-provinces option contradicts the chapter and absurdly makes spies replace the army. The guilds-govern option misattributes provincial rule to guilds and confuses spies with currency. The emperor-tours-every-village option denies the very problem the administration was built to solve.

**27. (D)** Long-term governance needs a steady income stream, and regular taxation — unlike sporadic plunder or gifts — reliably paid for the army, officials and public works; that is why it was central. The punishment option misreads tax as a penalty rather than the state's funding base. The enrich-the-guilds option inverts the flow; taxes funded the state, not the guilds. The 'replaced trade and economy' option is self-defeating, since taxes are drawn from a working economy, not a substitute for one.

**28. (B)** The dhamma-mahamatras existed to promote and oversee Ashoka's Dhamma among the people, a moral-guidance role distinct from revenue or war — exactly what the title implies. The cavalry option turns them into soldiers, confusing administration with the army. The tribute option turns them into tax officials, a different function. The stone-carvers option mistakes the officials who spread Dhamma for the craftsmen who physically engraved the edicts.

**29. (D)** Administration carried the centre's reach into distant provinces, working alongside the army and treasury — not in place of them — since force and funds remained essential; that corrects the teacher. The poetry option trivialises the officials' real function. The abolished-taxes option contradicts the need for a treasury that officials helped administer. The private-army option fragments the empire's force into rival pieces, the opposite of centralised imperial strength.

**30. (A)** Ashoka's empire stretched across most of the subcontinent including the northwest toward Afghanistan, while the far south lay outside it — the correct in/out pairing. Excluding the Ganga plains around Pataliputra is impossible, since that heartland held the capital. Excluding the northwest while keeping 'only' the east shrinks the empire far too much. The last option wrongly excludes both the east and the Ganga heartland, again removing core territory the empire certainly held.

**31. (B)** Ashoka fought Kalinga to enlarge his empire, and the slaughter afterwards moved him toward Dhamma — so the student has the sequence backwards, which is the correct diagnosis. The 'never fought there' option denies the war the chapter centres on. The 'already Buddhist' option invents a fact the chapter does not state and still misses the cause-effect reversal. The 'Buddhism forbade war' option imposes a blanket rule the chapter never asserts and contradicts the war's occurrence.

**32. (B)** The strongest evidence is Ashoka's own edicts, in which he openly admits his remorse — a first-person record addressed to his subjects, which the chapter highlights. The Megasthenes option is impossible in timing, since the envoy belonged to Chandragupta's reign, before Ashoka. The Kautilya option is likewise anachronistic and concerns statecraft, not Ashoka's grief. The later-temple option offers indirect, second-hand evidence far weaker than Ashoka's own words.

**33. (C)** Without the remorse that turned him to Dhamma, Ashoka would likely have continued conquering and never produced the Dhamma edicts — the counterfactual the chapter's logic supports. The immediate-collapse option has no basis; remorse or not, the empire did not fall in a single year. The Brahmi-decipherment option confuses a much later scholarly event with Ashoka's choices. The Chandragupta option is impossible, since his grandfather ruled long before and could not be affected by Ashoka's later decisions.

**34. (C)** Dhamma was a moral and ethical code — honesty, non-violence, tolerance, dutiful conduct — meant for everyone, not a new faith; that is the chapter's sense. The new-religion option misreads it; Dhamma was ethical guidance, not a replacement creed. The tax option confuses a moral code with revenue. The warfare option is the very opposite of Dhamma's emphasis on non-violence.

**35. (A)** Carving edicts on rock and pillars across the land gave Ashoka a permanent, public medium present in many distant places — well suited to reaching a vast, scattered population, which is why the chapter calls him a great communicator. The reciting-soldiers option is impractical and not the means the chapter describes. The printing option is anachronistic for the period. The single-temple option fails the very goal of reach, since one central building cannot serve far-flung peoples.

**36. (B)** Prakrit is a spoken language and Brahmi is the script used to write it — different categories, which is the exact correction. Swapping them, so Brahmi is the language and Prakrit the script, is the classic reversal trap. Calling both scripts (and Greek-only) is doubly wrong. Calling both languages denies that Brahmi is a script at all.

**37. (C)** Prakrit was common speech, so the people could understand directly, and stone is durable and public, surviving centuries — both choices serve the same goal, which is the correct reasoning. The Sanskrit-not-invented-and-cheapest option offers two false reasons. The secret-tongue option contradicts the whole purpose, since Prakrit was chosen for accessibility, not secrecy. The 'only language that could be carved' and 'paper did not exist anywhere' option states two falsehoods about carving and materials.

**38. (B)** Brahmi is the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts and Prakrit was the common people's everyday speech — the correct significance of each. The Roman-alphabet option is false, and Prakrit was popular speech, not a refined court tongue. The never-deciphered option is wrong, since Brahmi has been read, and Prakrit was no priestly code. The last option once again reverses language and script, the chapter's recurring trap.

**39. (C)** The Lion Capital from Ashoka's Sarnath pillar is the national emblem, and the flag's dharmachakra — the wheel of dharma — comes from the same Mauryan art; all three links are correct. The Sanchi-stupa-and-war-chariot option misnames the emblem and misreads the wheel. The Dhauli-elephant-and-trade-routes option does the same with different errors. The dancing-figure-and-sun option misidentifies both the emblem and the wheel's meaning.

**40. (D)** Architecturally, the capital is the carved top of the column, and Sarnath mattered because the Buddha first taught there — both correct. The capital-city option confuses the architectural term with a city. The treasury option misdefines 'capital' and misplaces the Kalinga war. The main-edict option misdefines the term and wrongly relocates Chandragupta's defeat of the Nandas to Sarnath.

**41. (A)** From 232 to 185 BCE is roughly half a century, illustrating how fast an empire can crumble once its strongest ruler is gone — which fits the chapter's theme of fragility. 'Two centuries' miscalculates the gap and draws the wrong lesson of stability. 'Within a single year' overstates the speed and is contradicted by the dates. 'More than three hundred years' grossly overstates the interval and reverses the chapter's point about decline.

**42. (A)** The paradox is that an empire delivers unity and prosperity while resting on force and tribute, and the fragility follows because a weakening centre lets distant regions break away — the integrated statement the chapter offers. The 'small and large' option misstates the paradox and falsely says empires keep no army. The 'no kings beneath' option contradicts the layered structure and wrongly says empires refuse tribute. The 'spends nothing on an army' option contradicts the chapter's stress on a strong, costly army.

**43. (C)** Rebellion is likeliest when a weak ruler follows a strong one (the opportunity) and tribute rises (the grievance) — the chapter's two ingredients together. A run of strong emperors removes the opportunity by keeping provinces in check. Lighter tribute in peacetime removes the grievance. Better roads bind the province closer to the centre, working against breakaway rather than for it.

**44. (D)** Because Ashoka placed edicts across the territory he held, their geographic spread maps roughly how far his empire and influence reached — the correct use. Edicts are moral and administrative proclamations, not records of royal birthdays, so they cannot date Alexander's birth. They do not list daily market prices, so iron prices are out of reach. Nor do they register guilds, so they cannot count them city by city.

**45. (D)** Ashoka's edicts are his own words and best show his thinking; the Indica is the Greek envoy's outsider account — the correct match of source to strength. The first distractor swaps the authors, falsely making Ashoka write the Indica and a foreigner the edicts. Calling both Ashoka's compositions ignores Megasthenes entirely. Calling both foreign reports ignores that the edicts are Ashoka's own.

**46. (C)** The Nanda dynasty (5th century BCE) comes first, then Alexander reaches the northwest around 326 BCE, then Chandragupta founds the Maurya empire around 321 BCE, then Ashoka fights Kalinga in his reign (after 268 BCE): (ii), (iv), (i), (iii). Putting Alexander before the Nandas is wrong. Putting Chandragupta before Alexander reverses 326 and 321 BCE. Starting with Chandragupta puts the Mauryas before the Nandas, which is the classic ordering error.

**47. (D)** Within the dynasty the order is founder Chandragupta, then his son Bindusara, then Ashoka and his Kalinga war, then the empire's end around 185 BCE: (i), (ii), (iii), (iv). Starting with Bindusara puts him before his own father. Placing Kalinga before Bindusara's reign skips a generation. Putting the empire's end before the Kalinga war is impossible, since Kalinga fell within Ashoka's reign, decades before 185 BCE.

**48. (A)** Different coins and measures mean the missing support is a common currency and standard measures, and the result is harder trade and uneven taxation that weaken the economy — the correct diagnosis and effect. The army option is wrong, since the empire is said to have a strong army. The communication option is wrong, since it has excellent roads. The art-and-learning option misnames the failing support and draws a consequence (losing officials) that does not follow.

**49. (A)** Alexander overran Persia before India, and the Maurya empire arose only a few years after he departed, so he could not have touched it — the correct timeline relation. Conquering both is impossible because the Mauryas did not yet exist in his time. Conquering the Mauryas but not Persia reverses what actually happened. The 'both defeated him' option is false; Persia fell to him and the Mauryas postdate him.

**50. (D)** Tributaries, satraps and officials are three forms of the same answer — projecting the emperor's authority into lands he cannot rule directly — which is why the chapter treats them together. The succession option imports an unrelated function about choosing emperors. The praise-poetry option trivialises all three roles. The 'avoiding tribute' option contradicts the fact that tributaries exist precisely to deliver tribute to the centre.

**51. (A)** A tributary is a king who keeps his throne and pays tribute, while a satrap is an appointed governor with no realm of his own — the precise contrast. The first distractor swaps the two definitions. The 'larger territory' option invents a size rule that does not exist. The Greek/Indian option assigns ethnicities to the roles that the chapter never specifies.

**52. (C)** A well-planned, food-secure, prosperous city reflects strong administration (planning) and control over resources (food and prosperity) working together — the two supports illustrated. The 'absence of army and currency' option lists absences, not supports at work. The 'tribute alone, no economy' option contradicts a prosperous city, which implies a working economy. The 'refusal of art and isolation' option again names negatives that a flourishing planned city does not display.

**53. (A)** A strong army is costly and depends on a thriving economy and steady taxes to pay and sustain it, so force alone cannot hold an empire — the chapter's reasoning. The 'forbidden from provinces' option invents a rule contradicting the army's defensive role. The 'unpaid volunteers' option falsely erases the very cost the chapter emphasises. The 'tributaries defend everything' option wrongly claims the emperor kept no army, which the chapter denies.

**54. (B)** Since Dhana Nanda fell because lost loyalty gave Chandragupta his opening, retained loyalty would plausibly have let the Nandas endure and resist longer — the chapter-grounded counterfactual. The 'Ashoka never born' option is an unrelated leap with no basis. The 'Alexander conquers all India' option ignores that his retreat was caused by his own soldiers, not Nanda loyalty. The 'two Arthashastras' option is irrelevant to the Nanda king's conduct.

**55. (B)** Art and learning supply the shared culture that binds an empire's many peoples — a cohesion neither army nor administration can provide — so neglecting it leaves precisely that gap, which is distinct from what the other supports do. The tribute option falsely ties revenue to arts patronage. The lose-every-soldier option absurdly makes armies depend on poets. The no-fort option ties forts to artists in a way the chapter never claims.

**56. (A)** Communication networks let commands, troops and news move quickly across distance, so the centre could govern regions the emperor never visited — something an army alone cannot accomplish. The 'replaced the army' option is false; messages do not fight battles. The 'collect tribute, replace officials' option confuses communication with administration's revenue role. The 'abolish currency' option is a non sequitur, since communication does not make a shared currency unnecessary.

**57. (C)** Orderly self-governing guilds made trade prosper, and that prosperity boosted the king's tax revenue, so leaving them alone served his interest — the conclusion that follows from both facts. The 'run by soldiers' option contradicts the very self-government described. The 'collect tribute' option misreads guilds as tribute-gatherers. The 'tiny workshops with no rules' option flatly contradicts the rule-making, price-fixing guild in the passage.

**58. (D)** The chapter's deepest lesson is that holding an empire together is harder than founding one, so even the greatest decline — the theme tying the whole chapter together. The 'endures forever' option contradicts the Maurya collapse and the fragility argument. The 'only a strong emperor' option ignores the six interlocking supports. The 'tribute alone' option denies the need for army and administration the chapter insists on.

**59. (A)** Dropping art and learning removes the binding culture, separate provincial coins fracture unified trade and taxation, and abandoning officials severs the centre's reach into distant lands — three of the chapter's supports failing at once, which is the correct multi-effect answer. The 'grows stronger' option misreads support-cutting as efficiency; the chapter treats each as load-bearing. The 'nothing changes' option denies they are real supports. The 'army disbands instantly' option invents a military collapse none of these three choices directly causes.

**60. (D)** The incorrect pairing is Megasthenes as the architect of statecraft who wrote the governing manual: that manual is the Arthashastra by Kautilya, while Megasthenes was the Greek envoy who wrote an outsider's account, the Indica. The other three pairings are correct: Chandragupta founded the empire by displacing the Nandas, Kautilya wrote the science of governance, and Ashoka governed by Dhamma and edicts after the Kalinga war. The question asks for the wrong match, which is the Megasthenes one.
