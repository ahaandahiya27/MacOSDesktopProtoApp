Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

A TV presenter says, "Tomorrow Delhi will be 32 °C with light rain in the evening." This statement describes:

  • Athe altitude of Delhi
  • Bthe humidity of Delhi only
  • Cthe weather of Delhi✓
  • Dthe climate of Delhi

Working

✓

"Tomorrow … 32 °C with light rain in the evening" describes the day-to-day state of the atmosphere — that is weather. Climate would be the long-term average; altitude and humidity-only are red herrings.

Q2

Which statement correctly distinguishes weather from climate?

  • Aweather is the long-term average; climate changes hour to hour
  • Bweather is the day-to-day state; climate is the average over many years✓
  • Cweather and climate mean exactly the same thing
  • Dclimate is measured with a thermometer; weather is not measured at all

Working

✓

Weather is the short-term, day-to-day state of the atmosphere; climate is its average taken over many years. Option (A) reverses the two; (C) wrongly equates them; (D) is false — both are measured.

Q3

The average weather of a place is usually taken over a period of at least:

  • Atwenty-five years✓
  • Bone month
  • Cone year
  • Done week

Working

✓

By convention, climate is the average weather taken over a long period, usually at least 25 years (often quoted as ~30). A week, month or single year is far too short to smooth out year-to-year weather variation.

Q4

On one winter day it snowed heavily in a hill town. A visitor said, "This proves the town's climate has become colder." The best reply is:

  • Acorrect, because snow always means a colder climate
  • Bcorrect, because climate changes from day to day
  • Cwrong, because snow has nothing to do with temperature
  • Dwrong, because a single day's weather does not establish climate✓

Working

✓

A single day's snowfall is one weather event; climate is the long-term average, so one cold day cannot establish that the climate has changed. (A) and (B) wrongly treat one day as climate; (C) confuses weather's daily change with climate.

Q5

Mumbai receives heavy rain every June to September, year after year. This repeated yearly pattern is part of Mumbai's:

  • Aweather for one day
  • Bclimate✓
  • Caltitude
  • Dwind speed only

Working

✓

A pattern that repeats year after year (heavy June–September rain) is part of the long-term average — Mumbai's climate. One day's rain would be weather; altitude and wind speed are single factors, not the overall pattern.

Q6

The scientific study of weather is called:

  • Ameteorology✓
  • Bastronomy
  • Cgeology
  • Dbiology

Working

✓

The scientific study of weather is meteorology (its practitioners are meteorologists). Geology is rocks/Earth, astronomy is space, biology is living things.

Q7

Which of the following can change within a few hours?

  • Athe climate of a desert
  • Bthe altitude of a hill station
  • Cthe latitude of a place
  • Dthe weather of a city✓

Working

✓

Weather can change within hours. Climate (A) changes only over decades; altitude (B) and latitude (C) of a place are essentially fixed.

Q8

Chennai stays warm and humid almost all year, while Delhi has hot summers and cold winters. This long-term contrast is best described as a difference in their:

  • Aclimate✓
  • Bweather on one particular day
  • Ccloud colour
  • Dwind direction for one hour

Working

✓

Comparing Chennai (warm, humid all year) with Delhi (hot summers, cold winters) over the long run is a comparison of climate. The other options describe single-moment weather details, not the long-term pattern.

Q9

A farmer in Punjab chooses to sow wheat in winter and rice in the monsoon. This decision is based mainly on the region's:

  • Aweather on the day of sowing
  • Bclimate✓
  • Csoil colour only
  • Dwind speed at noon

Working

✓

Crop choices for a whole season rest on the region's typical long-term conditions — its climate — not on the weather of the single day of sowing, nor soil colour or noon wind speed.

Q10

Which pair lists two factors that help decide the climate of a place?

  • Athe colour of houses and the number of cars
  • Baltitude and distance from the sea✓
  • Cthe day of the week and the time of day
  • Da single afternoon's cloud cover

Working

✓

Altitude and distance from the sea are genuine climate-shaping factors (higher = cooler; coastal = milder). House colour, car count, weekday and one afternoon's cloud cover do not determine a region's climate.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

Shimla is much cooler than the plains nearby, even in summer. The main reason is that Shimla has a high:

  • Ahumidity
  • Bwind speed
  • Caltitude✓
  • Drainfall on one day

Working

✓

Temperature falls with height (about 6.5 °C per km), so a high-altitude town like Shimla is cooler than the nearby plains. Humidity, wind speed and one day's rainfall do not explain a steady year-round coolness.

Q12

Coastal cities such as Mumbai have milder temperatures than inland cities at the same latitude. This is because the nearby sea:

  • Aincreases the altitude
  • Bblocks all sunlight
  • Cmoderates the temperature, reducing extremes✓
  • Dremoves all humidity from the air

Working

✓

The sea heats and cools slowly, so it moderates the air temperature of nearby land, reducing the gap between hot and cold extremes. It does not raise altitude, block all sunlight, or remove all humidity (in fact coasts are more humid).

Q13

As you go higher up a mountain, the temperature generally:

  • Aincreases
  • Bstays exactly the same
  • Cfirst rises and then becomes zero
  • Ddecreases✓

Working

✓

As altitude increases, temperature generally decreases. So the answer is "decreases"; it does not rise, stay constant, or behave like (C).

Q14

The Indian monsoon brings heavy rain because seasonal winds carry large amounts of moisture from the:

  • AIndian Ocean✓
  • BHimalayan snow
  • CThar Desert
  • Ddry plateau

Working

✓

The Indian monsoon winds pick up huge amounts of moisture from the warm Indian Ocean and bring rain over land. The Thar Desert and dry plateau are dry sources; Himalayan snow does not feed the monsoon rains.

Q15

Two towns lie at the same latitude. Town P is on the coast; Town Q is far inland. Compared with Q, town P is most likely to have:

  • Amuch greater day-to-night temperature swings
  • Bno rainfall at all
  • Csmaller temperature swings and higher humidity✓
  • Da higher altitude

Working

✓

Because the sea moderates temperature and adds moisture, the coastal town P has smaller day-to-night temperature swings and higher humidity than inland Q. (A) reverses this; coasts still get rain (B is false); being on the coast does not mean higher altitude (D).

Q16

The ultimate source of energy that drives almost all of Earth's weather is:

  • Athe Moon
  • Bthe Sun✓
  • Cthe wind
  • Docean currents alone

Working

✓

The Sun is the ultimate source of the energy that heats air, land and sea, drives winds and the water cycle — i.e. almost all weather. The Moon, wind and ocean currents are not the original energy source (wind and currents are themselves Sun-driven).

Q17

Weather is often hard to predict far in advance mainly because:

  • Athermometers cannot measure temperature
  • Bthe Sun stops shining at night
  • Cclimate never changes
  • Dthe atmosphere is a constantly changing system with many interacting factors✓

Working

✓

Weather is hard to forecast far ahead because the atmosphere is a constantly changing system with many interacting factors, so small differences grow quickly. (A), (B) and (C) are simply false statements.

Q18

During a single day the air temperature is usually lowest:

  • Ajust before sunrise✓
  • Bin the early afternoon
  • Cat noon
  • Dat midnight exactly, always

Working

✓

Through a normal day the air is usually coolest just before sunrise, after the ground has lost heat all night, and warmest in the early afternoon. Noon and "exactly midnight, always" are traps; early afternoon is the hottest, not the coolest.

Q19

Reliable day-to-day weather forecasts are typically trustworthy only for about:

  • Athe next hundred years
  • Bthe next ten years
  • Cthe next few days✓
  • Dno period at all

Working

✓

Day-to-day weather forecasts are reliable only for roughly the next few days; they cannot be trusted for ten or a hundred years (that is the realm of climate projections, which work on averages, not daily detail).

Q20

On a clear day the air over land usually becomes warmest in the early afternoon rather than exactly at noon because:

  • Athe Sun is closest to Earth then
  • Bthe Moon adds heat in the afternoon
  • Chumidity always falls to zero at noon
  • Dthe ground keeps absorbing heat for a while after the Sun is highest✓

Working

✓

Even though the Sun is highest at noon, the ground keeps absorbing more heat than it loses for a while afterwards, so the air keeps warming and peaks in the early afternoon (thermal lag). The Sun's distance, the Moon, and "humidity falling to zero" are false explanations.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

Which instrument is used to measure the highest and lowest air temperatures reached during a day?

  • Aa rain gauge
  • Ban anemometer
  • Ca barometer only
  • Da maximum–minimum thermometer✓

Working

✓

A maximum–minimum thermometer records both the highest and the lowest temperatures reached during a day. A rain gauge measures rain, an anemometer measures wind speed, a barometer measures air pressure.

Q22

The amount of rainfall at a place is measured using a:

  • Amaximum–minimum thermometer
  • Brain gauge✓
  • Cweighing balance
  • Dmeasuring tape on the ground

Working

✓

Rainfall is measured with a rain gauge, which collects rain in a graduated container and reports it in millimetres. The other instruments measure temperature, mass, or length — not rainfall.

Q23

A rain gauge collects 25 mm of rain on Monday and 15 mm on Tuesday. The total rainfall over the two days is:

  • A10 mm
  • B20 mm
  • C375 mm
  • D40 mm✓

Working

✓

Total rainfall = 25 + 15 = 40 mm. (10 mm subtracts instead of adds; 20 mm averages; 375 = 25 × 15 multiplies — all traps.)

Q24

The "maximum temperature" reported for a day means the:

  • Aaverage of all readings that day
  • Bhighest temperature reached during that day✓
  • Ctemperature at exactly midnight
  • Dlowest temperature reached during that day

Working

✓

The day's "maximum temperature" is simply the highest temperature reached during that day — not an average, not the midnight value, and certainly not the minimum.

Q25

Humidity in a weather report is usually expressed as a:

  • Alength in millimetres
  • Btemperature in degrees Celsius
  • Cpercentage✓
  • Dspeed in km/h

Working

✓

Humidity (relative humidity) is the amount of moisture in the air as a percentage of the maximum the air can hold, so it is reported as a percentage. Millimetres are for rain, °C for temperature, km/h for wind.

Q26

On a weather chart, the difference between the day's maximum and minimum temperature is called the:

  • Atemperature range✓
  • Brainfall
  • Chumidity
  • Dwind speed

Working

✓

The gap between the day's highest and lowest temperatures is the temperature range (max − min). Humidity, rainfall and wind speed are different quantities.

Q27

A maximum–minimum thermometer on a certain day reads: maximum 34 °C, minimum 22 °C. The temperature range for that day is:

  • A56 °C
  • B22 °C
  • C12 °C✓
  • D28 °C

Working

✓

Temperature range = maximum − minimum = 34 − 22 = 12 °C. (56 adds the two; 22 and 28 just copy single readings — traps.)

Q28

Which weather element does a rain gauge NOT measure?

  • Athe depth of collected rainwater
  • Bhow much rain fell
  • Crainfall in millimetres
  • Dthe air temperature✓

Working

✓

A rain gauge measures only rainfall (depth of collected water, in mm). It does NOT measure air temperature — that needs a thermometer. The other three options all describe what a rain gauge does measure.

Q29

Consider this weather record for one week: Day : Mon Tue Wed Thu Fri Max (°C) : 30 31 33 36 34 Min (°C) : 22 23 24 22 25 On which day was the temperature range the largest?

  • AMonday
  • BWednesday
  • Call days had equal range
  • DThursday✓

Working

✓

Compute each day's range (max − min): Mon 30−22 = 8, Tue 31−23 = 8, Wed 33−24 = 9, Thu 36−22 = 14, Fri 34−25 = 9. Thursday's range (14) is the largest. (Picking the highest max alone, or assuming all equal, are traps.)

Q30

Using the same table as the previous question, the highest maximum temperature of the week was recorded on:

  • AMonday
  • BThursday✓
  • CTuesday
  • DFriday

Working

✓

The highest maximum temperature in the row 30, 31, 33, 36, 34 is 36 °C, recorded on Thursday. (Don't confuse "highest maximum" with "largest range" from the previous question — both happen to fall on Thursday here, but for different reasons.)

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

A week's record shows daily rainfall (mm): Mon 0, Tue 12, Wed 0, Thu 30, Fri 8. The total rainfall for the week was:

  • A30 mm
  • B42 mm
  • C10 mm
  • D50 mm✓

Working

✓

Total weekly rainfall = 0 + 12 + 0 + 30 + 8 = 50 mm. (30 takes only the wettest day; 42 forgets the 8; 10 is unrelated — traps.)

Q32

From the rainfall record in the previous question (Mon 0, Tue 12, Wed 0, Thu 30, Fri 8), the day with the heaviest rainfall was:

  • ATuesday
  • BFriday
  • CMonday
  • DThursday✓

Working

✓

The heaviest single day is the largest value, 30 mm on Thursday. Tuesday (12) and Friday (8) are smaller; Monday is 0.

Q33

A humidity table reads: 06:00 → 90%, 12:00 → 55%, 18:00 → 70%. The humidity was lowest:

  • Aat 06:00
  • Bat noon✓
  • Cat 18:00
  • Dit stayed constant

Working

✓

Reading the table, humidity is 90% at 06:00, 55% at noon, 70% at 18:00. The lowest value, 55%, is at noon — air is often driest in the warm middle of the day. It did not stay constant.

Q34

A weather report gives: max 28 °C, min 18 °C, humidity 85%, rainfall 12 mm. Which figure tells you how hot it got?

  • Athe humidity, 85%
  • Bthe maximum temperature, 28 °C✓
  • Cthe rainfall, 12 mm
  • Dthe minimum temperature, 18 °C

Working

✓

"How hot it got" is the highest temperature reached, i.e. the maximum temperature, 28 °C. Humidity is moisture, rainfall is rain depth, and the minimum (18 °C) tells how cool it got, not how hot.

Q35

Over four days a city's maximum temperatures were 30, 32, 34 and 36 °C. The average maximum temperature was:

  • A33 °C✓
  • B30 °C
  • C36 °C
  • D132 °C

Working

✓

Average = (30 + 32 + 34 + 36) ÷ 4 = 132 ÷ 4 = 33 °C. (36 is just the highest, not the average; 30 the lowest; 132 forgets to divide — traps.)

Q36

A place that is hot all year, with very heavy rainfall and dense forests, has a climate best described as:

  • Ahot and dry (desert)
  • Bpolar (very cold)
  • Chot and wet (tropical)✓
  • Dcold and dry

Working

✓

Hot all year + very heavy rainfall + dense forest is the hot-and-wet tropical (rainforest) climate. A desert is hot-and-dry, polar is very cold, and cold-and-dry does not fit "hot" and "heavy rain".

Q37

The polar regions are characterised by:

  • Aheavy rain and dense rainforest
  • Bextreme cold and ice for most of the year✓
  • Chot dry sand dunes
  • Dmild temperatures all year

Working

✓

Polar regions are marked by extreme cold and ice cover for most of the year. Heavy rain/rainforest, hot sand dunes and "mild all year" describe other climates.

Q38

A desert region with very high daytime temperatures and almost no rainfall has a climate best called:

  • Apolar
  • Bhot and dry✓
  • Ctropical rainforest
  • Dcold and humid

Working

✓

Very high daytime temperature with almost no rainfall is the hot-and-dry (desert) climate. Polar is cold, tropical rainforest is wet, and cold-and-humid does not fit "very hot, no rain".

Q39

Of the following, the region with the greatest variety (biodiversity) of plants and animals is usually the:

  • Atropical rainforest✓
  • Bpolar ice cap
  • Chot sandy desert
  • Dhigh mountain snowfield

Working

✓

Tropical rainforests, with warmth and abundant water all year, support the greatest variety (biodiversity) of life. Polar ice, hot sandy deserts and high snowfields are comparatively species-poor.

Q40

Which set of conditions is typical of a tropical rainforest climate?

  • Alow temperature and heavy snow
  • Bhigh temperature and almost no rain
  • Chigh temperature and heavy rainfall✓
  • Dlow temperature and very dry air

Working

✓

A tropical rainforest climate combines high temperature with heavy rainfall. The cold/snow options and the "high temperature but almost no rain" (that is desert) options are wrong.

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

A polar bear has a thick layer of fat (blubber) under its skin mainly to:

  • Ahelp it float and feel lighter
  • Bmake it run faster on land
  • Cinsulate its body and store energy in the cold✓
  • Dattract a mate by its colour

Working

✓

Blubber is a thick fat layer that insulates the polar bear against extreme cold and stores energy for lean times. It is not mainly for floating, running speed, or attracting a mate.

Q42

The white fur of a polar bear is most useful because it:

  • Akeeps the bear cool in summer heat
  • Bhelps it blend with the snow while hunting✓
  • Cmakes the bear easy for prey to see
  • Dreflects away all the bear's body heat

Working

✓

White fur lets the polar bear blend with the snow and ice so it can approach prey unseen (camouflage). It does not keep it cool, make it easy to see, or reflect away its own body heat.

Q43

Polar bears have relatively small ears and short tails. The main advantage of these small extremities is that they:

  • Areduce heat loss from the body✓
  • Bimprove the bear's hearing in storms
  • Chelp the bear swim in a straight line
  • Dincrease the surface area exposed to cold

Working

✓

Small ears and a short tail mean less exposed surface area, so the bear loses less body heat in the cold. (D) is the opposite (and wrong); hearing in storms and "swimming straight" are not the reason.

Q44

Penguins in very cold regions often huddle together in large groups. This behaviour mainly helps them to:

  • Acatch fish more easily on land
  • Bfly long distances
  • Ccool down in the heat
  • Dreduce heat loss and keep warm✓

Working

✓

Huddling together puts the birds' warm bodies close, cutting the exposed surface area of each and reducing heat loss, so the group stays warmer. It is not for catching fish, flying, or cooling down (it is already freezing).

Q45

A polar bear has wide, large paws. These are most useful for:

  • Adigging deep underground burrows
  • Bclimbing tall trees
  • Cspreading its weight on snow and gripping ice✓
  • Dflying short distances

Working

✓

Wide, large paws spread the bear's weight so it does not sink into soft snow and help it grip slippery ice. They are not for burrowing, tree-climbing, or flying.

Q46

Which combination of features would best suit an animal living in a polar climate?

  • Athin fur, large ears, dark colour
  • Bno fur, very long tail, bright colour
  • Cthick fur, small ears, white colour, layer of fat✓
  • Dthin skin, wide green stripes, webbed wings

Working

✓

A polar animal is best served by thick fur, small ears, white colour and a fat layer — all reduce heat loss and aid camouflage. The other combinations (large ears, dark/bright colour, thin skin, green stripes) would lose heat or fail to camouflage in snow.

Q47

Many rainforest monkeys have long, strong tails and limbs. The main reason is to:

  • Aclimb and swing among trees✓
  • Bkeep warm in freezing cold
  • Cstore fat for winter
  • Dcamouflage against white snow

Working

✓

Long strong limbs and a grasping tail let rainforest monkeys climb and swing among the trees where they live and feed. Warmth, fat storage and snow camouflage are cold-climate needs, irrelevant in a hot wet forest.

Q48

A red-eyed tree frog and many rainforest insects are bright green. This colouring mostly helps them to:

  • Ablend in with the green leaves✓
  • Bstay warm in the cold
  • Cglow in total darkness
  • Dfloat on water

Working

✓

Bright green colouring lets a tree frog or insect blend in with the green leaves (camouflage from predators). It is not for keeping warm, glowing in the dark, or floating.

Q49

The toucan, a rainforest bird, has a very large beak that helps it:

  • Asurvive freezing polar winters
  • Breach and feed on fruit on thin branches✓
  • Cdig burrows in dry sand
  • Dswim underwater for fish

Working

✓

The toucan's large light beak lets it reach fruit on thin branches that could not hold its weight, and handle the fruit. It is not for polar winters, sand-burrowing, or underwater fishing.

Q50

The lion-tailed macaque is well known as an animal that lives in the:

  • Apolar ice caps
  • Bhot sandy deserts
  • Ctropical rainforests of the Western Ghats✓
  • Dcold high mountain snowfields

Working

✓

The lion-tailed macaque is a monkey of the tropical rainforests of the Western Ghats in India. It does not live in polar caps, deserts, or snowfields.

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

In dense rainforests where it is hard to see far, many animals such as howler monkeys and parrots communicate using:

  • Abright body heat
  • Bwhite winter fur
  • Cthick layers of fat
  • Dloud calls and sounds✓

Working

✓

In dense forest, vision is blocked by foliage, so howler monkeys, parrots and others rely on loud calls and sounds to communicate over distance. "Body heat", "winter fur" and "fat layers" are not communication methods.

Q52

Migration is best described as the:

  • Agrowth of fur in winter on one animal
  • Bchange of an animal's colour during the day
  • Cseasonal long-distance travel of animals to escape harsh climate or find food✓
  • Dbuilding of a permanent nest in one place forever

Working

✓

Migration is the seasonal long-distance travel of animals to escape harsh climate or to find food and breeding grounds. Growing winter fur, a daily colour change, or staying put in one nest are not migration.

Q53

Siberian cranes are seen at Bharatpur in India during winter. The best explanation is that the cranes:

  • Amigrate from colder Siberia to escape its harsh winter✓
  • Bwere born in India and never leave
  • Cchange into a different species in winter
  • Dcome to India only when it is summer there

Working

✓

Siberian cranes appear in India in winter because they migrate from far colder Siberia to escape its harsh winter, returning when it warms. They are not Indian residents, do not change species, and arrive in India's winter (not summer).

Q54

The main trigger that tells migratory birds it is time to travel is usually a change in:

  • Atemperature, day length, and food availability✓
  • Bthe colour of the leaves on one tree
  • Cthe price of grain in markets
  • Dthe number of people watching them

Working

✓

Migration is triggered chiefly by changes in temperature, day length, and food availability — the natural cues of an approaching harsh season. Leaf colour on one tree, grain prices and onlookers are not the triggers.

Q55

Migration is a useful strategy mainly because, instead of enduring a harsh season in one place, the animal:

  • Astops eating until the season passes
  • Bmoves to a region where conditions are more favourable✓
  • Cgrows much larger overnight
  • Dturns invisible to predators

Working

✓

Rather than endure a harsh season where it is, the migrating animal moves to a region with more favourable conditions (food, warmth, breeding sites). It does not stop eating, grow overnight, or turn invisible.

Q56

An animal has a thick coat of fur, a small surface area compared with its body size, and white colouring. It is best adapted to live in a:

  • Apolar region✓
  • Btropical rainforest
  • Chot dry desert
  • Dshallow warm lake

Working

✓

Thick fur, a small surface area for the body's volume, and white colouring all reduce heat loss and give snow camouflage — classic polar-region adaptations. They would be useless or harmful in a desert, rainforest or warm lake.

Q57

A monkey with grasping hands and a long tail is suddenly placed in a polar ice region. It would most likely struggle because its body is adapted for:

  • Afreezing cold and snow
  • Bflying over oceans
  • Cburrowing in hot sand
  • Dclimbing trees in a warm, wet forest✓

Working

✓

A monkey's grasping hands and long tail are adaptations for climbing trees in a warm, wet forest, so it is ill-suited to bare, freezing polar ice (no trees, deadly cold, no fur/fat for it). It is not adapted to cold, ocean-flight, or hot-sand burrowing.

Q58

Why do polar animals tend to have a small surface-area-to-volume ratio (a rounder, compact body) rather than a long, thin one?

  • Aa compact body loses heat more slowly, helping survival in the cold✓
  • Ba compact body loses heat faster, which is helpful in the cold
  • Cit makes them taller than predators
  • Dit has no effect on heat at all

Working

✓

A rounder, compact body has a smaller surface-area-to-volume ratio, so it loses heat more slowly — an advantage in the cold. (B) is the reversed (false) version; the body shape does affect heat, and it is unrelated to height.

Q59

Which of the following is the clearest example of an adaptation to a HOT, WET tropical climate rather than a cold one?

  • Aa thick layer of blubber under the skin
  • Bwhite fur for snow camouflage
  • Cstrong limbs and a grasping tail for climbing wet trees✓
  • Dsmall ears to reduce heat loss

Working

✓

Strong limbs and a grasping tail for climbing wet trees is a hot-wet tropical-forest adaptation. Blubber, white snow-fur, and small heat-saving ears are all cold-climate adaptations, the opposite of what the question asks.

Q60

Four animals are described by a single feature each. Which feature would be LEAST helpful for surviving in a polar climate?

  • Aa thick double layer of fur
  • Ba wide paw that spreads weight on snow
  • Ca layer of fat under the skin
  • Dvery large, thin ears with a big surface area✓

Working

✓

Very large, thin ears have a big surface area, which would lose heat fast — the worst feature for surviving polar cold. Thick fur, wide weight-spreading paws and a fat layer all help in the cold, so the large ears are LEAST helpful. --- *Solutions complete: 60 / 60.*
