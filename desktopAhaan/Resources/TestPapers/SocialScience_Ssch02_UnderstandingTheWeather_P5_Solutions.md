## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a particular place and time; the dated entry with its noon drizzle is exactly that. Climate is the average of the weather taken over many years, which the 'decade after decade' paragraph describes. Having an exact number does not make a record climate, nor does the absence of one make it weather; and a record cannot be both at once just because it names the same town.

**2. (C)** Climate is the long-run average of weather over many years, so a single fine afternoon cannot redraw it; that afternoon is merely one day's weather. A wet climate can certainly have an occasional dry day, and weather and climate are distinct ideas, not synonyms.

**3. (A)** Latitude and day-length are not the only things that mould a climate; places sharing a latitude can still have their own long-term patterns. Both towns sit at essentially the same distance from the Sun, and the number of instruments cannot influence the actual climate.

**4. (B)** An immediate task hinges on the present state of the air, which is the weather; a choice spanning two decades depends on the area's usual long-term pattern, which is the climate. The two decisions therefore call on different scales of information, not the same one.

**5. (B)** The pattern across most years on record is the climate; a single unusual day is weather and cannot overturn a many-year average. The two halves of the report describe different things, one long-term and one momentary, so they are not both climate nor both weather.

**6. (C)** A climate label describes what is usual over many years, not a rule for every single day; individual days of weather can depart from the average, so a desert can still see a cold spell or a shower. Deserts do have dry climates, and weather and climate remain distinct concepts.

**7. (B)** The troposphere is the layer in contact with the ground, so the surface heated by the Sun, the water vapour rising from land and sea, and the rising warm air all meet there to form clouds, rain and storms. It is not the highest or thinnest layer, it is not in empty space, and oceans store water at the surface, not within a layer of air.

**8. (D)** Warm air expands and rises, lifting the top of the troposphere over the tropics; cold air contracts, so the layer sits lower over the poles. Cold air contracts rather than expands, all parts of Earth are roughly the same distance from the Sun, and rainfall does not set a layer's thickness.

**9. (B)** Air is a mixture of gases that does have weight, and the weight of the air column pressing down is atmospheric pressure. The atmosphere is neither rock nor purely water, and saying it is weightless yet somehow drives wind contradicts the idea of pressure that makes wind blow.

**10. (D)** Higher up there is less air stacked above, so the pressure drops and the thinner air carries less oxygen in each breath, which is why climbers feel breathless. Pressure falls with height rather than rising, it does not stay fixed, and it does not suddenly reach zero on a mountain pass.

**11. (D)** Pressure falls as one climbs, so the station with the highest pressure (1010 mb, P) is lowest and the one with the lowest pressure (700 mb, R) is highest, with Q in between. The largest number marks the lowest place, not the highest, the change with height is steady rather than random, and differing readings show the stations are at different heights.

**12. (A)** The barometer does read lower at the top, and the cause is exactly that less air lies above the summit to press down, so the reason properly explains the assertion. Both statements are true and they are linked as cause and effect.

**13. (A)** Temperature, atmospheric pressure, humidity and precipitation are all conditions of the air, so all four are weather elements. Each of the other sets smuggles in a land or water feature (rivers, mountains or soil type), which is not a condition of the atmosphere.

**14. (D)** Sea-level pressure sits near 1013 mb, so 1009 is the pressure; an everyday temperature is around 28 degrees; and humidity, a percentage, cannot exceed 100, so 64 percent fits. Reading 1009 as a temperature or a humidity is impossible, and swapping the 28 and 64 between temperature and humidity gives an unreasonable 64-degree reading.

**15. (A)** Weather elements describe the changing condition of the atmosphere; the time of sunrise is fixed by the regular motion of the Earth and Sun, not by the air, so it does not belong. It is not a kind of precipitation, and a barometer measures pressure, not sunrise.

**16. (B)** Words like 'warm' mean different things to different people, while a number on a fixed scale carries the same meaning for everyone, letting readings from many places be compared and combined. Numbers do not slow the weather, words are not banned, and people read instrument values all the time.

**17. (B)** Warming makes the liquid expand, and since the bulb is full the extra volume can go only up the thin tube, so the reading rises. The glass does not meaningfully widen, no air is pushed into a sealed tube, and the liquid does not float out.

**18. (D)** Celsius and Fahrenheit are simply two scales for reporting the same temperature, so the same warmth shows up as different numbers, and nothing is wrong; in fact 20 degrees Celsius equals 68 degrees Fahrenheit. The larger number is not automatically hotter on a different scale, Fahrenheit measures temperature not pressure, and one temperature legitimately gives different readings on different scales.

**19. (C)** The range is the high minus the low, 39 minus 21, which is 18 degrees; the mean is the two added and halved, (39 plus 21) divided by 2, which is 30 degrees. Adding instead of subtracting for the range gives 60, and confusing the two figures swaps 18 and 30.

**20. (C)** The ranges are W 6, X 27, Y 13 and Z 6, so Town X swings the most. A high minimum, a high maximum or a low maximum on its own does not decide the range, which is always the high minus the low.

**21. (D)** The ranges are W 6, X 27, Y 13 and Z 6, so W and Z share the smallest range of 6 degrees and are equally steady. The largest numbers, the lowest minimum, or the highest minimum do not by themselves mean the smallest range, which is the high minus the low.

**22. (B)** A bigger range means a bigger swing between the daytime high and the night-time low, so city Q's temperature varied much more even though both cities average 28 degrees. A matching mean with a wider swing does not make Q hotter all day, says nothing about rainfall, and two places certainly can share a mean while differing in range.

**23. (A)** M's mean is (30 plus 20) over 2, which is 25; N's mean is (26 plus 24) over 2, also 25, so the means match. M's range is 10 and N's is 2, so M swings more. A higher maximum does not make M warmer all day, N does not have a larger mean or range, and the ranges are clearly not equal.

**24. (D)** Precipitation covers every kind of water that falls from the sky, including rain, snow, sleet and hail. It is not limited to monsoon rain, it is not the invisible vapour still in the air, and it is certainly not air pressure.

**25. (C)** Hail is precipitation, hard ice that drops from the sky, whereas dew is moisture condensing onto cool surfaces such as grass without falling from a cloud. Dew does not fall as ice, neither is invisible vapour or wind, and neither is measured in percent or millibars.

**26. (A)** A rain gauge reports the depth the rain would form on flat ground, so 30 mm is a depth, not a time or a volume. Two gauges a few steps apart should record almost the same amount, so a wildly different reading points to a blocked or faulty gauge, not a real tenfold change over one small yard.

**27. (B)** A rain gauge is scaled to read the depth of liquid water, so frozen snow must be melted first and the resulting water measured. Snow is much fluffier than its meltwater, so its solid depth is misleading; snow is still precipitation, so zero is wrong; and there is no fixed doubling rule.

**28. (C)** Rain over a single small garden should fall in nearly equal depth everywhere, so a reading that is half the other points to a blockage or bad placement rather than a real change. Rain does not halve with every metre, a gauge does not switch to measuring temperature, and depth reflects how much fell, not how long it rained.

**29. (D)** Precipitation is water that falls from the sky, while humidity is the water vapour that remains in the air, so humidity is not precipitation. Humidity is not falling ice, it is reported as a percentage rather than in millimetres, and it is not the weight of the air.

**30. (D)** Wind blows from high to low pressure, and a bigger pressure difference over the same distance drives a faster wind. G-H differ by 28 mb while J-K differ by only 6 mb, so the wind is stronger between G and H. A smaller difference gives weaker wind, wind speed does depend on the difference, and air moves from high toward low, not the reverse.

**31. (D)** Air moves from higher to lower pressure, so with M at 1018 mb and N at 1002 mb the wind blows from M to N. It does not run from low to high, it does not simply circle in place, and the flow between two regions is sideways, not purely upward.

**32. (A)** Air does move to even out pressure differences, so the reason is true; but it does so by flowing from high to low pressure, which makes the assertion's 'low toward high' false. Hence the assertion is false while the reason is true.

**33. (D)** A wind vane points to show where the wind comes from, and an anemometer's spinning cups measure how fast it blows. Swapping the two reverses their jobs, and a barometer, thermometer, rain gauge or hygrometer measures pressure, temperature, rainfall or humidity, not wind direction or speed.

**34. (A)** A wind sock hangs limp when the air is nearly still and streams out straight when the wind is strong, so a limp-then-straight change means the wind strengthened over the morning. A straight sock signals strong wind, not calm; the sock shows wind, not pressure; and it does not measure rainfall.

**35. (C)** Slowly turning cups mean a low wind speed, and the wind vane points to where the wind comes from, here the north-west, so the wind is gentle and from the north-west. A vane does not show speed, the cups do not show direction, and the slow cups indicate weak wind rather than high pressure.

**36. (C)** A depression shows up first as falling pressure, which then drives a strengthening wind, the rising moist air builds cloud, and finally the cloud yields rain. Beginning with rain or with wind reverses the chain, and putting cloud before the pressure fall misses the earliest warning sign.

**37. (D)** A barometer measures the pressing weight of the air, while a wind vane and an anemometer measure the direction and speed of moving air, so all three deal with air. The rain gauge alone measures fallen water, making it the odd one out.

**38. (C)** Humidity needs a hygrometer and rainfall needs a rain gauge, and the station has neither, so those two elements cannot be recorded. It does have a thermometer, barometer, wind vane and anemometer, so temperature, pressure, wind direction and wind speed are all covered.

**39. (B)** Humidity is the amount of water vapour the air holds, reported as a percentage of the most it could hold. The weight of the air in millibars is pressure, the depth of rain in millimetres is rainfall, and wind speed is a different element again.

**40. (B)** A high relative humidity such as 92 percent means the air is nearly full of vapour, so very damp, while 28 percent means it is far from full, so dry. The high reading is not dryness, 92 percent does not guarantee rain, the 28 is a percentage not a temperature, and two different percentages cannot mean equal vapour.

**41. (A)** Drier air, far below its limit, can take up much more moisture, so clothes dry faster on the low-humidity Monday. Damp air near its limit accepts little more, drying is about humidity rather than a squeezing pressure, and humidity clearly does affect how fast washing dries.

**42. (A)** Rain needs the vapour to condense, which requires the air to cool sufficiently; high humidity on its own does not guarantee that, so a 94 percent day can stay dry. The hygrometer is not faulty, humidity and rain are related, and 94 percent means very damp air, not dry air.

**43. (C)** At the same temperature, lower humidity means the air is further from full and can absorb more vapour, so evaporation is easier in town A and harder in the damp town B. Town B is the moister, not drier, town; measurable humidity does not mean rain; and humidity is not a measure of which town is hotter, especially when their temperatures are equal.

**44. (C)** A barometer measures the pressing weight of the air (pressure) and a hygrometer measures the water vapour in it (humidity), so they measure different elements. They do not both measure pressure or both measure humidity, and neither measures rainfall or wind speed.

**45. (D)** Humidity is measured by a hygrometer, not a barometer, so that pairing is wrong. Temperature with a thermometer, rainfall with a rain gauge, and wind speed with an anemometer are all correctly matched.

**46. (D)** The Sun is the engine of weather: its uneven heating of land, sea and air sets up the temperature and pressure differences that drive winds and other changes. The Moon's gravity, the spinning of instruments, and the barometers are not causes of the weather; instruments only measure it.

**47. (C)** The sand heats faster than the pond, the warm air over it rises and lowers the pressure there, and air flows in from the higher-pressure pond, which is the breeze. The heating is uneven rather than equal, sunlight does not physically blow air sideways, and the Sun warms rather than freezes the sand.

**48. (A)** Near the tropics the Sun is high overhead, so its rays fall almost straight down and pack heat into a small area; at the poles the same rays arrive at a slant and spread over a larger area, giving less heat per patch. The tropics are not closer to the Sun, the poles do not get more sunshine hours overall, and no special cold stream targets the tropics.

**49. (A)** Temperature tracks the Sun: it rises while the Sun heats the ground through the morning, peaks soon after midday, and falls through the night once that heating stops, which shows the Sun is the main heat source. A barometer measures pressure, humidity does not move the Sun, and wind speed does not set day and night.

**50. (A)** With the Sun absent all night the ground keeps radiating heat away and never stops cooling, so the coldest moment is at the end of that long Sun-free stretch, just before dawn, not at midnight. The Sun does not heat at midnight, the barometer does not raise temperature, and humidity does not warm the air through the night.

**51. (C)** Nature's clues warn of weather only locally and only roughly, with no exact figures and little notice, which is their main weakness. They do not yield precise numbers, are not always right, and certainly do not pool data from across the country.

**52. (B)** Meteorology is the scientific study of weather: it measures each element with instruments, gathers data from many places over time, and uses that to forecast ahead, which nature's clues cannot do reliably. It does not depend on a single animal, it does use instruments, and its whole point is to predict, not just describe today.

**53. (B)** An AWS uses sensors that record data automatically with no person present, so it can keep gathering readings in remote or dangerous places. It does not need an hourly human operator, is not limited to daytime city use, and measures weather, not a thirty-year climate average.

**54. (C)** The India Meteorological Department is the official body that collects weather data across India and issues forecasts and warnings, including cyclone alerts. The other names are not the recognised national weather authority.

**55. (B)** Forecasts give advance warning so that people and authorities can prepare and reduce harm, as with fishermen staying ashore and drains being cleared. A forecast cannot prevent a storm forming, does not slow how often weather changes, and depends on instruments rather than replacing them.

**56. (D)** Falling pressure, rising humidity, strengthening wind and thickening dark cloud together are the classic signs of an approaching storm, so wet, stormy weather is on the way. Clear, calm, cloudless or unchanged conditions all contradict the worsening trend in every reading.

**57. (A)** A depression is a region of low pressure that can grow into a storm, so a steadily falling barometer is an early warning of an approaching depression. Falling pressure does not signal fair weather, a fixed station does not change altitude, and the log itself shows the wind strengthening, not ending.

**58. (B)** Pressure sits near 1013 mb, so 1007 is the pressure; humidity is a percentage at or below 100, so 58 is the humidity; and of the two temperatures the larger, 33, is the maximum and the smaller, 19, is the minimum. Calling 1007 a humidity or a temperature is impossible, and the maximum cannot be smaller than the minimum.

**59. (A)** Pressure falls and the air tends to be cooler with height, so traveller 2's lower pressure of 905 mb and cooler reading point to the higher position on the ridge. A smaller pressure number marks a higher place, not a lower one; pressure is not the same everywhere; and the larger pressure number belongs to the lower valley.

**60. (C)** Pressure does fall as one climbs, a barometer reads it in millibars, and a sharp drop warns of a possible storm, so every part of that statement is right. The others fail somewhere: pressure falls rather than rising and is read by a barometer not a thermometer; humidity is read by a hygrometer in percent and 100 percent means saturated, not dry; and rainfall is read by a rain gauge in millimetres while high humidity does not guarantee rain.
