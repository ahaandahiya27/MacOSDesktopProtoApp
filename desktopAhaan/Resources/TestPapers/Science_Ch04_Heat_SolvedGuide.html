Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

A small cup of boiling water at 100 °C and a large bucket of warm water at 40 °C are placed side by side. Which statement is correct?

  • ABoth hold exactly the same amount of heat
  • BThe cup has the higher temperature, but the bucket may hold more total heat because it has far more water✓
  • CThe bucket has the higher temperature but the cup holds more heat
  • DThe cup holds more heat because hotter always means more heat

Working

✓

Temperature tells how hot something is; heat is the *total* energy. The cup at 100 °C is hotter (higher temperature), but the bucket holds far more water, so it can hold more total heat even at a lower temperature. "Hotter always means more heat" (D) is the classic trap.

Q2

Heat is best described as:

  • Aa measure of how hot or cold a body is
  • Bthe average vibration of one atom
  • Cthe number marked on a thermometer
  • Da form of energy that flows from a hotter body to a colder one✓

Working

✓

Heat is a form of energy that flows from a hotter body to a colder one until both reach the same temperature. The "degree of hotness" (A) defines *temperature*, not heat; (B) and (C) confuse temperature with heat.

Q3

Two identical mugs are filled — one with 100 mL of water at 90 °C, the other with 200 mL of water at 90 °C. Which has the higher **temperature**?

  • Athe 100 mL mug
  • Bthe 200 mL mug
  • Cboth are at the same temperature✓
  • Dthe one that cools first

Working

✓

Temperature is the degree of hotness and does not depend on how much water there is. Both mugs are at 90 °C, so both are at the same temperature — the larger one simply stores more heat.

Q4

When a hot iron rod is dipped into a beaker of cold water and left alone, heat flows:

  • Afrom the water to the rod until the rod is hotter
  • Bin both directions equally, so nothing changes
  • Cfrom the rod to the water until both reach the same temperature✓
  • Donly if the water is stirred

Working

✓

Heat always flows from the hotter object to the colder one. The rod is hotter, so heat flows from rod to water until both reach a common (equal) temperature; it does not flow back to make the rod hotter.

Q5

A spoon left in a cup of hot tea slowly becomes warm. This shows that:

  • Athe spoon is making its own heat
  • Bheat flows from the hotter tea to the cooler spoon✓
  • Ctemperature and heat are the same thing
  • Dthe tea is gaining heat from the spoon

Working

✓

The tea is hotter than the spoon, so heat flows from the hotter tea to the cooler spoon, warming the spoon. The spoon makes no heat of its own, and the tea loses (not gains) heat.

Q6

Most thermometers work because the liquid inside them:

  • Aexpands (takes up more space) when heated and contracts when cooled✓
  • Bchanges colour when heated
  • Cturns into a gas when heated
  • Dgets heavier when heated

Working

✓

A liquid expands (takes up more space) when heated and contracts when cooled. The thermometer turns this volume change into a moving thread; it does not rely on colour change, boiling, or weight change.

Q7

When the bulb of a thermometer is warmed, the liquid thread rises up the tube. The thread rises because the liquid:

  • Aexpands and needs more room, so it climbs the narrow tube✓
  • Bis pushed up by air from below
  • Cbecomes lighter than the glass
  • Dis boiling inside the bulb

Working

✓

When warmed, the liquid in the bulb expands and needs more room, so it is forced up the narrow tube, raising the thread. It is not pushed by air or made lighter than glass.

Q8

The tube of a thermometer is made very **narrow** mainly so that:

  • Aless liquid is needed to fill it
  • Bthe liquid cannot escape
  • Ceven a small expansion of liquid moves the thread a large, easy-to-read distance✓
  • Dthe glass stays cooler

Working

✓

A very narrow bore means even a tiny expansion of the liquid pushes the thread a large, easily seen distance, making fine temperatures readable. (Less liquid, no escape, cooler glass are not the main reason.)

Q9

A laboratory thermometer is dipped in melting ice and the liquid settles at the mark halfway between the 0 and 10 lines. The temperature is:

  • A5 °C✓
  • B0 °C
  • C10 °C
  • D15 °C

Working

✓

Halfway between the 0 and 10 marks is 5 °C. (Melting ice is actually 0 °C, but the question fixes the thread's *position* at the midpoint, which reads 5.)

Q10

Which range is typical for a **clinical** thermometer used to measure human body temperature?

  • A35 °C to 42 °C✓
  • B−10 °C to 110 °C
  • C0 °C to 100 °C
  • D0 °C to 50 °C

Working

✓

A clinical thermometer is built to read human body temperatures, so its scale runs only about 35 °C to 42 °C. The wide −10 to 110 range belongs to a laboratory thermometer.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

A clinical thermometer has a sharp narrow bend (a "kink") in its tube. The kink is there to:

  • Amake the thermometer cheaper
  • Blet the liquid expand faster
  • Cstop the liquid thread from slipping back, so the reading holds until you read it✓
  • Dmeasure two temperatures at once

Working

✓

The kink (a sharp narrow bend) stops the liquid thread from slipping back when the thermometer is removed from the body, so the reading stays put until you read it.

Q12

Why does a **laboratory** thermometer usually have **no kink**?

  • ALaboratory liquids never expand
  • BA kink would crack the glass in a lab
  • CIt is read while still in contact with the substance, so the thread must move freely up and down✓
  • DLaboratory thermometers are always digital

Working

✓

A laboratory thermometer is read *while still in contact* with the substance, so its thread must rise and fall freely as the temperature changes. A kink would trap the thread, so it is left out.

Q13

A laboratory thermometer is marked from −10 °C to 110 °C. It would be the wrong tool to measure:

  • Athe temperature of warm milk at 45 °C
  • Bthe temperature of ice-cold water at 4 °C
  • Cthe temperature of a beaker of water being heated to 80 °C
  • Dthe exact body temperature of a feverish patient to one-tenth of a degree✓

Working

✓

A lab thermometer spans a huge range, so its divisions are coarse (usually 1 °C). It cannot read a fever to one-tenth of a degree the way a clinical thermometer can, so it is the wrong tool for option (D). It handles 4, 45 and 80 °C fine.

Q14

Before using a clinical thermometer, you give it a few sharp jerks/shakes. The reason is to:

  • Awarm it up before use
  • Bclean the bulb
  • Cbring the thread back down below normal body temperature so the next reading is correct✓
  • Dtest whether the glass is strong

Working

✓

Shaking jerks the trapped thread back down past the kink, below normal body temperature, so the next patient's reading starts from a low value and is correct. It is not for warming or cleaning.

Q15

A clinical thermometer reads in the range 35 to 42 with small marks every 0.1 between the numbers. The liquid stops on the 2nd small mark past 37. The reading is:

  • A37.0 °C
  • B39.0 °C
  • C37.02 °C
  • D37.2 °C✓

Working

✓

Each small mark is 0.1 °C. Two small marks past 37 means 37 + 2 × 0.1 = 37.2 °C. (37.02 wrongly treats each mark as 0.01.)

Q16

The normal temperature of a healthy human body is about:

  • A42 °C
  • B37 °C✓
  • C30 °C
  • D25 °C

Working

✓

The normal temperature of a healthy human body is about 37 °C (roughly 98.6 °F). 42 °C would be a dangerous fever; 25–30 °C is far too low.

Q17

On a laboratory thermometer scale, the big numbered marks are 10 apart and there are 10 equal small divisions between them. Each small division therefore stands for:

  • A1 °C✓
  • B0.1 °C
  • C10 °C
  • D5 °C

Working

✓

Ten equal divisions span 10 °C, so each division = 10 ÷ 10 = 1 °C. (0.1 °C would be right only if there were 100 divisions in that gap.)

Q18

A laboratory thermometer reads three small divisions above the 60 mark, where each small division is 1 °C. The temperature is:

  • A60 °C
  • B90 °C
  • C30 °C
  • D63 °C✓

Working

✓

Each small division is 1 °C, so three divisions above 60 means 60 + 3 = 63 °C. (90 and 30 ignore the stated 1 °C division size.)

Q19

A patient's clinical thermometer shows the thread halfway between the 38 and 39 marks. The reading is:

  • A38.0 °C
  • B38.5 °C✓
  • C39.0 °C
  • D37.5 °C

Working

✓

Halfway between the 38 and 39 marks is 38 + 0.5 = 38.5 °C.

Q20

Normal human body temperature of about 37 °C is the same as roughly 98.6 on the Fahrenheit scale. A child with a reading of 37 °C is therefore:

  • Aat about the normal body temperature✓
  • Brunning a high fever
  • Cdangerously cold
  • Dbelow the range any thermometer can show

Working

✓

37 °C is about 98.6 °F, the normal body temperature, so a 37 °C reading means the child is at about normal — not feverish and not dangerously cold.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

Heat travelling through a solid metal rod, from the hot end to the cold end, without the rod itself moving, is an example of:

  • Aconvection
  • Bradiation
  • Cconduction✓
  • Dreflection

Working

✓

Heat moving through a solid from the hot end to the cold end, while the solid itself stays put, is conduction. Convection needs the material to move; radiation needs no medium.

Q22

One end of a metal spoon is held in a flame. After a while the other end becomes hot even though it was never in the flame. This is because:

  • Athe heat radiated across the air gap
  • Bhot metal floated up to the handle
  • Cthe spoon created its own heat
  • Dheat was conducted along the metal from the hot end to the cold end✓

Working

✓

Metal is a good conductor, so heat is conducted along the spoon from the flame end to the cool end, warming the handle. There is no air gap to radiate across along the metal, and the spoon makes no heat.

Q23

Conduction is the **main** way heat travels through:

  • Aliquids only
  • Bsolids✓
  • Cgases only
  • Dempty space

Working

✓

Conduction is the main way heat travels through solids (their particles pass energy along while staying in place). Fluids transfer heat chiefly by convection; empty space, only by radiation.

Q24

Of the four objects below, heat will travel **fastest** by conduction through the:

  • Awooden ruler
  • Bplastic comb
  • Crubber band
  • Diron nail✓

Working

✓

Metals are good conductors and non-metals (wood, plastic, rubber) are poor conductors, so heat travels fastest through the iron nail.

Q25

A metal handle and a wooden handle are left in the same warm room for hours. When you touch them, the metal feels **colder** than the wood even though both are at room temperature. This is because:

  • Ametal is actually at a lower temperature than wood
  • Bwood is warmer than the room
  • Cyour hand makes mistakes about metal only
  • Dmetal conducts heat away from your hand quickly, so your skin cools faster and feels colder✓

Working

✓

Both are at room temperature, but metal conducts heat away from your warm hand quickly, cooling your skin fast, so it *feels* colder. Wood, a poor conductor, draws little heat, so it feels warmer. The metal is not actually colder.

Q26

A cook stirs hot soup with a metal spoon and leaves it in the pot; soon the spoon's handle is too hot to hold. Replacing it with a wooden spoon helps because wood:

  • Ais a good conductor, spreading heat away from the soup
  • Bis a poor conductor (insulator), so little heat reaches the handle✓
  • Cabsorbs the soup's heat and stores it safely
  • Dreflects the heat back into the soup

Working

✓

Wood is a poor conductor (insulator), so little heat reaches the handle, keeping it cool enough to hold. A good conductor (A) would make the handle hot — the opposite of what helps.

Q27

Heat travelling through water or air by the actual **movement of the heated fluid** is called:

  • Aconduction
  • Bconvection✓
  • Cradiation
  • Dinsulation

Working

✓

Heat carried by the actual movement of a heated fluid (the warm fluid itself rising and circulating) is convection. Conduction passes heat without bulk movement; radiation needs no medium.

Q28

When water in a pan is heated from below, the warm water at the bottom:

  • Astays at the bottom because it is heavier
  • Binstantly turns to steam
  • Cconducts heat to the top without moving
  • Dbecomes lighter, rises, and cooler water sinks to take its place, setting up a circulating current✓

Working

✓

Heated water at the bottom expands, becomes lighter (less dense), and rises; cooler, denser water sinks to replace it. This sets up a circulating convection current. Warm water does not stay at the bottom.

Q29

Why is a room heater most effective when placed near the **floor** for heating, while an air-conditioner is fixed **high** on the wall?

  • AWarm air rises and cool air sinks, so heating from low and cooling from high lets convection currents spread air through the whole room✓
  • BIt is only about which is cheaper to install
  • CElectric wires must be near the floor
  • DThe position makes no difference to how the air moves

Working

✓

Warm air rises and cool air sinks. Heating from near the floor lets warm air rise and spread; cooling from high on the wall lets cool air sink and circulate. Both use convection currents to mix the whole room.

Q30

In a beaker of water heated at the centre of the bottom, a few coloured crystals dropped in will trace a path that:

  • Astays still at the bottom
  • Bshoots straight up and disappears
  • Crises in the middle and sinks along the sides, showing a convection current✓
  • Dsinks straight down only

Working

✓

The crystals reveal the convection current: heated water rises in the middle (over the flame) and sinks along the cooler sides, tracing a looping path. It neither stays still nor only sinks.

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

Heat from the Sun reaches the Earth across empty space. It travels by:

  • Aconduction, through the air
  • Bconvection, through rising hot air
  • Call three equally
  • Dradiation, which needs no material medium✓

Working

✓

Between the Sun and Earth there is near-empty space with no air or matter to carry heat, so the heat must travel by radiation, which needs no material medium. Conduction and convection both need matter.

Q32

Which mode of heat transfer can take place even through a **vacuum** (no air, no liquid, no solid)?

  • Aconduction
  • Bradiation✓
  • Cconvection
  • Dnone — heat always needs a medium

Working

✓

Only radiation can cross a vacuum, because it needs no medium. Conduction needs particles in contact and convection needs a moving fluid, so neither works in a vacuum.

Q33

Standing near a glowing campfire, you feel warmth on your face even though the hot air rises straight up and does not blow toward you. The warmth you feel reaches you mainly by:

  • Aconduction through the ground
  • Bconvection blowing sideways
  • Cthe fire pushing cold air away
  • Dradiation travelling straight from the fire to your skin✓

Working

✓

The hot air rises straight up, so convection is not warming your face; instead, heat radiation travels in straight lines from the fire directly to your skin. That is why you feel warm facing the fire.

Q34

All warm objects give off heat as radiation. Compared with a cool object, a **hotter** object:

  • Agives off no radiation
  • Bradiates less heat each second
  • Cradiates more heat each second✓
  • Dradiates the same as a cold object

Working

✓

Every warm object radiates heat, and the hotter it is the more heat it radiates each second. So a hotter object radiates more than a cooler one.

Q35

A teacher claims: "Radiation can pass through empty space, but conduction and convection both need some material (matter) to travel through." This statement is:

  • Afalse — all three need matter
  • Bfalse — only convection needs matter
  • Cfalse — radiation also needs matter
  • Dtrue✓

Working

✓

The statement is true: radiation can cross empty space, while conduction (through particles) and convection (through a moving fluid) both need matter to travel through.

Q36

A material that lets heat pass through it easily is called a heat **conductor**. Which list contains only good conductors?

  • Awood, plastic, copper
  • Bair, wool, rubber
  • Cglass, cork, iron
  • Dcopper, aluminium, iron✓

Working

✓

Good conductors are metals: copper, aluminium and iron. The other lists mix in wood, plastic, air, wool, rubber, glass or cork, which are insulators (poor conductors).

Q37

A material that does **not** let heat pass through easily is called an **insulator**. Which is the best insulator?

  • Atrapped air (as in wool or a sweater)✓
  • Biron
  • Caluminium
  • Dsilver

Working

✓

Trapped air is one of the best everyday insulators (poor conductors), which is why wool and sweaters keep us warm. Iron, aluminium and silver are all good conductors.

Q38

Cooking pots are often made with a metal body but a **wooden or plastic handle**. The reason is that the metal body should ____ heat while the handle should ____ heat.

  • Ablock; conduct
  • Breflect; absorb
  • Cconduct; not conduct (insulate)✓
  • Dstore; release

Working

✓

The body should conduct heat (a metal pot to cook food), while the handle should *not* conduct heat (a wooden/plastic insulator) so it stays cool enough to hold. So: conduct; not conduct (insulate).

Q39

Which everyday object depends on a material being a **poor** conductor of heat?

  • Aa copper-bottomed cooking pan
  • Bthe metal blade of a knife
  • Cthe plastic or wooden handle of a frying pan✓
  • Dan aluminium cooking foil

Working

✓

The plastic or wooden handle works *because* it is a poor conductor, keeping the grip cool. The copper bottom, knife blade and aluminium foil all rely on being good conductors.

Q40

A jacket "keeps you warm" not by making heat but by:

  • Abeing a poor conductor that slows the escape of your body's heat✓
  • Bproducing heat from the cloth
  • Creflecting sunlight onto you
  • Dconducting your heat away quickly

Working

✓

A jacket makes no heat; it is a poor conductor (with trapped air) that slows the escape of your body's own heat, so you stay warm. It does not produce or reflect heat to warm you.

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

Two cups of equally hot tea are left out: cup P is bare metal, cup Q is wrapped in a thick woollen cloth. After ten minutes:

  • Acup Q is hotter, because wool is a poor conductor and slows heat loss✓
  • Bcup P is hotter
  • Cboth are equally hot
  • Dcup Q is hotter only if the tea was stirred

Working

✓

Wool is a poor conductor, so the woollen wrap slows heat loss from cup Q, keeping it hotter than the bare metal cup P after ten minutes. Metal conducts heat away quickly, cooling P faster.

Q42

Why do **woollen** clothes keep us warmer than an equal thickness of smooth cotton in winter?

  • AWool produces heat from the body's sweat
  • BWool traps a lot of air, and trapped air is a poor conductor that slows heat loss✓
  • CWool conducts the body's heat to the surface faster
  • DWool is heavier, and heavy things are always warmer

Working

✓

Wool's fibres trap a lot of air, and trapped air is a poor conductor, so it slows the escape of body heat — that is why wool feels warmer than smooth cotton. Wool does not make heat or conduct faster.

Q43

Wearing **two thin sweaters** can feel warmer than **one thick one** of the same total thickness because the two-layer arrangement:

  • Atraps an extra layer of air between the sweaters, and trapped air is a poor conductor✓
  • Bis heavier
  • Cproduces more heat
  • Dreflects more light

Working

✓

Two thin sweaters trap an extra layer of air between them, and trapped air is a poor conductor, so the layered arrangement slows heat loss better than a single thick layer of the same total thickness.

Q44

A bird fluffs up its feathers on a cold day. This keeps it warmer because fluffing:

  • Atraps more air among the feathers, and trapped air slows heat loss✓
  • Bmakes its body produce more heat
  • Cmakes the feathers conduct heat better
  • Dreflects sunlight onto the bird

Working

✓

Fluffing feathers traps more air among them, and trapped air is a poor conductor, so it slows the bird's heat loss and keeps it warmer. The bird is not making extra heat or improving conduction.

Q45

In **summer**, light-coloured (white) clothes are preferred over dark clothes because light colours:

  • Aabsorb more heat from sunlight
  • Bmake the body produce less heat
  • Cconduct heat to the skin faster
  • Dreflect away most of the sunlight, so the body absorbs less heat and stays cooler✓

Working

✓

Light (white) clothes reflect away most of the sunlight, so the body absorbs less heat and stays cooler — ideal for summer. Dark clothes would absorb more and feel hotter.

Q46

In **winter**, dark-coloured clothes can feel warmer in the daytime Sun because dark colours:

  • Areflect sunlight away
  • Btrap more body heat than light colours
  • Cabsorb more of the Sun's heat than light colours do✓
  • Dproduce heat from the Sun's light

Working

✓

Dark colours absorb more of the Sun's heat than light colours, so on a sunny winter day dark clothes feel warmer by soaking up sunlight. They do not reflect it or make heat themselves.

Q47

Two identical metal cans, one painted shiny white and one painted dull black, are left in bright sunshine. After an hour:

  • Athe white can is hotter
  • Bthe black can is hotter, because dark surfaces absorb more radiation✓
  • Cboth are at exactly the same temperature
  • Dthe white can is hotter only if it is bigger

Working

✓

Dark, dull surfaces absorb more radiation than shiny white ones, so the black-painted can heats up more in the Sun and ends up hotter than the white can.

Q48

A coastal town has a **sea breeze** during the day. This means that during the day the wind blows:

  • Afrom the sea toward the land✓
  • Bfrom the land out to the sea
  • Cstraight upward only
  • Dthere is no wind in the daytime

Working

✓

A sea breeze blows *from the sea toward the land*. (A land breeze would blow from land to sea — the opposite.)

Q49

During the **day**, the sea breeze blows from sea to land because:

  • Athe sea heats up faster than the land
  • Bthe land heats up faster than the sea, so air rises over the warm land and cooler air flows in from over the sea✓
  • Cthe land stays cooler than the sea all day
  • Dthe Sun pulls air toward the water

Working

✓

By day the land heats up faster than the sea. Air rises over the warm land, and cooler air flows in from over the sea to take its place — that incoming wind is the sea breeze (sea → land).

Q50

At **night** along the coast, a **land breeze** blows from land to sea. This happens because at night:

  • Athe sea cools faster than the land
  • Bthe land cools faster than the sea, so air rises over the warmer sea and cooler air flows out from the land✓
  • Cthe land stays hotter than the sea all night
  • Dthere is no temperature difference at night

Working

✓

At night the land cools faster than the sea, so now the sea is warmer. Air rises over the warmer sea and cooler air flows out from the land — a land breeze (land → sea).

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

A fisherman notices the wind blowing **out to sea** in the early morning before sunrise. This is a:

  • Asea breeze, caused by the warm land
  • Bsign of a coming storm only
  • Cbreeze that needs the Sun directly overhead
  • Dland breeze, because at night the land has cooled below the sea's temperature✓

Working

✓

Wind blowing out to sea before sunrise is a land breeze: overnight the land has cooled below the sea's temperature, so air flows from the cooler land toward the warmer sea. It is not a sea breeze and does not need the Sun overhead.

Q52

Sea breezes and land breezes are both examples of which mode of heat transfer at a large (coastal) scale?

  • Aconduction
  • Bconvection✓
  • Cradiation
  • Dreflection

Working

✓

Both breezes are caused by warm air rising and cooler air flowing in to replace it, which is convection — here acting at the large coastal scale.

Q53

The sea breeze and the land breeze blow in **opposite** directions because:

  • Athe wind always reverses every hour
  • Bby day the land is warmer than the sea, while by night the land is cooler than the sea✓
  • Cthe sea only heats up at night
  • Dthe Moon reverses the wind

Working

✓

The direction depends on which surface is warmer. By day the land is warmer than the sea (sea breeze, sea → land); by night the land is cooler than the sea (land breeze, land → sea). The reversed temperature difference reverses the wind.

Q54

A clinical thermometer is **not** suitable for measuring the temperature of boiling water at 100 °C because:

  • Aits scale only goes up to about 42 °C, far below 100 °C✓
  • Bits liquid would freeze
  • Cit has no kink
  • Dit is too long

Working

✓

A clinical thermometer's scale stops at about 42 °C, far below 100 °C, so it simply cannot show boiling-water temperature (and the heat could burst it). The kink is unrelated to this limit.

Q55

To follow the temperature of water as it is heated steadily from cold up to nearly boiling, the right instrument is a:

  • Aclinical thermometer, because of its kink
  • Bclinical thermometer, because it reads in °C
  • Cany thermometer, since all read up to 110 °C
  • Dlaboratory thermometer, because its range (about −10 °C to 110 °C) covers the whole rise✓

Working

✓

The water rises from cold to nearly boiling, so you need the wide range of a laboratory thermometer (about −10 °C to 110 °C). A clinical thermometer (35–42 °C) cannot cover that range, and not all thermometers read to 110 °C.

Q56

On a cold morning, the metal frame of a bicycle feels colder to the touch than its rubber grips, although both have been outside all night at the same temperature. The best explanation is:

  • Athe metal is genuinely many degrees colder than the rubber
  • Bthe rubber grips were warmed by the Sun overnight
  • Cmetal conducts heat away from your hand faster than rubber, so the metal feels colder✓
  • Drubber makes its own heat

Working

✓

Both are at the same temperature, but metal conducts heat away from your hand much faster than rubber, cooling your skin quickly, so the metal *feels* colder. The metal is not actually colder, and rubber makes no heat.

Q57

Tea stays hot longer in a thermos flask partly because the flask has a near-**vacuum** between its two walls. The vacuum chiefly stops heat loss by:

  • Aradiation
  • Breflection of light
  • Cconduction and convection, since both need matter to travel through✓
  • Dabsorption of sunlight

Working

✓

Conduction and convection both need matter to travel through, so removing the matter (a vacuum between the walls) blocks those two paths and greatly slows heat loss. (Radiation is cut separately by the silvered, shiny walls.)

Q58

A laboratory thermometer reads exactly halfway between the −10 and the 0 marks. The temperature is:

  • A5 °C
  • B−5 °C✓
  • C−10 °C
  • D0 °C

Working

✓

Halfway between the −10 and 0 marks is −5 °C. (Reading it as +5 ignores the minus sign — a designed trap.)

Q59

Houses in very hot, sunny regions are often **whitewashed** (painted white) on the outside because a white surface:

  • Aabsorbs the most sunlight, warming the house
  • Bconducts heat into the walls quickly
  • Creflects most of the sunlight, so the house stays cooler inside✓
  • Dproduces a cooling breeze

Working

✓

A white surface reflects most of the sunlight instead of absorbing it, so a whitewashed house absorbs less heat and stays cooler inside in hot, sunny regions.
