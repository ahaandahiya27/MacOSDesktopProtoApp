Worked Solutions

1. (D) The chapter dates the welcome back millennia: Arab merchants came in the 7th century and Jews even earlier, so 'only recently' is plainly wrong. The Tibetan and Baha'i arrivals are genuine modern examples, but they show the ethos CONTINUED into modern times, not that it BEGAN then. Scheduled-tribe recognition of the Siddis is a modern legal status laid over a community brought to India centuries ago; it dates the protection, not the arrival, and so does not establish a recent start.

2. (C) 'Atithi devo bhava' ('a guest is like God') honours the particular guest, whereas 'vasudhaiva kutumbakam' ('the whole world is one family') is the broader civilisational ideal embracing all humanity. The first distractor reverses the two scopes. Neither saying is a trade rule, so the second is false. And both are timeless values the chapter presents as guiding India across the ages, not split between ancient and modern eras.

3. (D) 'A home to many' captures India's characteristic spirit, or ethos, of acceptance that drew many communities over millennia. India is the receiver, not a conqueror dragging peoples home, so the first distractor inverts the relationship. Acceptance was not sold to paying traders only; refugees and even enslaved-origin communities were drawn in, refuting the second. And newcomers kept their own identities rather than melting into one Indian identity, so the third contradicts the chapter's core point.

4. (A) The chapter ties vasudhaiva kutumbakam to concrete enrichment: foods, words, crafts, music and faiths brought by newcomers broadened, not weakened, the culture, so welcoming outsiders strengthened it. The Siddis were not kept apart; they were drawn into society and constitutionally protected, so the first distractor misstates the evidence. Atithi devo bhava never demanded newcomers drop their customs, which contradicts the second. And India did not expel communities, so the pluralism pairing is false.

5. (D) The chapter projects the value forward: in a world where diverse peoples share space ever more closely, treating humanity as one family is a working principle for living together, which is exactly why modern examples like the Tibetans and Baha'is are cited. 'One family' means kinship, not one nation ruling others, so the first distractor twists it. The value addresses all of society, not traders alone, refuting the second. And the chapter calls it relevant NOW, not a dead relic, so the third reverses the point.

6. (D) Fleeing attack for one's beliefs is seeking refuge; chasing trade profit is seeking opportunity, so the order is refuge then opportunity. The first distractor reverses them. The second names persecution (the thing fled) and asylum (a synonym for refuge) rather than the two motive categories the chapter uses. And conquest is not one of the chapter's two paths, so the last is outside the framework.

7. (B) The Siddis break the model because they came by neither voluntary path: they were brought enslaved. The Parsis fit the model squarely as refuge-seekers and did not conquer, so the first is false. The Arab merchants came freely for trade, not under force, so the second misdescribes them. And the Tibetans came as refugees fleeing danger, not as rulers, so the third is wrong.

8. (D) The Polish children fled wartime danger and the Cochin Jews fled persecution, so both came for refuge; the Armenians traded and the Arab merchants who founded the Mappila community traded, so both came for opportunity. 'All four for trade' ignores the two refuge cases. The third option misfiles the Armenians as refugees and the Jews as traders. And none of these four were enslaved, so the last is false.

9. (C) The chapter's core distinction is choice: a refugee is pushed out by danger or persecution and has little real choice, while the immigrant comes freely seeking opportunity. Keeping traditions was open to all newcomers, so the first distractor invents a false limit. Refugees did join Indian society, the Tibetans, Parsis and Jews among them, so the second is false. And both refugees and immigrants appear across ancient and modern times, so the era split in the third is invented.

10. (A) To emigrate is to leave a country and to immigrate is to enter one, so leaving Persia is emigrating and entering India is immigrating. The first distractor swaps the two directions. A single move is naturally both an emigration from the old land and an immigration into the new, so the 'cannot also be' claim is false. And a refugee fleeing persecution is precisely a kind of migrant, so 'not migration' is wrong.

11. (C) The Tibetans fled danger and were sheltered, so 'refuge from danger' is the right match. The Siddis did not come for trade; they were brought enslaved, so the first distractor is false. The Parsis came for refuge from persecution, not trade, so the second misfiles them. And the Arab merchants came freely for trade, not as persecuted refugees, so the third reverses their motive.

12. (D) The Siddis fit neither voluntary path because they were brought enslaved, revealing that involuntary arrival is a third reality the simple two-path model leaves out. The Parsis chose refuge and settled rather than ruled, so the first is false. The Armenians came to trade, not to conquer, so the second is wrong. And because the Siddis genuinely break the model, 'none of the three' is false.

13. (C) The synagogue is the Jewish house of worship, the fire temple is the Zoroastrian (Parsi) one, and the church belongs to Christian communities such as the Armenians and Syriac Christians, so all three differ. The first distractor swaps synagogue and fire temple and wrongly assigns the church to the enslaved-origin Siddis. The second hands these buildings to Arab Muslims, Tibetan Buddhists and Parsis, none of whom owns them. And the third scrambles all three assignments.

14. (B) The Bene Israel and Cochin Jews are two of India's Jewish communities, both preserving their faith and building synagogues. African-origin enslaved arrivals describe the Siddis, not the Jews, so the first is false. The Kerala Muslim community formed by Arab settlers is the Mappila, refuting the second. And Zoroastrian fire-temple builders in Gujarat are the Parsis, so the third names the wrong faith entirely.

15. (D) Surviving synagogues alongside full participation in Indian life is the model of identity-preserving integration. It refutes the idea that acceptance demanded giving up one's religion, so the first is false. It refutes total separation, since the Jews were part of Indian society, so the second is wrong. And it has nothing to do with ruling the older inhabitants; the theme is coexistence, not domination, so the third is off-point.

16. (D) The Syriac Christians are among the world's oldest Christian communities, proving Christianity's roots in India are ancient. They did not first arrive in the 20th century, refuting the first. They were not introduced by the African-origin Siddis, who are a separate community, so the second is false. And while the Armenians were also Christians, Christianity in India is not limited to them; the Syriac Christians are a distinct, older Kerala community, so the third is wrong.

17. (A) Both are old communities that kept their faith while integrating, the chapter's model of identity-preserving integration. Neither was enslaved; that path belongs to the Siddis, so the first is false. Neither came as a conquering army, so the second is wrong. And the entire point is that they KEPT their religions, so the claim that they abandoned them in the third is the opposite of the truth.

18. (A) The Syriac Christians group with communities such as the Jews and Parsis who settled and preserved their faith, an instance of identity-preserving integration. They did not conquer Kerala, refuting the first. They did not abandon their religion; preserving it is the point, so the second is false. And they were not enslaved; that was the Siddis, so the third misclassifies their arrival.

19. (A) Zarathushtra was the human prophet who founded Zoroastrianism; the one God worshipped is Ahura Mazda. The first distractor swaps prophet and God. Zoroastrianism worships one God, not many gods of nature, so the second is false.
