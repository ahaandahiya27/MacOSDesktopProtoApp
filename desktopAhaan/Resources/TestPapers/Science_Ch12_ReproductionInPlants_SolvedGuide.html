Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

Which of the following is a feature of **asexual** reproduction?

  • Aonly one parent is involved and no gametes fuse✓
  • Bgametes fuse to form a zygote
  • Ctwo parents are always needed
  • Dseeds are always produced

Working

✓

Asexual reproduction needs only one parent and involves no fusion of gametes (no fertilisation); the offspring are clones. Two parents and gamete fusion belong to sexual reproduction, and seeds are not always produced (e.g. a stem cutting).

Q2

A gardener cuts a piece of a rose stem and plants it; it grows into a new rose plant. This is an example of:

  • Avegetative propagation✓
  • Bsexual reproduction
  • Cspore formation
  • Dfertilisation

Working

✓

Growing a whole new plant from a piece of stem of one parent — with no flower, pollen or seed — is vegetative propagation, a kind of asexual reproduction.

Q3

New Bryophyllum plants grow from buds present on the:

  • Atips of its roots
  • Bmargins of its leaves✓
  • Cinside of its flowers
  • Dsurface of its seeds

Working

✓

Bryophyllum is the classic example: tiny buds form along the notches at the margins of its leaves, and each can grow into a new plant.

Q4

The "eyes" of a potato are actually:

  • Aseeds formed after fertilisation
  • Bovules waiting to be dispersed
  • Cbuds that can grow into new plants✓
  • Dtiny roots

Working

✓

The 'eyes' of a potato are buds on the swollen underground stem (tuber); planting a piece with an eye grows a new plant. They are not seeds or roots.

Q5

Yeast reproduces by a process in which a small outgrowth forms on the parent and detaches. This is called:

  • Abudding✓
  • Bspore formation
  • Cfragmentation
  • Dpollination

Working

✓

In yeast, a small outgrowth (bud) forms on the parent, grows, and detaches to become a new yeast cell — this is budding (also seen in Hydra).

Q6

Spirogyra, a green alga, multiplies mainly by breaking into pieces, each piece growing into a new individual. This is:

  • Afragmentation✓
  • Bbudding
  • Cvegetative propagation by leaf
  • Dcross-pollination

Working

✓

Spirogyra breaks (fragments) into pieces, and each piece grows into a new filament. This mode is fragmentation.

Q7

Bread mould (Rhizopus) and ferns both reproduce by:

  • Abudding
  • Bseed dispersal by wind
  • Cstem cuttings
  • Dspore formation✓

Working

✓

Both bread mould (Rhizopus) and ferns reproduce by spore formation — tiny spores that spread and germinate into new individuals.

Q8

Spores are especially useful to fungi because they:

  • Acan survive unfavourable conditions and spread easily by wind✓
  • Bneed two parents to form
  • Care heavy and stay near the parent
  • Dare produced only inside flowers

Working

✓

Spores have a tough protective coat, so they can survive unfavourable conditions, and being light they are carried far and wide by wind — both are why spores are so successful for fungi and ferns.

Q9

Which list contains **only** plants grown by vegetative propagation in everyday gardening?

  • Apotato, ginger, sugarcane, money plant✓
  • Bmango, coconut, balsam, maple
  • Cyeast, Hydra, Spirogyra, Rhizopus
  • Ddrumstick, madar, castor, lotus

Working

✓

Potato (tuber buds), ginger (underground stem), sugarcane (stem cuttings) and money plant (stem cuttings) are all everyday examples of vegetative propagation. The other lists mix in seed-bearing plants or budding/fragmenting organisms.

Q10

A student says, "Plants grown from a cutting of one parent are exact copies of that parent." This statement is:

  • Awrong, because cuttings always mix two parents' traits
  • Bwrong, because cuttings produce seeds first
  • Ccorrect, because asexual offspring are clones of the single parent✓
  • Dcorrect only if the plant also flowers

Working

✓

In asexual reproduction there is a single parent and no mixing of gametes, so the offspring are genetically identical — clones. Hence the statement is correct, and cuttings do not first make seeds.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

The **male** reproductive part of a flower is the:

  • Apistil
  • Bovary
  • Cstamen✓
  • Dstigma

Working

✓

The stamen is the male reproductive part of the flower (it makes pollen). The pistil/carpel, with its ovary and stigma, is the female part.

Q12

A stamen is made up of:

  • Astigma and style
  • Bpetal and sepal
  • Covary and ovule
  • Danther and filament✓

Working

✓

A stamen has two parts: the anther (which makes pollen) sitting on top of a stalk called the filament.

Q13

Inside the flower, pollen grains are produced by the:

  • Astigma
  • Banther✓
  • Covary
  • Dovule

Working

✓

Pollen grains, which carry the male gametes, are produced in the anther of the stamen.

Q14

The pistil (carpel) of a flower consists of, from top to bottom:

  • Astigma, style, ovary✓
  • Banther, filament, ovary
  • Cpetal, sepal, ovule
  • Dovary, style, anther

Working

✓

The pistil (carpel) is the female part: from top to bottom it is the stigma, then the style, then the ovary (which holds the ovules).

Q15

The female gametes of a flowering plant are found inside the:

  • Aanther
  • Bovules✓
  • Cfilament
  • Dstigma

Working

✓

The female gametes (eggs) are inside the ovules, which lie within the ovary of the pistil.

Q16

A flower that has **both** stamens and pistil is described as:

  • Abisexual✓
  • Bunisexual
  • Casexual
  • Dseedless

Working

✓

A flower that carries both stamens (male) and a pistil (female) is bisexual. If it had only one of them it would be unisexual.

Q17

A papaya tree bears separate male flowers and female flowers. These flowers are best described as:

  • Abisexual
  • Bspore-bearing
  • Cunisexual✓
  • Dself-pollinating

Working

✓

Separate male and female flowers means each flower has only one kind of reproductive part, so these flowers are unisexual (as in papaya).

Q18

Pollination is correctly defined as the transfer of pollen from the:

  • Aovary to the ovule
  • Bseed to the soil
  • Cstigma to the anther
  • Danther to the stigma✓

Working

✓

Pollination is, by definition, the transfer of pollen from the anther (where it is made) to the stigma (where it lands).

Q19

The sticky, often feathery part of the flower on which pollen lands is the:

  • Afilament
  • Bstigma✓
  • Canther
  • Dsepal

Working

✓

The stigma, the sticky and often feathery top of the pistil, is where pollen lands. The anther produces pollen and the filament holds the anther up.

Q20

When pollen from a flower is carried to the stigma of **another flower on the same plant**, the pollination is:

  • Aself-pollination✓
  • Bcross-pollination
  • Cfertilisation
  • Dfragmentation

Working

✓

Pollen reaching the stigma of the SAME plant — even a different flower on that plant — is still self-pollination. Cross-pollination needs a different plant of the same kind.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

Cross-pollination occurs when pollen reaches the stigma of:

  • Athe same flower
  • Ba flower on a different plant of the same kind✓
  • Canother flower on the same plant
  • Dany plant of any kind

Working

✓

Cross-pollination is when pollen reaches the stigma of a flower on a DIFFERENT plant of the same kind. Same flower or same plant would be self-pollination.

Q22

Which set of flower features would most suit pollination by **insects**?

  • Adull colour, no scent, no nectar
  • Bbright colour, sweet scent, and nectar✓
  • Cvery light, dry, powdery pollen and no petals
  • Dflowers hidden underwater

Working

✓

Insects are drawn by sight, smell and food, so insect-pollinated flowers tend to be brightly coloured and sweetly scented, with nectar as a reward.

Q23

Wind-pollinated flowers typically have pollen that is:

  • Asticky and heavy
  • Bwrapped in a hard fruit
  • Cbrightly coloured and scented
  • Dlight and produced in large amounts✓

Working

✓

Wind cannot aim, so wind-pollinated flowers make pollen that is light and in large amounts, raising the chance that some lands on a stigma. Sticky/heavy or showy pollen suits insects.

Q24

A flower is small, green, scentless and produces huge amounts of light, dry pollen. Its likely pollinating agent is:

  • Ainsects
  • Bwater only
  • Cwind✓
  • Dbirds attracted by colour

Working

✓

Small, green, scentless flowers offer no attraction to insects; producing huge amounts of light, dry pollen is the give-away that the agent is wind.

Q25

Fertilisation in a flowering plant is the:

  • Atransfer of pollen to the stigma
  • Bscattering of seeds by wind
  • Cripening of the ovary into a fruit
  • Dfusion of the male gamete with the egg inside the ovule✓

Working

✓

Fertilisation is the fusion of the male gamete (from the pollen) with the female gamete (egg) inside the ovule, forming a zygote. Pollen transfer and fruit ripening are separate steps.

Q26

Which statement correctly separates pollination from fertilisation?

  • Athey are two names for the same event
  • Bpollination needs water but fertilisation does not
  • Cfertilisation happens first, then pollination
  • Dpollination is the transfer of pollen; fertilisation is the fusion of gametes✓

Working

✓

This is the classic trap: pollination is only the TRANSFER of pollen to the stigma; fertilisation is the later FUSION of the male and female gametes. They are different events, pollination first.

Q27

After a pollen grain lands on the stigma, the male gamete reaches the ovule by travelling through a:

  • Apollen tube growing down the style✓
  • Bhollow filament
  • Chole in the petal
  • Droot hair

Working

✓

After landing on the stigma, the pollen grain grows a pollen tube down through the style to carry the male gamete to the ovule.

Q28

Pollination must happen **before** fertilisation can occur because:

  • Athe ovary ripens into the fruit first
  • Bpollen has to reach the stigma before the male gamete can fuse with the egg✓
  • Cseeds are dispersed before the gametes meet
  • Dthe petals must fall off first

Working

✓

Fertilisation needs the male gamete (carried by pollen) to reach the egg; that gamete cannot get there until pollen has first been transferred to the stigma. So pollination must come before fertilisation.

Q29

The immediate product formed when the male gamete fuses with the female gamete is the:

  • Afruit
  • Bseed coat
  • Cstigma
  • Dzygote✓

Working

✓

The fusion of the male and female gametes forms a single cell called the zygote, which later develops into the embryo of the seed.

Q30

After fertilisation, the **ovary** of the flower develops into the:

  • Aseed
  • Bpollen
  • Cfruit✓
  • Dnew flower

Working

✓

After fertilisation, the ovary ripens and develops into the fruit (which encloses the seeds).

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

After fertilisation, each **ovule** develops into a:

  • Afruit
  • Bseed✓
  • Cstamen
  • Dpetal

Working

✓

After fertilisation, each ovule inside the ovary develops into a seed. (The ovary itself becomes the fruit.)

Q32

A fruit is found to contain many seeds inside it. This tells us the flower's ovary had:

  • Aonly one ovule
  • Bmany anthers
  • Cno ovules
  • Dmany ovules✓

Working

✓

Each seed comes from one ovule, so a fruit with many seeds must have formed from an ovary that contained many ovules.

Q33

After fertilisation, what usually happens to the petals, stamens and stigma of the flower?

  • Athey grow into the fruit
  • Bthey turn into seeds
  • Cthey become the ovary
  • Dthey wither and fall off✓

Working

✓

Once fertilisation is done, the parts that are no longer needed — the petals, stamens and stigma — usually wither and fall off, while the ovary grows into the fruit.

Q34

Which sequence of events is in the correct order?

  • Apollination → fertilisation → fruit and seeds form✓
  • Bfertilisation → pollination → fruit forms
  • Cseed dispersal → pollination → fertilisation
  • Dfruit forms → pollination → fertilisation

Working

✓

The correct order is: pollination (pollen reaches stigma) → fertilisation (gametes fuse) → fruit and seeds form. The other orders reverse or misplace these steps.

Q35

A mango fruit develops from the flower's ovary, and inside it the single big stone (seed) develops from the:

  • Aanther
  • Bfilament
  • Cstigma
  • Dovule✓

Working

✓

The fruit forms from the ovary, while the seed (the mango stone) forms from the ovule inside that ovary.

Q36

Seed dispersal mainly helps plants by:

  • Apreventing overcrowding and spreading plants to new places✓
  • Bmaking seeds heavier
  • Cspeeding up pollination
  • Dstopping fertilisation

Working

✓

Dispersal carries seeds away from the parent, which prevents overcrowding and lets the plant colonise new places where there is room, light, water and minerals.

Q37

If all the seeds of a plant simply fell and grew right below the parent, the seedlings would most suffer from:

  • Atoo few of their own kind nearby
  • Bbeing unable to photosynthesise
  • Ccompetition for light, water, minerals and space✓
  • Dtoo much room to spread

Working

✓

If seeds all grew right under the parent, the seedlings would crowd together and fight one another (and the parent) for light, water, minerals and space — exactly what dispersal avoids.

Q38

Coconut seeds are well suited to dispersal by **water** because they are:

  • Atiny and dry
  • Bcovered with hooks
  • Clight, with a spongy, buoyant husk that floats✓
  • Dexplosive when ripe

Working

✓

A coconut floats because of its light, fibrous, spongy and buoyant husk, so it is well suited to dispersal by water (it can even cross the sea).

Q39

Drumstick and maple seeds have wing-like or hairy structures. These are adaptations for dispersal by:

  • Awater
  • Bwind✓
  • Canimals that eat them
  • Dexplosion

Working

✓

Wings (maple) and hairs (drumstick) let seeds catch the air and be carried far, so these are adaptations for dispersal by wind.

Q40

Seeds of plants such as Xanthium that bear tiny hooks or spines are usually dispersed by:

  • Abursting open suddenly
  • Bfloating on water
  • Cbeing blown far by wind
  • Dsticking to the fur of animals✓

Working

✓

Tiny hooks or spines catch on the fur of passing animals, so the seeds are carried away stuck to the animal — dispersal by animals.

Set 5 · Q41–Q50

Q41

The pods of castor and balsam burst open when ripe and fling their seeds out. This is dispersal by:

  • Awind
  • Bwater
  • Cinsects
  • Dexplosion (sudden bursting)✓

Working

✓

Castor and balsam fruits dry, build up tension, and suddenly burst open, flinging seeds away from the parent — dispersal by explosion (sudden bursting).

Q42

The light, hairy seeds of the madar (Calotropis) plant are matched to which agent of dispersal?

  • Awind✓
  • Bwater
  • Canimals' digestive systems
  • Dexplosion

Working

✓

Madar (Calotropis) seeds are very light and bear silky hairs that act like parachutes, so they are carried by wind.

Q43

A juicy berry is eaten by a bird; the hard seeds pass out unharmed far away. This describes seed dispersal by:

  • Awind
  • Banimals✓
  • Cwater
  • Dexplosion

Working

✓

When an animal eats a juicy fruit, the tough seeds pass out unharmed in its droppings far from the parent — dispersal by animals.

Q44

Which seed feature is **least** likely to help with wind dispersal?

  • Abeing very light
  • Bhaving a hairy parachute
  • Cbeing heavy with a thick stony coat✓
  • Dhaving flat wings

Working

✓

Wind dispersal relies on seeds being light, often with hairs or wings to catch the air. A seed that is heavy with a thick stony coat is the least suited to being blown about.

Q45

A plant lives in still pond water and produces light, spongy floating fruits. The most suitable dispersal agent is:

  • Awind through a forest canopy
  • Banimal fur
  • Cwater✓
  • Dexplosion

Working

✓

A still-water plant with light, spongy floating fruits is adapted for dispersal by water, which carries the floating fruit to new spots.

Q46

Reproduction by seeds (sexual reproduction) differs from vegetative propagation mainly because seeds:

  • Aresult from two parents' gametes and so offspring can vary✓
  • Bare formed without any flower
  • Calways produce exact clones of one parent
  • Dcannot be dispersed

Working

✓

Seeds form from the fusion of gametes from (usually) two parents, so the offspring can vary and differ from the parents. Vegetative propagation, in contrast, makes identical clones from one parent.

Q47

A farmer wants every new sugarcane plant to be identical to one prized parent plant. The best method is to:

  • Acollect and sow its seeds
  • Bwait for wind pollination
  • Ccross-pollinate it with another variety
  • Dplant stem cuttings from that parent✓

Working

✓

To get plants identical to one prized parent, the farmer should use vegetative propagation — stem cuttings — because asexual offspring are clones. Seeds or cross-pollination would introduce variation.

Q48

Hydra, a tiny water animal, develops a small bulge that grows and separates into a new individual. This same mode of reproduction is also seen in:

  • Apotato
  • Bferns
  • Cyeast✓
  • Dcoconut

Working

✓

Hydra reproduces by budding (a bulge grows and separates). Among the choices, yeast also reproduces by budding.

Q49

Which of these correctly pairs an organism with its mode of asexual reproduction?

  • ARhizopus (bread mould) - spore formation✓
  • BSpirogyra - spore formation
  • Cyeast - fragmentation
  • Dpotato - budding

Working

✓

Bread mould (Rhizopus) reproduces by spore formation — the correct pairing. Spirogyra fragments, yeast buds, and the potato propagates vegetatively, so the other pairings are wrong.

Q50

A money-plant cutting placed in a glass of water grows roots and becomes a new plant. This shows that vegetative propagation can occur from a:

  • Aseed
  • Bspore
  • Cstem✓
  • Dpollen grain

Working

✓

A money-plant cutting is a piece of STEM that grows roots and becomes a new plant, showing vegetative propagation from a stem.

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

Which statement about pollen is correct?

  • Apollen is produced inside the ovary
  • Bpollen becomes the seed after dispersal
  • Cpollen carries the male gamete and is made in the anther✓
  • Dpollen lands on the filament during pollination

Working

✓

Pollen carries the male gamete and is produced in the anther. It is not made in the ovary, does not become the seed, and lands on the stigma (not the filament).

Q52

In a bisexual flower, self-pollination is possible because:

  • Ait has flowers on two different plants
  • Bit has no pistil
  • Cit produces spores instead of pollen
  • Dboth the anther (pollen) and the stigma are present in the same flower✓

Working

✓

A bisexual flower has both the pollen-bearing anther and the stigma in the same flower, so pollen can reach its own stigma — self-pollination is possible.

Q53

A drumstick seed has papery wings; a coconut has a spongy husk. The reason their structures differ is that they are adapted to:

  • Athe same dispersal agent
  • Bdifferent dispersal agents - wind for one, water for the other✓
  • Canimal dispersal in both cases
  • Dexplosive dispersal in both cases

Working

✓

Different seed structures match different agents: a drumstick's papery wings suit wind, while a coconut's spongy husk suits water. So they are adapted to different dispersal agents.

Q54

Why can a banana plant produce fruit even though its cultivated variety makes no usable seeds?

  • Ait reproduces only by spores
  • Bits seeds are dispersed by wind before we see them
  • Cit is grown by vegetative propagation from its stem, not from seeds✓
  • Dit does not need to reproduce at all

Working

✓

Cultivated bananas are seedless, yet they keep being grown because new banana plants are raised by vegetative propagation from the parent (its stem/sucker), not from seeds.

Q55

A flower is pollinated successfully, but no fertilisation follows and no fruit forms. The most reasonable explanation is that:

  • Athe ovary turned straight into seeds
  • Bthe male gamete never fused with the egg in the ovule✓
  • Cthe petals failed to wither
  • Dthe stigma rejected the stamen

Working

✓

Pollination (pollen on the stigma) does not guarantee fertilisation. If the male gamete never fuses with the egg in the ovule, there is no zygote, so no seed and no fruit form.

Q56

Place these in the correct life-cycle order: (i) seed germinates into a new plant, (ii) flower is pollinated, (iii) fruit with seeds forms, (iv) fertilisation occurs.

  • A(i) → (ii) → (iii) → (iv)
  • B(ii) → (iv) → (iii) → (i)✓
  • C(iv) → (ii) → (i) → (iii)
  • D(iii) → (i) → (ii) → (iv)

Working

✓

The life-cycle order is: the flower is pollinated (ii), fertilisation occurs (iv), a fruit with seeds forms (iii), and a seed then germinates into a new plant (i). So (ii) → (iv) → (iii) → (i).

Q57

Which observation would best show that a plant reproduced **sexually** rather than by vegetative propagation?

  • Athe new plant grew from a buried stem tuber
  • Bthe new plant grew from a stem cutting
  • Cthe new plant grew from a leaf-margin bud
  • Dthe new plant grew from a seed formed inside a fruit✓

Working

✓

Sexual reproduction makes seeds (from fertilisation) inside fruits, so a plant grown from a seed formed in a fruit shows sexual reproduction. Tubers, leaf-buds and cuttings are all vegetative (asexual).

Q58

A bee crawls into a bright, scented flower to drink nectar and leaves dusted with pollen, which it carries to the next flower. The bee is acting as:

  • Aan agent of fertilisation
  • Ban agent of pollination✓
  • Can agent of seed dispersal
  • Da parent in vegetative propagation

Working

✓

The bee carries pollen from one flower to another's stigma, so it is acting as an agent of pollination. (Fertilisation is the later fusion of gametes, which the bee does not perform.)

Q59

Two strawberry varieties are deliberately cross-pollinated to combine their best traits in the offspring. This deliberate crossing is called:

  • Afragmentation
  • Bhybridisation✓
  • Cbudding
  • Dself-pollination

Working

✓

Deliberately cross-pollinating two different varieties to combine their good traits in the offspring is called hybridisation.

Q60

A gardener notices new plants sprouting directly from the underground stem of a ginger plant, with no flowers, seeds or pollen involved anywhere. The gardener should conclude that the ginger reproduced by:

  • Asexual reproduction through pollination
  • Bseed dispersal by animals
  • Cvegetative propagation (asexual reproduction)✓
  • Dspore formation

Working

✓

New plants growing straight from the underground stem, with no flower, seed or pollen, is vegetative propagation — a form of asexual reproduction. --- *Solutions complete: 60 / 60.*
