1. (B) Climate is the average of weather over a long span of years; the defining feature here is the multi-year averaging that smooths out individual days. Using two elements does not change weather into climate — a single day can list both temperature and rainfall and still be weather. The format of the output (chart versus sentence) is irrelevant to whether something is climate. Covering one year is not enough either; a year of daily readings is still a year of weather, and climate needs averaging across many years.

2. (D) Climate is a repeating long-term pattern. P's totals are large and steady with rain spread through the year (wet, even); Q's are small and squeezed into two months (dry, seasonal) — and crucially both patterns recur across all five years, which is what lets you read climate from them. Slight year-to-year wobble does not make their climates the same; the totals differ roughly tenfold. Five years already shows a clear repeating signal here. Concentrating a small total into two months does not make Q wetter — Q receives far less rain overall.

3. (B) The blogger generalised one wet week (weather) into a claim about climate, the long-term average — the central weather-versus-climate trap. Climate does not flip day to day; that is precisely the property the blogger ignored. Rainfall is an element of weather (and contributes to climate too), so it is wrong to say it is never weather. Deserts do occasionally receive rain, so the error is not the belief that they never do — it is reading one wet week as the climate.

4. (D) A long average is robust: individual extreme days, hot or cold, are weather and barely move a 40-year mean. The presence of extremes does not make the climate unstable — extremes are expected within a stable climate. There is no reason to call the records errors; real extremes coexist with a steady average. And the average is not held fixed by extremes 'cancelling' — it stays steady because each extreme is one day among roughly fourteen thousand, far too small a share to shift the mean.

5. (A) Whether it is raining now is the instantaneous state of the atmosphere — pure weather, answerable from one report. 'Which month is usually driest' asks about a typical, averaged pattern — climate, needing many years. Both cannot come from one day, because 'usually driest' is an average. Both do not need long records either, because the 'right now' question is settled by current weather. The reversed pairing is wrong because 'right now' is the very thing a single report tells you.

6. (A) Asha gives a recurring yearly pattern (climate); Bina gives the present state of the air (weather). Mentioning rain or temperature does not make a statement weather — a climate description also uses those elements. The labels are not assigned by length or detail, so Asha's longer statement is not therefore weather. And the two are entirely compatible: a town with a wet-and-cold-winter climate can certainly be drizzling on a given afternoon.

7. (A) Day-to-day temperature changes, even large ones, are normal weather and say nothing about the long-term climate. A 15 °C swing between two days is perfectly possible, so the data need not be wrong. Two different days do not prove a climate shift — climate is the many-year average. Temperature is in fact one of the main elements used to describe climate, so it is not excluded from climate at all.

8. (C) Choosing clothes for the next hour depends on present conditions (weather); choosing which months to repair flood defences depends on which months usually flood (climate). Not both are climate — the next hour is governed by today's weather. Not both are today's forecast — repair scheduling spans a whole year and needs the averaged seasonal pattern. The reversed match fails because flood-prone months are a recurring climate feature, not something that changes hour by hour.

9. (D) Climate is judged from the pattern across years: the first city is remarkably steady, the second highly variable, and that contrast persists over all six years. Even if the two six-year means happened to be similar, the spread (stability versus large swings) is itself a climate difference, so 'same climate' is wrong. A place with variable yearly figures still has a climate — a variable one. And steady temperatures do not mean 'no weather'; weather still occurs daily even where the yearly average is constant.

10. (C) Wind speed is measured by an anemometer; rainfall needs a rain gauge and humidity needs a hygrometer — both distinct instruments, so they are the correct pair that the anemometer cannot give. Temperature and sunshine being Sun-related does not put them on one instrument; temperature uses a thermometer and they remain separate measurements. Humidity and temperature are not read by one hygrometer — a hygrometer measures humidity, not temperature. Sunshine is not 'falling matter' and rainfall and sunshine are measured quite differently, so that pairing's reasoning is wrong.

11. (B) For a given storm, a wider mouth catches proportionally more volume but over proportionally more area, so the DEPTH is the same — that station-independence is exactly why depth is reported. Rain gauges can measure volume; depth is preferred for comparability, not because volume is unmeasurable. Depth is an amount, not a rate, so it does not tell you how fast the rain fell. And a wider gauge collects MORE litres, not fewer, which is precisely why raw litres would not compare fairly between stations.

12. (A) A maximum-minimum thermometer mechanically marks the day's highest and lowest temperatures, so they can be read later — that is why the observer's absence does not matter. A reading of the current instant alone would miss the unobserved afternoon peak, so 'exact moment only' is wrong. It measures temperature, not rainfall, so it is not a combined instrument. And the max and min are not averages — they are the genuine extremes the instrument captured, not a mean of the day's readings.

13. (C) Daily range is max minus min: Mon 11, Tue 7, Wed 19, Thu 8, Fri 10 — Wednesday is largest at 19 °C. Thursday's high minimum (26 °C) actually shrinks its range to 8 °C, so a high minimum points the wrong way. Tuesday's low maximum gives only a 7 °C range, the smallest. Monday's range is 11 °C; having a low pair of readings does not by itself make a large gap between them.

14. (B) The highest maximum is Wed (36 °C) and the lowest minimum is also Wed (17 °C), so Wednesday holds both the hottest day and the coolest night. Thursday's minimum of 26 °C is the WARMEST night, not the coolest — a high minimum is a warm night. Tuesday's maximum of 27 °C is the lowest of the week, so it cannot be the hottest day. Monday's minimum is 19 °C, warmer than Wednesday's 17 °C, so Monday is not the coolest night; the order of the days in the week carries no temperature meaning.

15. (A) Days with rain are Tue, Fri, Sat, Sun = 4 days; total = 9 + 15 + 30 + 6 = 60 mm; average per rainy day = 60 / 4 = 15 mm. There are not 7 rainy days — three days had zero, and dividing by 7 instead of 4 is the trap. Dividing the correct total by 7 gives about 8.6 and is the same trap with the right day-count omitted. And the total is 60, not 54, with 4 rainy days, not 3 — Tuesday's 9 mm must be counted.

16. (B) Relative humidity is the fraction of the air's moisture-holding capacity that is currently filled, and that capacity rises sharply as the air warms. From morning to afternoon the temperature climbs from 18 °C to 33 °C, so the same quantity of vapour fills a much smaller share of the now-larger capacity — the percentage falls even though little or no vapour actually left (no rain fell and the air barely moved). The student's conclusion is therefore wrong (A): the drop is not evidence that vapour escaped. Relative humidity and the absolute amount of vapour do NOT always move together — a temperature change alone shifts the percentage (C). And 90% relative humidity is entirely normal on a cool, moist morning, so the morning figure is not an error (D).

17. (C) Once a gauge overflows it has hit its limit, so the recorded value is only a minimum — the real total could be much higher. It is therefore wrong to call 150 mm the exact amount. An overflowing rain gauge does not switch to measuring wind. And overflow does not mean rain stopped; rain may well have continued, with the extra simply spilling out unmeasured.

18. (A) Daily ranges are 28-18=10, 30-20=10, 35-21=14, 31-17=14; their average is (10+10+14+14)/4 = 48/4 = 12 °C. Subtracting one overall lowest minimum from an average maximum mixes incompatible quantities and gives a wrong 18. The average of the maxima alone (31) is not a range at all. Taking the single biggest max minus the single smallest min (35-17=18, or pairing extremes across days for 47) confuses the whole-period spread with the typical daily range.

19. (D) After noon the ground still gains heat faster than it sheds it, so the air keeps warming until gains and losses balance — that imbalance produces the afternoon lag. The Sun's distance does not change between noon and 2 p.m. in any way that matters; that idea is a misconception. A working thermometer responds quickly, so instrument lag is not the cause. And humidity does not fall to zero, nor is dryness the reason — the lag is about stored heat accumulating after the Sun's peak.

20. (D) Uneven solar heating sets up temperature and pressure differences (driving wind) and evaporates water (feeding clouds and rain) — a single energy source branching into both. Sunlight's physical push on air is far too weak to be the cause of wind; the cause is heating-driven pressure differences. The Sun warms, it does not cool, the oceans, and warming is what drives evaporation. The Moon does not supply the energy for weather; the Sun is the single ultimate source for both wind and rain.

21. (A) The equator is hot because sunlight arrives nearly overhead, packing its energy onto a small area; near the poles the slanted beam spreads the same energy over more ground, so it warms less. The few thousand kilometres of difference in distance to the Sun (out of about 150 million km) is utterly negligible, so 'closer to the Sun' is wrong. The equator does not get 24-hour daylight; that long-day effect is a polar-summer feature. Cities and machines are not why the equator is warm — it is the directness of the Sun's rays.

22. (D) Sustained darkness in winter drives temperatures very low, and although polar summer brings long days, the Sun stays low and weak, so the region's climate remains cold overall. The midnight Sun does not make the poles hot; its slanted rays deliver little heat. Darkness does not trap heat to make the region hot — losing incoming sunlight cools it. And the extremes do not 'cancel' into a mild climate; the net result over the year is cold.

23. (B) Same clock time, different exposure, different temperature — this isolates sunlight as the heating agent. If the clock controlled temperature the two spots would read the same, so the clock is not the cause. Shade is cooler because it receives no direct sunlight, not because cold air selectively sinks into it. And the shaded thermometer is not faulty; it correctly reports that an unlit spot is cooler than a sunlit one at the same moment.

24. (D) The atmosphere's many interacting factors amplify tiny differences over time, so distant daily detail is unpredictable, yet the long-run average (the season) stays reliably known — both facts fit together. The Sun does not weaken a year out, so that is not why distant days are unknowable. Climate is very much studiable — indeed it is what lets us predict the season. And thermometers do not stop after a day; instrument failure is not the limit, the atmosphere's sensitivity is.

25. (B) Both effects share one cause: when the Sun is low (winter, or near the poles) its beam hits at a slant and spreads thin, delivering less heat per unit ground. The Earth-Sun distance change across seasons is tiny and is not the reason. Winter days are SHORTER, not longer, so that statement is factually wrong as well. And it is not that winter air physically blocks the light; the weakening comes from the angle of the rays, the same principle that cools the poles.

26. (D) The equator gets concentrated overhead rays year-round (hot); the poles get the same beam smeared over more area at a low angle (cold) — directness of sunlight sets the climate contrast. The two regions do NOT receive equal energy per unit area; that is precisely the difference. Long polar summer days do not overcome the weak slant, so the poles are not warmer. And direct rays carry more heat per area than slanting ones, so the equator-cold / pole-hot claim is exactly backwards.

27. (B) A tropical rainforest is hot all year (small swing) and very wet — City A's 29/27 °C with 2300 mm fits exactly. City B's huge 45-to-8 swing with almost no rain is a hot desert, not a rainforest. City C is freezing (polar), regardless of resembling B's low rainfall. City D is warm but its larger seasonal swing and only moderate 700 mm rainfall fall short of the constant heat and heavy rain a rainforest needs.

28. (A) Constant warmth, very high humidity, abundant year-round rain and a tall canopy are textbook rainforest, and both climbing limbs (for moving among trees) and loud calls (to communicate where dense leaves block vision) suit it. The data are far too warm and wet for a polar region, and there is no ice to grip. They are far too wet for a desert, and tails do not store water. They are far too warm for a snowfield, and there is no snow to dig. Only the rainforest reading ties every clue together.

29. (B) Fat insulates and stores energy, a compact body conserves heat (low surface-area-to-volume), and white fur hides the animal in snow — all cold-climate features, which is why such an animal would overheat and stand out in a warm green rainforest. They do not help it lose heat; they do the opposite, so it would not stay cool. White fur camouflages against snow, not green leaves, so it would be conspicuous, not hidden. None of these features store water — that is a desert idea, not their purpose here.

30. (D) A compact body has less surface area for its volume, so it radiates heat away more slowly — X is favoured in the cold, while slender Y sheds heat too quickly. Extra surface area loses far more heat than the weak polar Sun could add, so Y does not benefit. Body shape strongly affects heat loss, so the two are not equal. And a compact body exposes LESS relative surface, so X loses heat slower, not faster — the reasoning in that option is inverted.

31. (C) Wide paws serve double duty — distributing weight on soft snow and paddling in water — and the fur and fat together are the insulation that keeps the bear warm in freezing air and sea. The fat's chief role is insulation and energy storage, not floating to ambush prey. The dense fur is for warmth, not for cooling in summer. And swimming is for hunting and moving between ice, not for tree-climbing, which polar bears do not do.

32. (B) The white coat conceals the bear against snow while it stalks seals — its primary job is camouflage, not warming by colour. In fact white reflects rather than absorbs sunlight, so 'white absorbs the most heat' is physically wrong. Standing out to other bears is not why it is white. And being visible to prey would hurt hunting, so making the hunt 'fair' is not an adaptation's purpose — concealment is.

33. (C) By packing together, birds on the inside expose almost no surface to the wind, cutting the group's overall heat loss — the point of huddling. It does not increase exposed surface; that would lose more heat, the opposite of the goal. Huddling on ice has nothing to do with herding fish. And it traps WARM, not cold, air among the birds; trapping cold air would not help them stay warm.

34. (D) Ear surface area governs heat exchange: big thin ears (with flapping) dump excess heat in the warmth, small ears minimise heat loss in the cold — one principle, two opposite climates. The ears are explained by heat regulation here, not primarily by hearing. Large ears SHED heat rather than trap it, so 'large ears trap heat to keep the elephant warm' is wrong. And ear size tracks climate strongly, not body size alone, which is the very point the question makes.

35. (D) Both cases are camouflage: green blends with leaves, white blends with snow — colour is favoured when it matches the local background. Colour here is about concealment, not warming; neither green nor white is chosen to absorb heat. Neither animal glows in the dark, so visibility to its own kind is not the point. And being the brightest to scare predators is the opposite of camouflage, which works by NOT being noticed.

36. (A) Grasping hands and a long tail are for climbing trees and loud calls are for cutting through dense foliage — all rainforest tools useless on bare, treeless, freezing ice, and the monkey also lacks insulation for the cold. The features are not cold-survival traits, so the problem is far more than unfamiliar terrain. They are not burrowing-in-sand traits either — that is a desert idea. And the monkey cannot fly, so 'flying over ocean' misdescribes it entirely.

37. (C) A constant warm, wet climate yields plentiful, diverse resources year-round, which can sustain a great many species — that is why rainforests are so biodiverse. Polar cold does not speed reproduction, and size is not the driver of variety. Rainforests are dense with plants, not nearly bare; abundant plant life is exactly what supports the animals. And biodiversity is set by climate and resources, not by the number of human visitors.

38. (C) A toucan's long light beak lets it pluck fruit from branches too thin to bear its weight, and a polar bear's dense fur is insulation against the cold — each feature matched to its real job. The beak does not store fat, and the fur warms rather than cools the bear, so that pairing fails on both counts. The beak is for feeding, not digging burrows, and the bear's fur camouflages it against snow, not leaves. The beak does not heat the head, and fur is for warmth, not for faster swimming, so the last pairing is wrong throughout.

39. (C) Double fur, fat, a compact body and white colour all conserve heat and hide the animal in snow — the perfect set for cold, icy Habitat N. Thick fur is for cold, not for heavy rain, so Habitat M's warmth and rain do not call for it. White hides an animal in snow, not against green leaves, so it would stand out in M. And insulation is only an advantage where heat must be conserved; in warm M these features would cause overheating, so the habitats are not equal.

40. (D) An adaptation is a heritable feature that suits an animal to its environment over many generations; green that consistently matches rainforest leaves and lowers predation is exactly that. Green colour does not keep a frog warm, and warmth is not the issue in a hot rainforest. The frog is not a desert animal cooling by reflection. And it does not glow to lure prey in darkness — its colour works as daytime camouflage among leaves.

41. (D) An adaptation is inherited and shaped over many generations to fit the climate — successive polar bears with thick fur and fat are the textbook case. One bear feeling warm on a mild day is a momentary experience, not an inherited feature. A monkey learning a trick in a morning is learned behaviour, not a heritable adaptation. A bird sheltering indoors once is a one-off act, not a feature passed down over long times.

42. (B) Migration tracks the predictable seasonal climate cycle — the bird avoids the long northern winter and returns when conditions there improve, a pattern governed by the season, not one day's weather. It is not the weather of a single departure day that drives the journey. The same cranes return year after year; they do not die out and get replaced. And a bird does not transform into another species; it is the recurring climate pattern, not a weather-triggered transformation, that explains the back-and-forth.

And temperature is central to defining a polar region — it is the persistence of cold, not its absence from the definition, that the child overlooked.
