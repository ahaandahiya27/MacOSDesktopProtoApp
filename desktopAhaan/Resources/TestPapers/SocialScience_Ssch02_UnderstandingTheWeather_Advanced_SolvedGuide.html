Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

The single best one-line difference between *weather* and *climate* is that weather is:

  • Athe average of temperature alone, while climate adds rainfall
  • Babout the sky, while climate is about the land
  • Cmeasured by instruments, while climate is only guessed
  • Dthe state of the atmosphere at a particular place and time, while climate is its long-term average over many years✓

Working

✓

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at *one place and one time* — today's heat, drizzle and breeze; **climate** is the long-term *average* of that weather over many years. Confusing the snapshot with the average is the classic trap.

Q2

Almost all of the weather we experience happens in the lowest layer of the atmosphere, the:

  • Atroposphere✓
  • Bmesosphere
  • Cstratosphere
  • Dthermosphere

Working

✓

Nearly all weather — clouds, rain, storms — happens in the **troposphere**, the lowest layer where almost all the air's water vapour sits. The stratosphere above is calm and dry.

Q3

The five elements that together describe the weather are temperature, precipitation, atmospheric pressure, wind and:

  • Asunshine
  • Bhumidity✓
  • Caltitude
  • Dlatitude

Working

✓

The five weather elements are temperature, precipitation, atmospheric pressure, wind and **humidity**. Altitude and latitude shape weather but are *places on Earth*, not elements you read off instruments.

Q4

A meteorologist reads the morning data and writes "33 °C, light drizzle, 1003 hPa, breeze from the west, RH 78%." The "1003 hPa" is a measure of:

  • Atemperature
  • Bhumidity
  • Catmospheric pressure✓
  • Dwind speed

Working

✓

Pressure is quoted in hectopascals (hPa), so **1003 hPa** is the **atmospheric pressure**. Temperature is the °C, humidity the RH%, and wind the "breeze from the west."

Q5

Temperature is measured with a thermometer; the instrument that measures the *amount of rainfall* is the:

  • Abarometer
  • Banemometer
  • Chygrometer
  • Drain gauge✓

Working

✓

Rainfall is caught and measured by the **rain gauge**. The barometer reads pressure, the hygrometer humidity, the anemometer wind speed.

Q6

A rain gauge collects rain in a measuring cylinder and the reading is given in **millimetres**. Those millimetres describe:

  • Athe depth to which water would stand on flat ground if none drained away✓
  • Bthe total volume of water that fell on the city
  • Cthe number of raindrops per minute
  • Dthe speed at which the rain fell

Working

✓

A rain gauge reading in millimetres is the **depth** the water would stand on level ground if none drained or soaked away — not a volume. That is why two places can both report "20 mm" regardless of their size.

Q7

Two rain gauges, one with a wide funnel and one with a narrow funnel, are left side by side in the same shower. They will record:

  • Aa larger reading for the wide funnel
  • Bthe same rainfall in mm, because depth does not depend on funnel width✓
  • Ca larger reading for the narrow funnel
  • Dno reading unless the funnels are equal

Working

✓

Both gauges record **the same rainfall in mm**: a wider funnel catches more water *but* spreads it over a wider cylinder, so the depth comes out the same. Depth does not depend on funnel width.

Q8

The instrument that measures **wind speed** is the anemometer; the one that shows the **direction** the wind blows *from* is the:

  • Abarometer
  • Bhygrometer
  • Cwind vane✓
  • Drain gauge

Working

✓

The **wind vane** swings to show the direction the wind blows *from*; the anemometer's spinning cups measure how *fast* it blows.

Q9

A wind vane points steadily to the east. Meteorologists would call this:

  • Aa west wind
  • Ba still day
  • Ca north wind
  • Dan east wind✓

Working

✓

A wind vane points *into* the wind — toward where the air comes from. Pointing east means the air arrives from the east, so it is named an **east wind**.

Q10

Winds are always named after the direction **from which** they come because:

  • Athat direction tells you what kind of air — its temperature and moisture — is arriving✓
  • Bit sounds older and more poetic
  • Cthe wind vane can only point that way
  • Dthe law requires it

Working

✓

Winds are named for where they come **from** because **that direction tells you the kind of air arriving** — a sea wind is moist, a desert wind dry, a polar wind cold. The name is really a forecast in disguise.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

Relative humidity is best described as:

  • Athe total mass of all water on Earth
  • Bhow much water vapour the air holds compared with the most it could hold at that temperature, as a percentage✓
  • Cthe depth of rain in millimetres
  • Dthe pressure of the water in clouds

Working

✓

Relative humidity is **how much vapour the air holds compared with the most it could hold at that temperature, written as a percentage**. At 100% the air is saturated and dew or rain can form.

Q12

The instrument used to measure humidity is the:

  • Athermometer
  • Bbarometer
  • Chygrometer✓
  • Dwind vane

Working

✓

Humidity is measured with the **hygrometer**. (A common version uses a wet-and-dry-bulb pair of thermometers.)

Q13

On a calm evening the air cools after sunset but no extra moisture is added. The relative humidity will:

  • Afall, because cold air holds more vapour
  • Bdrop to zero
  • Cstay exactly the same
  • Drise, because cooler air can hold *less* vapour, so the same amount fills a larger share of the limit✓

Working

✓

Cooler air can hold **less** vapour, so as the evening cools the *same* amount of moisture fills a larger share of the (now smaller) limit — relative humidity **rises**, even though no water was added. Keep cooling and it reaches 100% and dew forms.

Q14

Dew forms on grass on a clear, still night chiefly because:

  • Athe ground cools by radiating heat to the open sky, and the chilled air right above it can no longer hold all its vapour, which condenses✓
  • Bthe ground heats up and pushes water out
  • Cclouds squeeze water onto the grass
  • Dthe wind blows water from rivers

Working

✓

On a clear, still night **the ground radiates its heat away to the open sky and cools; the chilled air just above it can no longer hold all its vapour, which condenses** as dew. Clouds or wind would prevent that cooling.

Q15

A clear, cloudless night is usually **colder** than a cloudy one because:

  • Aclouds make their own heat
  • Bclouds act like a blanket, trapping the heat the ground radiates; with no clouds, that heat escapes to space✓
  • Cclear nights are always in winter
  • Dthe stars cool the air

Working

✓

**Clouds act like a blanket**, absorbing and returning the heat the ground radiates at night. With a clear sky that heat escapes straight to space, so cloudless nights turn colder.

Q16

The barometer reading is *falling* steadily through the afternoon. This most often warns of:

  • Aclear, dry, settled weather ahead
  • Ba coming heat wave with no clouds
  • Capproaching unsettled or stormy weather, since low pressure goes with rising, cloud-forming air✓
  • Dno change at all

Working

✓

A **falling** barometer signals **approaching unsettled or stormy weather**: low pressure goes with rising air that cools, condenses and builds clouds. Rising pressure means settled, sinking air.

Q17

Why does **warm air tend to lower** the atmospheric pressure beneath it?

  • Awarm air is heavier and presses down harder
  • Bwarmth has no link to pressure
  • Cwarm air turns instantly into rain
  • Dwarm air expands, becomes less dense and rises, so it presses down less✓

Working

✓

**Warm air expands, grows less dense and rises**, so it presses down less — lowering the pressure beneath it. That is why hot regions and storm centres are low-pressure areas.

Q18

Air generally moves from a region of **high pressure to low pressure**. This moving air is what we call:

  • Awind✓
  • Bhumidity
  • Cprecipitation
  • Da cloud

Working

✓

Air flowing from high pressure to low pressure *is* **wind**. The bigger the pressure difference, the stronger the wind.

Q19

"Precipitation" is a single word that covers rain and also:

  • Aonly fog
  • Bsnow, sleet and hail✓
  • Cwind and dust
  • Dsunshine and shade

Working

✓

Precipitation is any water falling from the sky: rain, **snow, sleet and hail**. Fog hangs in the air rather than falling, and wind/dust are not water.

Q20

Hail can fall even in summer because hailstones form:

  • Aon the warm ground and bounce up
  • Bonly when the surface temperature is below 0 °C
  • Chigh inside tall storm clouds where the air is freezing, and are tossed up and down gaining icy layers before they drop✓
  • Dfrom snow on mountains blown by wind

Working

✓

Hailstones grow **high inside tall storm clouds where the air is below freezing; up-and-down currents toss them, adding icy layers** until they are heavy enough to fall — so hail can crash down even on a hot summer afternoon.

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

The systematic, scientific study of the atmosphere and weather is called:

  • Aastronomy
  • Bcartography
  • Cgeology
  • Dmeteorology✓

Working

✓

The scientific study of the atmosphere and weather is **meteorology**; a person who does it is a meteorologist. Astronomy studies the stars, geology the rocks, cartography maps.

Q22

In India, the official body that records weather data and issues forecasts and warnings is the:

  • AIndia Meteorological Department (IMD)✓
  • BISRO
  • CNDMA
  • DSurvey of India

Working

✓

India's official weather body is the **India Meteorological Department (IMD)**, which records data and issues forecasts and warnings. ISRO runs space, NDMA manages disasters, Survey of India makes maps.

Q23

An **Automated Weather Station (AWS)** is valuable mainly because it:

  • Apredicts the weather by itself without any scientists
  • Brecords data continuously and sends it automatically, even from remote places where no observer can sit✓
  • Cstops storms from forming
  • Dmeasures only rainfall

Working

✓

An Automated Weather Station's value is that it **records continuously and transmits data automatically, even from remote spots** where no observer could be posted — filling gaps in the network.

Q24

Long before instruments, people forecast weather by reading Nature's clues — ants moving eggs to higher ground, frogs croaking loudly, pine cones closing up. These clues worked because:

  • Ait was pure coincidence every time
  • Bthe animals had been trained
  • Canimals and plants can sense changes in humidity and pressure that come before rain✓
  • Dthe Sun told them

Working

✓

The old animal-and-plant signs worked because **living things sense the humidity and pressure changes that precede rain** — ants, frogs and pine cones react to real physical cues, not magic or chance.

Q25

Pine cones **close their scales** in damp air and open them in dry air. A closed pine cone therefore hints at:

  • Adry, sunny weather coming
  • Bstrong wind only
  • Ca coming cold wave only
  • Ddamp, possibly rainy weather, because high humidity makes the scales swell shut✓

Working

✓

Pine-cone scales swell shut in damp air, so a **closed cone hints at high humidity and possibly rain**; open scales mean dry air. It is a natural hygrometer.

Q26

The proverb "Red sky at night, sailor's delight; red sky in morning, sailor's warning" can carry real information because weather in many places moves:

  • Afrom west to east, so a red sunset means clear weather is to the west and approaching, while a red sunrise means the clear belt has passed and clouds may follow✓
  • Bfrom east to west, so a red dawn (light through eastern clear air onto western clouds) hints at incoming bad weather
  • Cstraight up and down
  • Donly at night

Working

✓

In much of the world weather moves **from west to east**, so a red *sunset* (clear air to the west, lit through to clouds) means fine weather is approaching, while a red *sunrise* means the clear belt has already passed eastward and cloud may follow — the kernel of truth in the old rhyme.

Q27

A "heat wave" is declared not by a single hot afternoon but when:

  • Ait rains heavily for a week
  • Bthe temperature stays unusually high, well above normal, for several days together✓
  • Cthe wind stops for one hour
  • Dthe barometer rises once

Working

✓

A heat wave is declared when **the temperature stays unusually high, well above normal, for several days running** — a sustained spell, not one hot afternoon.

Q28

The chief danger of a heat wave to people is:

  • Aflooding of low areas
  • Bfrostbite
  • Cheatstroke and dehydration, especially for the very young, the old and outdoor workers✓
  • Dlightning strikes

Working

✓

The chief danger of a heat wave is **heatstroke and dehydration**, hitting the very young, the elderly and outdoor workers hardest. Forecasts let them take shelter and drink water.

Q29

Why does accurate weather forecasting **save lives**, beyond mere convenience? Because warnings let people:

  • Abuy umbrellas
  • Bchoose what to wear
  • Ccancel cricket matches
  • Devacuate before a cyclone, store water before a drought, and stay indoors during a heat wave✓

Working

✓

Forecasts save lives because warnings let people **evacuate before a cyclone, store water before a drought and stay indoors during a heat wave** — preparation, not just knowing what to wear.

Q30

A coastal district receives an IMD "red alert" for a cyclone. The most sensible community response is to:

  • Amove people from low, exposed areas to safe shelters and secure boats and loose objects in advance✓
  • Bwait until the wind actually arrives
  • Cgo to the beach to watch
  • Dignore it, since forecasts are guesses

Working

✓

On a cyclone red alert the right move is to **shift people from low, exposed areas to safe shelters and secure boats and loose objects in advance** — acting on the warning rather than waiting for the wind.

Set 4 · Q31–Q40

Q31

At 6 a.m. a thermometer reads 18 °C and by 2 p.m. it reads 31 °C. The single biggest reason for the daily rise is:

  • Athe Earth moving closer to the Sun by afternoon
  • Bthe Sun climbing higher, so its rays heat the ground more strongly through the morning✓
  • Cmore rain at noon
  • Dlower pressure at noon

Working

✓

The day warms because **the Sun climbs higher through the morning and heats the ground more strongly**; the warmed ground then warms the air. The Earth's distance from the Sun barely changes over a day.

Q32

The hottest part of a clear day is usually **early-to-mid afternoon, not exactly noon**, because:

  • Athe Sun is brightest at 3 p.m.
  • Bclouds always gather at noon
  • Cthe ground keeps absorbing more heat than it loses for a while after the Sun's peak, so temperature lags behind✓
  • Dthe thermometer is slow

Working

✓

The hottest time lags past noon because **the ground keeps absorbing more heat than it loses for a while after the Sun's peak**, so the air goes on warming into the early afternoon.

Q33

A maximum–minimum thermometer is read each day to find the day's highest and lowest temperatures. The **range** for the day is:

  • Athe maximum plus the minimum
  • Bthe minimum only
  • Cthe average of the two
  • Dthe maximum minus the minimum✓

Working

✓

The day's temperature **range = maximum − minimum**, found from a max–min thermometer. It tells how much the temperature swung that day.

Q34

On Monday a town's max was 34 °C and min 22 °C; on Tuesday max 30 °C and min 24 °C. Which day had the **larger temperature range**, and by how much?

  • AMonday, by 6 °C✓
  • BTuesday, by 6 °C
  • CMonday, by 12 °C
  • Dboth equal

Working

✓

Monday's range = 34 − 22 = **12 °C**; Tuesday's = 30 − 24 = 6 °C. So **Monday had the larger range, by 6 °C**.

Q35

Coastal cities like Mumbai usually have a **smaller day-to-night temperature range** than inland cities like Nagpur mainly because:

  • Athe coast is always cloudy
  • Bthe sea, which warms and cools slowly, moderates the air over nearby land✓
  • Ccoastal cities have more thermometers
  • Dinland cities are higher up

Working

✓

Coasts have a smaller day–night swing because **the sea warms and cools slowly and moderates the air over nearby land**. Inland, without that buffer, days are hotter and nights colder.

Q36

Climbing a high mountain, you feel the air get **colder** as you go up the troposphere. This is because:

  • Ayou are nearer the Sun so it should be hotter, but wind cools you
  • Bsnow on top makes its own cold
  • Cthe air is thinner and is mostly warmed from the ground up, so it cools with height✓
  • Dpressure has no effect on temperature

Working

✓

Up a mountain it gets colder because **the air is thinner and is warmed mainly from the ground up**, so temperature falls with height — even though you are technically a little nearer the Sun.

Q37

As you climb, the **atmospheric pressure** also:

  • Arises, because air piles up at the top
  • Bbecomes zero at once
  • Cstays exactly the same everywhere
  • Dfalls, because there is less air above you pressing down✓

Working

✓

Pressure **falls with height because there is less air above you pressing down**. That is why climbers carry oxygen and sealed packets puff up high on a mountain.

Q38

Fog and cloud are really the same thing — tiny water droplets in the air. The difference is simply that:

  • Afog touches the ground while cloud floats higher up✓
  • Bfog is made of ice and cloud of water
  • Cfog is at night and cloud by day
  • Dcloud is heavier than fog

Working

✓

Fog and cloud are the same tiny droplets; the only difference is that **fog sits on the ground while cloud floats higher up**. Walk into a hill fog and you are walking through a cloud.

Q39

Low, grey, sheet-like clouds that often bring steady drizzle are broadly the **stratus** family; tall, towering clouds that pile up and bring thunderstorms are the:

  • Acirrus family
  • Bcumulus (cumulonimbus) family✓
  • Cstratus family again
  • Dfog family

Working

✓

Tall, piled-up clouds that bring thunderstorms are the **cumulus / cumulonimbus** family; low grey sheets that drizzle are stratus; high wispy ones are cirrus.

Q40

Thin, wispy, high clouds made of ice crystals, often the first sign that weather may change in a day or two, are the:

  • Acumulus clouds
  • Bstratus clouds
  • Ccirrus clouds✓
  • Dnimbus sheets

Working

✓

Thin, wispy, high ice-crystal clouds are **cirrus**, often the first hint that the weather may change in a day or two.

Q41

We hear thunder **after** we see lightning because:

  • Athe thunder is made later than the flash
  • Bour ears are slower than thunder
  • Cthunder comes from a different cloud
  • Dlight travels far faster than sound, so the flash reaches us almost at once while the sound lags✓

Working

✓

We see lightning before we hear thunder because **light travels far faster than sound** — the flash arrives almost instantly while the bang lags behind.

Q42

You see a lightning flash and count "one-thousand-one, one-thousand-two, one-thousand-three" — about 3 seconds — before the thunder. Since sound travels roughly 1 km in 3 seconds, the storm is about:

  • A1 km away✓
  • B3 km away
  • C9 km away
  • Dright overhead

Working

✓

Sound covers about 1 km every 3 seconds, so a 3-second gap puts the storm roughly **1 km away**. (A 9-second gap would mean about 3 km.)

Q43

The safest action during a thunderstorm is to:

  • Ashelter under a tall, isolated tree
  • Bget indoors, away from open fields, water and tall lone objects✓
  • Cstand on a hilltop to watch
  • Dhold a metal umbrella up high

Working

✓

In a thunderstorm the safest place is **indoors, away from open fields, water and tall lone objects**. A solitary tree or hilltop is exactly where lightning tends to strike.

Q44

Why is a "weather forecast" always given as a **probability or a range** ("70% chance of rain") rather than a flat certainty? Because:

  • Ameteorologists are lazy
  • Bthe law forbids certainty
  • Cthe atmosphere is so complex and ever-changing that even good data leaves some uncertainty✓
  • Dinstruments never work

Working

✓

Forecasts are given as probabilities because **the atmosphere is so complex and ever-changing that even good data leaves some uncertainty** — not because forecasters are careless.

Q45

The further **ahead** a forecast tries to look (one day vs ten days), the:

  • Amore accurate it becomes
  • Blonger the thermometer must be
  • Ccheaper it is to make
  • Dless reliable it becomes, because small errors grow over time✓

Working

✓

A ten-day forecast is **less reliable than a one-day forecast because small errors grow over time** — the further ahead you look, the fuzzier it gets.

Q46

Weather **satellites** add to ground stations because from space they can:

  • Aphotograph whole cloud systems and track storms moving across regions✓
  • Bmeasure the depth of rain in a single bucket
  • Cfeel the wind directly
  • Dread the barometer on the ground

Working

✓

Satellites add to ground stations by being able to **photograph whole cloud systems and track storms across regions** from above — a view no single ground gauge can give.

Q47

A farmer hears a reliable forecast of heavy rain next week. The wisest use of that warning is to:

  • Aignore it and plant as usual
  • Badjust sowing, harvesting or drainage so the crop is protected and the rain is used well✓
  • Csell the farm
  • Dwater the fields more right now

Working

✓

A farmer should use a heavy-rain forecast to **adjust sowing, harvesting or drainage** so the crop is protected and the water used well — turning the warning into a plan.

Q48

Humidity feels "sticky" on a hot, muggy day because high humidity:

  • Amakes the air heavier to lift
  • Braises the air pressure sharply
  • Cslows the evaporation of sweat from our skin, so we cannot cool down easily✓
  • Dcarries dust

Working

✓

Muggy air feels sticky because high humidity **slows the evaporation of sweat from the skin**, so the body cannot shed heat and we feel hotter.

Q49

In a hot, **dry** desert, sweat evaporates fast, so a person may feel cooler than the thermometer suggests; in a hot, **humid** coast the same temperature feels hotter. The deciding factor is:

  • Athe wind direction only
  • Bthe time of day only
  • Cthe altitude
  • Dthe humidity, because it controls how fast sweat evaporates✓

Working

✓

The same temperature feels cooler in a dry desert and hotter on a humid coast because **humidity decides how fast sweat evaporates** — fast evaporation cools you, slow evaporation does not.

Q50

Which everyday observation best shows that **air has weight / exerts pressure**?

  • Aa straw lets you drink because you lower the pressure inside and the outside air pushes the liquid up✓
  • Ba balloon bursting in the Sun
  • Csmoke rising from a fire
  • Da kite needing string

Working

✓

A drinking **straw** shows air pressure at work: you lower the pressure inside, and the *outside* air pressing on the drink pushes it up the straw — proof that air has weight and pushes.

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

Sea breeze blows from sea to land by **day**; land breeze from land to sea by **night**. The day-time sea breeze happens because:

  • Athe sea heats faster than the land
  • Bthe land heats faster than the sea, so air rises over the warm land and cooler sea air flows in to replace it✓
  • Cthe Sun pulls the sea
  • Dnight air is heavier

Working

✓

A daytime sea breeze blows inland because **the land heats faster than the sea; the air over the warm land rises and cooler sea air flows in to replace it**. At night the land cools faster and the flow reverses (land breeze).

Q52

Put these in the correct chain for a sea breeze: (i) cooler air over the sea moves in to replace it; (ii) land heats up and the air above it rises; (iii) the Sun warms the coast in the morning.

  • A(ii) → (iii) → (i)
  • B(i) → (ii) → (iii)
  • C(iii) → (ii) → (i)✓
  • D(iii) → (i) → (ii)

Working

✓

The sea-breeze chain runs **(iii) the Sun warms the coast → (ii) the land heats and its air rises → (i) cooler sea air moves in to replace it.** That replacing flow is the breeze.

Q53

A "drought" is essentially a long shortfall of:

  • Asunshine
  • Bpressure
  • Cwind
  • Drainfall over a season or more, leaving little water for crops and people✓

Working

✓

A drought is essentially a long shortfall of **rainfall over a season or more**, leaving too little water for crops and people.

Q54

Why can a single very heavy downpour **still leave a region in drought**?

  • Aone burst may run off quickly without soaking in or refilling reservoirs, so the long-term shortfall remains✓
  • Bheavy rain always ends a drought
  • Cdrought means too much rain
  • Dthe rain gauge missed it

Working

✓

One cloudburst can leave a drought because **the water may run off fast without soaking in or refilling reservoirs**, so the long-term shortfall remains. Droughts are about sustained totals, not a single storm.

Q55

The water that evaporates from oceans, rivers and plants, rises, cools, condenses into clouds and falls again as precipitation describes the:

  • Arock cycle
  • Bwater (hydrological) cycle✓
  • Ccarbon cycle
  • Dday–night cycle

Working

✓

Water evaporating, rising, cooling, condensing into clouds and falling again is the **water (hydrological) cycle** — the engine behind all precipitation.

Q56

Condensation — the step that *makes* clouds and dew — is water changing from:

  • Aliquid to gas
  • Bsolid to gas
  • Cgas (vapour) to liquid✓
  • Dliquid to solid

Working

✓

Condensation, which makes clouds and dew, is water changing from **gas (vapour) to liquid**. Evaporation is the reverse.

Q57

A balloon released from the ground rises and **expands** as it climbs because the air around it:

  • Agets warmer
  • Bpulls it up magnetically
  • Cgets thicker
  • Dpresses on it less, since pressure falls with height✓

Working

✓

A balloon expands as it rises because **the surrounding air presses on it less — pressure falls with height** — so the gas inside pushes out further.

Q58

Standardised weather instruments are kept in a white, louvred **Stevenson screen** so that they:

  • Ameasure the true air temperature in shade with free airflow, not direct sunshine or rain✓
  • Blook neat in the garden
  • Cstay hidden from people
  • Dkeep warm at night

Working

✓

Instruments sit in a white, louvred Stevenson screen so they **read the true air temperature in shade with free airflow**, shielded from direct sun and rain that would give false highs.

Q59

Two towns report the same 30 °C, but one reports RH 30% and the other RH 85%. A visitor will most likely feel **more uncomfortable** in the town with:

  • ARH 30%, because dry air burns
  • BRH 85%, because the muggy air stops sweat from evaporating✓
  • Cneither — temperature alone decides comfort
  • Dthe one with more wind only

Working

✓

At the same 30 °C the **RH 85%** town feels worse, because the muggy air stops sweat from evaporating and cooling the body; the RH 30% town's dry air lets sweat work.

Q60

The deepest reason weather forecasting has improved so much is the combination of:

  • Amore folklore and proverbs
  • Btaller thermometers
  • Cdense networks of stations and AWS, satellites, and computers that model the atmosphere from all that data✓
  • Dwarmer winters

Working

✓

Modern forecasting has leapt forward thanks to **dense networks of stations and AWS, satellites, and computers that model the atmosphere** from all that data — folklore alone could never reach today's accuracy.
