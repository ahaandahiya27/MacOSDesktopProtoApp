# Solutions — Social Science (Class 7) · Paper 2 (Advanced)
## Chapter 6: The Age of Reorganisation

Each answer is keyed, then worked. The recurring move is to reason from cause to
effect across the dynasties that filled the space left by the Mauryas, and to
read the cultural blending of the age from its evidence — coins, inscriptions,
sculpture and poetry. Two traps recur: the Maurya empire broke up from *within*
(invaders came after, not before), and the Gandhara (Greco-Roman, grey schist,
northwest) and Mathura (indigenous, red sandstone, Gangetic plain) schools must
never be swapped.

---

**1. (C)** The age is named for fragmentation: the political map broke into many competing kingdoms and was redrawn again and again. A single unifying empire or wholesale foreign rule is the opposite of what happened.

**2. (A)** The deepest cause was internal strain — governing a vast territory from one centre, with the heavy cost of a huge army and bureaucracy, stretched Mauryan resources. The empire had a capital, taxes and the Brahmi script, so those "explanations" are false.

**3. (D)** Pushyamitra had been the Mauryas' commander-in-chief before founding the Shunga dynasty around 185 BCE — power passing from the army's head, not from a poet, a tax collector or a chaplain.

**4. (B)** The Heliodorus pillar records an Indo-Greek ambassador who had taken up the worship of Vasudeva (Vishnu) — vivid proof that foreigners adopted Indian religion. It is not a stupa, a copy of Ashoka's capital or a Roman record.

**5. (B)** The Satavahanas rose in the Deccan, in the country between the Godavari and Krishna rivers — not the Gangetic plain, the far northwest or the Tamil south.

**6. (D)** Writing inscriptions in Prakrit means the Satavahanas used the everyday spoken language for public records. It does not mean Sanskrit was uninvented or forbidden; it was a choice to reach ordinary people.

**7. (A)** Gautamiputra Satakarni is the Satavahana king remembered for rolling back Shaka power and styled lord of the Deccan. Pushyamitra, Menander and Kharavela belonged to other lines.

**8. (C)** A famous inscription about his reign was composed by his mother, the queen Gautami Balashri. Megasthenes, Kauṭilya and Faxian are from other times and places.

**9. (A)** A queen composing a royal inscription shows that royal women of this age could hold real prestige and a public political voice — not that they were invisible, ruled only as a last resort, or were barred from ritual.

**10. (C)** Menander, known in Indian tradition as Milinda, is the best-remembered Indo-Greek king, partly through his recorded dialogue with the monk Nagasena. Seleucus, Alexander and Kanishka are different rulers.

**11. (D)** That dialogue survives as the *Milinda-pañha*, the "Questions of Milinda." The *Arthaśhāstra*, the *Periplus* and the *Indica* are unrelated texts.

**12. (B)** Indo-Greek coins are prized because they carry realistic portraits of named kings, which lets historians fix who ruled and when. They were real currency, not iron, and they did bear writing.

**13. (D)** The Shakas (Scythians) were originally Central Asian steppe herders pushed south and west — not people of the Tamil coast, the Gangetic delta or Sri Lanka.

**14. (B)** Rudradaman's Junagadh rock inscription is celebrated as one of the earliest long royal inscriptions written in *Sanskrit* — not English or Greek, and not a sea-voyage log.

**15. (C)** The same inscription records that Rudradaman repaired the dam of the Sudarshana lake, first built in Mauryan times — not the Great Wall, a road or a stupa.

**16. (A)** Kanishka was the greatest Kushana ruler and a noted patron of Buddhism. Pushyamitra, Rudradaman and Gautamiputra ruled other dynasties.

**17. (C)** The Kushana empire was unusual because it straddled both Central Asia and northern India, linking the two — not confined to a Tamil port, the Deccan or the islands.

**18. (A)** Kanishka's chief city Purushapura is modern Peshawar — not Patna, Mathura or Ujjain.

**19. (B)** The Kushanas are famous for fine *gold* coinage — not iron, clay or wood.

**20. (D)** Kanishka is traditionally linked with convening a major Buddhist council, which helped spread Mahayana Buddhism — not a horse-sacrifice, a sea expedition or a Satavahana coronation.

**21. (A)** The Gandhara school shows strong Greco-Roman influence — wavy hair, flowing robes, life-like bodies. The red-sandstone indigenous style is Mathura's, and Gandhara certainly did depict the Buddha.

**22. (D)** The Mathura school is an indigenous Indian style working chiefly in red sandstone — not Greek schist, Chinese jade or Roman marble.

**23. (B)** Both schools mark a turning point as among the first to make images of the Buddha in human form. They did not ban imagery, invent Brahmi or mint coins.

**24. (C)** Grey schist points to workshops in the northwest, near the Greco-Iranian world — not the Tamil south, Sri Lanka or the Gangetic delta.

**25. (B)** Controlling the Silk Route let the Kushanas tax and profit from the long-distance trade between China and the West — the opposite of stopping trade or shunning money.

**26. (C)** The most famous commodity carried west from China was silk — which gives the route its name; not tea, iron ore or salt in this context.

**27. (A)** Buddhism spread into Central Asia and China largely along the trade routes, carried by merchants and monks — not by sea to the Americas, only in the Tamil country, or by Roman armies.

**28. (D)** The Tamil region (Tamilakam) was shared chiefly among the Cheras, Cholas and Pandyas — not the northern dynasties or the later Vakatakas/Pallavas.

**29. (D)** The body of early Tamil poetry illuminating the south is the Sangam literature — distinct from the Arthaśhāstra, the Vedas or the Pali canon.

**30. (A)** "Sangam" originally meant an assembly or academy of poets — not a coin, an elephant or a tax.

**31. (C)** Roman gold coins at sites like Arikamedu show a brisk sea trade between south India and the Roman world. They do not mean Rome ruled the south or that Tamils minted Roman coins.

**32. (B)** The Greek-authored handbook listing the Indian Ocean ports and goods is the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* — not the *Milinda-pañha*, the Junagadh inscription or the *Indica*.

**33. (C)** The most prized south Indian exports to Rome were spices such as pepper, plus fine cloth and gems — not Deccan silk, Roman wine or iron tools.

**34. (B)** Correct northern order: the Shungas first, then the Shakas, then the Kushanas — that is sequence (ii) → (iii) → (i).

**35. (D)** "Foreign rulers were absorbed" is best supported by their adopting Indian languages, religions and titles — not by destroying temples, banning trade or staying away.

**36. (A)** A matrimonial alliance is also an instrument of diplomacy linking two ruling houses — not a sacrifice, a tribute scheme or a parade.

**37. (A)** The ashvamedha's political point is to proclaim and test a ruler's standing as a supreme overlord — not to choose a successor by lot, settle a marriage or start a trade route.

**38. (B)** The Mauryan collapse plus many new powers shows that a large empire's fall often opens space for new regional kingdoms — it does not end political life, strengthen the same dynasty or erase the region.

**39. (D)** Repeated invasion alongside cultural growth supports the idea that contact with outsiders can enrich rather than erase a culture — not that invasion always destroys, that cultures never change, or that ideas never crossed borders.

**40. (C)** The Indo-Greeks were the first in India known to issue coins bearing the king's portrait and name. The wheel, cities and rock-writing all long predate them.

**41. (D)** Controlling Deccan ports on both coasts let the Satavahanas profit from trade flowing between north, south and overseas — not cut themselves off, abandon currency or stop farming.

**42. (C)** A dynasty is a succession of rulers from the same family — not a single king, a coin or a guild.

**43. (A)** The move toward Sanskrit in royal inscriptions marks its rising prestige as a language of power and learning — it did not end spoken languages, ban Prakrit or bring English.

**44. (B)** Coins are valuable evidence because their portraits, names, metals and find-spots reveal rulers, dates, wealth and trade links — they were inscribed, used as money, and can survive for millennia.

**45. (B)** The stupa most expanded in this period, later famous for its carved gateways, is at Sanchi — not Peshawar, Arikamedu or Junagadh.

**46. (D)** The Kushanas borrowed grand titles like "king of kings" partly from Central Asian and Iranian traditions of kingship — not from the Roman Senate, the Tamil Sangam or a village council.

**47. (C)** "Greco-Buddhist art" applies most directly to the Gandhara school — not Mathura sculpture, Sangam poetry or Satavahana coins.

**48. (A)** A red-sandstone Buddha in a rounded indigenous style from the north Indian plains belongs to the Mathura school — not Gandhara, Roman or Chinese workshops.

**49. (A)** The age's lesson is that political division can still go hand in hand with flourishing trade, art and exchange — not that it always means decline, isolation or a block on new ideas.

**50. (D)** Kushana gold coinage was fed partly by the long-distance trade they taxed, including inflowing Roman gold — not Sri Lankan mines, Chinese tribute alone or melted iron.

**51. (C)** Bactria, the Indo-Greeks' base, lay in Central Asia around modern northern Afghanistan — not the Tamil coast, the Gangetic delta or Sri Lanka.

**52. (B)** The Sudarshana lake's repair across centuries shows that public works could outlast the dynasty that built them, maintained by later rulers — not that nothing lasting was built or that lakes were never used for irrigation.

**53. (C)** Rulers advertised on coins and in inscriptions to legitimise their power and be remembered, since competition was fierce — not because one empire required it, to teach reading, or for no reason.

**54. (D)** The Sangam poems are valued because they describe everyday life, chiefs, trade and values of the early Tamil south — not Kushana king-lists, Greek text or only the Silk Route.

**55. (B)** The "foreign" dynasties often patronised Indian religions, including Buddhism, rather than suppressing them — they did not destroy Buddhism, impose Greek gods or ban religion.

**56. (A)** Competition was expressed through warfare, matrimonial alliances and grand rituals like the ashvamedha — so the option that is NOT a means of competition, and thus the correct "except," is abolishing the very idea of kingship.

**57. (B)** A hoard mixing Roman, Kushana and local coins is best read as evidence of a connected, trading world spanning India and far beyond — not isolation, no economy or coins as toys.

**58. (A)** By region: the Satavahanas in the Deccan, the Kushanas in the northwest and Central Asia, and the Cholas in the Tamil south — the only pairing that matches.

**59. (C)** The Age of Reorganisation gave way in the 4th century CE to the rise of the Gupta empire — not the earlier Mauryas or the much later Sultanate or Mughals.

**60. (D)** The best summary: many competing kingdoms whose rivalry coincided with vibrant trade and a great blending of cultures — not one unbroken empire, total collapse, or culture-erasing foreign rule.
