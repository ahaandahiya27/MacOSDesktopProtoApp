Worked Solutions

1. (A) Seeing the flame only proves light reaches your eye; it is the geometric sharpness of the shadow's edge that shows the rays move in straight paths and are cleanly cut off by the body. Light cannot pass through an opaque candle, it is not bent around its edges to soften them, and no colour change occurs.

2. (A) The law of reflection is obeyed point-by-point on any surface; velvet looks dull because its tiny facets face random directions, scattering rays, but each ray still reflects symmetrically. So it cannot be true for one surface only, and the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, never double it.

3. (A) A rough surface sends neighbouring rays off at differing angles, destroying the orderly bundle needed for an image; this scattering is diffuse reflection. Regular reflection is exactly what forms images, the glass is not absorbing all the light, and no colour-splitting is happening.

4. (D) The angle from the normal is 90 minus 25, that is 65 degrees, for both the incident and reflected rays; the two rays together span 65 plus 65, which is 130 degrees. Taking 50 doubles the surface angle wrongly, 25 is only the grazing angle, and 65 is just one of the two equal halves.

5. (A) A far source sends near-parallel rays, so the cone of shadow narrows and the shadow shrinks toward the coin's true size; a near source spreads the shadow wide. A distant source still casts a shadow, and shadows are colourless, so they do not split into a spectrum.

6. (D) Because light travels straight, a ray can thread all three holes to your eye only if they fall on a common straight line with the source. Equal hole sizes, a curved arrangement, or matching colours do not by themselves let the straight ray pass.

7. (D) The card's image sits as far behind the mirror as the card is in front, namely 60 cm, so card-to-image is 60 plus 60, which is 120 cm. The 160 figure uses your 80 cm distance, 60 forgets the equal image distance, and 100 mixes the two object distances.

8. (D) Both she and her image approach the mirror at 1.5 metres per second, so they close on each other at the sum, 3 metres per second. Quoting 1.5 counts only her motion, 0.75 halves it, and the gap certainly changes since both are moving.

9. (B) Distance from a plane mirror never changes image size; it only changes how big the image looks to the eye, not its true size, which stays equal to the object. So it neither shrinks to a third, nor grows threefold, nor reaches any summed distance value.

10. (D) The two images lie 3 m and 5 m behind the mirror, so they are 5 minus 3, that is 2 m, apart. Adding gives 8, doubling each and adding gives 16, and 10 doubles the friend's distance alone.

11. (B) A flat mirror obeys equal angles point by point, so a correctly tilted mirror routes light from one student's face to the other's eye. It does not retro-reflect straight back, it does not favour a student by height, and a plane mirror does not disperse light.

12. (A) A mirror clock is read by taking 12:00 minus the apparent time: 12:00 minus 8:10 gives 3:50. Reading it directly as 8:10 ignores the reversal, and 4:50 and 2:10 come from subtracting from the wrong reference.

13. (D) The digits 1 and 0 are each left-right symmetric and the string 101 reads the same backwards, so the mirror leaves it looking identical. A mirror flips left-right not the digit shapes' symmetry, does not swap 1 and 0, and does not turn things upside down.

14. (C) W and O are each vertically symmetric and WOW is a palindrome, so the left-right mirror flip leaves it identical. A vertical mirror does not invert a W into an M (that is a top-bottom flip), does not scramble the symmetric letters, and does not rotate the word.

15. (B) Because the rear-view mirror laterally inverts, the engine carries the pre-inverted (backwards) form so the reflection comes out readable. Normal lettering would appear backwards in the mirror, an upside-down trick addresses the wrong axis, and colour has nothing to do with readability.

16. (C) A and T are symmetric about a vertical centre line, so they survive the left-right flip; F and J are not vertically symmetric and appear reversed. Hence not all four, not the asymmetric F and J, and not none.

17. (C) The student inside already sees a left-right reversed message; a plane mirror reverses left-right once more, restoring the original readable text. It does not reverse it twice in the same sense, it does not flip top-bottom, and the mirror does change it rather than leaving it reversed.

18. (C) A vertical mirror swaps left and right but not up and down, so a hand pointing right (3) reflects to point left (9), while a vertical hand stays vertical. It does not stay at 3, does not flip to 6 (that needs a horizontal mirror), and the up-pointing minute hand, not the hour hand, stays at 12.

19. (A) A plane-mirror image is virtual: reflected rays merely seem to come from behind the mirror, where no real light converges, so nothing can be captured. The image is not real, card thickness is irrelevant, and a plane mirror image is erect, not self-cancelling.

20. (B) A convex lens gives a real inverted image of a far object (catchable) and a virtual erect enlarged image of a very near one (not catchable). So it is not real in both cases, the pairing is not swapped, and a convex lens certainly can make a real image.

21. (D) Only a real image, such as the convex lens's image of a far object, can land on a screen. The magnifier's close-up image, the plane-mirror face, and the convex-mirror view are all virtual and so cannot be projected.

22. (B) A concave mirror inverts and reduces a far object into a real image, but a very near object gives a virtual, erect, magnified image. So the two are not both of one kind, and the pairing must not be reversed.

23. (A) A concave mirror enlarges only a close object; a far object gives a diminished, inverted real image, so the 'always bigger' claim fails. It is neither true for all positions nor brightness-dependent, and it does not always shrink either.

24. (C) Concentrating sunlight to a hot spot needs converging, which a concave mirror does by bringing parallel rays to a focus. A convex mirror diverges, a plane mirror neither focuses nor concentrates, and a concave lens diverges rather than focuses (and is a lens, not a mirror).

25. (A) A concave mirror gives a real, inverted image for distant objects and switches to a virtual, erect, enlarged image once the object is very near. The sequence is therefore not reversed, and it does not stay one single type throughout.

26. (B) Only a concave mirror, with the tooth very close to it, produces an enlarged, erect, virtual image, which is what a dentist wants. A convex mirror only diminishes, a plane mirror keeps true size, and a concave mirror inverts only for far objects.

27. (C) A convex mirror always gives a smaller, erect, virtual image; its value is the wide field of view, not magnification. So the claim is not correct, not lighting-dependent, and the image is erect, not inverted.

28. (C) A convex mirror diverges reflected rays to take in a wide scene and always keeps the image erect. A concave mirror narrows the view and can invert, a plane mirror's view is not wider than a convex one of the same size, and a lens is not a mirror.

29. (A) A lens thicker in the middle is convex; held close, it gives an enlarged, erect, virtual image, like a magnifying glass. A concave lens always diminishes, and the two mirror options are mirrors, not lenses.

30. (B) A distant object through a convex lens forms a real, inverted image that can fall on a wall. A virtual image cannot be projected, and the projected image of a far object through a single convex lens is inverted, not erect.

31. (C) A concave lens spreads parallel rays outward, so they never concentrate to a burning focus. It transmits rather than reflects the light, does not absorb it all, and a flat-faced lens does not disperse sunlight into a rainbow.

32. (D) A concave lens always forms a virtual, erect, diminished image, so the print looks smaller and upright. It does not enlarge, does not invert close print, and changes the size rather than leaving it unchanged.

33. (C) Of the three mirrors only a concave one can converge reflected light to a real image; plane and convex mirrors give virtual images for any object. So not all three, not the convex, and the claim that none can is also false.

34. (D) The hollow inner bowl curves inward like a concave mirror and can invert; the bulging back curves outward like a convex mirror and always gives an erect, diminished image. The pairing is not reversed, neither surface is a lens, and a curved spoon is not flat.

35. (C) Close-up magnification needs a concave mirror; a wide field of view behind a vehicle needs a convex mirror. The two roles are not swapped, a plane mirror neither magnifies nor widens, and lenses are not mirrors.

36. (C) A lens thinner in the middle is concave and always gives a diminished, erect, virtual image. A convex lens (thicker middle) would enlarge, a mirror is not a lens, and a concave lens never enlarges.

37. (C) A concave mirror with the source at its focus sends out a tight, near-parallel beam, which is what a torch needs. A convex mirror would spread the light, a plane mirror would not concentrate it, and a lens is not a reflector.

38. (B) A convex mirror always gives an erect but diminished image, matching the plane mirror's erectness while shrinking the object at every position. A near-object concave mirror enlarges, a distant-object concave mirror inverts (not erect), and a concave lens is a lens, not a mirror as the question demands.

39. (C) A concave mirror and a convex lens both bring parallel rays toward a point, so they converge; the convex mirror and concave lens both spread rays, so they diverge. The other pairings each mix one converging with one diverging device.

40. (D) A plane mirror gives virtual same-size, a convex mirror gives virtual diminished, and a concave mirror gives real inverted, but no chapter mirror gives a real image that is also erect. So the impossible one is the real-and-erect-enlarged case.

41. (C) A convex lens magnifies near print (virtual erect) and projects a far object as a real inverted image, and it is a lens. A concave lens only diminishes, while the two mirror options are mirrors, not lenses.

42. (D) A convex lens is thick in the middle, a concave lens thin in the middle, a concave mirror dips inward, and a convex mirror bulges outward, so the first pairing is right throughout. The others swap lens thicknesses or mirror curvatures, or wrongly call curved devices flat.

43. (A) A plane mirror image is virtual and same-size, a convex mirror image is virtual and diminished, and a concave lens image is virtual and diminished, so that row is fully correct. The others wrongly call a plane image real, a convex image magnified, a distant concave image virtual, or a convex lens always-virtual.

44. (B) A focusing camera lens, a magnifying glass and a concave reflector are all converging devices, so none stands apart. The magnifier does not diverge, the camera lens does not spread light, and a concave reflector gathers rather than scatters.

45. (D) A prism's non-parallel faces keep the separated colours apart, while a slab's parallel faces let them rejoin so no band shows. The prism does not add colour, is ordinary glass, and is not curved like a lens; the colours come from the white light itself.

46. (A) Red bends least and violet bends most, so least-to-most runs red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. The reverse lists most-to-least, three colours is incomplete, and the scrambled list is out of order.

47. (A) Through a prism violet is deviated most and red least, the opposite of the student's claim. The colours do not bend equally, the red-most ordering is wrong, and the bending order does not depend on brightness.

48. (B) A prism can only separate colours that are already present; a single colour comes out as that colour, merely deviated. It cannot create a spectrum from one colour, cannot turn one colour into white, and does not block the light.

49. (B) The prism separates and the disc recombines, proving white light is the seven colours mixed together. The prism does not create colour, white is not a single pure colour, and the disc blends rather than destroys the colours.

50. (D) Each droplet refracts and disperses sunlight like a small prism, producing the rainbow's colours. A mirror would not split colours, a lens would magnify not disperse, and a filter would remove colours rather than spread them.

51. (A) Rainbow colours are simply the components of white sunlight pulled apart by raindrops; nothing new is created. So the droplets neither manufacture colours nor make a subset of them, and the colours originate in the sunlight, not the sky.

52. (B) With parallel faces, the slight separation entering the slab is reversed leaving it, so the colours overlap back into white. The glass does bend the light, does not absorb colours, and does not reflect it all away.

53. (D) A still lake reflects, a prism disperses, and a convex lens projecting an inverted picture forms a real image, matching the order. The other rows mislabel one or more demonstrations or assign the wrong device.

54. (B) Fast spinning makes the colours reach the eye too quickly to be seen apart, so they blend to white; stationary, each colour is seen on its own. The colours do not vanish in motion, no white paint is created, and the still disc shows colours, not white.

55. (A) A straight beam in a single medium shows light moves in straight lines; the slab only shifts it, without bending the overall path. Light does not droop, does not fan into a cone in a uniform medium, and does not self-disperse.

56. (A) Shaving mirrors, solar cookers and headlight reflectors all use concave mirrors, for magnification or for converging and beaming light. They are not convex or plane, and they are not a mix of types.

57. (C) A convex mirror spreads reflected rays, always giving a virtual, erect, diminished image and a wide view, ideal for rear-view use. It does not converge or form real images, and it never magnifies.

58. (B) Converging devices are the concave mirror and convex lens; diverging ones are the convex mirror and concave lens, so the first statement is right. Grouping by name alone (both concave, both convex) is wrong, and not all four converge.

59. (B) A distant object gives a real inverted image in both a concave mirror and a convex lens, while a convex mirror always gives a virtual diminished image, so that row holds throughout. The others wrongly make plane images real, convex-lens images always virtual, or convex-mirror images real.

60. (D) A broad source means the ball blocks all rays only over a small core, while around it some parts of the lamp peek past, giving partial light, the grey rim. Light is not bent around the opaque ball, the wall does not tint it, and rays do not slow and spread in air to make the fuzziness.
