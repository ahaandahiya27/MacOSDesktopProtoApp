## Worked Solutions

**1. (A)** A government is the set of institutions that make rules, carry them out and ensure they are followed. It is not one leader alone, the army, or the whole electorate.

**2. (B)** Government's core is the legislative, executive and judicial functions - making, enforcing and upholding rules. It does not run all businesses, dictate religion, or write art.

**3. (B)** Every country has some government, yet they differ in how power is held and used. Not only democracies count, they are not identical, and a country needs some government to run.

**4. (B)** Making rules is the legislative function. Carrying them out is executive, ensuring they are followed is judicial, and 'ceremonial' is not one of the three functions.

**5. (C)** Putting laws into action is the executive function. Making laws is legislative, judging disputes is judicial, and 'electoral' is not one of the functions here.

**6. (A)** Ensuring laws are obeyed and settling disputes is the judicial function, the work of the courts. The others make or carry out laws.

**7. (C)** A court judging whether something follows the law is the judicial function. Lawmaking is legislative and enforcing is executive.

**8. (D)** Governments differ by who holds power, how they form, their parts and their aims - not merely size, army, or age.

**9. (B)** The key difference is who holds power and how rulers are chosen. Population, language and land do not by themselves decide the type of government.

**10. (C)** Democracy means 'rule of the people' (from Greek demos + kratos). One-king rule is monarchy, priestly rule is theocracy, and army rule is not democracy.

**11. (B)** In a democracy the people are the source of power and choose their representatives. A king, a religious head, or rich families holding power describe other forms.

**12. (B)** 'Rule of the people' means leaders are chosen by and accountable to the people, not life-appointed, army-selected, or born to rule.

**13. (C)** The committee story illustrates representation: students choose a few to act on behalf of all. It is not about sports, taxes, or royal inheritance.

**14. (B)** A representative is chosen to act for others. Birth-rule is monarchy, wealth-rule is oligarchy, and a dispute-settler is a judge.

**15. (A)** Electing a few to act for all mirrors representative democracy. The other forms concentrate power in a king, religious leaders, or a single ruler.

**16. (B)** Equality is a core democratic principle. Family-inherited power, religious rule and decisions by a wealthy few describe monarchy, theocracy and oligarchy.

**17. (D)** Universal adult franchise grants every citizen above a set age the right to vote. Limiting the vote to landowners, men, or the government denies this principle.

**18. (C)** Suffrage (Latin suffragium) is the right to vote. Tax-paying, lawmaking and judging are different things.

**19. (D)** An independent judiciary judges fairly, even against the government, upholding the law. A judiciary that always favours the government, is party-controlled, or merely advises a king is not independent.

**20. (C)** Fundamental rights guard citizens' freedoms, which even the government and the majority cannot simply take away. They are not a blank cheque for the majority, rulers-only, or election-only.

**21. (C)** Accountability means the government must answer to the people who elected it. It is not freedom from questions, foreign answerability, or court-ignoring.

**22. (A)** In a direct democracy citizens decide issues themselves. Not voting, obeying a king, or letting a few decide describe non-democratic or representative forms.

**23. (D)** A referendum (Latin 'a thing to be referred') is a direct citizen vote on one question. Electing representatives, judges deciding, or a king's law are different.

**24. (C)** In a representative democracy citizens elect representatives to decide for them. Voting on every law is direct democracy, no vote is undemocratic, and inherited seats are not democratic.

**25. (A)** With millions of people, deciding every issue by direct vote is impractical, so representatives are elected. Citizens do have opinions, direct democracy is not illegal, and representatives are elected, not kings.

**26. (D)** India is a parliamentary democracy, where real executive power lies with the PM and council of ministers. It is not presidential, an absolute monarchy, or a theocracy.

**27. (B)** Despite having a President, India is parliamentary: the PM and ministers, drawn from Parliament, hold real executive power and need the Lok Sabha's confidence. India does have a President (a largely nominal head), is not a monarchy, and the President is not directly elected by all voters.

**28. (C)** The USA is a presidential democracy, where the president is elected and works independently of the legislature. India is the parliamentary example; the UK a constitutional monarchy.

**29. (C)** In a parliamentary system the head of government comes from and answers to the legislature, unlike a presidential system where the president is separately elected and independent. Both hold elections, and neither is ruled by a law-making king.

**30. (B)** Real executive power in India rests with the PM and the council of ministers. The President is a largely nominal head, the Chief Justice heads the judiciary, and there is no ruling royal family.

**31. (B)** The US president is elected and operates independently of the legislature. Being chosen by the legislature describes a parliamentary head; a monarch inherits; and judges do not appoint the president.

**32. (A)** The three organs are the legislature (makes laws), executive (carries them out) and judiciary (upholds them). The other lists are not the three organs.

**33. (D)** Separation of powers keeps the legislature, executive and judiciary independent so none dominates the others. It is not one-person control, total silence, or abolition.

**34. (B)** By keeping the organs independent, separation of powers prevents any one from seizing total control - a check against abuse. It does not let one organ control another or remove laws.

**35. (B)** One ruler controlling all three organs means no separation of powers, a hallmark of dictatorship. A flag, population size and geography are unrelated.

**36. (D)** A monarchy is ruled by a king or queen, usually by inheritance. Elected representatives, religious leaders and wealthy-family rule describe democracy, theocracy and oligarchy.

**37. (A)** In an absolute monarchy the king really rules. Nominal, ceremonial power describes a constitutional monarch; absolute monarchs are not elected or court-chosen.

**38. (C)** In a constitutional monarchy the monarch's power is largely nominal while an elected parliament governs. The monarch does not hold all power, is not elected, and does exist.

**39. (D)** A constitutional monarchy (UK) is a parliamentary democracy where the monarch's power is nominal, so a king and democracy can coexist. An absolute monarchy is not a democracy, and the UK does have a monarch.

**40. (C)** The UK is both a democracy and a monarchy - a constitutional monarchy. It is not an absolute monarchy, an election-free state, or government-less.

**41. (B)** A theocracy is rule by religious law and leaders. An elected parliament is democracy, a nominal king a constitutional monarchy, and wealthy-family rule oligarchy.

**42. (A)** In a dictatorship one person or a small group wields absolute, unchecked power. Shared elected power, direct voting and a ceremonial monarch describe other forms.

**43. (A)** Kratos ('power, rule') gives both theo-cracy and demo-cracy. Mon-archy, olig-archy and an-archy come from the other root, arkhe ('rule').

**44. (C)** A dictatorship concentrates unchecked power with no real accountability - the opposite of democracy's elections, rights and independent courts.

**45. (A)** An oligarchy is rule by a small powerful group, often the wealthy. Universal voting is democracy, inherited single rule is monarchy, and sacred-law rule is theocracy.

**46. (A)** Oligarchy is from oligos ('few') + arkho ('to rule'). 'People + power' gives democracy, 'one + rule' monarchy, and 'god + power' theocracy.

**47. (D)** Democracies can slide toward oligarchy when a small powerful group seizes too much control. Wide voting, independent courts and protected rights strengthen democracy, not weaken it.

**48. (C)** Choosing rulers is an ancient idea, seen in early India, Greece and Rome. It is not new, India-absent, or impossible in the past.

**49. (A)** The Lichchhavis were part of the Vajji mahajanapada, known for its republican assembly. Magadha, the Cholas and the Kushanas are different powers.

**50. (D)** The Uttaramerur inscriptions record a Chola village election system. They are not Gupta, Mauryan, or Indo-Greek.

**51. (A)** The Uttaramerur inscriptions prove India had organised local elections long ago, so elections were not purely modern, kings-only, or Greek-only.

**52. (D)** Early republics in India, Greece and Rome show these ideas arose in several ancient civilisations - not one only, not in the 20th century, and not absent from the ancient world.

**53. (B)** In a republic the head of state is elected rather than a hereditary monarch. Inheritance, religious selection and foreign appointment describe other arrangements.

**54. (C)** Democracy concerns who rules (the people); a republic concerns the head of state being elected rather than royal. They overlap but differ, so they are not identical, vote-banning, or king-requiring.

**55. (D)** India is a democratic republic: the people rule and the head of state is elected, not a king. It is not a monarchy, a theocracy, or an absolute monarchy.

**56. (D)** The UK is a democracy that keeps a monarch (nominal head) - a constitutional monarchy. It has not banned elections, is not religious rule, and does have a parliament.

**57. (A)** Elections alone are not enough; real democracy also needs equality, free speech, rights and an independent judiciary. An army, a king, or a lone vote do not make a democracy.

**58. (D)** Democracy is more than majority rule: it protects minorities and individuals, follows a constitution, and limits power through separation of powers. The majority cannot simply vote away fundamental rights or seize the courts.

**59. (D)** The chapter notes democracy is the world's most popular form of government today, despite its flaws. It is not rare, unused, or the same as dictatorship.

**60. (A)** Real power held by one small wealthy group, despite votes, is an oligarchy dressed as a democracy - exactly the drift the chapter warns about. It is not a healthy democracy, a constitutional monarchy, or a direct democracy.
