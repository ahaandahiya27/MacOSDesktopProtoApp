## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** The timeline places the internal weakening and the assassination before the Indo-Greek entry, and a cause must come before its effect, so a later arrival cannot have produced the earlier collapse. The commander (Pushyamitra Shunga) was an insider of the Mauryan army, not an Indo-Greek, so that is not the flaw. The Indo-Greeks and Mauryas were different peoples, so equating them is simply wrong. The northwest had in fact belonged to the Mauryas, so denying it misstates the chapter. The only flaw in the reasoning itself is confusing later-in-sequence with cause.

**2. (D)** 'Reorganisation' is justified because political reshaping went hand in hand with thriving art, trade and literature, not cultural loss. A single reunifying emperor never appeared, so that claim is false and would not support either label. Foreign rulers held only parts of the north, not every kingdom. Far from leaving no evidence, the age is known precisely through its coins, inscriptions and monuments, so that statement reverses the truth and would wrongly support 'decline'.

**3. (B)** The defining mark against a 'dark age' is the simultaneous flourishing of art, trade and literature. No single empire kept the subcontinent peaceful, so that is false. Writing did not disappear; the age is reconstructed from its written inscriptions and coin legends. Rome traded with India but did not rule any of its kingdoms, so the last option is also false.

**4. (A)** The key contrast is internal-coup versus later outside entry: Pushyamitra was the Mauryan commander-in-chief who killed the last emperor, while the Indo-Greeks were foreigners who entered the weakened northwest afterwards. They were not simultaneous foreign invaders, nor were their origins reversed (Pushyamitra was Indian, the Indo-Greeks foreign). Neither came to power by election, so that option is false.

**5. (B)** The ashvamedha stakes a sovereignty claim and the marriage forges a diplomatic alliance; both are politics carried out through other forms. Neither is merely a private or religious act, which is exactly the trap the chapter warns against. Neither is a tribute-collection mechanism, and neither selects a successor, so those options misread the purpose of both acts.

**6. (A)** One record of a queen performing the rite shows women could hold real authority, that the Vedic sacrifice survived, and that the rite carried a sovereignty claim. It does NOT show women ruled more commonly than men; a single example cannot establish that women outnumbered male rulers. That over-generalisation is the inference the record cannot support, so it is the correct 'except' choice.

**7. (A)** A mother-derived royal name plus a recorded land-grant by that mother jointly point to the high standing of royal women. The naming was after the mother, not a god, so the first distractor is false. Nothing here suggests renunciation of the throne. 'Gautami' is the mother's name, not a city, so that misreads the same evidence. The two facts converge on women's standing.

**8. (A)** All three items in the correct set concern royal women exercising naming-prestige, property and ritual sovereignty, which directly refutes the men-only claim. Ship-coins, Roman gold and a milestone speak to trade, not women's power. The Bharhut Stupa, Heliodorus pillar and Anicut concern religion, diplomacy and irrigation. Sangam poems, the Shaka era and Kanishka's coins concern poetry, timekeeping and religion. Only the first set bears on the gender claim.

**9. (D)** The resolving principle is patronage across traditions: a Vedic-reviving dynasty could still adorn a Buddhist monument. There is no evidence of a secret conversion, and inventing one is unnecessary. The Bharhut Stupa is genuinely Buddhist, not a relabelled Vedic temple. Buddhism plainly had not died out, since its monument was being actively embellished. Only the cross-tradition patronage principle dissolves the puzzle.

**10. (B)** A samvat is indeed a counted era from a fixed event, as the Shaka samvat shows. A stupa is a Buddhist dome, not a dam (that is an anicut). An anicut is a river-dam, not a Buddhist monument (that swap is the trap). The ashvamedha is a horse-sacrifice, not a poetry anthology (those are the Sangam works). Only the samvat pairing holds.

**11. (B)** The Shungas held north-central India, the Satavahanas the Deccan, Kharavela ruled Kalinga on the eastern coast, and the Pandyas were one of the Tamil far-southern crowned kingdoms; only the first set matches every region. Each other option scrambles at least two of these well-attested placements, for example putting the Shungas in the Deccan or the Pandyas in the northwest, which the chapter never supports.

**12. (B)** The ship-coin and Roman gold mark sea trade while the Silk-Route milestone marks land trade, so together they show commerce by both sea and land. They are evidence of exchange, not of military conquest by Rome or China. They contradict the idea of village-only trade. And because they include both a sea route and a land route, they disprove the 'single route' claim.

**13. (D)** Coins demonstrate trade, but rule would need administrative traces such as governors or garrisons, which are absent, so trade is the sound inference. The coins were genuine Roman pieces paid for goods, not Indian forgeries. Distance plainly did not block contact, since the coins did arrive. And there is no record of Indian kings being crowned at Rome, so that is invented.

**14. (D)** An inscription fixes a concrete deed in stable wording, while a flattering poem may inflate its subject, so the inscription is the firmer evidence. Poems were not inherently more accurate; the point is the opposite. Queens did not personally carve their grants, so that reason is false even though it picks the right source. And sources clearly do tell us about women, since the chapter reconstructs their standing from exactly such records.

**15. (C)** Coins typically carry names, titles and portraits, while inscriptions narrate deeds and gifts in words, so each has a distinct strength. The first distractor reverses these roles. Both sources can in fact give a ruler's name, so calling them unreliable on that point is wrong. Coins are not forgeries; both are authentic ancient evidence.

**16. (D)** Kanishka's many-gods coins reveal religious leanings and Indo-Greek coins reveal names and portraits, so coins can recover identity, titles and beliefs when texts are scarce. They are not limited to measuring metal wealth. They reveal which gods were honoured, not refused. And their value comes from what they record, not from being unused, since they did circulate in trade.

**17. (A)** The Satavahanas of the Deccan traded by sea through western ports, while the Kushanas controlled the overland Silk Route, so the two routes must be kept apart. Saying both used the Silk Route ignores Satavahana sea trade. Reversing the pair gives the Kushanas the sea and the Satavahanas the land, which is backwards. Saying both used sea ports ignores the Kushana Silk-Route dominance.

**18. (D)** The Satavahanas and Tamil kingdoms both traded with Rome by sea, while the Kushanas profited from the overland Silk Route, so the Kushanas are wrongly grouped with the maritime traders. They did not all use the Silk Route. The Satavahanas reached Rome by sea, not land. And the Tamil kingdoms used the sea, not the Silk Route, so the last option also misassigns the routes.

**19. (C)** Familiarity with a region's products follows naturally from trading for them, so the notes evidence commerce, not rule. Roman writers wrote about many lands they never conquered, so the first distractor is false. The notes show goods moving toward Rome, not Tamil kings ruling Italy. And buyers learn about goods through trade, not only through colonisation, so the last claim is wrong.

**20. (D)** Repeated praise for giving shows that generosity was valued in a ruler, which is the reliable reading. The poems praise gift-giving, so a ban on gifts is the opposite of what they show. They name and praise chiefs, so chiefs clearly existed and wealth was not perfectly equal. And the poems are about love and heroism, not Vedic sacrifice, so the last option misdescribes their content.

**21. (C)** An anicut is a river-dam for irrigation, exactly what the Grand Anicut did for the Kaveri delta. A dome-shaped monument is a stupa, not an anicut. A counted era is a samvat. A marriage treaty is a matrimonial alliance. Only the irrigation-dam definition fits the term.

**22. (C)** The anicut irrigated the delta into fertile rice land, the economic foundation of Chola power, which a fort could not provide. It was a dam, not a fortress, so it had no primary military role. It raised and diverted water rather than blocking ships. And it was an irrigation work, not a boundary marker, so the last option misreads its purpose.

**23. (D)** The Maurya breakup came first; only after it weakened the frontier did the Indo-Greeks enter, the Satavahanas then rose in the Deccan, and Kanishka's Kushanas came later still. Placing Kanishka or the Satavahanas before the Maurya breakup reverses the sequence the chapter sets out. Placing the Indo-Greek entry before the breakup contradicts the cause-and-effect order the chapter stresses.

**24. (C)** The Shungas were an Indian dynasty of the heartland and the Indo-Greeks were foreigners in the northwest, so the labels are correct. The Indo-Greeks were foreign, not indigenous, so the first distractor mislabels them. The Satavahanas were an indigenous Deccan power, not foreign. The Cholas were an indigenous Tamil kingdom, not foreign, so the last option is wrong too.

**25. (B)** The Bharhut railing is heartland Buddhist art adorned under the Shungas, while the Indo-Greek coin is the main portrait record of a northwestern foreign king, so the contrast holds. The railing is not Mathura red sandstone, and a coin is not sculpture, so the first distractor is wrong. The railing was made under an Indian dynasty, not a foreign one. And neither object is a sea-trade token for pepper, so the last option is false.

**26. (A)** A Greek portrait beside an Indian god in Indian script literally combines two cultures on one coin, supporting cultural blending. It cannot show rejection of Indian languages and gods, since an Indian god is named on it. One coin says nothing about ruling the whole subcontinent. And striking coins shows engagement with money and trade, not indifference to it.

**27. (C)** A Greek ambassador honouring an Indian deity directly shows cultural blending and the adoption of Indian belief. It cannot show Greek gods being forced on Indians, since the object honours an Indian god. It is the opposite of refusing contact with Indian religion. And it records reverence, not the destruction of shrines, so the last option contradicts the object itself.

**28. (C)** Toga-folds and wavy hair mark the Greco-Buddhist Gandhara school, which visibly merges a foreign form with an Indian Buddhist subject, so it best shows confluence; the spotted red-sandstone figure is the indigenous Mathura style. Swapping the two schools, as the other options do, reverses the trap the chapter warns against, and the indigenous Mathura piece is essentially one local tradition, so it is not the better example of blending.

**29. (D)** Gandhara visibly joins a Greek form to an Indian Buddhist subject, which is exactly what confluence means, whereas Mathura is largely a single indigenous tradition. Gandhara was strongly shaped by Greek form, so saying it had no foreign influence is wrong. Mathura was the indigenous school and Gandhara the blended one, so reversing them is false. And Gandhara is sculpture, not Tamil poetry.

**30. (A)** The textbook has swapped the two: Gandhara is the Greek-Buddhist blend and Mathura is the indigenous red-sandstone school, so restoring that fixes both halves. Gandhara was sculpture, not Tamil poetry. Neither school was purely Greek; Mathura was indigenous and Gandhara only partly Greek. And both were sculpture schools, not poetry, so the last option is false.

**31. (C)** Both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion: because Indian culture stayed dominant and absorbed the newcomers' contributions, the foreigners are counted as contributors rather than destroyers. The assertion is not false, and the reason is not false; the chapter affirms both. So only the option affirming both and linking them is correct.

**32. (D)** Indian themes staying central while foreign forms were taken in shows absorption and reshaping, not erasure. It is not violent expulsion, since foreign elements were retained, just reframed. It is the opposite of being wholly Hellenised, since the Indian core remained dominant. And art clearly continued, so a halt in activity is false.

**33. (B)** The Kushanas grew rich from the overland Silk Route, not from western Deccan sea ports, so that statement is the misdescription; the Deccan sea trade belonged to the Satavahanas. The Shungas did revive Vedic ritual and adorn Bharhut, the Satavahanas did trade by sea as ship-coins show, and the Cholas did build the Grand Anicut, so those three are all accurate.

**34. (B)** The counter-example is the coexistence of many rival kingdoms with thriving art, trade and Sanskrit literature, which directly refutes 'barren'. There was no single unifying emperor, so that option is false. Art and trade did not stop; they flourished. And foreign rulers absorbed rather than banned Indian culture, so the last option contradicts the chapter.

**35. (A)** Kharavela combined kingship over Kalinga with personal devotion to Jainism, which is why he is called a monk-king. He did not abdicate to wander, since he kept ruling. He was a Kalinga ruler, not a Kushana governor of Gandhara. And he followed an existing faith rather than founding a new religion.

**36. (C)** Knowing Kharavela mainly through one carved inscription shows that the age is recovered largely from inscriptions, not running chronicles. Continuous court chronicles did not survive for these kingdoms, which is the very gap inscriptions fill. Roman ledgers did not list Indian kings systematically. And Sangam poems are southern Tamil works, not records of an eastern king like Kharavela.

**37. (C)** The three crowned Tamil kingdoms are the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas, all of the far south. Each distractor swaps in a non-Tamil power: the Satavahanas of the Deccan, the Shungas of the heartland, or the Kushanas of the northwest. Only the all-Tamil list is correct.

**38. (C)** Both halves of the pupil's claim are wrong: the Shakas are the Indo-Scythians, not the Indo-Greeks, and they ARE tied to the lasting Shaka samvat. The first distractor still misidentifies them as Indo-Greeks. The second wrongly says no lasting calendar survives. The third both misidentifies them as Kushanas and invents a Roman calendar link. Only the option correcting both errors is right.

**39. (A)** A calendar still in national use is a living legacy, so the age left lasting marks, not just ruins. It plainly did produce something that outlived it, contradicting the first distractor. The Shaka era is Indian, not a Roman creation. And a calendar woven into Indian life shows deep contact with Indian traditions, not its absence.

**40. (B)** Kanishka was the famous Kushana ruler tied to the Silk Route, so that triple match is correct. Pushyamitra was a Shunga, not a Kushana. Kharavela ruled Kalinga, not the Satavahana realm, and the Anicut was a Chola work. Gautamiputra was a Satavahana, not an Indo-Greek, and the Heliodorus pillar was set up by a Greek ambassador, so each other option mismatches power or deed.

**41. (A)** The Kushanas controlled the overland Silk Route, a land artery the sea-trading Satavahanas could not hold. The western Deccan sea routes were exactly the Satavahanas' own sphere, so they are not the Kushana source. The Kaveri canals were a Chola irrigation work, not a trade route. And the Sangam 'circuit' is not a trade route at all, so only the Silk Route fits.

**42. (C)** The Silk Route was a network of overland routes from China through Central Asia to the Mediterranean. It was not a sea lane, which is how the Satavahanas and Tamils reached Rome. It was not a Chola delta road, and it was not a poets' circuit, so only the overland-network description is accurate.

**43. (B)** The chapter links the great Sanskrit epics, the Mahabharata and Ramayana, to this age, and their flourishing argues against any 'dark age' label. The Sangam works are Tamil, not Sanskrit, and do not prove the north produced nothing. The Hathigumpha inscription is a royal record, not the age's only literature. And the Arthashastra belongs to the earlier Mauryan court, not to this period.

**44. (B)** The right reason is that the evidence is largely physical, since continuous written chronicles did not survive, even though people could read and write, as the inscriptions themselves prove. The first distractor keeps the pupil's false claim that no one could read or write. The age did leave buildings, such as stupas. And its history was not all Roman translation, so those options are wrong.

**45. (A)** Coins, cave inscriptions and sculptures are all surviving physical objects of the age. Election ballots, newspapers, emails, photographs, passports, constitutions, voter rolls and tax records are all modern documents that did not exist then, so any list containing them is wrong. Only the all-ancient, all-physical list qualifies.

**46. (D)** The coin evidences Greek-Indian cultural blending while the inscription evidences a queen's high standing, so they illuminate different themes. Neither shows Roman rule, which would need administrative traces. Neither concerns the Silk Route, since one is a northwest coin and the other a Deccan land-grant. And both are in fact reliable physical sources, so dismissing them is wrong.

**47. (D)** Gandhara's blending, Kanishka honouring Indian and other gods, and a Greek ambassador revering an Indian deity all show foreigners absorbing and enriching Indian culture, the heart of the 'contributors' case. Roman gold, ship-coins and the Anicut concern trade and irrigation among indigenous powers. The Bharhut Stupa, Sangam poetry and Tamil kingdoms are indigenous, not foreign. Kharavela and the Kaveri likewise involve no foreign rulers, so only the first set bears on the claim.

**48. (A)** The Shungas revived Vedic ritual while still adorning the Buddhist Bharhut Stupa, the supported reading. They were an indigenous heartland dynasty, not foreigners from Alexander's governors (that describes the Indo-Greeks). The Silk Route was the Kushanas' source of wealth. And the Grand Anicut was a Chola work, so each distractor borrows another power's trait.

**49. (A)** A ship-coin from a Deccan port best supports flourishing maritime trade, and it certainly does not prove the dynasty lost its land, since the Satavahanas held the Deccan. The ship marks trade, not sea-worship. There is no evidence of a war-fleet built against the Kushanas. And the dynasty did not survive 'only at sea', so the land-loss claim is unsupported.

**50. (A)** A royal marriage could convert a rivalry into an alliance and extend a kingdom's influence, so it was diplomacy, not merely a wedding. Weddings did not all include a horse-sacrifice, which is a separate sovereignty ritual. Marriage was not a tribute mechanism. And calling it a purely private event is exactly the misreading the chapter rejects.

**51. (C)** The flaw is over-generalising the sea-trade pattern to the Kushanas, whose wealth actually flowed from the overland Silk Route, not the sea. The argument does not deny Satavahana sea trade; it accepts it. It does not deny that the Cheras and Pandyas were Tamil kingdoms. And it makes no claim about Rome lying on the Silk Route, so only the over-generalisation is the real fault.

**52. (C)** The foreign rulers were largely absorbed into a still-dominant Indian culture, as their Indian-themed coins and art show. They did not erase Indian culture, nor did they stay wholly apart, since blending is everywhere visible. And no single foreign empire unified the subcontinent, so the last option is false.

**53. (B)** The rebuttal points to division and creativity coexisting: rival kingdoms alongside new art, trade and Sanskrit works. There was no single unifying empire, so that claim is false. Creativity did not pause and resume; it ran throughout. And foreign rulers absorbed rather than suppressed Indian culture, so the last option contradicts the chapter.

**54. (D)** All three are supported: the Satavahanas held the Deccan, their ship-coins evidence sea trade, and a widowed Satavahana queen is recorded performing the ashvamedha. Since every claim is accurate, any option excluding one of them is wrong, leaving 'all three' as the only correct answer.

**55. (D)** A Greek-robed Buddha unites a foreign style with an Indian subject in one image, distilling the age's confluence, which is why it is so emblematic. It does not show rejection of foreign influence; it embraces it. It does not turn the Buddha into a Greek god; it gives an Indian subject a Greek form. And it is itself active art, disproving any claim that Indian art stopped.

**56. (C)** Confluence is blending into new shared styles with a dominant Indian core preserved, not replacement. The pupil's 'complete replacement' is exactly the error being corrected. Keeping cultures strictly separate is the opposite of blending. And spreading one unchanging culture with no mixing again misses the blending that confluence requires.

**57. (D)** A single coin can establish the ruler's Greek-style portrait and his honouring of an Indian god, hence cultural blending. It cannot map the kingdom's exact boundaries, narrate campaigns year by year, or reveal army size and tax revenues, all of which would require far more evidence than one coin supplies.

**58. (A)** The Maurya breakup came first and weakened the frontier, enabling later foreign entry, so it is the enabling event. The Indo-Greek arrival followed the breakup and could not have caused it. The Satavahanas rose after the breakup, not before. And Kanishka's Kushana rule came later still, so none of these could have triggered the earlier Maurya collapse.

**59. (B)** Kharavela of Kalinga is known chiefly from the Hathigumpha inscription, so that match is correct. The Indo-Greek kings are known mainly from their coins, not Sangam poems, which are southern Tamil works. Tamil sea trade is evidenced by Roman gold hoards, not Bharhut railings, which are Shunga-era Buddhist art. And Satavahana queens are known from their own land-grant inscriptions, not Kanishka's coins, so each distractor misassigns the evidence.

**60. (B)** The fullest accurate picture combines political fragmentation, cultural confluence, sea and land trade, and the absorption of foreigners into a dominant Indian culture. There was no single peaceful empire, and foreign rulers did not erase Indian culture, so the first option is doubly wrong. The age was creatively rich, not a dark age. And the Mauryas fell from within, not from an outside invasion that then unified the subcontinent, so the last option misstates both cause and outcome.
