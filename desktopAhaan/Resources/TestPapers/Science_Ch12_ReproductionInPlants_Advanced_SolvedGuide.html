Set 1 · Q1–Q10

Q1

In a flower, the male reproductive part as a whole is called the:

  • Apistil
  • Bsepal
  • Cstamen✓
  • Dovule

Working

✓

The male reproductive part of a flower, taken as a whole, is the **stamen**. The pistil is the female part; sepals are the green protective parts; the ovule is a structure inside the ovary.

Q2

A stamen is made up of two parts. They are the:

  • Afilament and anther✓
  • Bstigma and style
  • Covary and ovule
  • Dpetal and sepal

Working

✓

A stamen has two parts: the slender stalk called the **filament** and the knob at its top called the **anther**, which makes pollen.

Q3

The three parts of a pistil, from top to bottom, are:

  • Aanther, filament, ovary
  • Bpetal, sepal, ovule
  • Covary, style, anther
  • Dstigma, style, ovary✓

Working

✓

The pistil runs **stigma → style → ovary** from top to bottom. The stigma catches pollen, the style is the tube, and the ovary at the base holds the ovules.

Q4

Pollen grains are produced inside the:

  • Aovary
  • Banther✓
  • Cstigma
  • Dstyle

Working

✓

Pollen grains are made inside the **anther**, the swollen tip of the stamen.

Q5

After fertilisation, the ovule develops into a ____ and the ovary develops into a ____.

  • Afruit; seed
  • Bseed; fruit✓
  • Cflower; fruit
  • Dseed; flower

Working

✓

After fertilisation the ovule becomes the **seed** and the surrounding ovary ripens into the **fruit**. This "ovule → seed, ovary → fruit" rule is the classic trap — keep them paired correctly.

Q6

A flower that has both stamens and a pistil is described as:

  • Aunisexual
  • Bincomplete
  • Csterile
  • Dbisexual✓

Working

✓

A flower carrying both stamens and a pistil is **bisexual** (e.g. mustard, rose). A flower with only one of the two is unisexual.

Q7

Which pair lists flowers that are typically **unisexual**?

  • Apapaya and watermelon✓
  • Bmustard and rose
  • CChina rose and pea
  • Dhibiscus and mustard

Working

✓

**Papaya and watermelon** typically bear unisexual flowers (separate male and female flowers). Mustard, rose, China rose and pea are bisexual.

Q8

The transfer of pollen from the anther to the stigma of the **same** flower (or another flower on the same plant) is called:

  • Across-pollination
  • Bfertilisation
  • Cself-pollination✓
  • Dgermination

Working

✓

Pollen moving from anther to stigma of the same flower (or another flower on the same plant) is **self-pollination**.

Q9

Cross-pollination strictly requires that pollen lands on the stigma of a flower on:

  • Aa different plant of the same kind✓
  • Bthe same plant
  • Cany nearby plant of any kind
  • Dthe same flower

Working

✓

Cross-pollination means pollen reaches the stigma of a flower on **a different plant of the same kind** — a different individual, but the same species.

Q10

A plant bearing only unisexual flowers, with male and female flowers on **separate** plants, can reproduce sexually only through:

  • Aself-pollination
  • Bvegetative propagation
  • Ccross-pollination✓
  • Dbudding

Working

✓

If male and female flowers sit on separate plants, the only sexual route is **cross-pollination**, because no single plant has both gamete types in one flower.

Set 2 · Q11–Q20

Q11

Flowers pollinated by wind are usually:

  • Alarge, brightly coloured and scented
  • Bclosed and pollen-free
  • Csticky and nectar-rich
  • Dsmall, dull, with light pollen and feathery stigmas✓

Working

✓

Wind-pollinated flowers are usually **small, dull, with light pollen and feathery stigmas** — they spend nothing on attracting animals and everything on catching air-borne pollen.

Q12

Bright petals, scent and nectar are adaptations that attract:

  • Awind
  • Binsects and other animals✓
  • Cwater currents
  • Dgravity

Working

✓

Bright petals, scent and nectar are all lures for **insects and other animals**, the pollinators that carry pollen for the plant.

Q13

The pollen of wind-pollinated flowers is typically produced in **large amounts** because:

  • Ainsects eat most of it
  • Bit is heavy and sinks quickly
  • Cthe stigma rejects most grains
  • Dwind delivery is wasteful, so a lot is lost✓

Working

✓

Wind carries pollen blindly, so most grains never reach a stigma; the flower compensates by making **huge amounts** so that at least some succeed.

Q14

When a pollen grain lands on a compatible stigma, it grows a structure called the:

  • Afilament
  • Bpollen tube✓
  • Cstyle
  • Dovary wall

Working

✓

On a compatible stigma the pollen grain grows a **pollen tube** that pushes down through the style.

Q15

The purpose of the pollen tube is to:

  • Ahold the petals open
  • Bmake the flower smell sweet
  • Ccarry the male gamete down to the ovule✓
  • Ddisperse the seeds

Working

✓

The pollen tube's job is to **carry the male gamete down to the ovule** so fertilisation can happen.

Q16

Fertilisation in flowering plants is best defined as the fusion of:

  • Athe male gamete with the egg (female gamete) inside the ovule✓
  • Btwo pollen grains
  • Cthe anther with the stigma
  • Dtwo ovaries

Working

✓

Fertilisation is the **fusion of the male gamete with the egg (female gamete) inside the ovule**, producing a zygote.

Q17

The cell formed immediately after fertilisation is the:

  • Agamete
  • Bspore
  • Czygote✓
  • Dbud

Working

✓

The single cell formed right after the gametes fuse is the **zygote**, the first cell of the new plant.

Q18

A pea pod is botanically a fruit. The peas inside it are the:

  • Aseeds✓
  • Bovaries
  • Canthers
  • Dstyles

Working

✓

A pea pod is the fruit (ripened ovary); the peas inside are the **seeds** (ripened ovules).

Q19

A mango stone (the hard inner part you cannot eat) is best described as:

  • Athe ovary wall
  • Bthe seed of the mango✓
  • Ca cluster of pollen
  • Dthe stigma

Working

✓

The hard mango stone is **the seed of the mango** (the ovule, hardened). The juicy part is the fleshy fruit around it.

Q20

If a flower's ovary contains many ovules, the resulting fruit will most likely contain:

  • Ano seeds
  • Bexactly one seed
  • Conly pollen
  • Dmany seeds✓

Working

✓

Each fertilised ovule becomes one seed, so an ovary with many ovules yields a fruit with **many seeds** (think of a papaya or watermelon).

Set 3 · Q21–Q30

Q21

Reproduction that involves only **one** parent and produces offspring identical to it is called:

  • Aasexual reproduction✓
  • Bsexual reproduction
  • Ccross-pollination
  • Dfertilisation

Working

✓

Reproduction from a single parent giving identical offspring is **asexual reproduction**.

Q22

Offspring produced by asexual reproduction are best described as:

  • Aa mixture of two parents' traits
  • Balways larger than the parent
  • Cunable to reproduce again
  • Dclones — genetically identical to the single parent✓

Working

✓

Asexual offspring are **clones — genetically identical to the single parent**, because no mixing of two parents' material takes place.

Q23

The production of a new plant from a root, stem or leaf of the parent — without seeds — is called:

  • Apollination
  • Bvegetative propagation✓
  • Cfertilisation
  • Dgermination

Working

✓

Growing a new plant from a root, stem or leaf without seeds is **vegetative propagation**.

Q24

A potato grown from a piece of potato with a "bud" (an eye) is an example of vegetative propagation by:

  • Aleaf
  • Broot
  • Cstem✓
  • Dseed

Working

✓

A potato's "eyes" are buds on a swollen underground **stem**, so growing a plant from a potato piece is propagation by stem.

Q25

The tiny notches along the margin of a *Bryophyllum* leaf can each grow into a new plant. This is vegetative propagation by:

  • Aroot
  • Bleaf✓
  • Cstem
  • Dflower

Working

✓

*Bryophyllum* grows tiny plantlets in the notches of its **leaf** margin — propagation by leaf.

Q26

Sweet potato and dahlia are propagated using their:

  • Aleaves
  • Bflowers
  • Croots✓
  • Danthers

Working

✓

Sweet potato and dahlia store food in swollen **roots** that can sprout new plants.

Q27

A gardener who wants a new rose bush **identical** to a prized parent rose should use:

  • Aa cutting (vegetative propagation)✓
  • Bseeds from the parent's fruit
  • Ccross-pollination with another variety
  • Dwind pollination

Working

✓

To get a plant identical to a prized parent, take a **cutting** (vegetative propagation); seeds would mix in another parent's traits through pollination.

Q28

Which of the following is **not** an advantage of growing plants by vegetative propagation?

  • Athe new plants are identical to the parent
  • Bthey often flower and fruit earlier than seed-grown plants
  • Cplants that make few or no viable seeds can still be multiplied
  • Dit increases the variety (genetic mixing) of offspring✓

Working

✓

Vegetative propagation makes clones, so it does **not** increase variety — that statement is the odd one out. Its real advantages are identical offspring, earlier flowering, and multiplying plants that set few seeds.

Q29

In yeast, a small bulge grows on the parent cell, enlarges and pinches off as a new cell. This method is called:

  • Afragmentation
  • Bspore formation
  • Cpollination
  • Dbudding✓

Working

✓

In yeast a bulge swells on the parent cell and pinches off — this is **budding**.

Q30

*Hydra* (a tiny animal) and yeast both reproduce by budding. The common feature is that:

  • Aa bud grows out of the parent body and detaches as a new individual✓
  • Btwo parents are needed
  • Cseeds are formed
  • Dpollen is transferred

Working

✓

Budding in both yeast and *Hydra* shares the feature that **a bud grows out of the parent body and detaches as a new individual**; only one parent is involved.

Q31

*Spirogyra*, a green pond alga, breaks into pieces that each grow into a new filament. This is:

  • Abudding
  • Bself-pollination
  • Cfragmentation✓
  • Dseed dispersal

Working

✓

*Spirogyra* breaks into pieces that each regrow — **fragmentation**.

Q32

Bread mould and ferns reproduce by tiny structures, made in large numbers, that survive harsh conditions. These are:

  • Aseeds
  • Bspores✓
  • Cbuds
  • Dovules

Working

✓

Bread mould and ferns reproduce by **spores**, made in large numbers and able to survive hard times.

Q33

Spores are well suited to surviving unfavourable conditions mainly because they have:

  • Abright colours
  • Bsweet nectar
  • Ca tough protective covering and are very light✓
  • Da hard stone like a mango

Working

✓

Spores survive unfavourable conditions because they are **light and have a tough protective covering**, letting them wait and travel until conditions improve.

Q34

Scattering seeds away from the parent plant is called seed:

  • Agermination
  • Bdispersal✓
  • Cpollination
  • Dfertilisation

Working

✓

Scattering seeds away from the parent is seed **dispersal**.

Q35

The drumstick and maple have **winged**, lightweight seeds. They are dispersed mainly by:

  • Awater
  • Banimals
  • Cexplosion
  • Dwind✓

Working

✓

Winged, lightweight seeds of drumstick and maple ride on **wind**.

Q36

The coconut, with its thick fibrous husk that traps air and floats, is dispersed by:

  • Awater✓
  • Bwind
  • Cbursting
  • Dinsects

Working

✓

The coconut floats because of its air-trapping husk, so it is dispersed by **water**.

Q37

*Xanthium* and *Urena* seeds have tiny hooks or spines. These help them disperse by:

  • Aclinging to the fur of animals✓
  • Bfloating on water
  • Ccatching the wind
  • Dbursting open

Working

✓

Hooked, spiny seeds of *Xanthium* and *Urena* disperse by **clinging to the fur of animals**.

Q38

When the dry fruit of castor or balsam suddenly bursts and flings its seeds out, the agent of dispersal is:

  • Awind
  • Bexplosion (the plant itself)✓
  • Cwater
  • Danimals

Working

✓

When castor and balsam fruits dry and burst to fling seeds out, the agent is **the plant's own bursting — explosion**.

Q39

A juicy, brightly coloured, tasty fruit such as a guava is an adaptation that encourages dispersal by:

  • Awind
  • Bexplosion
  • Cwater only
  • Danimals that eat the fruit and drop the seeds✓

Working

✓

A juicy, tasty fruit is bait: **animals eat the fruit and drop or pass the seeds** elsewhere.

Q40

Seed dispersal benefits a plant species mainly because it:

  • Akeeps all seedlings crowded around the parent
  • Bprevents the seeds from ever germinating
  • Creduces competition for water, minerals and sunlight and helps colonise new places✓
  • Dmakes the seeds heavier

Working

✓

Dispersal helps the species by **reducing competition for water, minerals and sunlight, and by letting it colonise new places** instead of crowding under the parent.

The "waking up" of a seed — when it begins to grow into a seedling under suitable conditions — is called:

Q42

Lily and members of the lily family commonly reproduce vegetatively through underground:

  • Aanthers
  • Bstigmas
  • Cbulbs✓
  • Dpollen grains

Working

✓

Lilies reproduce vegetatively through underground **bulbs**.

Q43

The protective outer green leaf-like parts that enclose a flower bud before it opens are the:

  • Asepals✓
  • Bpetals
  • Cstamens
  • Dcarpels

Working

✓

The green, leaf-like parts enclosing a bud before it opens are the **sepals**.

Q44

Money plant cuttings root easily in water. The part that produces the new roots and shoots is the:

  • Aflower
  • Bstem (node)✓
  • Cseed
  • Danther

Working

✓

A money-plant cutting roots from its **stem node**, which produces both new roots and shoots.

Q45

A key difference between sexual and asexual reproduction is that **sexual** reproduction:

  • Aneeds only one parent
  • Binvolves the fusion of male and female gametes and produces variation✓
  • Calways produces clones
  • Dnever involves flowers

Working

✓

Sexual reproduction **involves the fusion of male and female gametes and produces variation**, whereas asexual reproduction needs one parent and makes clones.

Q46

Which sequence correctly orders the events of sexual reproduction in a flowering plant?

  • Afertilisation → pollination → seed → fruit
  • Bseed formation → pollination → fertilisation
  • Cfruit formation → pollination → fertilisation
  • Dpollination → fertilisation → seed and fruit formation✓

Working

✓

The correct order is **pollination → fertilisation → seed and fruit formation**: pollen must arrive, gametes must fuse, then the seed and fruit form.

Q47

A farmer notices a banana plant that produces seedless fruit. The most reliable way to grow more of these plants is:

  • Asowing seeds from the fruit
  • Bwaiting for wind pollination
  • Cvegetative propagation using suckers/offshoots✓
  • Dcross-pollination with a wild banana

Working

✓

Seedless banana cannot be grown from seed, so use **vegetative propagation with suckers/offshoots** to make identical plants.

Q48

Pollen grains failing to reach a stigma at all would directly prevent:

  • Apollination, and therefore fertilisation and seed formation✓
  • Bphotosynthesis
  • Cthe leaves from growing
  • Dthe roots from absorbing water

Working

✓

If pollen never reaches a stigma, **pollination fails, so fertilisation and seed formation cannot happen** — the reproductive chain is broken at the first step.

Q49

In maize (corn), the tassel at the top releases pollen and the silky threads lower down catch it. Each silky thread leads to one developing grain. Each silk is therefore acting like a:

  • Astigma-and-style leading to an ovule✓
  • Bfilament
  • Cpetal
  • Dsepal

Working

✓

Each corn silk catches pollen and leads down to one developing grain, so a silk acts as **a stigma-and-style leading to an ovule**.

Q50

Two rose varieties — one with large blooms, one disease-resistant — are deliberately cross-pollinated to combine their good traits. This process is called:

  • Afragmentation
  • Bbudding
  • Cgermination
  • Dhybridisation✓

Working

✓

Deliberately cross-pollinating two varieties to combine good traits is **hybridisation**.

Set 6 · Q51–Q60

Q51

A student claims "all flowers can self-pollinate." A correct objection is that:

  • Ano flower has both stamens and pistil
  • Bpollen cannot move at all
  • Cunisexual flowers lack one of the parts, so a single such flower cannot self-pollinate✓
  • Dstigmas never receive pollen

Working

✓

"All flowers self-pollinate" is wrong because **unisexual flowers lack one of the parts, so a single such flower cannot self-pollinate** — it has no internal partner.

Q52

Why are clones from one parent a possible **disadvantage** when a new disease appears?

  • Aclones grow too slowly to matter
  • Bbeing genetically identical, if one is susceptible they all are✓
  • Cclones cannot be infected at all
  • Dclones always carry seeds that resist disease

Working

✓

Because clones are genetically identical, **if one is susceptible to a new disease they all are**, so the whole crop can fall together.

Q53

A floating fruit must be both buoyant and waterproof. The coconut achieves buoyancy mainly through its:

  • Ahard inner shell only
  • Bsweet liquid inside
  • Cair-trapping fibrous husk✓
  • Dbright colour

Working

✓

The coconut stays buoyant chiefly through its **air-trapping fibrous husk**, which works like a built-in float.

Q54

In wind-pollinated grasses the stigmas are large and **feathery**. The advantage of this shape is that it:

  • Aattracts insects
  • Bstores nectar
  • Cmakes the pollen heavier

✓

A large feathery stigma **increases the surface area to trap airborne pollen**, a perfect match for wind pollination.

Q55

A complete bisexual flower loses its anthers to a pest before they mature. That flower can still form seeds only if:

✓

Q58

The chief reason a desert plant might rely on asexual reproduction is that it:

Q59

A gardener grafts a bud from a sweet-orange tree onto a hardy root-stock. The fruit produced by that bud will taste like:

Q60

Tracing a single sunflower seed back through the events that formed it, the correct chain is:

  • Aovule → pollination → fertilisation → seed
  • Bfertilisation → pollination → fruit → seed
  • Cseed → ovule → pollination → fertilisation
  • Dpollination → fertilisation → ovule fertilised → seed✓

Working

✓
