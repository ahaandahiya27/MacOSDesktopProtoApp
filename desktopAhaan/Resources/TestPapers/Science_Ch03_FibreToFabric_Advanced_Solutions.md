# Science — Chapter 3: Fibre to Fabric · Paper 2 (Advanced) Solutions

**1. (B)** Wool (from sheep, goat, yak, camel) and silk (from the silkworm) both come from animals, so they are animal fibres.

**2. (C)** Cotton, jute and flax all come from plants, so they are plant fibres made of cellulose.

**3. (C)** Wool is made of the protein keratin — the same tough protein found in hair, nails and horn.

**4. (B)** Silk is made of the protein fibroin, secreted by the silkworm.

**5. (B)** Plant fibres such as cotton are made of cellulose, not protein.

**6. (D)** Sheep, yak, goat and camel all yield wool; a "woolly dog" does not — it is not a wool-yielding animal.

**7. (B)** Pashmina is the fine inner wool of the Changthangi goat of Ladakh.

**8. (C)** Beneath the coarse outer (beard) hair lies the soft, fine inner wool that is actually woven into fabric.

**9. (A)** The whole coat of wool removed in one piece during shearing is the fleece.

**10. (B)** Wool processing begins with shearing (cutting the fleece), then scouring (washing it).

**11. (A)** Shearing is the painless cutting off of the fleece using clippers (the outer skin layer is dead).

**12. (B)** Like our own hair and nails, the uppermost skin layer that carries the wool is dead, so cutting it does not hurt the sheep.

**13. (B)** Scouring washes the sheared wool in tanks to remove grease, dust and dirt.

**14. (B)** The sequence is Shearing → Scouring → Sorting, so the missing step is scouring.

**15. (A)** The small fluffy fibres picked out of the hairy skin are the burrs that must be removed.

**16. (B)** Sorting separates the hairy skin (with its wool) into wool of different textures.

**17. (C)** Natural wool comes in a few shades; dyeing gives it the wide range of colours we see.

**18. (C)** The final step turns the processed fibres into thread by spinning into yarn.

**19. (B)** Sorter's disease is anthrax, caused by *Bacillus anthracis* spores present in raw wool.

**20. (B)** Anthrax inhaled from raw wool is a respiratory infection, so it mainly attacks the lungs.

**21. (A)** Sericulture is the rearing of silkworms to produce silk fibre.

**22. (B)** The silkworm is the caterpillar (larva) of the silk moth.

**23. (C)** A silkworm is both a caterpillar and a larva — the two words describe the same stage — so both statements are true.

**24. (B)** The life cycle runs egg → larva (silkworm) → pupa (in the cocoon) → adult moth.

**25. (C)** The common silkworm feeds on mulberry leaves (hence "mulberry silk").

**26. (B)** Moriculture is the growing of mulberry trees to feed silkworms.

**27. (A)** Sericulture (rearing silkworms) and moriculture (growing their mulberry food) are the two silk-related terms.

**28. (B)** Before turning into a pupa, the silkworm spins a silk cocoon around its body for protection.

**29. (B)** A single cocoon yields one continuous silk thread that can be up to about a kilometre long.

**30. (B)** Reeling is unwinding the silk thread from a softened cocoon onto a reel.

**31. (B)** Cocoons are boiled or steamed to loosen the gum (sericin) and kill the pupa so the long thread can be unwound unbroken.

**32. (B)** Tasar silk comes from a silkworm that feeds on arjun leaves, not mulberry.

**33. (B)** India is one of the world's largest silk producers.

**34. (B)** Natural fibres are breathable and biodegradable, unlike petroleum-based synthetics.

**35. (C)** Polyester, nylon and acrylic are man-made synthetic fibres.

**36. (B)** Most synthetic fibres are made from petroleum-based chemicals (petrochemicals).

**37. (B)** Synthetic fibres are non-biodegradable and can persist in the environment for more than 200 years.

**38. (B)** Synthetic clothes shed microplastics — tiny plastic fragments under 5 mm — in every wash.

**39. (B)** Cotton is breathable and absorbs sweat, so a cotton shirt feels cooler than a polyester one.

**40. (B)** Wool fibres trap a layer of still air, and trapped air is a poor conductor of heat, keeping body warmth in.

**41. (B)** Protein fibres (wool, silk) burn with a "burning hair" smell and leave a brittle bead; cellulose fibres do not.

**42. (C)** Cotton fibre grows in the seed pods (bolls) of the cotton plant.

**43. (B)** Jute and flax fibres are obtained from the stems of their plants.

**44. (A)** Linen is woven from the fibre of the flax plant.

**45. (C)** Animal fibres are made of protein (keratin/fibroin); plant fibres are made of cellulose.

**46. (A)** Angora wool comes from the Angora rabbit (and Angora goat).

**47. (A)** The yak provides wool in the cold high regions of Tibet and Ladakh.

**48. (B)** The natural grease in raw wool is washed out during scouring.

**49. (B)** Spinning twists loose fibres together so they hold firmly as a single thread (yarn).

**50. (A)** Silk reeling must be gentle because the thread is one long continuous filament that snaps if handled roughly.

**51. (A)** The six steps are shearing → scouring → sorting → removing burrs → dyeing → spinning.

**52. (B)** Silk is an animal fibre spun by the silkworm caterpillar, not a plant fibre.

**53. (B)** Sheep are selectively bred to grow soft, fine and long wool fibres.

**54. (B)** Synthetic fibres are hydrophobic (water-repelling), hold little water, and so dry quickly.

**55. (B)** Masks protect sorters from inhaling anthrax spores and fibre dust in raw wool.

**56. (B)** The defining difference is the source: natural fibres come from plants or animals, while synthetic fibres are man-made from chemicals.

**57. (B)** After the cocoon stage the silkworm becomes a pupa, which then develops into the adult moth.

**58. (B)** A polyester-cotton blend combines polyester's strength and easy care with cotton's comfort and breathability.

**59. (C)** Polyester is a synthetic fibre that sheds non-biodegradable microplastics, so it is the worst river polluter of the four.

**60. (A)** "Fibre to fabric" traces the journey: raw fibre source → cleaned/processed fibre → spun yarn → woven fabric.
