Instructions: Each question has four options (A), (B), (C), (D); exactly one is correct. This hardest paper layers several traps at once: a single clue (heat, light, colour, gas, irreversibility, dissolving) is never decisive; mass of the solid product is not the same as conservation of total mass; one event can be physical and chemical together; controlled rust set-ups isolate exactly one factor, so read which factor each pair actually changes; galvanising's sacrificial action is chemical protection, not mere sealing; crystallisation, dissolving, magnetising and state-changes stay physical no matter how dramatic; and counting 'clues' or classifying lists requires checking every member, not the first. 1. (B) Warmth alone is the weakest of the four clues, because dissolving an anhydrous salt or other purely physical events can also warm a beaker without forming any new substance. A colour change here from yellow to orange and an insoluble solid settling from a clear liquid (a precipitate) are genuine signs that something new appeared. Inability to recover the starting liquid by a physical step is in fact strong evidence that a new substance formed, the opposite of leaving doubt. So the temperature rise is the observation that, on its own, decides nothing.

2. (D) Burning magnesium combines the metal with oxygen from the air, so the solid oxide is heavier than the metal by the mass of oxygen added; the 2.6 g increase is that captured oxygen, not new matter conjured from energy. Matter is never created, so the gain cannot be invented mass and is not a mere weighing error. The escaping white smoke is oxide carried away in the air, so it was not destroyed but simply lost from the tray; magnesium itself is not vanishing into nothing. Hence the gain is added oxygen and the smoke is displaced oxide.

3. (B) Melting wax, ice subliming, and drawing copper thinner are all physical: the substance keeps its identity and only its state or shape changes. Wax vapour burning forms new substances such as carbon dioxide and water, and milk curdling produces new substances responsible for the sour taste and solid curd; both are chemical. That makes exactly two chemical changes. Subliming is a state change, not chemical, and curdling genuinely is chemical, so neither one nor three nor four is correct.

4. (C) To isolate the effect of air while keeping water constant, the two tubes compared must both have water and differ only in whether air can reach it. T1 (boiled water under oil) has water but its air is excluded, and T2 (tap water open) has both water and air; their only difference is the air, so this pair isolates air. Comparing T2 with T4 changes two things at once (both water and air), so it cannot isolate air alone. T1 versus T4 compares two non-rusting tubes and differs in water as well, and T3 versus T4 both keep a drying agent so neither truly holds water. Only T1 versus T2 changes air alone.

5. (C) Galvanising works because zinc is more reactive than iron, so even where the coating is broken the zinc keeps corroding in preference to the iron and protects the exposed steel. Tin is less reactive than iron, so at a scratch the iron is the one that corrodes, and the local set-up can make that rusting worse than for bare steel. The coatings therefore do not behave identically, neither turns steel into stainless steel, and the difference is about which metal is more reactive, not about which surface holds more water.

6. (C) Crystallisation and evaporation both leave the very same alum, so all three portions undergo physical changes regardless of how fast or by what means the water is removed. Dissolved alum and solid alum are the same substance in different states, not different substances, and the rate of cooling or the sun's heat does not create anything new. The genuine difference is only in crystal quality: slow cooling grows big pure crystals, fast cooling gives many small ones, and open sun-drying can trap impurities. Hence all three are physical.

7. (D) The milky stage is a genuine chemical change: carbon dioxide in the breath reacts with limewater to make insoluble calcium carbonate, the white cloudiness. Continuing to add excess carbon dioxide does not undo chemistry; it converts that solid into a soluble substance, which is why the liquid clears, so the clearing is also chemical, not just bubbles rising or a solid evaporating away. The student is wrong because both the clouding and the clearing involve new substances forming, not a purely physical mist.

8. (C) Iron is more reactive than copper, so it pushes copper out of the solution: copper metal deposits on the nail, making it reddish-brown and heavier, and the iron that goes into solution forms iron sulphate, which is pale green. New substances form, so the change is chemical, not physical mixing. The brown coat is copper, not rust; the nail gains rather than loses mass; and dissolving like sugar would leave the solution unchanged, which is not what happens. So the displacement description is the correct one.

9. (C) Recovering the original by a purely physical method shows that no new substance was ever formed, so R is a reliable test. Q is false: breaking a clay pot cannot be undone by simple means yet makes no new substance, so it is physical despite being irreversible. P often holds but is not airtight as a rule, since reversibility is only a rough guide and is not the defining feature. Therefore only R can be trusted, and claims that lean on irreversibility or treat reversibility as decisive in both directions are wrong.

10. (C) To isolate moisture while keeping air, Aria must compare two open-air nails that differ only in being dry or moist, which her dry-air-versus-moist-air pair does. (D) The recognised clues of a chemical change include heat being given out, a gas evolving, a new smell, a precipitate forming, a colour change, and the original being unrecoverable by a physical method. All six listed observations match clues on that list, so all six count. It would be wrong to discard warmth or the recovery failure, both of which are listed clues, and a colour change is itself one of the standard clues. Hence the count is six, with the caution that any single clue alone is not decisive.

The grey-black surface scale, however, is iron that has combined with oxygen during heating to form a new oxide, which is a chemical change at the surface even though the bulk stays iron. So two events are physical and one is chemical. Treating the glow or the hammering as chemical is wrong, and dismissing the scale as mere flaking ignores the new substance formed.

(D) Dissolving lets the salt and the soluble impurity go into solution while the insoluble clay can be filtered out; slow crystallisation then grows pure salt crystals, leaving the soluble coloured impurity behind in the remaining solution, which is the principle that makes crystallisation purify. Strong heating does not selectively burn clay or a coloured impurity away, hand-picking cannot remove a soluble impurity, and skipping the filtration leaves the clay mixed in while a dissolved impurity does not leave as a gas. 60. (A) Rust is iron joined with oxygen and water, so the iron that converts to rust picks up the mass of that oxygen and water; that is why 12 g of iron lost from the sheet ends up as more than 12 g of flakes, here 21 g. The extra is added oxygen and water, not trapped air bubbles and not matter made from nothing, and the steady thinning of the solid sheet is normal rusting rather than a sign of impurity. So the flakes outweigh the iron consumed because rust is heavier than the iron it formed from.
