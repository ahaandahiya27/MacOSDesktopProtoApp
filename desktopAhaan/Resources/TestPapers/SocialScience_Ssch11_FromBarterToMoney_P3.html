Worked Solutions

1. (D) Barter is defined by goods or services being swapped directly for one another with no money involved at all. The location of the exchange, the relative weight of the items, and the occupations of the traders are irrelevant; an identical fair purchase paid for with rupees would not be barter. The only decisive fact is the complete absence of money from the transaction.

2. (A) Barter requires goods-for-goods with money entirely absent, which fits only the book-for-pen swap. Brij's deal uses a note, so money is present; receiving change does not turn it into barter. Cyrus's QR payment simply moves rupees electronically, so money is still involved. That all three end with a pen is irrelevant to whether money was used.

3. (A) The defining test of barter is that no money is involved; the shopkeeper's goods are priced in rupees and paid for in money, even if payment is delayed, so they are money transactions. Barter is not restricted by location or by the number of people, and deferred payment is a normal, lawful money arrangement rather than something illegal.

4. (B) Barter only works when each trader wants exactly what the other offers at the same time, which is the double coincidence of wants; finding such a perfect match is the core difficulty here. Inflation concerns rising prices in a money economy, durability is an advantage rather than an obstacle, and a coin shortage is irrelevant because no money is being used in barter.

5. (D) Money is accepted by all, so the fisherman converts fish into money with any buyer and then uses that money to buy sandals, breaking the requirement that the sandal-maker personally wants fish. Money does not change anyone's tastes, and while it sidesteps spoilage and divisibility issues, the precise problem here is the missing match of wants, which universal acceptability solves.

6. (A) A double-coincidence failure is precisely when each party wants something the other does not have to offer, as with the stool-for-medicine mismatch. Uncertainty over how many sacks equal a goat is a lack of a common measure of value, rotting tomatoes is a storage/durability problem, and an indivisible cow is an indivisibility problem; each is a different barter defect.

7. (B) Without an agreed yardstick of value, traders cannot settle how much one good is worth in terms of another, which is exactly the dispute here. Both herders do want each other's goods, so the wants coincide; the sheep's perishability and the carrying of dates are not what the argument is about, so portability and durability are not the issue.

8. (C) A common measure lets each item carry a single price in one unit, so any two goods can be compared at a glance instead of negotiating a separate ratio for every pair. It does not equalise weights, change what people want, or preserve perishables; those describe unrelated barter problems.

9. (B) This is an indivisibility problem: a living cow cannot be cut into a fraction equal to three sacks, so small trades fail. There is clearly demand for rice, inflation is a money-economy idea not relevant to a single swap, and the weight of rice is not what blocks the deal.

10. (D) Value stored in perishable guavas is lost as they spoil, so they fail as a store of value, unlike durable money. Easy divisibility would be an advantage rather than a weakness, the double-coincidence problem concerns matching wants rather than spoilage, and perishable fruit is certainly not legal tender.

11. (D) Money holds value reliably over time because it neither perishes nor needs feeding, and it can be stored and split as required. It cannot be eaten, does not grow by itself merely from being kept, and performs no farm labour; those are misplaced expectations imported from thinking of cattle as wealth.

12. (A) Commodity money is everyday goods that double as a medium of exchange, which is exactly what grain, cattle, salt, and cowries were. Fiat money has value by law and trust rather than usefulness, digital money is electronic, and central-bank-issued legal tender is modern currency; none fits these tangible early goods.

13. (A) Historically people bartered first, and money emerged afterwards precisely to overcome barter's difficulties such as the double coincidence of wants and the lack of a common measure. Money did not precede barter, the two did not begin together, and barter's real problems are exactly why money was needed.

14. (B) Money arrived after an earlier era of barter, as a remedy for problems like mismatched wants; it was not present from the start. Digital payment is the most recent stage, coins cannot predate their makers, and paper currency is later still, so none of those alternatives is correct.

15. (A) A modern note's value rests on shared trust and the backing of the Reserve Bank of India, not on the material it is printed on. The paper is ordinary, the ink is not worth the note's face value, and colour is merely a design and security feature, so none of those gives the note its worth.

16. (B) Banknote paper is inexpensive, so value cannot come from the material; it comes from trust and the central bank standing behind it. The paper is not gold, the note's accepted value far exceeds any scrap-paper price, and weight has nothing to do with what it can buy.

17. (C) The promise and the Governor's signature show that the Reserve Bank guarantees the note, and this institutional backing is what underpins public trust in it. It does not entitle the holder to gold at a shop, does not create a debt the bearer owes, and the paper holds no hidden treasure.

18. (D) Fiat money is valuable through legal-tender status and trust rather than through any precious substance, which is exactly how the modern note works. The note holds no silver, cannot be melted for metal, and its worth is not the printing cost; those describe commodity-money thinking that does not apply.

19. (A) Using money right now to obtain goods is money acting as a medium of exchange. Storing value refers to keeping wealth for later, deferred payment refers to settling debts in the future, and a measure of value is about pricing rather than the act of paying; here the money is actively changing hands for goods.

20. (B) Holding money to preserve purchasing power for the future is the store-of-value function. It is not being used to buy anything now (medium of exchange), is not pricing goods (measure of value), and no future debt is being settled (deferred payment); it is simply being kept.

21. (C) Expressing the worth of different goods in one unit so they can be compared is the common-measure-of-value function. No payment is being made (medium of exchange), no wealth is being kept for later (store of value), and no future repayment is set (deferred payment); the prices are simply being stated for comparison.

22. (D) Fixing a sum to be paid at a later date for goods already supplied is money serving as a standard of deferred payment. Payment is not immediate, no wealth is being stored away, and the function is not merely about comparing prices; it is about settling a future obligation in money.

23. (D) Buying now is the medium of exchange, saving for the future is the store of value, and pricing goods for comparison is the common measure of value, matching the order given. The other lists scramble these roles, attaching the wrong function to each described action.

24. (C) A medium of exchange is money actually changing hands to complete a purchase; merely writing a price for comparison is the measure-of-value function, not exchange. Paying a vendor with cash, tapping a card, and scanning a QR code to settle a fare all transfer money to obtain goods or services, so each is a medium of exchange.

25. (A) Coins solve barter's woes at once: they are easy to carry, can be split for small or large deals, last a long time, and everyone accepts them, so the herder is no longer tied to finding someone who wants a goat. Coins do not multiply like animals, need no feeding, and certainly do not change what buyers want.

26. (C) Stamping marks acted as a guarantee of the coin's metal content and genuineness, helping people accept it readily in exchange. The marks did not add weight or value, were not merely decorative, and stamping metal certainly does not convert it into paper money.

27. (B) Minting gives each coin a known, uniform weight and value, so trades are settled by simple counting and confidence rather than weighing every time. The metal itself need not be more valuable, coins are not food, and minting actually provides a common measure rather than removing the need for one.

28. (D) Mixing metals into an alloy gives a coin the toughness to withstand long use, supporting money's durability. An alloy does not make the coin worth solid gold, coins are not edible, and coins are struck from metal rather than printed like notes.

29. (B) The obverse is the principal face, traditionally the 'heads' side, which is where the main emblem appears. The reverse or 'tails' is the other face, and the edge and rim are the coin's sides, not the emblem-bearing face.

30. (A) Legal tender is money that the law requires to be accepted in settlement of payments inside the country. It cannot be lawfully refused for that purpose, it is the home country's money rather than foreign-only, and its legal-tender status has nothing to do with gold content.

31. (A) Paper notes let people carry large sums easily, a big gain in portability over bulky coins. Paper is far less precious than coin metal, notes can certainly be lost or torn, and the value rests on trust and backing rather than any costliness of the paper.

34. (D) A cheque directs the bank to transfer rupees from the payer's account to the payee's, whereas cash passes notes directly; both move the same rupees by different methods. The cheque is not a new currency, not barter, and does not change the value of the money being paid.

35. (D) A debit card spends the holder's own deposited money immediately, whereas a credit card lets them spend borrowed money repaid afterwards. Neither card creates new rupees, neither is barter, and the difference is about own-money versus borrowed-money, not domestic versus foreign use.

36. (C) UPI moves existing rupees electronically from the payer's account to the shop's, so it is a payment method, not new money. It creates no new currency, is not barter because money is plainly involved, and no coins are minted; the phone merely instructs the transfer.

37. (D) A UPI payment transfers pre-existing rupees between accounts rather than creating any new money. No notes are printed, no gold is involved, and it is not barter; the same money simply changes hands electronically.

38. (C) Cash and digital payments both move the identical rupees; the only difference is physical notes versus electronic account transfers. Digital payment is not barter, uses the same rupee, and neither method creates or destroys money - they only move it.

39. (B) These tools transfer the same rupees electronically, so they change how we pay, not what the money is. They do not introduce a separate currency, do not convert rupees into metal, and do not revive barter, since money is still firmly in use.

40. (A) A bank safeguards deposits, may pay interest, and lends out a portion to borrowers while letting depositors withdraw as required; this is its core role. It does not barter the money for goods, melt notes into metal, or print new currency - only the Reserve Bank issues currency.

41. (C) Banks underpin safe saving and account-to-account digital payments, so losing them would hit precisely those activities. Farming, walking about, and speaking a language do not depend on banks, so they would be unaffected; the link is specifically to handling and moving money.

43. (B) An account balance can be transferred instantly by cheque, card, or UPI, which is what makes electronic payment convenient. 44. (C) People first bartered, then used commodity money like grain and cowries, then minted metal coins, then printed paper currency, and most recently adopted digital payments - the order in the correct option. The others jumble these stages, placing later forms before earlier ones.

45. (A) Commodity money is the stage between pure barter and minted metal coins, easing barter's problems while coins were still to come. It did not precede barter, did not come after paper currency, and certainly was not contemporary with digital payments, which are the most recent form.
