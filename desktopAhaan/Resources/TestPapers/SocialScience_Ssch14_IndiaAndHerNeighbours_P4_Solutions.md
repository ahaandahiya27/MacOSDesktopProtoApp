## Worked Solutions

**1. (B)** The chapter sorts neighbours by the kind of physical link: a land boundary makes a land neighbour, while a country reached only across the sea is a maritime neighbour. Size does not decide this, since both large and small countries appear in each group. North-versus-south is a direction, not the land-or-sea test. Shared language or currency is never the basis for neighbourliness in the chapter.

**2. (B)** Pakistan, Bhutan and Bangladesh all share land boundaries with India, while Sri Lanka lies across the sea and is a maritime neighbour, making it the odd one out. Pakistan has a land border, not a sea-only link. Bhutan is a landlocked Himalayan land neighbour, not a maritime island. Bangladesh shares a long land border, so the claim it has no border is false.

**3. (D)** The chapter treats maritime neighbours as fully genuine because the sea has carried trade, travel and culture between coasts for centuries. Saying only land joins countries ignores this sea-borne exchange. A sea link counts regardless of whether a country also has land neighbours. No bridge is needed for a maritime link to be real.

**4. (D)** Maritime neighbours exist because India's long coast faces the Indian Ocean, with lands like Sri Lanka and the Maldives across the water. India is not landlocked; it has an extensive coastline. It does not share land borders with islands. Many countries rim the Indian Ocean, which is exactly why India has maritime neighbours.

**5. (C)** Bangladesh shares a long land border to the east while the Maldives is reached across the sea to the south, so this pairing is correct. Nepal is landlocked and Sri Lanka is across the sea, so that pairing is reversed. China is a land neighbour across the Himalayas and Myanmar is a land neighbour, not an island. Bhutan is landlocked, not coastal, and Pakistan has a coastline, so it is not landlocked.

**6. (D)** Sri Lanka and the Maldives are both reached across the sea, so they are India's maritime neighbours. Nepal and Bhutan are landlocked Himalayan land neighbours. Pakistan and Afghanistan are western land-linked neighbours. Myanmar and Bangladesh are eastern land neighbours.

**7. (C)** Neighbourliness rests on a shared sea or border, not on size or exact distance, so the small Maldives is still a maritime neighbour. No bridge is needed for a maritime link. There is no rule that only large countries qualify. The Maldives has no land border and lies to the south, not the north, so it is maritime, not a northern land neighbour.

**8. (C)** Both are reached across the sea, but the Maldives is a chain of many small islands (a 'nation of islets') and Sri Lanka is one big island. The description that reverses these is wrong. Neither is landlocked or Himalayan. Neither shares any land border with India.

**9. (B)** Sri Lanka sits just off India's southern tip across a narrow strait, so it is the nearest land reached by sailing south. China lies to the north beyond the Himalayas, the opposite direction. Afghanistan is to the north-west by land. Bhutan is landlocked in the northern Himalayas, unreachable by sailing south.

**10. (B)** Pakistan lies to India's west and Afghanistan to the north-west, both historically land-linked. Sri Lanka and the Maldives are maritime neighbours to the south. Myanmar and Bangladesh lie to the east. Bhutan and Nepal lie along the northern Himalayas, not the west.

**11. (A)** Bangladesh lies to India's east, sharing a long land border and rivers that flow across the boundary. Afghanistan is to the north-west. The Maldives is a maritime neighbour to the south-west. Pakistan lies to the west. Only Bangladesh fits the eastern, river-sharing description.

**12. (D)** China is India's great northern neighbour across the Himalayas, and Myanmar lies on India's eastern land border as the gateway to South-East Asia. China is not to the south, nor Myanmar to the west. China is not to the west, nor Myanmar to the north-west. China is not to the east, nor Myanmar to the south.

**13. (D)** Afghanistan was tied to India by the old northern overland route through the north-west. The Maldives and Sri Lanka are reached across the sea. Indonesia lies far across the eastern seas, a maritime link, not an overland one.

**14. (C)** Moving clockwise from the north-west, Afghanistan sits north-west, China to the north, Myanmar to the east and Sri Lanka to the south. The other sequences swap these directions: China is not north-west, Myanmar is not north-west, and Sri Lanka is not north-west. Only the first ordering matches each country's real position.

**15. (A)** Afghanistan's ties ran along old overland routes to the north-west, whereas the sea-girt Maldives links by sea to the south. The reversed statement is wrong. Neither pair connects only through the northern Himalayas. They do not both connect by the southern ocean; only the Maldives does.

**16. (D)** An open border is a real, agreed boundary that people may cross without visas — open in access, not absent. Saying there is no boundary confuses 'open' with 'non-existent'. Nepal is a separate sovereign country, not part of India. An open border is the opposite of a sealed wall.

**17. (D)** An open border keeps the legal line between two nations while easing crossing, so the two stay separate sovereign states. Openness does not merge them. Borders exist by agreement, not only where walls stand. India and Nepal share a land border, not a maritime one.

**18. (A)** Both are land borders, but the India-China boundary is not the open, visa-free kind that India-Nepal is, and it runs through the high Himalayas. It is not visa-free like Nepal's. It is on land, not across the ocean. A mountain range marks a border rather than removing it.

**19. (A)** A boundary following a mountain range shows geography shaping borders. Not every border is coastal; many follow rivers or mountains. Mountains hinder but never wholly prevent contact, as old passes show. The example directly contradicts the idea that geography is irrelevant to borders.

**20. (B)** Both Pakistan and China have land boundaries with India. Neither is a sea-only neighbour. Pakistan in fact has a coastline, so 'both landlocked' is false. Neither shares the visa-free open arrangement that marks the India-Nepal border.

**21. (A)** The phrase points to deep common roots alongside a later political separation into distinct nations. It is not a claim of having nothing in common. They are not one country today. And they were closely connected historically, not unconnected.

**22. (C)** A landlocked country still depends on the sea for overseas trade and routes its goods through a neighbour's ports — for Nepal, mainly India's. Nepal has no coast of its own. Landlocked countries do trade abroad; they simply borrow a route to the sea. The far easier main gateway is Indian ports, not crossing the high Himalayas.

**23. (B)** Nepal and Bhutan are both landlocked Himalayan states without coastlines, relying on India to reach the sea. Neither is an island nation. Neither has a coastline, so they are not maritime neighbours. They are mountainous, not desert, lands.

**24. (D)** India's ports act as transit points so landlocked neighbours can ship goods overseas — India is their sea gateway. The coast does not give them beaches of their own. A neighbour's coast is far from useless; it provides the sea access landlocked states lack. A coastline cannot turn an inland country into islands.

**25. (D)** Steep mountain rivers give the falling water needed for hydroelectricity, which suits Bhutan's terrain. Being landlocked, it has no coastline for tidal power. It produces, not merely imports, power. Burning sea water is not a real source of electricity.

**26. (A)** Gross National Happiness counts wellbeing, environment, governance and culture beyond pure income. It is not a measure of military strength. Bhutan, being landlocked, has no islands to count. It is not a measure of skyscrapers either.

**27. (C)** A landlocked country has no coastline and depends on a neighbour for sea access, whereas a maritime neighbour is a coastal land reached over the sea. A landlocked country has no coastline at all, let alone a longer one. A maritime neighbour trades by sea by definition. Their sea access is plainly different, not identical.

**28. (D)** Nepal nestles in the Himalayas and is landlocked, with no coastline. Sri Lanka is a southern island with a coast. The Maldives is a low island chain in the sea. Bangladesh is a delta country with a coastline. Only Nepal fits the Himalayan, coastless description.

**29. (C)** Because a transboundary river serves both countries, sharing it sensibly calls for cooperation over its waters. Stopping the flow with dams would harm the downstream country and breed conflict. Ignoring the river does not make the shared dependence vanish. Refusing to talk worsens, rather than manages, the shared resource.

**30. (C)** A river crossing a border binds upstream and downstream neighbours, so joint management makes sense. Rivers do not halt at borders; that is why they are called transboundary. What one country does upstream affects the other downstream, so use is not independent. Shared rivers connect, rather than separate, water supplies.

**31. (A)** The Himalayan range stretches across several countries and is the source of major rivers feeding the region. It is not wholly within India; Nepal, Bhutan and others share it. It is a snow-fed mountain system, not a desert, and it gives birth to rivers rather than blocking them. It is a land feature, not a maritime one.

**32. (B)** Shared mountains and rivers mean the region's physical geography spills across borders, linking the countries. There is no single government over them; each is sovereign. They do not share one currency. The very fact that geography is shared shows borders do not fully separate it.

**33. (B)** Because rivers and ranges straddle borders, sensible management needs joint effort, encouraging cooperation. No single country can fully control a feature shared with others. Shared resources demand more discussion, not less. Geography has very practical effects, such as on water and trade.

**34. (A)** A river that flows across one or more borders on its way to the sea is transboundary. It is not a closed landlocked lake. A strait is a narrow sea channel, not a river. By definition it does not stay within one country.

**35. (B)** Common rivers tie neighbours' water security together, so cooperation is a practical necessity. The rivers are shared, not entirely each country's own. They cross borders rather than vanishing at them. Water is among the most important shared concerns, not unimportant.

**36. (A)** The echoes of Indian names reflect cultural influence carried peacefully by traders, travellers and teachers. There was no Indian conquest of Thailand. Thailand did not rule India. The pattern of names and epics is too consistent to be mere coincidence.

**37. (D)** Indian ideas, religion and art travelled to South-East Asia by peaceful exchange, not conquest. There were no large conquering Indian armies in these lands. The influence is ancient, not recent. Monuments and stories show clear traces, so denying any influence is false.

**38. (D)** Angkor Wat shows Indian religious and artistic ideas taking root and being adapted in South-East Asia. India did not rule Cambodia as a colony to build it. Far from having no contact, Cambodia absorbed strong Indian influence. The temple was built locally, not shipped from India.

**39. (C)** The Ramakien is a distinctly Thai version of the Ramayana, adapted to local culture. It is not an unchanged copy. It is an epic, not a trade route. It is a story, not a Buddhist school.

**40. (D)** Borobudur, a vast Buddhist monument reflecting Indian influence, stands in Indonesia. It is not in Bhutan, Afghanistan or the Maldives. Its location across the eastern seas shows Indian culture spreading by maritime contact.

**41. (C)** Overland routes carried Indian influence north-west to Afghanistan, while sea routes carried it to island Indonesia. Neither spread by conquest. The directions are not reversed — Afghanistan is overland, Indonesia maritime. Both clearly received Indian influence, so neither was isolated.

**42. (A)** An Indian name for the region plus shared temple styles shows long-standing maritime exchange and knowledge of South-East Asia. Far from unknown, the area had an Indian name. The temples reflect influence, not colonial conquest. A place-name does not amount to ownership.

**43. (D)** Buddhism arose in India and then spread outward, so India was a source of ideas for others. It did not import Buddhism from afar; the faith began there. Such spread shows openness, not sealing-off. Sharing culture so widely contradicts any unwillingness to share.

**44. (B)** Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana are the three great schools of Buddhism. Uttarapatha, Dakshinapatha and the Silk Route are trade routes, not schools. Kharif, Rabi and Zaid are cropping seasons. 'Sovereign, Secular and Socialist' are words from the Preamble, not Buddhist schools.

**45. (A)** The Uttarapatha was the great northern overland trade route running toward Central Asia via the north-west. It was overland in the north, not a southern sea route. It was a trade route, not a Buddhist school. It was a route, not a single peak.

**46. (B)** Uttarapatha was the northern route and Dakshinapatha the southern, both overland trade routes. They were routes, not Buddhist schools. They were not cropping seasons. They were land routes, not island neighbours.

**47. (A)** These routes were channels for goods, ideas and culture flowing between regions, knitting neighbours together. They opened India to exchange rather than sealing it off. They carried trade goods, not only soldiers. They are ancient, not modern, networks.

**48. (B)** Ancient trade routes prove India's neighbourly ties are centuries old. They are not a recent twenty-first-century creation. The routes carried trade and culture, not just armies. The links grew through trade and exchange, not conquest.

**49. (B)** A diaspora is people who have moved and settled away from their homeland, forming links between countries. A narrow sea channel is a strait, not a diaspora. A chain of islands is an archipelago. A school of Buddhism is a religious tradition, not a community abroad.

**50. (C)** Iran and Oman lie to India's west and have long been linked by sea trade. They are not eastern Pacific islands. They are not landlocked Himalayan states. They are nowhere near the Arctic. They belong to India's wider western, sea-linked neighbourhood.

**51. (D)** SAARC exists to foster cooperation and joint progress among South Asian nations. It is not a tool of conquest. It aims to encourage, not end, regional trade and contact. It does not replace member governments; each stays sovereign.

**52. (B)** SAARC embodies regionalism, the idea of neighbours cooperating for common good. The members do not merge into one nation; they remain separate. The aim is more ties, not cutting them. It is cooperation among equals, not rule by force.

**53. (A)** Regional peace and security rest on cooperation with every neighbour, so even a small island state matters. Size does not erase a member's importance. Small countries contribute to trade, security and friendship. Small island states are full neighbours and can take part.

**54. (B)** 'Bilateral' means involving two sides, so it is an agreement between two countries. An agreement among all nations would be multilateral or global. An internal arrangement involves no neighbour, so it is not bilateral. A country cannot make an agreement with the sea.

**55. (C)** A shared river is best managed by agreements among the sharing countries, balancing each side's needs. One country deciding alone breeds conflict over a shared resource. Leaving it unmanaged risks disputes and waste. A distant continent has no stake or standing to decide a local river.

**56. (A)** Both claims are wrong: Sri Lanka shares no land border and lies across the sea to the south, not the north, and Nepal is a landlocked Himalayan land neighbour, not a maritime one reached by sea. So neither claim holds, and the options crediting one or both are false.

**57. (C)** Sri Lanka really is a neighbour (A true), but the reason is wrong because a maritime link also makes a neighbour, so no land border is required (R false). R does not correctly explain A since R is false. A is not false; Sri Lanka is a neighbour. Both being false is wrong because A is true.

**58. (C)** Nepal depends on the sea for overseas trade (A true), and the reason explains why: its goods reach the ocean via Indian ports (R true and explanatory). So R does explain A. R is true, not false. A is true, not false, since landlocked Nepal still needs sea access.

**59. (C)** Indian culture is indeed present in Thailand and Cambodia (A true), but the reason is wrong because it spread by peaceful exchange, not conquest (R false). R cannot explain A since R is false. A is not false; the influence is real. Both being false is wrong since A is true.

**60. (A)** All three are wrong: a country's small size does not stop it being a maritime neighbour; an open border keeps Nepal a separate sovereign nation rather than part of India; and Buddhism arose in India and spread outward to lands like Indonesia, not the reverse. Since every statement fails, none is correct, so 'one', 'two' and 'all three' are all wrong.
