## Worked Solutions

**1. (C)** Red litmus turns blue only in a base. Of the four, only sodium hydroxide is basic. Hydrochloric acid is acidic and leaves red litmus red; sugar water and common salt water are both neutral and leave red litmus unchanged. So just one strip turns blue. Salt water is neutral, not basic, so it does not count. Only one liquid is basic, so 'three' is far too many. And red litmus does change colour, but only when a base is present, so 'none' is wrong.

**2. (D)** Every result listed is simply the neutral result: no litmus change, colourless phenolphthalein, yellow turmeric. Many neutral liquids share this fingerprint, including sugar water and salt water, so the tests cannot single out distilled water. Distilled water, being neutral, leaves phenolphthalein colourless (not pink) and turmeric yellow (not red), so those two 'corrections' misstate how a neutral liquid behaves.

**3. (B)** Vinegar is acidic, so methyl orange is red. Sodium hydroxide is basic, so it is yellow. Sugar water is neutral; methyl orange is not red in a non-acid, so it shows its yellow (non-acid) colour. Thus red, yellow, yellow. Treating sugar water as acidic (red) is the trap, but neutral solutions are not acids. Calling vinegar yellow or sodium hydroxide red simply reverses the indicator's known directions.

**4. (B)** The pink vanishing marks roughly the neutral point, where the base is used up. Eight further drops of an acid add acid in excess, making the mixture acidic, so blue litmus turns red. The endpoint is not 'locked'; adding more acid overshoots it. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acids, so it stays colourless rather than returning to pink. And once enough acid is present, even lime water's mixture is acidic, so 'can never become acidic' is false.

**5. (C)** The salt of a neutralisation is built from the metal part of the base and the acid part of the acid, so it depends on the exact pair. Potassium hydroxide with nitric acid gives potassium nitrate; sodium hydroxide with hydrochloric acid gives sodium chloride. They are different salts, so 'common salt in both' is wrong. At exact neutralisation no base is left over, and matter is not destroyed, so a solid salt remains in each.

**6. (A)** You neutralise each sting with the opposite family. The wasp sting is basic, so a mild acid such as vinegar relieves it. The ant sting is acidic, so a mild base such as baking soda paste relieves it. Reversing them (option with baking soda for the wasp) treats each sting with its own family and makes things worse, and using one remedy for both ignores that the two stings are opposite in family.

**7. (C)** P turns red litmus blue, so P is a base; china rose is green in a base, so P turns it green. Q turns blue litmus red, so Q is acidic and would turn china rose magenta. R is neutral and leaves china rose at its own colour, neither green nor magenta. Hence only P gives green. Choosing Q confuses the acid colour (magenta) with the base colour (green), and R, being neutral, does not give the base's green.

**8. (A)** China rose is magenta in acids, so this solution is acidic. An acid leaves red litmus red (only a base turns it blue), leaves phenolphthalein colourless (it is pink only in bases), and tastes sour. The soapy feel and red-litmus-to-blue belong to bases, not acids, and phenolphthalein does not go pink in an acid.

**9. (D)** Blue litmus to red, colourless phenolphthalein, yellow turmeric and magenta china rose are all the acid responses, so they agree on acid. China rose changing to magenta is precisely the acid colour, not evidence of a base. Phenolphthalein colourless and turmeric yellow happen in both acids and neutrals, so they do not prove neutral when litmus has already shown acid. The four readings are mutually consistent, not contradictory.

**10. (C)** Red litmus turning blue shows the effluent is basic, so it must be neutralised with an acid. Slaked lime is itself a base and would make a basic effluent more basic, so it is the wrong choice here (it is the right one only for acidic waste). Adding more base never neutralises a base, and common salt is a neutral salt that does not neutralise anything.

**11. (D)** The fizz comes from a base reacting with an acid to release gas. Vinegar is the acid, so baking soda fizzes in it. Lime water is basic, so there is no acid present to drive the gas-releasing reaction, hence little fizz. Vinegar is acidic and lime water basic (not the reverse), baking soda is a base not an acid, and a real gas-releasing reaction differs from trapped air, which would behave the same in both.

**12. (B)** A carbonate fizzes when it meets an acid, releasing a gas. Of the three liquids only hydrochloric acid is acidic, so fizzing appears there alone. Sodium hydroxide is a base and sugar water is neutral; neither supplies the acid needed for the gas, so no fizz. Chalk is not unreactive, but it fizzes only with the acid, not with all three.

**13. (C)** Phenolphthalein turns pink only in bases. Milk of magnesia and washing soda solution are both basic, so two turn it pink. Soda water is acidic (carbonic acid) and sugar solution is neutral, so phenolphthalein stays colourless in those. Hence exactly two turn pink, not one, three or zero.

**14. (C)** At the stop point just enough acid has neutralised the base, so the mixture is neutral and neither litmus changes. The extra drops of acid push it into the acidic range, so blue litmus turns red. Mixing does not 'lock' the mixture, so a change after the extra drops is expected. The overshoot is acidic (blue-to-red), not basic, so 'red litmus turns blue' is wrong.

**15. (A)** China rose magenta means X is acidic; china rose green means Y is basic. Phenolphthalein is colourless in an acid and pink in a base, so X stays colourless and Y turns pink. Saying X is pink and Y colourless reverses the families, and the 'both' options ignore that the two cups are opposite in family.

**16. (A)** Slaked lime is a base. Adding a base to acidic soil neutralises the acidity and helps, but adding it to soil that is already basic only increases the basicity and harms the crop. Lime is not neutral, so it does not 'balance any soil'; it is a base, not an acid; and it is not an inert salt, since it actively neutralises acid.

**17. (A)** Red litmus turning blue shows ammonia is basic. In a base turmeric turns red, china rose turns green, and phenolphthalein turns pink. The acid responses (turmeric yellow, china rose magenta, phenolphthalein colourless) all belong to acids, so any option mixing those in is wrong for a base.

**18. (D)** The damage comes from acid in the rain attacking the marble. A dilute base neutralises that acidic residue, which is helpful. The base reacts with the leftover acid, not with the marble itself, so it does not speed the damage. Adding more acid would worsen the attack, and marble is basic (a carbonate), not acidic, so the last option is also wrong.

**19. (B)** Turmeric turns red only in a base, and a base also turns red litmus blue. So a genuine base cannot leave litmus unchanged; the two reported results contradict each other. A neutral substance leaves turmeric yellow, so turmeric-red rules out neutral. Turmeric red is a base sign, not an acid sign, and salt water is neutral and leaves turmeric yellow.

**20. (C)** Every neutralisation makes a salt and water, and the salt is built from the base's metal and the acid's acid part, so potassium hydroxide with sulphuric acid gives potassium sulphate. There is no sodium here, so it cannot be sodium chloride. The acid is consumed, not regenerated, and the products are salt and water, not hydrogen gas plus leftover base.

**21. (A)** Pink phenolphthalein means solution 1 is a base. Solution 2 has colourless phenolphthalein and turns blue litmus red, the acid signs, so it is an acid. Solution 3 shows no change anywhere, the neutral fingerprint. So base, acid, neutral. Swapping acid and base, or misreading the neutral case, gives the other options.

**22. (B)** An antacid works by neutralisation: the mild base reacts with the excess hydrochloric acid to give a salt and water, lowering the acidity and easing the burn. Adding a base does not make the stomach more acidic; it reduces the acid. The relief is chemical neutralisation, not a sugar coating, and certainly not the making of a stronger acid.

**23. (B)** Blue litmus to red means the liquid is acidic. For an acid: red litmus is unchanged, phenolphthalein stays colourless, turmeric stays yellow, and china rose turns magenta. The base responses (red litmus to blue, phenolphthalein pink, turmeric red, china rose green) do not apply to an acid, so any option mixing those in is wrong.

**24. (D)** The pink vanishing marks the neutral point; further lemon juice makes the mixture acidic. China rose is magenta in an acid, so it shows magenta. It does not stay green, because the mixture is no longer basic. China rose does change colour in acids and bases (its green response is for bases), so 'only works on neutral liquids' is false, and the magenta is caused by the present acidity, not by soap's original family.

**25. (C)** Curd is acidic (lactic acid). Washing soda solution and milk of magnesia are both bases. Common salt water is neutral. So acids = curd, bases = washing soda and milk of magnesia, neutral = salt water. Placing salt water as an acid, or calling washing soda an acid, or calling milk of magnesia an acid, all misassign a family.

**26. (B)** China rose is green only in a base. Of the four, only milk of magnesia is a base. Lemon juice and vinegar are acids (china rose would be magenta), and sugar water is neutral (china rose keeps its own colour). So milk of magnesia is the only fit.

**27. (A)** At exact neutral, no acid or base is in excess, so neither litmus changes: blue stays blue and red stays red. If blue litmus turned red the mixture would be acidic (overshot with acid); if red litmus turned blue it would be basic (overshot with base). Turmeric red is the base colour, not a neutral marker, so it would actually signal an excess of base.

**28. (B)** Carbonic acid makes the drink acidic. In an acid blue litmus turns red, china rose turns magenta, and turmeric stays yellow (it turns red only in bases). Options that put china rose green or turmeric red are using base responses, and 'blue litmus stays blue' contradicts the drink being acidic.

**29. (D)** Sourness is a property of acids, and a more intense sour taste simply means a more strongly acidic liquid; intensity does not switch the chemical family. So the juice remains an acid, not a base and not neutral. Mild and strong sour substances are both acids, so 'only mildly sour things are acids' is also wrong.

**30. (B)** Bubbling stopping with no litmus change means the acid and base have neutralised each other and the mixture is neutral. Three further drops of lemon juice add acid in excess, so the mixture becomes slightly acidic. It is not 'locked' at neutral, baking soda is not automatically the stronger one, and an acid certainly can change a neutral mixture by making it acidic.

**31. (A)** The salt formed depends on the specific acid and base, drawing the metal from the base and the acid part from the acid. Hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide happens to give sodium chloride (common salt), but, say, nitric acid with potassium hydroxide gives potassium nitrate. So 'always common salt' is wrong, while 'never common salt' is also wrong because that one pair does make it.

**32. (D)** Phenolphthalein is pink only in a base and colourless in everything else, so a colourless reading could be an acid or a neutral solution; a litmus test is needed to decide between them. It is not the same colour in acids and bases (it is pink in bases, colourless in acids), it does not turn red, and it does change in bases, so the other statements misdescribe it.

**33. (C)** Litmus, turmeric and china rose are all natural (from a lichen and from plants); phenolphthalein is the synthetic, lab-made one. So a valid natural-plus-synthetic pair is china rose with phenolphthalein. Turmeric is natural (not synthetic), litmus is natural (not synthetic), and phenolphthalein is synthetic (not natural), so the other pairings mislabel one member.

**34. (C)** An acidic pond is neutralised by adding a base, and slaked lime is the cheap, widely used base for this. Vinegar and lemon juice are acids, which would worsen the acidity, and adding more acidic runoff is exactly the wrong direction.

**35. (B)** Methyl orange is red only in an acid. X turns blue litmus red, so X is the acid, and it turns methyl orange red. Y is a base (red litmus to blue) and would give yellow; Z is neutral and gives the non-acid (yellow) colour. So only X turns methyl orange red, not Y or both.

**36. (D)** Soapy feel, turmeric red, phenolphthalein pink and china rose green are all base properties, and they agree, so the liquid is a base. Soapy feel is a base property, not an acid one. Four tests can agree, and here they do, so 'neutral because they disagree' is false. Tasting is unnecessary and unsafe; the indicators already settle the family.

**37. (D)** Phenolphthalein is colourless and turmeric yellow in both acids and neutral solutions, so together they only show the solution is not a base. To tell an acid from a neutral solution you must use litmus. Hence the student cannot conclude 'acid' from these two alone; nor do they prove 'base' or 'neutral' specifically.

**38. (D)** Bitter taste, soapy feel, red litmus to blue, methyl orange yellow and china rose green are all base properties, and they agree, so the liquid is a base. Methyl orange yellow is the base colour (red is the acid colour), so it does not point to acid. The agreement of many tests supports 'base', not 'neutral', and the results are consistent rather than conflicting.

**39. (C)** Turning blue litmus red and turning china rose magenta are both acid responses, so the substance that gives both must be acidic. Orange juice is the only acid of the three, so it turns blue litmus red and china rose magenta. Washing soda solution is a base, so blue litmus would stay blue and china rose would turn green. Sugar water is neutral, so blue litmus stays blue and china rose keeps its own colour. Only orange juice gives both acid results.

**40. (B)** Leaving both litmus papers unchanged is exactly the neutral result and sets water apart from acids (which redden blue litmus) and bases (which blue red litmus) in one test. Turning blue litmus red would make it an acid; turning red litmus blue would make it a base; and phenolphthalein pink would make it a base too, none of which is true for neutral water.

**41. (A)** At exact neutralisation the acid and base are fully consumed, producing a salt and water. Boiling off the water leaves that salt as a solid. No acid is left to dry out, and the base is fully reacted too, so neither leftover survives. Matter is not destroyed, so a solid salt does remain.

**42. (C)** Soda water contains carbonic acid and is therefore acidic, so calling it a base is the wrong pairing. Vinegar is correctly an acid, while lime water, milk of magnesia and washing soda are all correctly bases. Only the soda water entry is mislabelled.

**43. (D)** China rose turning magenta is an acid response, so it confirms acidity in agreement with the litmus result. Turmeric red and phenolphthalein pink are base responses, which would contradict an acid; and red litmus turning blue is also a base sign. So only the china rose magenta result properly backs up 'acid'.

**44. (D)** China rose is magenta in acids and green in bases, so cup P (magenta) is the acid and cup Q (green) is the base. Magenta marks an acid, not a base, so saying P is a base is wrong, and green marks a base, not an acid. China rose does distinguish acids from bases, so 'neither' is also wrong.

**45. (D)** Methyl orange is yellow only when the solution is no longer acidic, i.e. once a base is present in excess. So yellow means the acid has been neutralised and the mixture has moved into the basic range. Methyl orange is not specifically the 'neutral' colour; an acid does not turn into a base; and the indicator has not run out, it has simply changed colour as designed.

**46. (C)** Lime water and washing soda solution are bases (two). Curd and tamarind water are acids, and milk is roughly neutral when fresh. So exactly two are bases, not one, three or four.

**47. (A)** Sugar is neutral and does not neutralise an acid; relief from an acidic sting needs a base such as baking soda paste. Sugar is not a base, sweetness has nothing to do with neutralising acids, and the bee sting is acidic (not basic), so treating it as basic is doubly wrong.

**48. (A)** An antacid is a base that reacts with the excess stomach acid to form a salt and water, which is precisely acid + base giving salt + water. Dissolving sugar is just mixing, an apple browning is a different chemical change, and ice melting is only a change of state, so none of those is a neutralisation.

**49. (A)** Turmeric turns red only in a base, so the solution is basic. In a base blue litmus is unchanged (it reddens only in acid), phenolphthalein turns pink, and methyl orange turns yellow. The acid responses (blue litmus to red, phenolphthalein colourless, methyl orange red) do not apply to a base, so the options containing those are wrong.

**50. (B)** When both litmus papers stop changing, no excess acid or base remains, so the mixture is neutral. A base can be fully neutralised by enough acid, so 'never cancelled' is wrong. Adding acid up to but not beyond the endpoint gives neutral, not automatically acidic, and neutralising a base does not create a stronger base.

**51. (B)** Red litmus turns blue only in a base. Ammonia solution and washing soda solution both turn it blue, so two bases. Vinegar is an acid and sugar water is neutral; both leave red litmus red. Hence exactly two bases.

**52. (D)** No litmus change plus colourless phenolphthalein is simply the neutral fingerprint, which salt water and sugar water share with pure water, so the tests cannot prove the liquid is water specifically. Pure water does not redden blue litmus, it does leave litmus unchanged (so 'never water' is wrong), and phenolphthalein stays colourless in neutral water rather than turning pink.

**53. (D)** In neutralisation the acid and base react to produce both a salt and fresh water; this water is a genuine product of the reaction, not the solvent that was already present and not leftover lemon juice. Saying there is 'no water' contradicts the very equation acid + base gives salt + water.

**54. (A)** Red litmus turns blue only in a base, and of the four only soap solution is basic. Vinegar and lemon juice are acids, which leave red litmus red, and distilled water is neutral, which also leaves it unchanged. So soap solution is the only fit.

**55. (A)** Turmeric turns red only in a base, so the moment its paper reddens the mixture has become basic, meaning enough soap solution (a base) was added to overshoot neutral. Turmeric red is not the neutral marker (neutral leaves turmeric yellow), the mixture is no longer acidic, and adding a base does not make a stronger acid.

**56. (B)** China rose magenta means A is acidic; china rose green means B is basic. Turmeric stays yellow in an acid and turns red in a base, so A gives yellow and B gives red. Reversing them, or claiming both are the same colour, ignores that A and B are opposite families and that turmeric reddens only for the base.

**57. (C)** An over-acidic stomach is eased by a base, and milk of magnesia is a base that neutralises the excess acid. Lemon juice, however, is an acid and would not neutralise stomach acid; it would add to the acidity. So they are not both bases, not both acids, and the families are not the other way round (milk of magnesia is the base, lemon juice the acid).

**58. (C)** Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a base, and a base also turns red litmus blue. So red litmus staying red while phenolphthalein is pink is a contradiction. A neutral solution would leave phenolphthalein colourless, not pink, so the no-change litmus does not make it neutral here. Pink phenolphthalein is a base sign, not an acid sign, and salt water (neutral) would leave phenolphthalein colourless.

**59. (B)** Neutralisation releases heat, so the beaker warms as the acid and base react. The warmth is not from the acid evaporating (evaporation would cool, not heat), nor from any 'stronger acid' (neutralisation consumes the acid), nor from sunlight, since the warming tracks the reaction itself.

**60. (A)** Methyl orange is red in acids and yellow in bases, so R is acidic and S is basic. Phenolphthalein is colourless in an acid and pink in a base, so R stays colourless and S turns pink. Reversing them confuses the families, and the 'both' options ignore that R and S are opposite in family.
