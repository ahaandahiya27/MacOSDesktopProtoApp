# Solutions — Social Science (Class 7) · Paper 2 (Advanced)
## Chapter 5: The Rise of Empires

Each answer is keyed, then worked. The recurring move in this chapter is to
reason from *cause to effect*: what turns a kingdom into an empire, what holds
one together, and how Magadha grew into the Maurya empire. Watch the standard
traps — an *emperor* outranks ordinary kings; Ashoka chose *Prakrit in Brahmi*
so common people could read his edicts; and *Kauṭilya* (Chāṇakya) — not Ashoka —
wrote the *Arthaśhāstra* and the *saptānga* theory.

---

**1. (C)** An empire is a collection of smaller kingdoms or territories brought under one ruler's supreme power. Trap: a single village or a trade guild is far too small, and a republic has no single supreme ruler — the defining feature of an empire.

**2. (A)** "Empire" descends from the Latin *imperium*, "supreme power" or "command." The root makes the meaning concrete: an empire is the reach of one supreme authority over many lands — not a farm, a river crossing or a market.

**3. (D)** A kingdom is one realm; an empire is larger and binds *many* kingdoms under one supreme ruler. An empire need not be smaller, and both kingdoms and empires keep armies and collect taxes, so the "no army" and "never taxes" choices are false.

**4. (B)** A strong army plus an organised administration is one of the six binding features. The other choices describe the *absence* of binding features — no laws, no trade, no currency — which would pull an empire apart, not hold it together.

**5. (B)** A tributary (vassal) had submitted to an emperor and paid tribute to keep his own throne — local rule under an overlord. That is the opposite of ruling with no overlord; a travelling merchant or an edict-writer is a different role entirely.

**6. (D)** A *śhreni* was a powerful association or guild of traders, craftsmen or farmers — an economic body, not a crown, a coin or a war elephant. Guilds strengthened Magadha's economy and so its rise.

**7. (A)** Magadha rose to dominance out of the sixteen *mahājanapadas*, the large states of the period. The Harappan cities long predate this era; the Mughals and Guptas come much later.

**8. (C)** Magadha's edge was a *combination*: rich farmland, iron ore, control of trade routes, and active guilds. Distance from rivers, no army or no farmland would each be a *weakness*, so they cannot explain its rise.

**9. (A)** Mahāpadma Nanda founded the Nanda dynasty, which ruled Magadha just before the Mauryas overthrew it. The Guptas, Cholas and Mauryas are different dynasties on different timelines.

**10. (C)** Alexander of Macedonia reached the north-west of the subcontinent around 326 BCE. The CE dates and the later BCE dates are off by centuries — anchor him to the late 4th century BCE.

**11. (D)** In the north-west Alexander fought the hard-won battle of the Hydaspes against King Porus. Ashoka, Chandragupta and Bindusara are all Mauryan rulers who come *after* Alexander's incursion.

**12. (B)** A *satrap* was the governor of a province in the Persian and later Greek administrative systems — a high official, not a coin, a monk or a road. Chandragupta later cleared these satraps from the north-west.

**13. (D)** Chandragupta strengthened his new empire partly by defeating the Greek satraps left behind after Alexander. The Harappan cities were long gone; the Mughals and the British are centuries away.

**14. (B)** Chandragupta Maurya is remembered as the first emperor to unite most of the subcontinent. He was an Indian ruler, not a Greek general, a Vedic author or a Harappan king.

**15. (C)** His mentor and chief adviser was Kauṭilya, also called Chāṇakya. Ashoka was Chandragupta's grandson; Alexander and Porus were adversaries, not advisers.

**16. (A)** The *Arthaśhāstra* is literally a treatise on the science of governance and economics (*artha* = wealth/statecraft). It is not about cooking, astronomy or poetry.

**17. (C)** Kauṭilya's *saptānga* ("seven limbs") theory describes a state as made of seven essential parts — not two kingdoms, the four varnas or sixteen guilds. The number to remember is *seven*.

**18. (A)** The seven limbs are the king, the ministers, the territory & people, the forts, the treasury, the army & justice, and the allies. The other lists describe landforms, metals or farm steps — distractors built to look like a "seven-item" set.

**19. (B)** The saptānga warns that a weakness in *any one* of the seven limbs harms the whole state — like a body losing a limb. So it is false that only the king matters, that a treasury is dispensable or that allies are useless.

**20. (D)** Ashoka ruled roughly 268–232 BCE. The CE option and the wrong-century BCE options miss him; place him in the mid-3rd century BCE, after Chandragupta and Bindusara.

**21. (A)** The Kalinga war — with its appalling death toll — filled Ashoka with horror and turned him toward *dhamma*. The other choices are battles or events he was not part of, or belong to other periods.

**22. (D)** *Annexation* is adding a conquered territory directly into one's own empire. It is the opposite of giving land away or abandoning a city; minting coins is unrelated.

**23. (B)** Kalinga is remembered above all for its terrible cost in human lives, which Ashoka came to regret — the turning point of his reign. So it was neither bloodless nor a failed nor an unrecorded campaign.

**24. (C)** An *edict* is an official declaration issued by an authority such as a king. It is not a war elephant, a coin or a guild meeting.

**25. (B)** Ashoka had his edicts engraved on rocks and pillars across the empire so they would last and be seen widely — not on perishable paper or cloth, nor hidden underground.

**26. (C)** The edicts were composed mostly in Prakrit, the everyday spoken language. Latin and English are foreign; refined Sanskrit alone would have shut out ordinary readers — exactly what Ashoka wanted to avoid.

**27. (A)** Ashoka chose Prakrit precisely so that ordinary people could understand his messages. The point was the *opposite* of restricting them to priests or foreigners; rhyme was never the goal.

**28. (D)** The script was Brahmi, the ancestor of most modern Indian scripts. Roman, Arabic and Chinese scripts are unrelated to the Mauryan edicts.

**29. (D)** *Dhamma* (dharma) broadly means moral law and one's ethical duties — compassion, honesty, respect. It is not a tax, a fort or a war strategy.

**30. (A)** The *dharmachakra*, the wheel of dharma, sits at the centre of the Indian national flag — a direct link to the Mauryan heritage. It is not on the rupee's edge, the Constitution's cover alone or the Red Fort gate.

**31. (C)** India's national emblem is the lion capital Ashoka set up at Sarnath. Kalinga, Taxila and Ujjain are important places, but the lion capital is from Sarnath.

**32. (B)** The "capital" of a pillar (Latin *caput*, "head") is the carved top of the column. Don't confuse it with a country's main city or money invested in trade; it is certainly not the base.

**33. (C)** The four lions of the Ashokan capital appear today on Indian currency notes and official seals — not on cricket bats, Greek postage or Mughal coins.

**34. (B)** Mauryan Pāṭaliputra stands where modern Patna is today. Delhi, Mumbai and Chennai are unrelated to the Mauryan capital.

**35. (D)** *Emperor* comes from the Latin *imperator*, "commander" — fitting for the supreme military and political authority over an empire. Not a farmer, a merchant or a priest.

**36. (A)** A *dynasty* is a line of rulers from the same family passing power down the generations. It is not a coin, a guild or a province.

**37. (A)** The Nanda dynasty was overthrown and replaced by the Mauryan dynasty (Chandragupta, guided by Kauṭilya). Not the Guptas, the Greek satraps or the Cholas.

**38. (B)** A *bureaucracy* is the body of appointed officials who run an empire's day-to-day administration. It is broader than the royal family alone, and it is not the cavalry or a group of poets.

**39. (D)** A common currency made trade and tax collection smoother across a vast area — a genuine binding feature. It did not stop trade, serve as decoration or replace the army.

**40. (C)** A strong army could defend the borders and hold conquered territory together — its real political purpose, not mere parades, carrying edicts or authorship.

**41. (D)** Good roads and messengers let the centre govern and supply distant provinces — essential for holding an empire together. They were used by the state too, not only traders; they did not replace the treasury or make coins unnecessary.

**42. (C)** Emperors supported art and learning because it added to the empire's prestige and helped bind its culture together. It did not weaken the state, and the Arthaśhāstra encouraged rather than forbade such support.

**43. (A)** "Control over resources" meant command of things like iron, fertile farmland and the taxes they yielded — real economic power. Seashells, nothing of value and foreign languages miss the point.

**44. (B)** The *Arthaśhāstra* is best described as an ancient handbook for running a state — defence, administration, justice and welfare. It is not a collection of folk songs, a trade-route map or a record of Ashoka's edicts.

**45. (B)** Ashoka's edicts were remarkable because, perhaps for the first time, a king spoke directly to his people, explaining his ideals. The opposite choices — silence, secret code, banning writing — describe the reverse.

**46. (D)** Correct order: the sixteen mahājanapadas first, then the Nanda dynasty that ruled Magadha, and last the Mauryan empire under Chandragupta that overthrew the Nandas. That is sequence (ii) → (iii) → (i).

**47. (C)** Both the *Arthaśhāstra* and the *saptānga* theory are credited to Kauṭilya (Chāṇakya). Ashoka, Alexander and Mahāpadma Nanda wrote neither.

**48. (A)** Tributary kings typically paid the emperor in gold, grain or elephants — real wealth and military assets. "Nothing at all," seashells and edicts are distractors.

**49. (A)** Modern trade and industry associations are often compared to the ancient guilds (*śhrenis*). They are nothing like satraps (governors), edicts (declarations) or pillars.

**50. (D)** Even great empires decline because they are fragile: over-stretch, weak successors, revolts and money troubles all strain the binding features. There is no law against lasting, emperors do not dissolve them for fun, and they certainly do decline.

**51. (C)** Magadha and then the Mauryas ruled from the capital city of Pāṭaliputra. Taxila, Kalinga and Sarnath are important sites, but not the Mauryan capital.

**52. (B)** In *saptānga*, "anga" means a limb or part — hence "seven limbs" of the state. Not a coin, a river or the king alone.

**53. (C)** After Kalinga, Ashoka's *dhamma* policy emphasised compassion, non-violence and the moral welfare of his people — a deliberate turn away from further conquest. He did not abolish officials or ban trade.

**54. (D)** We can read Ashoka's messages today because scholars in the 1830s deciphered the Brahmi script. There was no printed translation; one cannot ask the Mauryas directly; and decipherment was scholarship, not random guessing.

**55. (B)** An emperor stands above other kings, ruling an empire of many territories. That is greater authority, not weakness; emperors command armies and rule far more than a single village.

**56. (A)** *Annexation* comes from the Latin *annectere*, "to bind to" — fitting, since annexation binds a new territory into the empire. Not "to give away," "to destroy" or "to forget."

**57. (B)** Magadha's iron and fertile farmland supplied better weapons and tools plus the food surplus to feed a large army and bureaucracy — a double advantage. They were valuable on their own, aided trade rather than preventing it, and did not make Magadha abandon farming.

**58. (A)** Chandragupta Maurya was the founder who united the subcontinent; Ashoka was the emperor transformed by the Kalinga war. The other pairings are simply false.

**59. (C)** The blue *dharmachakra* on the flag links modern India directly to Ashoka and the Mauryan heritage — not the Mughal court, the British Raj or the Harappan cities.

**60. (D)** The best summary: great size plus the six binding features — army, administration, law & currency, resources, art & learning, and communication. Merely having a king, having no government or owning a single fort does not make an empire.
