Five Curious People Who Figured Out How Plants Eat

A 400-year detective story across India, Belgium, England, the Netherlands, and the United States.

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Nobody woke up one morning and just knew that plants make food from sunlight. It took five careful scientists over four centuries to piece it together — each one adding one missing brick to the wall.

1. Jan Baptista van Helmont (1640s, Belgium)

Van Helmont planted a 2 kg willow sapling in 90 kg of dried soil. He watered it with rainwater for five years. The willow grew into a 76 kg tree. The soil weighed almost exactly the same as when he started.

Where had the extra 74 kg of tree come from? Van Helmont's guess was "the water". He was half right. The real answer — carbon dioxide from the air — would take another 130 years to discover.

💡 What he taught us

Trees don't eat the soil. The soil is mostly just where the roots park.

2. Joseph Priestley (1771, England)

Priestley put a mouse under a sealed glass jar. The mouse soon collapsed — the air had gone bad. Then he repeated it but added a small mint plant. The mouse survived.

Something the plant produced was restoring bad air. We now know that something is oxygen. Priestley had discovered photosynthesis by accident, even though the word didn't exist yet.

💡 What he taught us

Plants make the air we breathe. Without plants, there is no us.

3. Jan Ingenhousz (1779, Netherlands)

Ingenhousz noticed something Priestley had missed: plants only restored the air when there was sunlight. In the dark, plants behaved like animals — they used up oxygen instead of making it.

This was the missing brick. The chemistry needed light to work. Photosynthesis is powered by the Sun.

💡 What he taught us

Sunlight is the engine. No light = no food.

4. Jagdish Chandra Bose (around 1901, India)

An Indian physicist working in Calcutta built a machine called the crescograph that magnified the movement of a plant by 10,000 times. Through it he showed that plants react to light, touch, heat, cold, electric shocks — even music — exactly like animals do, just much more slowly.

For 200 years Europeans had been calling plants "passive" and "unfeeling". Bose proved them wrong, with engineering rather than philosophy. Modern plant biology is built on his shoulders.

💡 What he taught us

Plants are alive in every sense of the word. They sense, react, and respond.

5. Melvin Calvin (1948–1961, United States)

Calvin used radioactive carbon-14 tags in CO₂ and followed the carbon atom by atom as it moved through a chloroplast. After 12 years of patient lab work he had mapped the entire dark reaction of photosynthesis — every step, every molecule.

The full cycle still bears his name today: the Calvin Cycle. Nobel Prize, 1961.

💡 What he taught us

Inside the leaf, photosynthesis is a careful chemical assembly line, not a single magic step.

📝 Stretch your thinking

Each scientist added one brick. Which brick will the next generation add? (You might be the one to add it.)
