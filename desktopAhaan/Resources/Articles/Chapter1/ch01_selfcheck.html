Question 1 — The classic

What three things does a green plant need to do photosynthesis?

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek.

Answer: Sunlight, water (from roots), and carbon dioxide (from air). The fourth ingredient — chlorophyll — is the catalyst the plant brings to the reaction.

If you got this: you understand the inputs of photosynthesis.

If you missed it: revisit ch01_t01_c02 (Photosynthesis — The Food-Making Process).

Question 2 — The cross-check

Why aren't autotrophs and heterotrophs the same word for the same thing?

🧠 Answer first

Pause.

Answer: Autotrophs make their own food (green plants, some bacteria). Heterotrophs cannot — they eat other living things to survive (all animals, all fungi, most bacteria). The two words come from Greek: auto = self, hetero = other, troph = food. Self-feeders vs. other-feeders.

If you got this: you understand the categories of nutrition.

If you missed it: revisit ch01_t01_c01 (Autotrophs and Heterotrophs).

Question 3 — The trap

Cuscuta wraps around other plants and steals their food. Is Cuscuta an autotroph or a heterotroph?

🧠 Think

It's a plant. Plants are autotrophs. So Cuscuta is… right?

Answer: Cuscuta is a heterotroph, even though it's a plant. It has no chlorophyll — it can't make food. It survives entirely by stealing from its host. The category isn't decided by "what kingdom you belong to" — it's decided by "can you make your own food."

If you got this: you understand the categories are based on behaviour, not biology.

If you missed it: revisit ch01_t02_c01 (Parasitic plants — Cuscuta).

Question 4 — The chemistry

The pitcher plant lives in soil that's poor in which nutrient — which is why it has to trap insects?

🧠 Pause

It's not water. It's not sunlight (those are abundant). It's a specific element.

Answer: Nitrogen. Pitcher plants typically grow in waterlogged bogs where bacterial decomposition is slow, so nitrogen never makes it back into the soil in usable form. Insects are roughly 12% nitrogen by weight — a perfect mineral supplement.

If you got this: you understand why insectivorous plants evolved.

If you missed it: revisit ch01_t02_c02 (Insectivorous plants).

Question 5 — The partnership

Plants need nitrogen. The air is 78% nitrogen. Why can't plants just absorb nitrogen straight from the air?

🧠 Think hard

It's there. They want it. What's stopping them?

Answer: Atmospheric nitrogen is N₂ — two nitrogen atoms held together by a triple bond, which is one of the strongest bonds in nature. Plants don't have the chemistry to break that bond. They need help. Rhizobium bacteria, which live in nodules on the roots of legume plants (chickpea, gram, peas), can break the triple bond and convert N₂ to ammonia — the plant-usable form.

That's why farmers rotate crops with legumes: the legume's bacterial partners refill the soil's nitrogen for free, and the next crop benefits.

If you got this: you understand the nitrogen cycle and why partnerships matter in biology.

If you missed it: revisit ch01_t03_c01 (Why Plants Need Nitrogen).
