NCERT Exercise Q&A — Chapter 1

Eight common NCERT exercise questions, with a model answer, what the examiner is checking, and the one-line mistake to avoid.

≈ 14 min read · exam-prep

The questions below are the NCERT-style questions teachers usually ask from Chapter 1. Each one shows: (a) a model answer at the right length, (b) what the examiner is really checking, and (c) the trap that costs students marks.

Q1. Why do organisms need food?

Model answer: Living organisms need food for three reasons: (i) to grow and repair body tissues, (ii) to get energy for work and life processes, and (iii) to protect against diseases. Food provides the raw material and the energy needed for every life process. Without food, growth stops, repair stops, and eventually life stops.

📝 Examiner is checking

Three distinct reasons, not just “for energy.”

One-mark trap: Writing only “for energy.” That gets one mark out of three.

Q2. Distinguish between a parasite and a saprotroph.

Model answer:

  • Parasite — feeds on a living host. Causes harm to the host. Example: Cuscuta (amarbel) on a tree.
  • Saprotroph — feeds on dead and decaying organic matter. Does not harm a living host. Example: bread mould (Rhizopus) on stale bread.

Both are heterotrophs (cannot make their own food). The difference is whether the food source is alive or dead.

📝 Examiner is checking

Two columns: source of food (living vs. dead) AND an example for each.

One-mark trap: Mentioning only the definition without examples. Always give at least one example per category.

Q3. How would you test the presence of starch in leaves?

Model answer:

  1. Pluck a green leaf from a plant that has been kept in sunlight for several hours.
  2. Boil the leaf in water for a few minutes to soften it and break the cell membranes.
  3. Transfer the leaf to a test tube containing alcohol and heat the test tube in a water bath (do not put alcohol directly on flame — it catches fire). The alcohol removes the green chlorophyll, turning the leaf pale.
  4. Dip the de-coloured leaf in warm water for a few seconds to soften it again.
  5. Place the leaf on a tile and pour a few drops of iodine solution on it.
  6. Observe: if the leaf turns blue-black, starch is present.

📝 Examiner is checking

The safety step (alcohol in water bath, not on flame) and the colour change (blue-black, not just “dark”).

One-mark trap: Skipping the alcohol step and going straight to iodine. The chlorophyll would mask the colour change.

Q4. The leaves of a plant are like its food factory. Justify the statement.

Model answer: The leaf is called a food factory because it contains all the requirements for photosynthesis:

  • It has chlorophyll, the green pigment that traps sunlight.
  • It has tiny pores called stomata for taking in CO₂ from the air and releasing O₂.
  • It has a network of veins that bring water and minerals from the roots.
  • It is broad and flat so it can catch the maximum sunlight.

Inside the leaf, sunlight + CO₂ + water are combined to make glucose — the plant's food. Glucose is then stored as starch and distributed across the plant.

📝 Examiner is checking

Four features — chlorophyll, stomata, veins, shape — plus the output (glucose, starch).

One-mark trap: Writing only “leaves are green and make food.” That misses three of the four features.

Q5. Why do farmers add manure or fertiliser to their fields?

Model answer: Plants take up minerals (especially nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium) from the soil to grow. When the same crop is grown again and again on the same field, the soil's mineral stock keeps falling and the crop gives lower yields. Farmers add manure (decomposed organic matter, e.g. cow dung) or fertiliser (chemical compounds) to put those minerals back. This makes the soil fertile and allows the plant to make enough proteins, chlorophyll, and other essentials needed for healthy growth.

📝 Examiner is checking

That you connect “minerals lost” → “minerals restored” → “healthy growth.”

One-mark trap: Writing “fertilisers feed the plant.” Plants make their own food — fertilisers provide minerals, not food.

Q6. How do plants growing in marshy areas obtain nitrogen?

Model answer: Plants growing in marshy or boggy areas live in soil that is very low in nitrogen, because waterlogged conditions wash nitrogen-containing minerals out. Such plants — like the pitcher plant and the Venus flytrap — have evolved to be partially insectivorous. They trap insects, digest their proteins using enzymes, and absorb the released nitrogen. They still photosynthesise for their energy needs — only their nitrogen need is met by eating insects.

📝 Examiner is checking

The qualifier: insectivorous plants still photosynthesise. They only eat for nitrogen, not for energy.

One-mark trap: Writing that insectivorous plants “cannot make their own food.” They can — they have green leaves and chlorophyll.

Q7. What is the role of stomata in photosynthesis?

Model answer: Stomata are tiny pores, mostly on the under-surface of a leaf. Each pore is surrounded by two bean-shaped “guard cells” that open and close it. Their two roles in photosynthesis are:

  • They allow CO₂ from the air to enter the leaf — CO₂ is one of the two raw materials of photosynthesis.
  • They release the O₂ produced during photosynthesis back to the atmosphere.

Stomata also help in transpiration (water vapour leaving the leaf), but that is a side effect, not their main role here.

📝 Examiner is checking

Both gases (CO₂ in, O₂ out) plus a mention of guard cells.

One-mark trap: Mentioning only CO₂ entry. Stomata are two-way.

Q8. Why is the relationship between fungus and algae in a lichen called symbiotic?

Model answer: Symbiosis means “living together for mutual benefit.” In a lichen:

  • The fungus gives the body (a tough outer covering), absorbs water and minerals, and protects the alga from drying out.
  • The alga has chlorophyll and prepares food by photosynthesis, which it shares with the fungus.

Neither could survive alone on a bare rock, a tree bark, or in the Arctic. Together they form the hardy crust we call a lichen. Because each partner is helping the other, the relationship is symbiotic.

📝 Examiner is checking

That both partners benefit, with the specific job each one does.

One-mark trap: Calling the fungus a parasite. In lichen the fungus does not harm the alga — the relationship is mutual.

🎯 Exam strategy

For a five-mark question, aim for: one definition + two examples + one diagram/equation + one application. That structure earns more marks than a long paragraph of facts.
