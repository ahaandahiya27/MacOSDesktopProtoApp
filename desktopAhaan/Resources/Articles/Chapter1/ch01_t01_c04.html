Stomata, xylem, and the plumbing of a leaf

Stomata are tiny openings on leaves that take in CO₂; xylem is the tube system that brings water up from the roots.

≈ 6 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

A leaf needs two ingredients to make food — water and air (specifically CO₂). It gets water from below: the roots suck up water and pass it through long pipes called xylem, all the way up the stem to the leaf. What this concept means

Plants take in water from the soil through their roots. The water travels upward through specialised tubes called xylem vessels in the stem and reaches the leaves. Carbon dioxide enters through tiny pores called stomata, located mainly on the underside of the leaf. Each stoma is bordered by two specialised cells called guard cells that control its opening and closing. When stomata open, CO₂ enters and water vapour exits — a process called transpiration. Sunlight, the energy source, falls directly on the leaf surface.

Why it works this way

Photosynthesis happens in the leaf, but the leaf can't gather water and CO₂ on its own. So a plant evolved a delivery system: roots harvest water from soil, xylem ships it upstairs, and stomata pull air in. Each piece of the plumbing is essential — block any one and food production stops.

💡 Did you know?

Stephen Hales, an English clergyman, in 1727 was the first to measure transpiration — he wrapped a sunflower in a glass jar and weighed the water that escaped. He also figured out that plants 'sweat'. Modern engineers borrow from xylem design when building water-purification membranes; some labs cut a thin slice of pine xylem and use it as a low-cost water filter — bacteria can't squeeze through the tiny pores.

Real-world applications

  1. Wilting plant — A potted plant left without water wilts because xylem can't pull water up.
  2. Celery in food colouring — A celery stalk in red water turns its leaves pink in an hour — visible xylem flow.
  3. Cactus night stomata — Cacti open stomata only at night to lose less water — special CAM photosynthesis.
  4. Over-watered plants die — Roots need oxygen; water-logged soil suffocates them so even available water can't be drawn up.
  5. Misting houseplants — Misting a fern increases humidity, reducing water loss through stomata.
  6. Waxy coating on apple skin — Apples, grapes, and cabbage leaves have a waxy cuticle that reduces water loss — crucial for photosynthesis to work.
  7. Stomata closing at night — At night, plants close their stomata to conserve water, even though photosynthesis has stopped.
  8. Desert plant leaves are tiny — Cacti have spines not leaves to reduce surface area and water loss, yet they still photosynthesize in their green stems.
  9. Damaged leaf veins in disease — When a plant disease blocks xylem (water transport), that part of the leaf can't hydrate and wilts.
  10. Translucent leaves of aquatic plants — Water plants have thin, translucent leaves with no waxy cuticle, since water loss isn't a problem underwater.

🌍 In your kitchen right now

Look at the food in front of you. Every vegetable, every grain, every fruit grew from photosynthesis. The tomato absorbed sunlight and turned it into sugar. The rice grain is 100% stored glucose made from sun. Even the salt from the ocean came from ocean plants and algae that photosynthesised. Your entire meal is ancient sunlight, locked into food just hours or days ago.

🧪 Try this at home

Tear a leaf in half and place it where half is in sunlight and half is in a paper envelope (dark) for 24 hours. Then dip both halves in iodine (ask an adult). The sunny half will turn blue-black (starch); the dark half won't. This proves photosynthesis needs light.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: Why does a plant placed in a completely dark cupboard slowly turn yellow?

Answer: Without light, photosynthesis stops. The plant uses up existing chlorophyll for energy but can't make new chlorophyll without glucose from photosynthesis. The chlorophyll is broken down, revealing yellow pigments underneath.

🧠 Try another

Question: If you had a sealed glass jar with a plant and a mouse inside, and you left it in sunlight for two weeks, what would happen?

Answer: The plant would photosynthesize, making glucose (food for itself) and oxygen (for the mouse to breathe). The mouse would respire, using the oxygen and producing CO₂ (for the plant to absorb). Both would survive because the plant is a producer and the mouse is a consumer in a tiny, closed ecosystem.

🧠 One more

Question: Is a plant always an autotroph?

Answer: Most plants are autotrophs, but some parasitic plants (like mistletoe or dodder) steal glucose from host plants. They're still plants, but they've partially or fully become heterotrophs. It shows that nutrition depends on what an organism can actually do, not just what it is.
