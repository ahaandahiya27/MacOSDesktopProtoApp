The Iodine Test for Starch

Iodine turns blue-black on starch — the simplest proof that a leaf has been photosynthesising.

≈ 6 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Want proof a leaf made food today? Boil it in water, then alcohol, then dip in iodine. Wherever it made starch, it turns deep blue-black. Wherever it didn't (covered parts, white edges), it stays yellow. Iodine 'develops' invisible food into a visible pattern....

What this concept means

The iodine test is a standard demonstration of photosynthesis. A leaf is destarched 24–48 hours in the dark, then exposed to light with part covered. After several hours it is boiled in water, then alcohol (to extract chlorophyll), and dipped in dilute iodine. Light-exposed areas turn blue-black; covered areas stay yellow-brown.

Why it works this way

Glucose is invisible, but plants store the day's surplus as starch — an insoluble polymer accumulating in chloroplasts. Iodine specifically binds amylose, making the photosynthetic record visible.

💡 Did you know?

The iodine test is a quick way to spot a virally-infected variegated houseplant — if a fully-green leaf suddenly tests yellow, it might have a virus shutting down photosynthesis.

Real-world applications

  1. Classic school experiment — Boil → alcohol → iodine; the most-repeated photosynthesis demo in 7th-grade labs worldwide.
  2. Variegated croton test — White parts stay yellow; green parts go blue. Visible proof chlorophyll is needed.
  3. Pre/post-darkness test — Test a leaf today, keep the plant in dark 48 hrs, retest. The second test is yellow — starch was used up.
  4. Forensic crop analysis — Inspectors test rice for starch content to verify quality grades and detect adulteration.
  5. Detecting fake basmati — Iodine staining of cooked grains differentiates true basmati from blends.
  6. Starch in a banana — Bananas are full of starch made by photosynthesis and converted to starch for fruit development and ripening.
  7. Why algae turn blue with iodine — Algae in a pond exposed to sun have starch throughout their cells — iodine turns them dark blue-black.
  8. Starch as plant communication — Some plants detect day-length by the amount of starch they produce — using starch as an internal clock.
  9. Potato starch extraction — Mashed potatoes release starch into water — a simple kitchen proof of starch storage in tubers.
  10. Bread rising with yeast — Yeast eats the glucose in bread dough that came from wheat photosynthesis.

🌍 In your kitchen right now

Look at the food in front of you. Every vegetable, every grain, every fruit grew from photosynthesis. The tomato absorbed sunlight and turned it into sugar. The rice grain is 100% stored glucose made from sun. Even the salt from the ocean came from ocean plants and algae that photosynthesised. Your entire meal is ancient sunlight, locked into food just hours or days ago.

🧪 Try this at home

Tear a leaf in half and place it where half is in sunlight and half is in a paper envelope (dark) for 24 hours. Then dip both halves in iodine (ask an adult). The sunny half will turn blue-black (starch); the dark half won't. This proves photosynthesis needs light.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: Why does a plant placed in a completely dark cupboard slowly turn yellow?

Answer: Without light, photosynthesis stops. The plant uses up existing chlorophyll for energy but can't make new chlorophyll without glucose from photosynthesis. The chlorophyll is broken down, revealing yellow pigments underneath.

🧠 Try another

Question: If you had a sealed glass jar with a plant and a mouse inside, and you left it in sunlight for two weeks, what would happen?

Answer: The plant would photosynthesize, making glucose (food for itself) and oxygen (for the mouse to breathe). The mouse would respire, using the oxygen and producing CO₂ (for the plant to absorb). Both would survive because the plant is a producer and the mouse is a consumer in a tiny, closed ecosystem.

🧠 One more

Question: Is a plant always an autotroph?

Answer: Most plants are autotrophs, but some parasitic plants (like mistletoe or dodder) steal glucose from host plants. They're still plants, but they've partially or fully become heterotrophs. It shows that nutrition depends on what an organism can actually do, not just what it is.
