Plants Talk to Each Other — Chemical Communication

A deep dive into how plants adapt when food-making isn't an option.

Chapter 1 Topic 3 ~8 min read

Plants can't shout, but they do talk — through smell.

This is one of nature's most ingenious strategies: when photosynthesis becomes too expensive or impossible, plants evolve alternative routes to survival. In this article, we'll explore the mechanisms, the evolutionary reasoning, and the staggering diversity of how plants feed themselves when they abandon the solar panel approach.

What this concept means

When tissue is damaged by herbivores, plants synthesise and release volatile organic compounds (VOCs) such as methyl jasmonate and green-leaf volatiles. Neighbouring plants — even of different species — perceive these VOCs and pre-emptively activate defence pathways: increased toxin production, thickened cuticles, and recruitment of natural enemies of the herbivore. Below ground, plants signal through mycorrhizal fungal networks and root exudates.

Why it works this way

Communication doesn't require nerves or brains — it just requires a shared chemical language. Plants evolved this language because immobile organisms need allies to survive mobile predators.

The evolutionary calculus is clear: if building leaves costs energy, if photosynthesis requires optimal light and water, and if other organisms are already doing the work nearby, a plant that bypasses those costs gains a competitive edge. Over millions of years, multiple plant lineages independently discovered this strategy.

Did you know?

Plant volatile signalling is mediated by the jasmonate, salicylate, and ethylene phytohormone pathways.

Real-world applications

These concepts aren't confined to textbooks or rainforest nature documentaries. They affect your garden, your food supply, and the health of ecosystems worldwide:

  1. Caterpillar-attacked tomato: A caterpillar nibble triggers the tomato plant to release green-leaf volatiles. Neighbouring tomatoes smell them and start producing bitter alkaloids.
  2. Acacia and giraffes: Browsing giraffes trigger acacias to release ethylene, warning downwind acacias to load tannins into their leaves — giraffes move upwind for fresh food.
  3. Recruiting parasitic wasps: Corn under caterpillar attack emits a specific terpene that attracts parasitic wasps; wasps lay eggs inside the caterpillars, killing them.
  4. Mycorrhizal warning network: A spruce attacked by aphids signals through mycorrhizal hyphae; nearby spruces begin upregulating defence proteins underground.
  5. Weed-and-crop chatter: Wheat under aphid attack releases beta-farnesene, the aphid alarm pheromone — chasing aphids away from neighbouring wheat.
  6. Plant kin recognition: Sea rocket plants grown next to siblings produce fewer competing roots than when grown next to strangers — they recognise relatives.
  7. Volatile crop pesticide: Researchers spray methyl jasmonate on greenhouse tomatoes to pre-activate their defences — reducing pesticide need.
  8. Wounded grass smell: That fresh-cut grass smell? It's a volatile defence cocktail — grass under attack telling its neighbours.
  9. Forest mother trees: Old Douglas firs share carbon and warning chemicals through fungal networks with younger trees nearby.
  10. Indian cropping intercrop: Marigold planted between vegetable rows releases volatile compounds that confuse pest insects — folk wisdom backed by chemistry.
  11. Forest eavesdropping: Insects eating one tree trigger volatile release; neighbouring trees boost chemical defences before damage.
  12. Mycorrhizal network communication: Fungal threads connect trees underground; a mother tree can send carbon to her seedlings via hyphae.
  13. Salicylic acid signals: Wounded leaves produce salicylic acid, a chemical messenger that primes defences in unharmed leaves.
  14. Volatile thiophenes: Some plants emit smell-compounds that attract wasp predators of the insect eating them.
  15. Allelopathy and root signalling: Black walnut roots release juglone, chemically poisoning competing plants nearby.

In your garden / kitchen

Look for these concepts in everyday life. Notice any orange threads on hedges (Cuscuta)? Hear about roses wilting despite water (parasitic fungi)? Spot fuzzy mould on bread (saprophytes)? See lichen on rocks (symbiosis)? Every one is an example of the non-photosynthetic feeding strategies covered here.

Mini experiment

Collect a piece of bark or log from a shaded forest floor. Place it in a clear plastic box with moist soil. After one week, observe with a magnifying glass. You'll see fine white threads (fungal hyphae) and possibly small mushrooms—saprotrophs at work, decomposing the wood and recycling its nutrients.

Beyond the textbook

Researchers in Japan recorded plants emitting ultrasonic clicks when stressed — possibly an audible form of plant communication that other plants and insects can sense.

Check your understanding: What is the key difference between parasitic and saprophytic nutrition?

Parasitic nutrition involves feeding on a living host plant, which is harmed in the process (one organism benefits, the other is harmed). Saprophytic nutrition involves feeding on dead organic matter like fallen logs or decaying leaves (the saprophyte benefits, nothing is harmed because the matter is already dead). Both are non-photosynthetic strategies, but parasitism harms a living organism while saprophagy recycles the dead.

Check your understanding: Why might a plant evolve parasitic behaviour instead of keeping photosynthesis?

Photosynthesis requires energy to build chlorophyll, maintain leaves, and lift water through xylem. In nutrient-poor, shaded, or water-scarce environments, the energy cost of photosynthesis may exceed the benefit. A parasite that taps into a neighbour's hard work avoids these costs. Over time, if parasitic plants survive and reproduce better than struggling photosynthetic neighbours, parasitism becomes the evolutionary winner.

Check your understanding: Give an example of symbiosis you've seen or know about.

Lichens on rocks are a classic example: an alga photosynthesises and feeds both itself and the fungus; the fungus provides structure and water absorption, helping the alga survive in harsh conditions. Both organisms benefit—that's mutualism. Other examples: beans and nitrogen-fixing bacteria, mycorrhizal fungi and tree roots, bees and flowering plants, or even fish and sea anemones (clownfish hiding in anemone tentacles for protection while keeping parasites away).
