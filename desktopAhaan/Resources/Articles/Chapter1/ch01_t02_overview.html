Chapter 1: Nutrition in Plants Overview

Some plants lack chlorophyll or live in nutrient-poor places. They survive by parasitising other plants, trapping insects, feeding on dead matter, or partnering with other organisms.

In this topic, we'll explore the strategies plants use when photosynthesis isn't enough—or isn't an option at all. You'll encounter parasitic plants, insect-eating plants, nutrient-recycling fungi, and partnerships that feed entire ecosystems.

By the end, you'll understand not just the "what" of plant nutrition, but the "why" behind evolution's most ingenious solutions to survival.

What you'll learn in this topic

  • Parasitic plants survive by tapping into host plants without producing their own chlorophyll.
  • Insectivorous plants trap insects to obtain nitrogen in nutrient-poor soils.
  • Saprophytes decompose dead matter, recycling nutrients back into soil.
  • Symbiotic relationships allow plants to partner with fungi, algae, and bacteria for mutual benefit.
  • Understanding non-photosynthetic nutrition reveals evolution's solutions to impossible growing conditions.
  • These strategies shape ecosystems and affect human agriculture.

Why this topic matters

Understanding plant nutrition goes beyond the textbook. These concepts explain why your garden soil needs enrichment, why certain regions face crop famines, how forests regenerate themselves, and why Indigenous farming practices (crop rotation, mixed planting) are scientifically sound. Agriculture feeds 8 billion people—and every bit of knowledge about soil, nutrients, and plant partnerships makes a difference.

This topic is also a bridge: it connects photosynthesis (Topic 1) to the broader food web, and it reveals the hidden partnerships that make all of life possible.
