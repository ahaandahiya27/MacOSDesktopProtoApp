Photosynthesis: the food-making process

Photosynthesis is how green plants combine water and CO₂ into glucose using sunlight, releasing oxygen as a by-product.

What this concept means

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants synthesise food in the presence of sunlight, using carbon dioxide from air and water from soil. The reaction occurs in chloroplasts, where the green pigment chlorophyll absorbs sunlight. The overall equation is: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (in presence of sunlight and chlorophyll). The product C₆H₁₂O₆ is glucose, which the plant uses for energy or stores as starch. Oxygen is released back to the atmosphere.

Why it works this way

Sunlight has energy; CO₂ and water are full of atoms but contain no energy. Chlorophyll is the molecule that captures light energy and forces those atoms to rearrange into glucose, which now stores that energy in chemical bonds. Plants can later 'spend' that energy by breaking glucose back down (respiration).

💡 Did you know?

Engineers are now copying photosynthesis in the lab. 'Artificial leaves' use special metal catalysts to split water with sunlight and make hydrogen fuel — a possible clean energy source. Scientists at IISc Bangalore and at Harvard are working on this. Joseph Priestley discovered in 1771 that plants 'restore' air spoiled by a candle — the first hint that plants make oxygen. He didn't yet know the reverse equation, but his single experiment with a mouse in a sealed jar with a mint plant changed how we think about air.

Real-world applications

  1. Hydrilla bubble experiment — Pondweed in sunlit water releases visible oxygen bubbles — exhaling photosynthesis output.
  2. Cool garden on a hot day — Leafy gardens cool the air partly because plants pull in CO₂ and release O₂ + water vapour.
  3. Forest air smell — That fresh forest smell is partly oxygen plus volatile compounds — photosynthesis output.
  4. Aquarium pearling — Healthy aquarium plants in bright light produce strings of bubbles — real-time photosynthesis.
  5. Iodine starch test — Decolorise a leaf, dip in iodine — areas that photosynthesised turn dark blue.
  6. School cafeteria rice and curry — Every grain of rice in your lunch was built from photosynthesis. The spices too — grown in sun by photosynthetic plants.
  7. Oxygen in your bedroom — The houseplant on your desk is releasing oxygen as it photosynthesises. It's cleaning your air while making food.
  8. Floating duckweed in a pond — Duckweed grows rapidly in sunlit water, photosynthesising so fast it can double in size every week.
  9. Grass after rain — After monsoon rains, grass grows explosively because water and sunlight — the two key photosynthesis inputs — are abundant.
  10. LED grow tents — Indoor farmers use red and blue LED lights that match the wavelengths chlorophyll absorbs best. Pure photosynthesis optimization.

🌍 In your kitchen right now

Look at the food in front of you. Every vegetable, every grain, every fruit grew from photosynthesis. The tomato absorbed sunlight and turned it into sugar. The rice grain is 100% stored glucose made from sun. Even the salt from the ocean came from ocean plants and algae that photosynthesised. Your entire meal is ancient sunlight, locked into food just hours or days ago.

🧪 Try this at home

Tear a leaf in half and place it where half is in sunlight and half is in a paper envelope (dark) for 24 hours. Then dip both halves in iodine (ask an adult). The sunny half will turn blue-black (starch); the dark half won't. This proves photosynthesis needs light.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: Why does a plant placed in a completely dark cupboard slowly turn yellow?

Answer: Without light, photosynthesis stops. The plant uses up existing chlorophyll for energy but can't make new chlorophyll without glucose from photosynthesis. The chlorophyll is broken down, revealing yellow pigments underneath.

🧠 Try another

Question: If you had a sealed glass jar with a plant and a mouse inside, and you left it in sunlight for two weeks, what would happen?

Answer: The plant would photosynthesize, making glucose (food for itself) and oxygen (for the mouse to breathe). The mouse would respire, using the oxygen and producing CO₂ (for the plant to absorb). Both would survive because the plant is a producer and the mouse is a consumer in a tiny, closed ecosystem.

🧠 One more

Question: Is a plant always an autotroph?

Answer: Most plants are autotrophs, but some parasitic plants (like mistletoe or dodder) steal glucose from host plants. They're still plants, but they've partially or fully become heterotrophs. It shows that nutrition depends on what an organism can actually do, not just what it is.
