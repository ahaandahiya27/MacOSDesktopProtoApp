Nutrition in Plants

How living green things feed themselves — and feed the world.

≈ 3 topics · 30 core concepts · for curious 12-year-olds

Every living thing on Earth needs food. But where does food come from? If you ask a farmer, they'll say it grows. If you ask a plant, it can't speak — but if it could, it might say: "I make it myself, from sunlight and air." That's the remarkable truth that opens this chapter. Green plants don't wait for food to arrive. They build it, atom by atom, using nothing but light, water, and carbon dioxide. This chapter explores how.

For 3.5 billion years, plants have been the foundation of nearly every food chain and ecosystem on Earth. They trap sunlight and convert it into chemical energy that you eat every day — whether you know it or not. Understanding plant nutrition is understanding life itself.

Why this chapter matters

Plant nutrition is not just a biology lesson — it's the story of how Earth became habitable. For billions of years, plants were the only organisms that could capture sunlight. They released oxygen into the atmosphere, making the air breathable for all other life. They still produce every oxygen molecule you breathe. They feed billions of humans, billions of livestock, and countless wild animals.

Climate change, food security, and the future of agriculture all hinge on understanding how plants feed themselves. When you finish this chapter, you'll look at a leaf differently — not as just a pretty green thing, but as a sunlight-powered factory that feeds the world.

The history of discovering photosynthesis

Scientists didn't always know that plants make their own food. Here's how we learned:

  • 1648 Jan Baptist van Helmont

    A Flemish alchemist grows a willow tree in a barrel, weighing the soil before and after. The tree gains 80 kg but the soil loses almost nothing. He concludes: plants don't get their mass from the soil. Radical idea. The soil is just for anchoring roots.

  • 1772 Joseph Priestley

    An English clergyman and chemist finds that a candle snuffed out in a sealed bottle will relight if a mint plant is added. Plants "restore" air spoiled by combustion. He doesn't yet know what's happening, but he's discovered oxygen production.

  • 1779 Jan Ingen-Housz

    A Dutch physician repeats Priestley's experiment but adds sunlight. He finds: only in daylight do plants restore air, and only in the green parts of the plant. Sunlight is essential. Conclusion: light must drive the food-making process.

  • 1804 Nicolas-Théodore de Saussure

    A Swiss chemist carefully measures gases. He discovers that both water and carbon dioxide are absorbed during photosynthesis. The carbon in a plant doesn't come only from the air — it comes from CO₂. The hydrogen and oxygen come from water.

  • 1882 Theodor Engelmann

    A German botanist shines a prism of light on bacteria clustered around a filament of algae. The bacteria bunch most densely where certain wavelengths hit. He's mapped which colours of light drive photosynthesis: red and blue.

  • 1937 Robin Hill

    An English biochemist isolates chloroplasts in a test tube and finds they release oxygen even without CO₂ present. Light reactions can run independently. This splits photosynthesis into two phases: light-dependent and light-independent.

  • 1957 Melvin Calvin

    An American biochemist uses radioactive carbon-14 as a tracer to follow CO₂ through the dark reactions (later named the Calvin cycle). He maps the exact pathway. In 1961, he wins the Nobel Prize. The chemistry that seemed impossible is now visible.
