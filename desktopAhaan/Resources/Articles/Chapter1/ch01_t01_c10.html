Photosynthesis Beyond Leaves — Green Stems & Algae

Anywhere there is chlorophyll there can be photosynthesis — including stems, algae, and even some bacteria.

≈ 6 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Most photosynthesis happens in leaves, but leaves aren't the only kitchen. A cactus has no leaves but its green stem cooks. Pond scum is millions of tiny algae running photosynthesis. Even some bacteria can do it. Wherever there is chlorophyll, there can be food-making....

What this concept means

While the leaf is the principal photosynthetic organ in flowering plants, photosynthesis can occur in any tissue containing chlorophyll. Green stems (cactus, sugarcane), unripe fruits, sepals, and even green roots of some orchids photosynthesise. Beyond flowering plants: green algae, red and brown algae, diatoms, and cyanobacteria all carry out the process.

Why it works this way

Photosynthesis isn't a property of 'leaves' or even 'plants' — it's a biochemical process that runs anywhere the right machinery is present.

💡 Did you know?

Researchers have engineered photosynthetic bacteria into yeast cells. They are exploring whether human cells could one day be augmented with chloroplasts — science fiction moving slowly toward reality.

Real-world applications

  1. Saguaro cactus stems — Saguaro has no leaves; its green ribbed stem is the photosynthetic organ.
  2. Spirulina supplement — That blue-green powder is a photosynthetic cyanobacterium. People are eating photosynthesising bacteria.
  3. Pond scum — Green pond scum is a film of Spirogyra and other algae doing massive amounts of photosynthesis.
  4. Ocean phytoplankton — Microscopic photosynthesisers in the ocean produce ~50 % of Earth's atmospheric oxygen.
  5. Coral reefs — Corals owe their colour to photosynthetic algae living inside them — animals partially fed by autotrophs in tissue.
  6. Green cactus stems in desert — Cacti replaced leaves with spines to survive droughts, but their green stems still photosynthesize.
  7. Algae coating on tree bark — Green algae on bark are photosynthetic and add oxygen to the tree, forming a symbiotic relationship.
  8. Grass shoots after mowing — The green shoots of freshly mown grass have photosynthetic tissue just below the surface.
  9. Lichen on rocks — Lichen is part algae (autotroph) and part fungus (heterotroph) — a symbiotic duo.
  10. Kelp forests in oceans — Kelp is a large algae that photosynthesize underwater using filtered blue-green light.

🌍 In your kitchen right now

Look at the food in front of you. Every vegetable, every grain, every fruit grew from photosynthesis. The tomato absorbed sunlight and turned it into sugar. The rice grain is 100% stored glucose made from sun. Even the salt from the ocean came from ocean plants and algae that photosynthesised. Your entire meal is ancient sunlight, locked into food just hours or days ago.

🧪 Try this at home

Tear a leaf in half and place it where half is in sunlight and half is in a paper envelope (dark) for 24 hours. Then dip both halves in iodine (ask an adult). The sunny half will turn blue-black (starch); the dark half won't. This proves photosynthesis needs light.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: Why does a plant placed in a completely dark cupboard slowly turn yellow?

Answer: Without light, photosynthesis stops. The plant uses up existing chlorophyll for energy but can't make new chlorophyll without glucose from photosynthesis. The chlorophyll is broken down, revealing yellow pigments underneath.

🧠 Try another

Question: If you had a sealed glass jar with a plant and a mouse inside, and you left it in sunlight for two weeks, what would happen?

Answer: The plant would photosynthesize, making glucose (food for itself) and oxygen (for the mouse to breathe). The mouse would respire, using the oxygen and producing CO₂ (for the plant to absorb). Both would survive because the plant is a producer and the mouse is a consumer in a tiny, closed ecosystem.

🧠 One more

Question: Is a plant always an autotroph?

Answer: Most plants are autotrophs, but some parasitic plants (like mistletoe or dodder) steal glucose from host plants. They're still plants, but they've partially or fully become heterotrophs. It shows that nutrition depends on what an organism can actually do, not just what it is.
