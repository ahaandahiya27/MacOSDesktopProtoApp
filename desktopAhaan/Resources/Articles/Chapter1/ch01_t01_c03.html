Chlorophyll: why leaves are green

Chlorophyll is the green pigment in leaves that absorbs sunlight to power photosynthesis.

≈ 6 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Have you noticed leaves are green? That's because of a special green molecule called chlorophyll, packed into tiny green factories called chloroplasts inside leaf cells. Chlorophyll is like a tiny solar panel — it grabs light from the Sun and uses that energy to start the food-making process. What this concept means

Chlorophyll is a green pigment present in the chloroplasts of plant cells, especially in the leaves. Its main function is to absorb light energy from the Sun and convert it into chemical energy that drives photosynthesis. Chlorophyll absorbs strongly in the red and blue wavelengths and reflects green, which is why leaves appear green. The concentration of chlorophyll varies seasonally — it breaks down in autumn, revealing yellow and red pigments that were always present.

Why it works this way

To use sunlight, you need a molecule that can grab a photon and give up an electron. Chlorophyll is one of the few molecules in nature whose structure does exactly that — its ring shape lets electrons spread out and get energised by visible light. The green colour is just a side-effect of which wavelengths chlorophyll grabs and which it ignores.

💡 Did you know?

Some sea slugs (Elysia chlorotica) eat algae, steal their chlorophyll-rich chloroplasts, and lodge them in their own skin — turning the slug into a 'solar-powered animal' for months. Scientists call this kleptoplasty. On Earth, plants aren't the only photosynthesisers: certain bacteria called cyanobacteria (in pond water) also use chlorophyll, and they were the original oxygen-producers two billion years ago — long before any plant existed.

Real-world applications

  1. Autumn leaf colours — When chlorophyll breaks down, hidden yellow/red pigments are revealed — autumn colour.
  2. Coin-on-leaf shadow — Place a coin on a leaf in sun for 2 days — the spot stays paler; no chlorophyll-making in dark.
  3. Yellow lawn patches — Yellow patches in a lawn usually mean nitrogen deficiency → not enough chlorophyll.
  4. Iron-deficient roses — Yellow leaves with green veins (chlorosis) signal an iron deficiency stopping chlorophyll synthesis.
  5. Blue/red grow lights — Indoor growers use blue/red LEDs because chlorophyll absorbs those — green light wastes energy.
  6. Leaf colour change in fall — As days shorten, trees break down chlorophyll to reabsorb nitrogen, leaving behind yellow xanthophyll and red anthocyanin.
  7. Spinach as a superfood — Spinach is dark green because it has very high chlorophyll content, which also means high iron and nutrients.
  8. Why shade plants are darker — Plants in low light evolve darker green leaves — more chlorophyll to catch fewer photons.
  9. Copper beech leaves — Some trees are bred to be brown not green, but they still have chlorophyll underneath the red pigment.
  10. Red algae in deep ocean — Algae below 200 metres are red, not green — they have different pigments that capture blue light that penetrates deep water.

🌍 In your kitchen right now

Look at the food in front of you. Every vegetable, every grain, every fruit grew from photosynthesis. The tomato absorbed sunlight and turned it into sugar. The rice grain is 100% stored glucose made from sun. Even the salt from the ocean came from ocean plants and algae that photosynthesised. Your entire meal is ancient sunlight, locked into food just hours or days ago.

🧪 Try this at home

Tear a leaf in half and place it where half is in sunlight and half is in a paper envelope (dark) for 24 hours. Then dip both halves in iodine (ask an adult). The sunny half will turn blue-black (starch); the dark half won't. This proves photosynthesis needs light.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: Why does a plant placed in a completely dark cupboard slowly turn yellow?

Answer: Without light, photosynthesis stops. The plant uses up existing chlorophyll for energy but can't make new chlorophyll without glucose from photosynthesis. The chlorophyll is broken down, revealing yellow pigments underneath.

🧠 Try another

Question: If you had a sealed glass jar with a plant and a mouse inside, and you left it in sunlight for two weeks, what would happen?

Answer: The plant would photosynthesize, making glucose (food for itself) and oxygen (for the mouse to breathe). The mouse would respire, using the oxygen and producing CO₂ (for the plant to absorb). Both would survive because the plant is a producer and the mouse is a consumer in a tiny, closed ecosystem.

🧠 One more

Question: Is a plant always an autotroph?

Answer: Most plants are autotrophs, but some parasitic plants (like mistletoe or dodder) steal glucose from host plants. They're still plants, but they've partially or fully become heterotrophs. It shows that nutrition depends on what an organism can actually do, not just what it is.
