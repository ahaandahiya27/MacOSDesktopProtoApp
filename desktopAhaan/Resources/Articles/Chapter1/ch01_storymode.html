A Day in the Life of a Leaf

Twenty-four hours, told from the leaf's point of view. First person. Real chemistry.

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine you are a single mango leaf hanging from the second branch of a tall tree. The Sun is just rising on a Tuesday morning in May. Here is your day.

5:30 a.m. — The waking

Dawn light is grey and soft. I am still cool from the night. My stomata — the tiny mouths on my underside — have been closed all night to save water. As the first rays warm me, my guard cells start to swell. Slowly my stomata yawn open. I am ready to breathe.

6:45 a.m. — The first sip of sky

The Sun is up. CO₂ from the air begins drifting through my open stomata into the spongy mesophyll inside me. My chloroplasts — there are about 50 in every one of my cells — are already humming. Their thylakoid stacks are catching photons.

Down below, my roots are sucking up water from the soil. The water travels straight up through tiny tubes in the trunk called xylem. By the time it reaches me, it has come up almost 12 metres in a thin invisible column. Plants are powerful pumps.

10:15 a.m. — Full sunlight, peak production

I am in full sun now. Every chloroplast in my cells is making sugar at maximum rate. The reaction inside me, simplified, is:

6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + sunlight → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂

For every six CO₂ molecules I pull in and six water molecules I split, I make one glucose molecule and let six oxygen molecules escape. The oxygen drifts out through my stomata. Somewhere a kid breathes it in.

This whole cycle happens trillions of times a second across the whole tree. Today my tree will release enough oxygen to keep two humans alive.

💡 Did you know?

One mature mango tree produces enough oxygen for two people for a year. A football-field-sized forest produces enough for several thousand.

1:00 p.m. — Too hot

The Sun is overhead and I am getting hot. Water is escaping me faster than my roots can replace it. I close my stomata partway to save water. This slows photosynthesis down but stops me from drying out. Trade-offs.

While my stomata are nearly shut, sugar production drops. I'm just enduring the afternoon.

4:30 p.m. — Late-day push

The Sun has tilted west and is gentler now. I reopen my stomata and the sugar factories restart. I am still making glucose, but I am also using some of it. Some sugar is shipped down through different tubes — phloem — to the trunk and roots for storage. Some is burnt right here in my own cells to fuel my repairs. Yes, plants respire too. Just like you.

7:15 p.m. — Sunset shutdown

The light is fading. My chloroplasts can no longer keep up the chemistry. Photosynthesis slows to nothing as darkness arrives. My stomata close. I switch fully to respiration mode — burning the sugar I made today to power my cells through the night.

I exhale CO₂ into the air. Yes, at night plants release CO₂. That is why people say "don't sleep under a tree", though the amount is tiny — far less than the O₂ I gave you all day.

11:00 p.m. — The night work

In the dark, my Calvin Cycle keeps running for a while, finishing the chemistry the light reactions started this afternoon. Then it too quiets down. I'll repair some torn chloroplast membranes, send a few last sugar shipments down to the roots, and wait. Tomorrow I'll do it all again.

For about ten months, until autumn turns me yellow and I fall.

📝 Stretch your thinking

You spent 7 minutes reading the day of a leaf. The leaf spent that day making food for half the planet. Which job is harder?
