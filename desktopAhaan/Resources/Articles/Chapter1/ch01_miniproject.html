Mini Project — Grow a Chickpea, Build a Plant Diary

A two-week project that turns Chapter 1 into something you can hold in your hand. Day-by-day plan with what to write in your notebook each day.

~14 days · 5–10 min per day

The textbook tells you how plants grow. This project shows you. By the end of two weeks you'll have a small chickpea plant and a science diary you wrote yourself.

What you'll need

  • A handful of dry kala chana (chickpeas) — uncooked
  • A small cup or pot with soil — garden soil is fine
  • Water
  • A sunny windowsill
  • The My Notebook button on the Chapter 1 card (or a real paper notebook + pencil)
  • A ruler — you'll measure once you have stems

Day 0 — Soaking (evening before Day 1)

Drop 5 chickpeas into a bowl with enough water to cover them. Leave them overnight on the kitchen counter.

Write in notebook: "Day 0 — I soaked 5 chickpeas. Each one was about [size] mm wide."

Day 1 — Planting

In the morning, the chickpeas should look puffy — almost double in size from the soaking. They drank water. Push 4 of them about 2 cm deep into the soil, evenly spaced. Water once. Keep the 5th seed aside (we'll compare).

Write: "Day 1 — I planted 4 chickpeas at 2 cm. I kept 1 as a control. The soaked chickpeas were [size] mm — bigger than yesterday because [why]."

Day 3 — First peek

You won't see anything above the soil yet. That's normal. Underground, the chickpea has split its seed coat and pushed out a little white tail (the radicle). That's the future root.

Write: "Day 3 — Nothing visible. Underground the radicle is probably growing. I'll wait."

Day 5 — Sprouts

You should see small green-yellow loops pushing out of the soil. That's the hypocotyl — the future stem — bending in a U-shape so the seed itself can be pulled up.

Write: "Day 5 — I see [number] sprouts. The loops are about [size] cm tall."

Day 7 — Cotyledons unfold

The two seed-halves (cotyledons) have come above ground and unfolded. They are pale green and look like baby leaves but aren't real leaves — they're the seed's lunchbox.

Above the cotyledons, the first TRUE leaves will appear in a day or two. They look completely different — smaller, deeper green, with proper leaf veins.

Write: "Day 7 — The cotyledons are out. The first true leaf is [colour] and about [size] mm long."

💡 Test of the day

Try eating one of your soaked chickpeas (clean it first). Now bite the dry control seed you saved. What's different? The soaked one has started its enzymes — its starches are turning to sugar. The first stage of digestion in plants.

Day 10 — Measure

Pick out the tallest plant. Measure with your ruler. Record the height.

Write: "Day 10 — Tallest plant is [X] cm. I count [N] true leaves. The leaves are growing in pairs on opposite sides of the stem (alternate vs opposite — chickpea is alternate)."

Day 12 — The nodule check

If your soil is real garden soil (not sterilised potting mix), gently lift ONE plant out by its base and rinse the roots in a bowl of water. Look for tiny round bumps on the roots.

Those bumps are nodules. Each contains millions of Rhizobium bacteria that pull nitrogen straight from the air and trade it to the plant for sugar. Re-plant the seedling if you want — it can recover.

Write: "Day 12 — I found [N] nodules on my plant. Each one has bacteria inside doing the nitrogen-fixing trick."

Day 14 — Project end

Compare your tallest plant on Day 14 to the dry control seed. The plant came from one seed. It is now [X] cm tall with [N] leaves and roots and bacterial partners. The control is still the same chickpea.

Final write-up — try a paragraph:

  • What surprised me most was…
  • The hardest part was…
  • One thing I'd do differently is…
  • One question I still have is…

📝 Stretch your thinking

You just did real science. Hypothesis → method → daily observation → measurement → write-up. That's the whole shape of how plant scientists work. The next 60 years of your education will use this same structure.
