Beyond the Book — The Tiny Dot That Changed Counting

Decimals look obvious now, but the world managed without them for thousands of years. Here is the story of the point.

≈ 6 min read · curious-kid level

The decimal point is the smallest mark in this whole chapter, yet it does an enormous job: it tells you where the whole numbers stop and the parts begin. Read one idea at a time.

1. Decimals are younger than you might think

People used fractions for thousands of years, but writing parts of a number as 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 only spread in Europe after Simon Stevin published a small book called De Thiende ("The Tenth") in 1585. He argued that ordinary people — surveyors, shopkeepers, sailors — would find tenths and hundredths far easier than clumsy fractions.

💡 Try this

Write three-quarters as a fraction (3/4) and as a decimal (0.75). Which one is faster to add to 0.5 in your head? That speed is exactly what Stevin was promising.

2. Each step right is one-tenth of the step before

Just as moving left makes a digit ten times bigger (ones → tens → hundreds), moving right past the point makes it ten times smaller: tenths → hundredths → thousandths. The decimal point is simply the doorway between the two directions. Place value never stops — it just keeps shrinking.

3. Some decimals never end

Write 1 ÷ 3 as a decimal and you get 0.3333… — the threes go on forever. So does 1 ÷ 7, which repeats the block 142857 over and over. A fraction with a tidy answer like 0.25 is actually the lucky exception; many simple fractions turn into never-ending decimals.

4. A famous surprise: 0.999… equals 1

Here is something that puzzles many people. The endless decimal 0.9999… is not almost 1 — it is exactly 1. One quick way to see it: 1 ÷ 3 = 0.333…, so 3 × (1 ÷ 3) = 0.999…, but 3 × (1/3) is just 1. Both lines describe the same number, so 0.999… and 1 must be the same too.

Use a calculator to divide 1 by 9, then by 11, then by 13. Watch which ones end and which ones fall into a repeating pattern. Can you spot a rule for which fractions terminate?
