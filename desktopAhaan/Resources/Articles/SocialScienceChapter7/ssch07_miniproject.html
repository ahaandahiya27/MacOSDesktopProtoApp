🪙 Design your own Gupta-style coin (about 50 min)

What you need

  • A circle of cardboard or air-dry clay
  • A pencil and fine pen
  • Paints or gold/silver markers
  • A reference picture of a Gupta coin (e.g. Fig. 7.4 or 7.6)

Steps

  1. Look closely at a Gupta coin showing a king (such as Samudragupta with the veena or Chandragupta I with Queen Kumaradevi).
  2. On one face of your cardboard or clay circle, draw a 'ruler' figure doing something that shows their personality or power.
  3. Add a short title around the edge, choosing a grand Gupta-style title like 'maharajadhiraja' or 'chakravartin'.
  4. On the reverse face, draw a deity or symbol — for example a seated goddess like Lakshmi, or the Garuda mount of Vishnu.
  5. Colour the coin gold or silver to imitate the real metal.
  6. Write two sentences explaining what your coin tells future historians about 'your' ruler.
  7. Display the coins as a class and see what messages each design sends.

👀 What you should see

You will notice how much information a single coin can carry — the ruler's image, titles, beliefs and even hobbies — and why historians treasure coins as sources.

🧠 Why it works

Designing a coin forces you to think like a Gupta ruler advertising power and like a historian decoding it, fixing the idea that coins are deliberate messages, not just money.

⏳ Build a Gupta-era timeline ribbon (about 60 min)

What you need

  • A long strip of paper or a roll of chart paper
  • A ruler and coloured pens
  • The chapter's timeline (Fig. 7.19) for reference

Steps

  1. Draw a straight line and mark it from 300 CE to 600 CE, with a tick every 50 years.
  2. Place the three Gupta rulers along the line in order: Chandragupta I, Samudragupta, Chandragupta II.
  3. Add the great minds in their approximate positions: Aryabhata and Varahamihira near 500 CE, Kalidasa, and Prabhavati Gupta.
  4. Below the main line, draw bands showing the overlapping rule of the Guptas, Vakatakas, Pallavas and Kamarupa.
  5. Mark the decline of the Guptas under the Hunas near the 6th century CE.
  6. Add small drawings or labels for key events (Iron Pillar, Prayaga Prashasti, Faxian's visit in the early 5th century CE).
  7. Display the ribbon and use it to quiz a partner on the order of people and events.

👀 What you should see

You will see at a glance that several dynasties ruled at the same time and that the great scholars clustered in the period of peace, especially around 500 CE.

🧠 Why it works

Laying events on a measured line turns a jumble of names and dates into a clear sequence, making it easy to remember who came before whom and that many kingdoms overlapped.
