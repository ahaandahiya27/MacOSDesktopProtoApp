Why the Iron Pillar resists rust: passivation [Class 10]

Rust forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water to make flaky iron oxide, which falls away and exposes fresh metal to keep corroding. The Delhi Iron Pillar escapes this through a process called passivation. The Gupta smiths used wrought iron unusually rich in phosphorus and free of sulphur and manganese. As the pillar is wetted and dried over and over, a thin, tightly bonded film of an iron-hydrogen-phosphate compound (sometimes called misawite) grows on the surface and slowly self-repairs. Unlike loose rust, this film clings fast and seals the metal beneath, blocking oxygen and water. Modern rust-proofing — from galvanising to stainless steel — uses the same principle of a stable protective surface layer, which is why metallurgists still study this 1,600-year-old object.

You'll want to know first: Knowing that iron normally rusts by reacting with oxygen and water.

Aryabhata, zero and the place-value system [Class 9]

Aryabhata's astronomy needed accurate, compact calculation, and classical Indian mathematics supplied a revolutionary tool: the decimal place-value system with a symbol for zero. In place value, the same ten digits mean different amounts depending on their position (the 3 in 30 versus in 300), and zero acts as a 'place-holder' so that 205 is clearly different from 25. This made arithmetic with large numbers far easier than with clumsy systems like Roman numerals. Indian scholars passed this system to the Arab world, where it became known to Europe as the 'Hindu-Arabic numerals' we all use today. So the numbers on every calculator and phone trace back to ideas that matured in the classical age in which Aryabhata worked.

You'll want to know first: Comfort with writing numbers using digits and understanding that position matters.
