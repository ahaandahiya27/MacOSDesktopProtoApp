Why the Iron Pillar resists rust: passivation [Class 10]

Rust forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water to make flaky iron oxide, which falls away and exposes fresh metal to keep corroding. The Delhi Iron Pillar escapes this through a process called passivation. The Gupta smiths used wrought iron unusually rich in phosphorus and free of sulphur and manganese. As the pillar is wetted and dried over and over, a thin, tightly bonded film of an iron-hydrogen-phosphate compound (sometimes called misawite) grows on the surface and slowly self-repairs. Unlike loose rust, this film clings fast and seals the metal beneath, blocking oxygen and water. Modern rust-proofing — from galvanising to stainless steel — uses the same principle of a stable protective surface layer, which is why metallurgists still study this 1,600-year-old object.

You'll want to know first: Knowing that iron normally rusts by reacting with oxygen and water.

Aryabhata, zero and the place-value system [Class 9]

Aryabhata's astronomy needed accurate, compact calculation, and classical Indian mathematics supplied a revolutionary tool: the decimal place-value system with a symbol for zero. In place value, the same ten digits mean different amounts depending on their position (the 3 in 30 versus in 300), and zero acts as a 'place-holder' so that 205 is clearly different from 25. This made arithmetic with large numbers far easier than with clumsy systems like Roman numerals. Indian scholars passed this system to the Arab world, where it became known to Europe as the 'Hindu-Arabic numerals' we all use today. So the numbers on every calculator and phone trace back to ideas that matured in the classical age in which Aryabhata worked.

You'll want to know first: Comfort with writing numbers using digits and understanding that position matters.

Can we trust a traveller's diary? [Class 10]

The Chinese pilgrim Faxian walked across Gupta India around 400 CE and left a diary that historians prize, because it shows the empire through an outsider's eyes. He described a prosperous, peaceful land where people moved about freely, charitable rest-houses and free hospitals cared for the poor, and harsh punishments were rare. Such an account is precious — but historians do not swallow it whole. Faxian was a Buddhist monk, so he naturally noticed monasteries and charity and may have passed quickly over things that did not interest him; he also met mostly the comfortable, town-dwelling people along the pilgrim routes, not the poorest villagers. So historians 'cross-check' him against Gupta coins, inscriptions and land-grant records before accepting a claim. A foreign traveller's diary is a wonderful source precisely because it is one point of view — and a careful reader always asks whose view it is.

You'll want to know first: Knowing that Faxian visited India and praised Gupta society.

When kings gave away land — and power [Class 11]

A quiet revolution of the Gupta age was the spread of 'land grants'. Kings rewarded brahmins, temples and officials with villages, and recorded the gift on copper plates that still survive. At first this seems generous, but look closer: many grants handed over not just the land but the right to collect its taxes and even to govern the people on it. Over generations this meant local grant-holders, not the distant king, controlled more and more of the countryside. Historians see in this the slow beginnings of a more decentralised, layered society — powerful local lords (later called sāmantas) under a weaker centre — that would shape the centuries after the Guptas. It is a good reminder that a single administrative habit, repeated for two hundred years, can reshape how an entire society is governed.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the Guptas made land grants recorded on copper plates.
