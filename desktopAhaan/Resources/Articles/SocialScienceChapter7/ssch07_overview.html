A New Power Emerges: The Rise of the Guptas

How the Gupta dynasty rose as the Kushana power faded, where and when it ruled, the famous rust-resistant Iron Pillar of Delhi, and the foundation laid by Chandragupta I.

  1. The Gupta Empire and Its SettingThe Gupta Empire was a powerful kingdom that flourished from about the 3rd to the 6th century CE, with its capital at Pataliputra (present-day Patna).
  2. A New Power Emerges After the KushanasAs the Kushana Empire weakened in the 3rd century CE, new kingdoms emerged and the Gupta dynasty rose to fill the gap.
  3. The Iron Pillar of Delhi and Gupta MetallurgyThe 1,600-year-old, 6-tonne Iron Pillar at Mehrauli in Delhi, raised in the reign of Chandragupta II, still stands without rusting — a marvel of ancient Indian metallurgy.

The Great Gupta Rulers

Chandragupta I's coins and alliances, the conquering warrior king Samudragupta and the Prayaga Prashasti, and Chandragupta II 'Vikramaditya', the great patron of art and learning, together with the diplomat queen Prabhavati Gupta.

  1. Chandragupta I: Founder of the EmpireChandragupta I laid the foundation of the Gupta Empire through his coins and strategic alliances, especially his marriage to the Lichchhavi princess Kumaradevi.
  2. Samudragupta, the Warrior KingSamudragupta, praised in the Prayaga Prashasti by his court poet Harisena, fought many wars to 'unify the Earth' and was also a patron of art, learning and trade.
  3. Chandragupta II 'Vikramaditya', Patron of the Golden AgeChandragupta II, also called 'Vikramaditya', was a renowned Gupta ruler under whom art, architecture, literature and science flourished, and who surrounded himself with learned men, poets and artists.
  4. Prabhavati Gupta, the Diplomat QueenPrabhavati Gupta, daughter of Chandragupta II, married into the Vakataka kingdom and became its regent ruler, keeping the Guptas and Vakatakas closely allied.

Governance, Society and Trade

The Gupta system of provincial rule and copper-plate land grants, the grand new royal titles, the thriving Indian Ocean trade, and the Chinese traveller Faxian's eyewitness account of Gupta society.

  1. Gupta Administration and Land GrantsInstead of ruling everything from the centre, the Guptas divided the empire into provinces and granted land to local rulers, priests and chieftains, recording these grants on copper plates.
  2. New Kings, New TitlesGupta rulers adopted grand titles like 'maharajadhiraja', 'samrat' and 'chakravartin' to proclaim their supreme authority over lesser kings.
  3. Thriving Trade in the Gupta AgeThe Gupta economy rested on land tax and a vibrant trade that linked India to the Mediterranean world, Southeast Asia and China through the Indian Ocean network.
  4. Faxian's Account of Gupta SocietyThe Chinese pilgrim Faxian, who visited India in the early 5th century CE, left a travelogue describing a prosperous, charitable Gupta society — though it reflects only his own viewpoint.

The Classical Age: Science, Literature and Art

Why the Gupta period is called the 'classical age' — the astronomy and mathematics of Aryabhata, the encyclopedic learning of Varahamihira, the poetry of Kalidasa, the codification of Ayurveda, and the artistic glories of Sarnath, Ajanta and Udayagiri, patronised through Nalanda and beyond.

  1. The 'Classical Age' and Its KnowledgeThe long peace and stability of the Gupta period led to such remarkable achievements in literature, science and art that historians call it the 'classical age' of India.
  2. Aryabhata: Mathematician and AstronomerAryabhata, who lived around 500 CE near Patna, wrote the Aryabhatiya, proposed that the Earth spins on its axis, and gave the length of the year accurate to within minutes.
  3. Varahamihira and Kalidasa: Science and PoetryVarahamihira of Ujjayini wrote the encyclopedic Brihat Samhita, while Kalidasa became the most celebrated poet of Sanskrit literature, author of the Meghadutam.
  4. Ayurveda and the Quest for Beauty in Gupta ArtDuring the Gupta period, Ayurveda was codified in texts like the Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita, and 'Gupta art' reached new heights at Sarnath, Ajanta and Udayagiri.

Decline, and Kingdoms of the South and Northeast

The decline of the Guptas under Huna invasions and internal conflict, the rise of the Pallavas of Kanchipuram in the south, the Kamarupa kingdom of the Brahmaputra valley in the northeast, and the lasting legacy of the Gupta era.

  1. The Decline of the GuptasBy the 6th century CE, repeated invasions by the Huna tribe from Central Asia and rising regional rulers weakened the Gupta Empire and led to its decline.
  2. The Pallavas of the SouthWhile the Guptas ruled the north, the Pallavas rose in the south with their capital at Kanchipuram, becoming great patrons of art, temples and learning.
  3. The Kamarupa Kingdom of the NortheastIn the northeast, the Kamarupa kingdom, ruled by the Varman dynasty, flourished over the Brahmaputra valley as a centre of culture and learning.
  4. The Lasting Legacy of the Gupta EraThe Gupta period was a time of remarkable progress whose advances in art, science, literature and governance shaped Indian and world history for centuries.
