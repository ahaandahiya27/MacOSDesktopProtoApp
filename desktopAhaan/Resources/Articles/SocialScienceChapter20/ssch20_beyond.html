How banks 'create' money by lending [Class 10]

Here is one of the most surprising ideas in economics: banks do not just move money around - in a sense, they help create it. When Anand deposits Rs 200, the bank keeps a small part as a reserve and lends most of it out, say to Shreya. Shreya spends that money, and whoever receives it may deposit it in their own bank, which again keeps a little and lends most out. The same original Rs 200 thus supports a chain of new deposits and loans far larger than itself. This is called the 'money multiplier' effect of fractional-reserve banking. It is enormously useful - it lets a small base of savings finance a much larger amount of productive activity. But it also explains why trust and rules matter so much: because banks lend out most deposits, they could not pay everyone back at the same time, so the RBI sets reserve rules and acts as a backstop to keep the system safe. Understanding this shows why the RBI's regulation is not red tape but the foundation of a stable economy.

Why a central bank's interest rate moves the whole economy [Class 10]

When the news says 'the RBI raised the rate' or 'cut the rate', why does it matter to everyone, not just banks? The answer is a chain reaction. The RBI sets the benchmark rate at which it lends to banks. If the RBI raises this rate, banks must pay more to borrow, so they raise the interest they charge on home loans, car loans and business loans. Higher loan rates make people and companies borrow and spend less, which cools down the economy and helps control rising prices (inflation). If the RBI cuts the rate, the opposite happens: loans get cheaper, people borrow and spend more, and the economy speeds up. So a single decision about one number ripples out to the cost of millions of loans and the spending of an entire nation. This is called monetary policy, and it is one of the most powerful tools a country has to keep its economy steady - which is why central banks like the RBI are so important.
