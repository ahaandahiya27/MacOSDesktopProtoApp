From the First to the Second Urbanisation

How India's earliest cities (the Harappan civilisation) faded, why urban life disappeared for a millennium, how the Second Urbanisation began in the Ganga plains, and how historians know about it.

  1. The First Urbanisation and Its DeclineIndia's First Urbanisation was the Harappan (Indus / Sindhu-Sarasvati) civilisation, which broke down in the early 2nd millennium BCE.
  2. A Thousand Years Without CitiesFor about a whole millennium after the Harappan decline, urban life was largely absent from India, though some villages and regional cultures continued.
  3. The Second Urbanisation and How We Know About ItIn the 1st millennium BCE a new wave of city-building — India's 'Second Urbanisation' — began in the Ganga plains and is known from archaeology and ancient literature.

Janapadas, Mahaajanapadas and Their Cities

How clans settled into janapadas, how these merged into the sixteen mahaajanapadas, the powerful states among them, and the well-fortified, moated capital cities that still survive as living cities today.

  1. Janapadas: Where the People Set FootA janapada was a territory settled by a clan and led by a rajaa (ruler), forming the earliest states of this period.
  2. The Sixteen MahaajanapadasBy the 8th–7th centuries BCE, janapadas merged into bigger states called mahaajanapadas; the most frequent list names sixteen of them.
  3. Fortified Cities, Moats and Living CapitalsMost mahaajanapada capitals were large, fortified cities with a moat and narrow gateways — and most are still living cities today, often around 2,500 years old.

Early Democratic Traditions and Systems of Governance

The assemblies (sabhaa and samiti) that advised rulers, the monarchies where the rajaa was supreme, and the ganas or sanghas — the 'early republics' like Vajji and Malla where the assembly even chose the ruler.

  1. Assemblies: Sabhaa and SamitiEach janapada had an assembly or council — the sabhaa or samiti — where the clan's affairs were discussed, and the rajaa was expected to take its advice.
  2. Monarchies: The Rule of the RajaaIn many mahaajanapadas the rajaa held ultimate authority and the position was hereditary, supported by ministers and an assembly of elders.
  3. Ganas and Sanghas: The Early RepublicsSome mahaajanapadas, called ganas or sanghas — like Vajji and Malla — were not monarchies; their assemblies took major decisions, even selecting the rajaa, making them among the world's earliest republics.

Innovations: Iron, Coins and the Varna-Jaati System

The technologies and social changes that powered the Second Urbanisation — the spread of iron, the first Indian coins (punch-marked coins), and the varna-jaati system that organised society.

  1. Iron Metallurgy and Its ImpactThe spread of iron tools and weapons by the late 2nd millennium BCE powered larger-scale farming and stronger armies, helping cities and states grow.
  2. The First Indian Coins: Punch-Marked CoinsGrowing trade led to India's first coins — silver 'punch-marked coins' stamped with symbols, soon traded across regions and even abroad.
  3. The Varna-Jaati SystemIndian society organised itself into a two-fold system of jaati (occupational community) and varna (four broad categories from Vedic texts), which was flexible early on but grew rigid over time.

Trade Routes and Developments Across India

The great trade and travel routes (Uttaraapatha and Dakshinaapatha) that connected India, the new cities of the east like Shishupalgarh, the southern kingdoms of the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas, and how the whole Subcontinent became one interconnected land before the mahaajanapadas ended.

  1. The Great Routes: Uttaraapatha and DakshinaapathaTwo famous routes — the Uttaraapatha in the north and the Dakshinaapatha to the south — connected India's regions for trade, pilgrimage and travel.
  2. New Cities and Kingdoms in the East and SouthThe Second Urbanisation spread eastward (Shishupalgarh, capital of Kalinga) and southward, where the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas emerged from about 400 BCE.
  3. One Interconnected Land — and the End of the MahaajanapadasBy about 300–200 BCE almost the whole Subcontinent was one vibrant, interconnected land, and around the same time the mahaajanapadas ceased to exist, giving way to empires.
