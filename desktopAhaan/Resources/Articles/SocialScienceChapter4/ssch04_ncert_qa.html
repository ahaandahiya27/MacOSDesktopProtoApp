Q1. Consider the quotation from Kautilya's Arthashaastra at the start of the chapter. What does Kautilya recommend for a kingdom, and is it very different today? (NCERT page 67)

Kautilya recommends that a kingdom be protected by fortifying its capital and frontier towns; that its land be able to sustain its own people and also outsiders in times of calamity; that it be beautiful and well-endowed with cultivable land, mines, timber and elephant forests, and good pastures rich in cattle; that it not depend only on rain for water (so it needs irrigation); that it have good roads and waterways; and that it have a productive economy with a wide variety of goods. This is not very different today: modern countries still value strong defence, food security and disaster relief, abundant natural resources, water supply that does not depend on rainfall alone, good transport (roads and waterways) and a strong, diverse economy. The vocabulary has changed, but the priorities of good governance are remarkably similar.

Q2. According to the text, how were rulers chosen in early Vedic society and in the ganas/sanghas? (NCERT page 71)

In the janapadas and many mahaajanapadas, the rajaa's position was hereditary — a ruler was usually the son of the previous one — but he was expected to take counsel from the assembly (sabhaa or samiti) of clan elders rather than rule alone, and some texts say an incompetent ruler could even be removed by the assembly. In the ganas or sanghas, such as Vajji (Vrijji) and Malla, rulers were not hereditary in this way: the assembly itself selected the rajaa, taking the decision through discussion and, if necessary, by vote. So in those 'early republics' the ruler was chosen by the assembly rather than simply inheriting the throne.

Q3. Imagine you are a historian studying ancient India. What types of sources would you use to learn more about the mahaajanapadas, and how would each contribute? (NCERT page 81)

I would use two main types of sources. Archaeological sources — the remains uncovered by excavation, such as city fortifications, moats, house plans, punch-marked coins and iron tools — would give me physical proof that the cities existed, along with dates and details about how people lived, traded and defended themselves. Literary sources — late Vedic, Buddhist and Jain texts, and the most ancient Tamil literature for the south — would give me the names of the mahaajanapadas, their capitals, rulers and assemblies, and descriptions of their society and beliefs that objects alone cannot reveal. Each source fills the other's gaps, and when archaeology and literature agree, my conclusions about the mahaajanapadas become much more reliable.

Q4. Why was the development of iron metallurgy so important for the growth of urbanism in the 1st millennium BCE? (NCERT page 81)

Iron, harder and sharper than copper or bronze, transformed both farming and warfare. Iron axes and ploughs could clear the dense forests and turn the heavy soils of the Ganga plains, allowing agriculture on a much bigger scale; this produced food surpluses that could feed city populations and provide revenue for states. Iron also made lighter, sharper weapons — swords, spears, arrows and shields — strengthening armies, which intensified competition between mahaajanapadas and, through warfare and alliances, helped build bigger kingdoms and empires. Because cities depend on surplus food, organised trade and effective states, and iron boosted all of these, the spread of iron metallurgy was central to the Second Urbanisation.
