Two Mini Projects for Chapter 15

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🔭 Build a working periscope from cardboard

~30 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A cardboard rectangle (~25 cm × 8 cm)
  • 2 plane mirrors (small, ~5 cm × 5 cm — pocket mirrors work)
  • Scissors and tape
  • A ruler and pencil

Steps

  1. Cut the cardboard to form a tube with two flat faces and rectangular cross-section (~5 × 5 cm).
  2. Cut a window at the TOP of one face, and a viewing window at the BOTTOM of the opposite face.
  3. Place the upper mirror at a 45° angle, reflecting the top window's image down inside.
  4. Place the lower mirror at a 45° angle (parallel to the upper), reflecting that image out the bottom viewing window.
  5. Tape everything in place.
  6. Look through the bottom window. You should see what's outside the TOP window — even if your head is below the wall.

👀 What you should see

You can see over walls, around corners, into next room from below window. The image is right-side up because the second mirror flips it back.

Why it works: Two reflections at 45° each (total 90° × 2 = 180°). Light from the top scene bounces off the top mirror (45° down), travels down the tube, bounces off the bottom mirror (45° toward your eye). Same principle as submarine periscopes. Two-mirror reflection is also the basis for how telescope eyepieces orient images.

🌈 Make a rainbow indoors with a glass of water

~15 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A clear glass full of water
  • A small mirror (pocket mirror)
  • A sunny window OR a bright torch in a dark room
  • A white wall or sheet of paper

Steps

  1. Fill the glass with water to the brim.
  2. Place the mirror INSIDE the glass at an angle, partly underwater.
  3. In a sunny room, position the glass so sunlight strikes the underwater part of the mirror.
  4. Adjust the mirror angle. Look at the wall — find a rainbow!
  5. Move the glass; the rainbow moves.

👀 What you should see

A clear rainbow spectrum appears on the white wall — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. The water acts as a prism (refracts light by colour); the mirror reflects the spread-out colours toward the wall.

Why it works: Sunlight enters water at an angle. Different colours bend by different amounts on entering (red least, violet most). When the now-separated colours hit the mirror, they reflect back. As they EXIT the water, they bend AGAIN, doubling the separation. Result: visible spectrum. Same physics as raindrops + sun = rainbow.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
