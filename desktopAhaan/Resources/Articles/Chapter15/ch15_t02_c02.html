The Prism & Dispersion

Chapter 15 · Topic 2 · Concept 2

White light is a secret rainbow

Sunlight looks white. A bulb looks white. But all that "white" is really a mix of seven colours blended so evenly your eye can't tell them apart. The proof was found by Isaac Newton in 1666 using a simple triangular piece of glass — a prism.

What a prism actually does

A prism is just a triangular block of glass. When a ray of white light hits one of its slanted faces, it refracts (bends) — but here's the trick:

Each colour bends a different amount. Violet bends the most, red the least. So a single white ray fans out inside the prism, and when it exits the other slanted face the colours have spread out into a band you can see.

VIBGYOR — the seven-colour code

Indian students remember the order with the word VIBGYOR:

  • V — Violet (bends the most)
  • I — Indigo
  • B — Blue
  • G — Green
  • Y — Yellow
  • O — Orange
  • R — Red (bends the least)

The technical name for this fanning-out is dispersion.

Why do different colours bend by different amounts?

Because in glass each colour travels at a slightly different speed. Violet light travels a tiny bit slower in glass than red light does — so glass's refractive index is slightly different for each colour. Violet has the highest n, red the lowest. Different speeds → different bending angles.

Newton's elegant follow-up

After splitting white light into a rainbow, Newton did the opposite experiment: he placed a second, inverted prism in the path of the coloured band. The seven colours recombined back into white light. That proved sunlight is the sum of those seven colours, not the source of them.

Try this at home

Make a prism with a glass of water. Stand a flat mirror at an angle inside a glass of water near a sunny window. The water + mirror combination acts like a prism — a tiny rainbow will appear on the wall or ceiling. Tilt the mirror to find the spot.

Where you see dispersion every day

  • Rainbows — millions of raindrops acting as prisms.
  • Diamond sparkle — diamonds have a very high refractive index AND strong dispersion. Each face acts like a tiny prism, throwing colours.
  • Old TV crystals — beveled glass edges throw rainbow patterns on a wall in sunlight.
  • CDs / DVDs — though not strictly prism dispersion (it's diffraction), you see the same VIBGYOR spread.

Looking ahead

In Class 10 dispersion is explored further in Chapter "Human Eye and the Colourful World" — including atmospheric scattering (why the sky is blue, why sunset is red).

In Class 12 / JEE Physics you'll learn the prism deviation formula δ = (μ − 1)A for small angles, the angular dispersion (μ_v − μ_r)A, and the design of achromatic prism combinations used in camera lenses.

In JEE Chemistry the same idea — different wavelengths travelling at different speeds — explains the basics of spectrophotometry. In NEET the eye's natural dispersion contributes to chromatic aberration, fixed by the brain.
