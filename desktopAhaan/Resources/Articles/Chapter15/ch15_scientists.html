One Curious Person Behind Chapter 15

The scientist most tied to light — their question, their experiment, and what changed because of them.

Every chapter has a person behind it — someone who first asked the right question, ran the right experiment, or wrote down the answer. This is a one-page portrait of the scientist most tied to this chapter's content.

Sir Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman

1888–1970 · Indian

🎧 In one line

Won India's first Nobel in Sciences (1930) for the discovery of the Raman Effect — how molecules scatter light, changing its colour.

C.V. Raman was born in Tiruchirapalli, Tamil Nadu, in 1888. By 16 he had completed his BSc at Presidency College Madras with top honours. He joined the Indian Civil Service as a financial officer in Calcutta — but in his spare time, in his own home, he did physics experiments. In February 1928, while studying why the Mediterranean Sea appeared so blue (during a ship voyage to Europe), he made a startling discovery. When light passes through a substance, MOST of it scatters at the same wavelength — but a tiny fraction (1 in 10 million photons) emerges at a DIFFERENT wavelength, having transferred energy to or from the molecules. This is the 'Raman Effect'. It became a powerful tool for analysing molecules — every chemical fingerprint can be identified by Raman spectroscopy. In 2025, ISRO's missions to Mars and the Moon carry Raman spectrometers to identify minerals on other worlds. Raman won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930 — the FIRST Asian and FIRST non-European Nobel laureate in Sciences. He founded the Raman Research Institute in Bangalore. February 28 (the day of his discovery) is celebrated as National Science Day in India. The chapter on Light that Class 7 students read is exactly the physics — refraction, scattering — that one Tamil boy spent his entire life understanding.
