The Concave Mirror

Chapter 15 · Topic 1 · Concept 3

The mirror that curves inward

A concave mirror is shaped like the inside of a spoon — the reflecting surface curves towards you. Because all the parts of the curve aim back towards a single spot, parallel rays of light all converge at one point after reflecting.

The focal point

Hold a concave mirror in sunlight. Parallel rays from the Sun all come in roughly parallel and, after bouncing, meet at a single point in front of the mirror. That point is called the focus, and the distance from the mirror to it is the focal length.

A sharp concave mirror can focus enough sunlight to set a piece of paper on fire — this is exactly how solar cookers work.

Two very different images

What you see in a concave mirror depends on how close you hold the object:

  • Close in (object inside the focus): the image is upright, magnified, and virtual — like a shaving mirror or a dentist's tooth mirror. The light hasn't actually converged behind the mirror; it just appears to come from there.
  • Far away (object beyond the focus): the image is upside down, smaller, and real — meaning the light really meets at a point in front of the mirror. You can catch this image on a screen.

Where concave mirrors are used

  • Shaving and makeup mirrors — magnify your face when you stand close.
  • Dentist's mirrors — give a magnified view of teeth.
  • Torch and headlight reflectors — a bulb at the focus produces a strong parallel beam.
  • Solar concentrators — focus sunlight to cook food or heat water.
  • Telescope primary mirrors — the world's biggest optical telescopes (Keck, JWST) use giant concave mirrors to gather faint starlight.

A useful intuition

Think of a concave mirror as a "collector": it grabs light from a wide area and squeezes it down to a small spot. That's exactly what your eye wants when it's trying to see something very faint, and it's also what a torch wants when it's trying to send out a strong beam. Same trick, used both ways.

Try this at home

The inside of a polished steel spoon is a (rough) concave mirror. Look into it. Then slowly move the spoon away — your reflection will shrink, blur, and at some point flip upside down. You've just crossed the focal point.
