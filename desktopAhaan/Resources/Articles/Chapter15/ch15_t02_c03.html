The Rainbow

Chapter 15 · Topic 2 · Concept 3

A rainbow is millions of tiny prisms in the sky

After a rain shower, if the Sun comes out behind you, you'll often see an arc of colours stretching across the sky. That arc is a rainbow, and it's made by the same physics as a glass prism — but with millions of raindrops doing the work instead.

What happens inside one raindrop

Pick a single raindrop. Sunlight enters the front of the drop:

  1. Refraction in: the white light bends and splits slightly as it enters the water — violet bends the most, red the least.
  2. Reflection at the back: the light hits the back curve of the drop and bounces back (it's like a tiny concave mirror).
  3. Refraction out: the light bends again as it leaves the drop. By now the colours have spread out into a fan.
The colours leave the drop at an angle of about 42° from the line connecting the drop and the Sun. Every raindrop that happens to be at that angle from your line of sight sends a beam of one specific colour to your eye.

Why a rainbow is an arc

Because every raindrop sitting at exactly the right angle (around 42° from the antisolar point — the point directly opposite the Sun) sends colour to your eyes, the set of all such drops forms a circle. From the ground you see the lower half of that circle as an arc. From an aeroplane, you can sometimes see the full circle.

Order of colours

Red is on the outside of the arc, violet on the inside. Why? Red leaves the drop at about 42°, violet at about 40°. The drops sending you red light are slightly farther from the centre of the bow than the drops sending you violet light.

Sometimes a second, fainter rainbow appears

If the conditions are right, you may see a secondary rainbow just outside the main one — fainter and with the colours reversed. This one is made by light that reflects twice inside each drop. The double reflection both dims the light and flips the colour order.

You will never reach the end of a rainbow

A rainbow isn't an object at a fixed place. It's an angle in the sky — a different one for every observer. Walk towards it and it moves away with you. No gold at the end, sorry.

Try this at home

Hose-rainbow. On a sunny day, stand with the Sun behind you and spray a fine mist from a garden hose into the air in front of you. A rainbow forms in the mist. Move your head — the rainbow moves too, because it depends on the angle from your eye.

Why rainbows appear after a shower, not during

You need three things: sunlight, raindrops, and clear air between you and the drops. During heavy rain there are drops in the way but no clear sunlight. Just after the rain, when the Sun is low and a few drops still hang in the air, the geometry lines up perfectly.

Looking ahead

The rainbow is also the easiest entry point to atmospheric optics. In Class 10 you'll meet related phenomena:

  • Twinkling of stars — refraction by air pockets of different density.
  • Apparent positions — the Sun visible before it actually rises (and after it sets) because of atmospheric refraction.
  • Scattering of light — why the sky is blue (Rayleigh scattering) and why sunsets are red.

In JEE / NEET Physics, the rainbow shows up as a worked example combining refraction + reflection + dispersion in a single drop — a favourite problem on geometric optics papers.
