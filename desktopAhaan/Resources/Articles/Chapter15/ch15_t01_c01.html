The Law of Reflection

Chapter 15 · Topic 1 · Concept 1

Light is the world's neatest bouncer

Roll a ball at a wall at an angle. It bounces off at the same angle, but going the other way. Light does exactly the same thing when it hits a mirror — and it does it more precisely than any tennis ball.

The rule, in words

Take a light ray heading towards a mirror. Imagine an invisible line stuck to the mirror, pointing straight out at 90°. We call that line the normal.

Now measure two angles, both from the normal:

  • The angle of incidence (i) — between the incoming ray and the normal.
  • The angle of reflection (r) — between the reflected ray and the normal.
Law of Reflection: i = r. Both rays and the normal lie in the same flat plane.

Two important details

First, the angles are always measured from the normal, never from the mirror itself. A ray that just grazes the mirror has an angle of incidence of nearly 90° — not 0°.

Second, "in the same plane" means all three lines — incident ray, reflected ray, and normal — lie flat on a single sheet of paper. The reflected ray doesn't twist out sideways.

Why does this matter?

Because it always works. Every mirror you've ever used, every telescope ever built, every shiny puddle, every periscope on a submarine — they all obey this one tiny rule. Once you trust it, you can predict where your reflection will appear, how big it will look, and even whether it will be upside-down.

Try this with a torch

Tape a mirror flat to the table. Shine a torch beam at it at a shallow angle so you can see the spot of light land. The reflected beam will land just as far away from the mirror on the other side. Try changing the angle — the same rule holds every time.

Diffuse vs regular reflection

Why can you see your face in a mirror but not in a wall? Both surfaces reflect light — but a mirror is so flat at the atomic scale that all parallel rays bounce off in parallel (regular reflection). A wall is rough, so parallel rays bounce in all directions (diffuse reflection). Same law, different surface.
