Lenses

Chapter 15 · Topic 3 · Concept 1

A lens is shaped refraction

A lens is a piece of transparent material — usually glass or clear plastic — with one or both surfaces curved. Every ray of light that passes through a lens is refracted twice: once as it enters, and once as it leaves. Because of the curve, parallel rays end up converging at (or diverging from) a single point called the focus.

Two shapes, two behaviours

LensShapeEffect on parallel lightMnemonic
ConvexThicker in the middleConverges to a focal point"convex = converging"
ConcaveThinner in the middleSpreads apart (diverges)"concave = caves in"

What you see through a convex lens

Hold a convex lens close to a small object (closer than its focal length):

  • You see a magnified, upright, virtual image.
  • This is exactly how a magnifying glass works.

Hold the same lens farther away (object beyond the focal length):

  • You see a smaller, inverted, real image.
  • This is how a camera, the human eye, and a cinema projector work — a real image of the world is formed on the sensor / retina / screen.

What you see through a concave lens

A concave lens always gives the same kind of image, no matter how far away the object is:

  • The image is smaller than the object.
  • It is upright.
  • It is virtual — you couldn't catch it on a screen.

That's why a concave lens is used in spectacles for short-sighted people (myopia) — it spreads the image out, pushing it back onto the retina.

The "F" word — focal length

The distance from the centre of a lens to its focal point is the focal length, written as f. A short focal length means a "strong" lens that bends light by a lot; a long focal length means a "weak" lens that bends light only a little.

A convex lens with f = 25 cm is what most reading magnifiers use. A camera lens often has f = 50 mm. A microscope objective has f = a few mm. The human eye lens has f ≈ 2.3 cm.

Try this at home

The eyeglass projector. If you wear reading glasses, take them off and hold one lens up against a bright window. Hold a sheet of paper a few centimetres behind the lens. Adjust the distance until a tiny, inverted image of the window appears on the paper — a real image. (If you don't have glasses, the lens from an old pair of binoculars or a hand magnifier works.)

Looking ahead

In Class 10 (Light — Reflection & Refraction) lenses become equations:

  • Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f (with strict sign conventions).
  • Magnification: m = hi/ho = v/u.
  • Power of a lens: P = 1/f (in metres), measured in dioptres (D). A +2 D lens has f = 0.5 m.

In Class 10 (Human Eye and Colourful World) you'll meet the eye's natural lens, and how concave lenses correct myopia while convex lenses correct hypermetropia and presbyopia. NEET Biology builds on this with the full optics of accommodation and corneal refraction.

In Class 12 / JEE Physics you'll meet the lensmaker's equation 1/f = (μ − 1)(1/R1 − 1/R2), the combined-lens formula 1/f = 1/f1 + 1/f2, and design problems for compound microscopes (M = (L/fo)·(D/fe)) and astronomical telescopes.
