The Convex Mirror

Chapter 15 · Topic 1 · Concept 4

The mirror that bulges outward

A convex mirror is shaped like the back of a spoon — its reflecting surface bulges away from you. Where a concave mirror gathers parallel rays into a single focal point, a convex mirror does the opposite: it spreads them apart.

The image you always get

No matter how close or far you hold an object in front of a convex mirror, the image always has the same three properties:

  • It is smaller than the object.
  • It is upright.
  • It is virtual — the reflected rays don't actually meet in front of the mirror; they only appear to come from a point behind it.

That predictable, never-flipping image is exactly why convex mirrors are the standard choice for safety mirrors.

Why "wider view"?

Because the surface curves outward, it bounces light back to your eye from a much larger area than a flat mirror of the same size. A small convex mirror can show you traffic on a whole corner; a flat mirror the same size would only show you a narrow strip.

That's why you see the warning "objects in mirror are closer than they appear" printed on car wing mirrors. The wider view comes at the cost of making everything look further away than it really is.

Where convex mirrors are used

  • Vehicle side-view mirrors — wider view of the road behind.
  • Security mirrors in shops — one mirror covers a whole aisle.
  • Mirrors at blind T-junctions — let drivers see traffic from both directions on a single mirror.
  • ATMs and bank counters — small convex mirror lets you see who is behind you.
  • Spherical Christmas ornaments — every shiny ball you've ever seen is a convex mirror, which is why your face looks tiny and warped in them.

Try this at home

Look at yourself in the back of a polished steel spoon. Your reflection is tiny, upright, and the view is wider than the spoon itself — you can probably see most of your face fit inside its small back. That's a convex mirror in action.
