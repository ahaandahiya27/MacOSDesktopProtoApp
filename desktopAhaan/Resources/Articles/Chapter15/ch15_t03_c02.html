The Periscope

Chapter 15 · Topic 3 · Concept 2

Seeing around the corner

A periscope is the simplest optical instrument you can build — two plane mirrors and a long tube. Light from above bounces off the top mirror, travels down inside the tube, bounces off the bottom mirror, and into your eye. You see what's at the top without being there.

Why two mirrors at 45°?

Each mirror is tilted at exactly 45° to the long axis of the tube. A ray of light coming in horizontally from the top hits the upper mirror at 45° and reflects through 90° — straight down the tube. At the bottom it hits the lower mirror, again at 45°, and reflects through another 90° — straight into your eye, horizontally.

The Law of Reflection (Topic 1) guarantees the angles work out. Two 90° turns in opposite directions cancel out, so the image you see is right-side-up.

Submarines use periscopes to look above the water surface without surfacing. Modern submarine periscopes are far more elaborate — they include prisms, magnifying lenses, and digital cameras — but the core idea is still two reflections at 45°.

Where else periscopes are used

  • Inside tanks and armoured vehicles — the crew can see the battlefield without exposing themselves.
  • At crowded events — selfie-stick periscopes let you see over a tall crowd.
  • In endoscopes (modern fibre-optic versions) — doctors use them to look inside the human body without surgery.
  • In observation posts — looking out from a trench or a bunker.

What the image looks like

Because the light is reflected twice (and the two reflections happen on different axes), the periscope image:

  • Is the same size as the original (because plane mirrors don't magnify).
  • Is upright (both reflections combined preserve the up-down).
  • Is laterally inverted — your right side is still swapped with the image's left, as with any plane mirror.

Try this at home

Build a simple periscope. You'll need a long cardboard tube (the inside of a paper-towel roll, end-to-end), two small plane mirrors, and tape.

  1. Cut a small viewing hole at each end — one near the top, on one side; one near the bottom, on the opposite side.
  2. Tape one mirror inside at the top, tilted at 45° so it faces the upper hole.
  3. Tape the second mirror inside at the bottom, tilted at 45° (mirrored arrangement) so light coming down hits it and reflects to the lower hole.
  4. Hold it up over a wall or behind a chair — and look. You'll see what's on the other side.

Looking ahead

The periscope is the simplest example of an optical instrument. In Class 12 / JEE Physics you'll meet:

  • Total internal reflection in prisms — modern periscopes use 45-45-90 prisms instead of mirrors because they're more efficient. (Most light is lost from a mirror's silvering; in a prism, TIR means 100% reflection.)
  • Compound microscopes and astronomical telescopes — multi-element systems with the same "redirect-and-magnify" idea.
  • Optical fibre endoscopes — the modern medical version of the periscope, with thousands of glass fibres each doing total internal reflection.
