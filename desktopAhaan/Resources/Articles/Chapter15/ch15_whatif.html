Three "What Ifs?" About Light

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if light moved 100 times slower?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Then it would travel at ~3 million m/s — still fast, but visible delays would start happening. A car 1 km away would appear to be at its position 0.3 ms ago — barely noticeable. But astronomers would see the Sun as it was 50 minutes ago (instead of 8 minutes); satellites' positions would be hard to calculate; GPS would need huge corrections. Most importantly: refraction effects would be MUCH stronger (light slowdown in materials is what causes refraction). Everything we see would look severely distorted at edges. Different physics, different world.

What if 2 — What if humans had eyes like a fly (compound eyes)?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: We'd see ~30,000 tiny low-resolution images simultaneously instead of one high-res image. Excellent for detecting MOVEMENT (any moving object across visual field triggers many small detectors). Poor for reading text or distinguishing colours subtly. Flies' compound eyes are why they're so hard to swat — they detect your hand's motion long before you reach them. Trade-off: motion sensitivity vs detail. Evolution gave us detail (we need to read facial expressions, recognise predators); flies got motion-detection (they need to escape).

What if 3 — What if you stood between two parallel mirrors?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: You'd see infinite copies of yourself, receding into both 'depths'. Each mirror reflects the other (and you in it), and that reflection is reflected by the first, and so on infinitely. In reality, each reflection is dimmer than the last (mirrors absorb ~5% of light each bounce), so within ~30 reflections everything fades to dark. You can see this in some Indian temple corridors and hairdressing salons designed with parallel mirrors. The 'infinity mirror' decoration.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
