The Plane Mirror

Chapter 15 · Topic 1 · Concept 2

The mirror you use every day

A plane mirror is just a flat sheet of glass with a thin metal coating (usually aluminium) on the back. The metal is the part that actually reflects; the glass is there to protect it from scratches.

Five facts about the image

Whenever you look into a plane mirror, the image has the same five properties:

  1. It is the same size as you.
  2. It is upright (not flipped upside down).
  3. It looks like it is as far behind the mirror as you are in front of it. Step back 1 metre, the image is now 2 metres away from you.
  4. It is laterally inverted — your right hand looks like the image's left hand.
  5. It is a virtual image: the light isn't really meeting behind the mirror, your brain just thinks it is. You couldn't catch the image on a screen.

Why "behind" the mirror?

Trace any ray from your face to the mirror and then bounce it back to your eye. Now extend the bounced ray straight backwards as if the mirror weren't there. Wherever those imaginary extensions cross — that's where your brain thinks the image lives. For a plane mirror, they always cross at the same distance behind the glass as the object is in front of it.

That's why a "virtual" image can never be caught on a screen — there's no light actually arriving at that point. It's a useful illusion created by the geometry of the bounces.

The handedness twist

Why does your reflection wave with the "wrong" hand? Imagine you're looking at a friend across the room. If they raise their right hand, you see them raise the hand on your left. A mirror does almost the same thing: it shows you yourself, but as if you were facing yourself. The mirror swaps front-to-back, not left-to-right — but because you imagine yourself turned around, it looks like a left-right flip.

Where plane mirrors are used

  • Bathroom and dressing-room mirrors — life-size, undistorted.
  • Periscopes — two plane mirrors at 45° let you see over walls (you'll meet this in Topic 3).
  • Optical instruments — periscopes, kaleidoscopes, some telescopes route light using flat mirrors.
  • Solar cookers — flat mirrors arranged to bounce sunlight onto a pot.

Try this at home

Tape a sheet of paper to a wall. Stand 1 metre away. Look at yourself in a small hand mirror and try to write your own name through the mirror, watching only the reflection. It feels impossible at first — that's the lateral inversion in action.
