Three "What Ifs?" About Soil

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if all earthworms vanished overnight?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Soil structure would collapse within a year. No worm tunnels means no aeration; plant roots suffocate. No worm castings means soil nutrient cycling slows dramatically; fertility falls. Dead plant matter doesn't get mixed into soil; it stays at the surface as a thick layer. Within 3-5 years, most current farmland would be much less productive. India's earthworm population (estimated 200+ billion individuals) does invisible work worth tens of thousands of crores annually. Charles Darwin's last book made this point — he called earthworms 'nature's plough' for good reason.

What if 2 — What if a Punjab farmer tried to grow rice instead of wheat?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Possible but inefficient. Punjab's alluvial soil drains well — too well for rice, which needs standing water for most of its growth. The farmer would have to flood the field continuously (huge water cost), and the well-drained soil would lose water faster than rice needs. Productivity would be 30-40% below comparable rice land in Bengal or Tamil Nadu. This is why Punjab specialises in wheat (and some rice in monsoon when water is abundant) while Bengal specialises in rice (clayey water-holding soil). Soil chooses crop, not the other way around.

What if 3 — What if a new soil layer 1 metre deep was deposited on Delhi overnight?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Existing trees would die (their roots can't reach the old soil). Plants that grow shallow roots would survive. The new layer would lack organic matter and microbial population — sterile until colonised by bacteria, fungi, and earthworms over decades. Buildings would survive (foundations go deep), but parks, lawns, and gardens would need to be entirely rebuilt. After 30-50 years, the new soil would start to function normally. New soil isn't instantly fertile — it takes time and life to BECOME soil.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
