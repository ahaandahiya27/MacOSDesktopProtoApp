Why Healthy Soil Is Alive

Microbes, earthworms and the unseen workforce · Class 7 Science

A handful of soil is a city of microbes

One teaspoon of fertile garden soil contains roughly 1 billion bacteria, several metres of fungal threads, and thousands of protozoa. They break down dead leaves, fix nitrogen, and release nutrients in forms plants can absorb.

Earthworms — nature's free ploughmen

Charles Darwin called earthworms 'nature's ploughmen'. They eat soil, digest the organic matter, and pass the rest as vermicompost — finer than the original and richer in nutrients. Their burrows aerate the soil and let water seep deep.

What kills soil life

Excess chemical fertilisers and pesticides poison microbes and earthworms. Repeated tilling exposes them to sun. Over a few decades these practices turn fertile soil into a lifeless dust that needs ever-more fertiliser to grow anything.

Restoring living soil

Organic farming, mulching with crop residues, no-till practices, and cover crops bring life back. Within 3–5 years even badly-degraded soil can recover its microbial population.

Mycorrhiza is a fungus–root partnership where fungi extend the root's reach 100×. Most healthy plants — from grass to a banyan tree — depend on mycorrhiza, but heavy fertiliser use destroys the relationship.

Try this at home

Dig a small pit in a vegetable garden. Count the earthworms in a 30 × 30 cm patch. A healthy garden gives 5–10 worms; a chemical-heavy lawn might give zero.
