Indian Soil-Loss Hotspots and What's Being Done

From Punjab's salinity to Uttarakhand's landslides · Class 7 Science

The scale of the problem

India loses about 5 billion tonnes of topsoil every year to erosion — roughly the weight of 15,000 Eiffel Towers. The Himalayan foothills, Western Ghats, Deccan plateau, and Punjab–Haryana are the worst hit.

Hotspot 1 — Himalayan landslides

Deforestation and road-cutting in Uttarakhand and Himachal trigger massive monsoon landslides. The 2013 Kedarnath disaster killed 5,000+ people partly because soil and rock on denuded slopes had nothing holding them in place.

Hotspot 2 — Punjab's salinity

Decades of canal irrigation without proper drainage have raised the water table. Salts dissolved in groundwater have come to the surface; nearly 10% of Punjab's farmland is now too salty to farm. The fix: drip irrigation + drainage channels + planting salt-tolerant grasses.

Government efforts

Programmes like the Watershed Development Programme and the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture fund check dams, contour bunding and afforestation. State agriculture departments also subsidise drip irrigation to reduce water-driven erosion.

It takes 500–1000 years of weathering to make 2.5 cm of topsoil. India loses that much every year on degraded slopes. Conservation isn't optional — once topsoil is gone, it doesn't come back in our lifetime.

Try this at home

Visit a construction site or a bare slope after a heavy rain. Look at the silty water running off — every cup that flows away is irreplaceable.

Looking ahead

Class 10 Geography (Resources and Development) covers soil types and conservation in depth. UPSC and SSC exams ask about ICAR (Indian Council of Agricultural Research) and the National Bureau of Soil Survey. Civil engineering electives cover slope stability and erosion control.
