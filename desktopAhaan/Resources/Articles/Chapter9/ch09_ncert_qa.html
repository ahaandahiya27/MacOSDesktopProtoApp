Q1. Tick the most suitable answer in the following: (a) In addition to the rock particles, the soil contains (i) air and water (ii) water and plants (iii) minerals, organic matter, air and water (iv) water, air and plants.

Model answer: (iii) minerals, organic matter, air, and water. Soil is composed of rock particles (sand, silt, clay), organic matter (humus, decaying remains), air (in pore spaces), and water. Plants live IN the soil but aren't part of it.

Q2. The water holding capacity is the highest in: (i) sandy soil (ii) clayey soil (iii) loamy soil (iv) mixture of sand and loam.

Model answer: (ii) Clayey soil. Clay particles are extremely fine — high surface area — which traps water effectively. Sandy soil drains water quickly through its large pore spaces; loamy is in between. That's why clay soils are used for rice paddies (which need standing water) and sandy soils for groundnut and watermelon (which don't).

Q3. Match the items in column I with those in column II. (Soil types → suitable crops, e.g. clayey → rice, loamy → wheat, sandy → cotton)

Model answer: Clayey → Rice (needs standing water). Loamy → Wheat (needs rich, moisture-retaining but well-drained soil). Sandy → Cotton (needs deep root penetration, good drainage). Real-world example: Bengal's clayey-loamy soils are India's rice belt; Punjab's loamy alluvial is wheat country; Deccan's black soil (technically clayey-loamy hybrid) is cotton country.

Q4. Explain how soil is formed.

Model answer: Soil forms over thousands of years through weathering (breaking down) of rocks. Three forces drive it: (1) Physical weathering — water freezing in rock cracks expands and breaks them, wind blasts particles loose, temperature changes crack rocks. (2) Chemical weathering — rainwater (slightly acidic from CO₂) dissolves some minerals; oxygen rusts iron-rich rocks. (3) Biological weathering — plant roots widen cracks; lichens secrete acids that dissolve rocks; earthworms break particles further. Over hundreds to thousands of years, this rock breakdown plus accumulated organic matter (dead plants, animals) becomes soil.

Q5. How is clayey soil useful for crops?

Model answer: Clayey soil's high water-holding capacity makes it ideal for crops that need wet roots — most famously rice paddy. Clay also tends to be richer in nutrients (small particles have high surface area for nutrients to stick to). Crops that thrive in clay: rice, jute, sugarcane, banana. Crops that hate it: any tap-rooted or drought-loving plant (potato, watermelon). Clay's drawback: poor drainage in heavy rain, can suffocate roots; needs amendment with organic matter for diverse cropping.

Q6. List the differences between clayey soil and sandy soil.

Model answer: Particle size: clay has tiny particles (<0.002 mm); sand has large (0.05–2 mm). Water holding: clay holds water well; sand drains fast. Air spaces: clay has few air pockets; sand has many. Drainage: clay is poor (waterlogs); sand is excellent. Feel: clay is sticky and smooth when wet; sand is gritty. Cropping: clay suits rice, jute; sand suits potato, groundnut, watermelon. Workability: clay is heavy and tough to plough; sand is light. The two are opposite ends of a spectrum.

Q7. Sketch the cross section of soil and label the various layers.

Model answer: From top to bottom: (a) Litter layer — fresh leaves and twigs at surface. (b) Topsoil or A-horizon — dark, organic-rich, where most roots grow. (c) Subsoil or B-horizon — lighter colour, clay-rich, fewer roots. (d) Weathered rock or C-horizon — partially broken parent rock. (e) Bedrock — solid unbroken rock. Total profile depth varies hugely: a forest may have 1+ metre of topsoil, while a desert may have only a few centimetres.

Q8. Razia conducted an experiment in the field related to the rate of percolation. She observed that it took 40 min for 200 mL of water to percolate through the soil sample. Calculate the rate of percolation.

Model answer: Rate of percolation = amount of water (mL) / time (min) = 200 / 40 = 5 mL/min. This is a moderately fast percolation rate. Sandy soil would percolate faster (~10+ mL/min), clayey soil slower (~1-2 mL/min). Razia's 5 mL/min suggests loamy or sandy-loam soil — good for most crops.
