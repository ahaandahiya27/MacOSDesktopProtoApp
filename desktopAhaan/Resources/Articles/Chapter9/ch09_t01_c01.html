The Four Soil Horizons

What you'd see in a hole 1 metre deep · Class 7 Science

Why soil isn't uniform

Soil looks like brown stuff. But dig a deep pit anywhere on land and you'll see something surprising: the colour, the texture, the smell — all change with depth. Soil is built in layers, called horizons. Each layer has its own story.

From top to bottom

  • O horizon — Organic litter. The very top: fallen leaves, twigs, dead insects, animal droppings. Hasn't been broken down yet. A few cm thick in temperate forests, almost nothing in dry grasslands.
  • A horizon — Topsoil. Dark, soft, full of humus and roots. This is where most plants grow. The colour comes from decomposed organic matter — the darker, the better for crops.
  • B horizon — Subsoil. Lighter brown or reddish. Holds minerals washed down by water from the layers above. Fewer roots reach here; less life.
  • C horizon — Weathered rock. Broken-up pieces of the rock below, slowly cracking into smaller bits by frost, water and roots over thousands of years.
  • R horizon — Bedrock. Solid stone. Sometimes hundreds of metres thick.
A thumb of topsoil takes roughly 100 years to form, and a metre takes 5,000-10,000 years. Once you wash it away by deforestation, you don't get it back in human time.

How the horizons formed

Two slow processes built every horizon you see:

  1. Weathering of bedrock from below — wind, rain, frost and roots crack rock into smaller and smaller pieces.
  2. Biological deposition from above — plants drop leaves, animals die, fungi and bacteria turn the remains into humus.

The horizons are the equilibrium between those two slow processes.

Try this at home

With permission, dig a 30 cm pit in a garden corner. Notice the dark top changing into lighter brown about a hand's-breadth down. If you keep going, you'll start hitting small stones — that's the C horizon. Few of us ever dig deep enough to see real bedrock, but every garden has its profile.

Looking ahead

Class 9 Geography (NCERT) covers India's six major soil types — alluvial (Indo-Gangetic plain), black (Deccan, perfect for cotton), red (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka), laterite (Western Ghats), arid (Rajasthan), and forest/mountain (Himalayas). Each is the result of a different combination of parent rock + climate + biology operating over the same horizon-forming processes you saw here. Class 11 Biology ('Mineral Nutrition') goes into how plants pick up specific elements (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, plus micronutrients) from these horizons — heavily tested in NEET.
