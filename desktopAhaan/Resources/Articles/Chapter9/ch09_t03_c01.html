Earthworms — Nature's Plough

How a soft-bodied creature rebuilds soil · Class 7 Science

The most important animal in the soil

Charles Darwin spent the last years of his life studying earthworms. He called them nature's plough and estimated that the worms in a single field can move many tonnes of soil per hectare per year. They are so important that an old farming proverb says: 'a good worm count is the best fertility test'.

What earthworms do (and how)

  • They eat soil. An earthworm ingests dirt + decaying leaves at one end and excretes pellets of dark, nutrient-rich castings at the other. Castings contain 5× more available nitrogen, 7× more phosphorus, and 11× more potassium than ordinary soil.
  • They tunnel. Their burrows let air, water, and roots reach 30–60 cm deep into the soil. Plant roots follow worm tunnels because it's the easiest path.
  • They mix layers. Worms ingest soil at one depth, deposit castings at another — slowly blending organic litter from the surface with mineral matter from below.
  • They breathe through skin. Earthworms have no lungs. Oxygen dissolves through their moist outer skin straight into their blood. That's why they MUST stay moist — and why they come to the surface after rain (their tunnels flooded).
A single hectare of healthy farmland can contain over 2 million earthworms, doing more soil engineering each year than any tractor.

Vermicompost — a modern use

Farmers and city dwellers now culture special earthworms (Eisenia foetida, the red wiggler) in bins, feeding them kitchen waste and harvesting the castings as organic fertiliser. Vermicompost is now sold in nurseries across India — a billion-rupee industry built on what worms naturally do.

Earthworms are an indicator species

Worm count is a fast, free way to grade soil health. Too few worms = chemically polluted, compacted, or too dry. Healthy soil has hundreds per square metre. Loss of worms = loss of fertility, even before plants visibly suffer.

Try this at home

Just after a rain shower, walk through a garden. You'll see earthworms on the surface — flooded out of their tunnels and breathing through their skin. Note the soft, segmented body and the slimy mucus that keeps it moist. Place one gently on dry concrete — it'll wriggle desperately to find moist soil. Return it to the garden.

Looking ahead

Class 11 Biology 'Structural Organisation in Animals' dissects the earthworm anatomically in detail — segmented body plan, closed circulatory system with five pairs of pseudo-hearts, nephridia for excretion, hermaphrodite reproduction. NEET asks earthworm morphology questions every year. Class 12 'Microbes in Human Welfare' covers vermicompost as a microbial-application biology topic.
