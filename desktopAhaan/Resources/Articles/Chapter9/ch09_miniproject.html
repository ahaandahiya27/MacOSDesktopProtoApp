Two Mini Projects for Chapter 9

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🌱 Percolation rate race — sand vs clay vs loam

~30 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • Three plastic cups with small holes punched in the bottom
  • Equal volume of dry sand, dry clay (from a riverbank, sieved), and loamy garden soil
  • Three measuring cups underneath
  • Water + a clock

Steps

  1. Place each soil sample (~100 g, same volume each) into the corresponding cup.
  2. Set the cups over the measuring cups to catch dripping water.
  3. Pour 100 mL water into each cup simultaneously.
  4. Time how long until water stops dripping out the bottom.
  5. Measure how much water collected below each (the rest is held by the soil).

👀 What you should see

Sand drains fastest — water collects below quickly, little held. Clay drains slowest — water either pools on top or barely drips through. Loam is in between.

Why it works: Particle size controls everything. Sand's large particles leave big air gaps (water races through). Clay's tiny particles pack tightly with microscopic gaps (water moves slowly + clay binds water tightly). Loam, mixing both, gives moderate drainage AND moderate retention — the sweet spot for most crops. This is why field farmers test percolation before deciding what to plant.

🪱 Vermicompost bin — make soil from kitchen waste

~2880 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A plastic bin or wooden box (~30 × 30 cm, with drainage holes)
  • Old newspaper for bedding
  • About 200 earthworms (Eisenia fetida — available at agricultural shops or nurseries)
  • Daily kitchen scraps: vegetable peels, fruit peels, used tea leaves, eggshells (NO onion, garlic, citrus, meat, dairy)
  • A few cups of garden soil
  • 6-8 weeks of patience

Steps

  1. Layer the bin: 5 cm shredded newspaper at bottom, 2 cm garden soil, then add the worms.
  2. Add a small handful of kitchen scraps every other day. Bury them under the bedding so flies don't visit.
  3. Keep moist but not wet (squeezed sponge feel).
  4. Keep in shade — direct sun cooks the worms.
  5. After 4-6 weeks, the bottom will be dark crumbly humus — vermicompost.
  6. Move worms to one side, scoop out the dark compost from the other side, refill bedding.

👀 What you should see

Kitchen scraps disappear within days. The bottom layer becomes dark, sweet-smelling, crumbly compost. The worm population grows from 200 to 1000+ in 3 months.

Why it works: Earthworms (Eisenia fetida is the species most used for composting) eat organic matter and excrete castings — concentrated humus enriched with their gut bacteria. The castings are richer in nutrients than the original food. They also aerate the bin as they tunnel. You're running a tiny version of what happens naturally in every forest topsoil. The compost you make is premium soil amendment — gardeners pay ₹100+ per kg for what you've made for free.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
