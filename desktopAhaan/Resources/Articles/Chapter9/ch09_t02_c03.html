Percolation rate and how to measure it

Percolation rate is the speed at which water seeps down through soil, measured in mL per minute.

≈ 2 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Pour a glass of water on dry sand vs dry clay. The sand drinks it up in seconds; the clay sits with a puddle on top for ages. The speed at which water moves DOWN through soil is its percolation rate. To measure it: pour a known volume of water (say 200 mL) onto a soil sample inside a tube and time how long it takes to disappear. Divide volume by time and you have percolation rate.

The textbook version

Percolation is the downward movement of water through soil. The percolation rate is calculated as: Percolation rate (mL/min) = Volume of water (mL) ÷ Percolation time (min). For example, if 200 mL of water takes 20 minutes to percolate, the rate is 200/20 = 10 mL/min. Sandy soil has a high percolation rate (water passes through quickly). Clay soil has a low percolation rate (water moves slowly). Loamy soil falls between the two. Percolation determines how often crops need watering, whether soil will get water-logged, and how much rain replenishes underground aquifers.

Why does this happen?

How fast water moves through soil depends on how big the gaps between particles are. Big gaps (sand) = fast flow. Small gaps (clay) = slow flow. Quantifying this with a number lets farmers and engineers compare soils and plan accordingly.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Watering a sandy potted plant — Watering once a day may not be enough — sand drains too fast. Sandy potted cactus, by contrast, only needs water every 1-2 weeks because cactus suits sandy soil. [Plants]
  • Why clay-bottomed ponds hold water — Village ponds traditionally have clay bottoms because clay's low percolation rate keeps water from seeping into the ground. Even temple kunds work this way. [Building]
  • Rainwater harvesting pits — When you dig a rainwater pit, you fill it with gravel + sand layers. Water percolates fast through these layers and reaches groundwater before evaporating or running off. [Weather]

Going deeper

Percolation is governed by Darcy's Law (1856): the flow rate depends on the soil's hydraulic conductivity (a material property), the cross-sectional area, and the pressure gradient. Hydraulic conductivity ranges from ~1 m/day for sandy soil to ~0.0001 m/day for clay — four orders of magnitude difference. India's groundwater crisis is partly a percolation problem: monsoon rains hit hard-packed urban surfaces and run off into drains rather than percolating into aquifers. Initiatives like Chennai's mandatory rainwater harvesting laws (2003) require permeable surfaces to allow recharge — a deliberate engineering of percolation.

Beyond the book

Mumbai's monsoon flooding has worsened over decades because concrete and tar surfaces have replaced soil — percolation is near zero in built-up city areas. Cities like Hyderabad and Chennai have mandated permeable paving and rainwater harvesting since the 2000s — engineering percolation back into urban surfaces.
