Soil

Chapter 9 · Class 7 Science

What is soil made of?

Pick up a handful of garden soil and you're holding a four-part recipe: broken-down rock, decayed plant and animal matter (humus), water, and air. Without any one of these, plants can't grow.

The soil profile

If you dig a deep pit, you'll see soil arranged in layers called horizons:

  • O horizon — fallen leaves and twigs on top.
  • A horizon (topsoil) — dark, soft, rich in humus and roots.
  • B horizon (subsoil) — lighter, holds minerals washed down from above.
  • C horizon — broken, weathered rock fragments.
  • R horizon (bedrock) — solid rock below everything.

Three soil types you'll meet

  • Sandy — large grains, water drains fast, holds little.
  • Clayey — tiny grains, water gets trapped, sticks when wet.
  • Loamy — a balanced mix. Goldilocks soil — perfect for most crops.
Paddy (rice) loves clay because clay holds standing water. Cotton thrives in mineral-rich black soil. Groundnut prefers loose sand.

Percolation: how fast water sinks in

Pour the same amount of water through sandy, loamy and clayey soils. Sand drains fastest, clay slowest, loam in between. Percolation rate tells farmers how often to irrigate.

Soil erosion — and how to stop it

Tree roots hold soil together. When forests are cut, rain washes away the fertile topsoil. It takes centuries to form even an inch of new topsoil, so we must protect it. Three classic techniques:

  • Terracing — flat steps on slopes.
  • Cover crops — keep the soil clothed all year.
  • Contour ploughing — across the slope, not down it.

Earthworms — nature's plough

Charles Darwin spent his last years studying earthworms. They eat soil and excrete castings rich in nutrients. Their tunnels let air and water reach deeper roots. Without earthworms, our farmland would lose much of its fertility within a single generation.
