Sand, Clay and Loam — Why Particle Size Matters

The three soils of the world, and the spaces between their grains · Class 7 Science

Soil is mostly just rock particles

About 45% of soil by volume is mineral matter — broken-up rock particles. About 25% is water, 25% is air, and 5% is organic matter. The exact proportions vary, but the IMPORTANT number is the particle size.

The official size classes

  • Sand — particles 0.05–2 mm across. You can see and feel each grain. The biggest of the three.
  • Silt — particles 0.002–0.05 mm. Feel like flour or talc.
  • Clay — particles smaller than 0.002 mm. So tiny you can't see individual grains; they feel sticky when wet because the particles have so much surface area.

A soil's name depends on which it contains the most of. Loam is a roughly equal mix of all three — the most fertile combination.

The difference between a single grain of sand and a single particle of clay is the same as the difference between a basketball and a grain of rice. Yet the SAME chemical material (mostly silicates) can become either one, depending on how much it's weathered.

Why particle size decides everything

Smaller particles → bigger total surface area for the same volume of soil. Clay has thousands of times more surface area per gram than sand. This affects three crucial properties:

  • Water-holding capacity: water clings to particle surfaces. More surface = more water held.
  • Nutrient-holding capacity: dissolved minerals bond to surfaces. Clay holds dozens of times more nutrients per gram than sand.
  • Drainage: bigger gaps between sand particles mean water drains fast. Tiny gaps in clay slow drainage to a trickle.

The texture triangle

Soil scientists classify soils using a triangular chart with three corners (sand, silt, clay). Any soil's position on the triangle tells you its type:

  • Loam: 40% sand, 40% silt, 20% clay (roughly).
  • Sandy loam: more sand, less clay — good for groundnuts.
  • Clay loam: more clay — holds water well, good for paddy.
  • Silty loam: more silt — common in river valleys.

Try this at home

Take three handfuls of dry soil from different spots (garden, vacant lot, construction site). For each: pinch a small amount between your fingers. Sand feels gritty. Silt feels smooth like flour. Clay feels slick and forms a ribbon when squeezed wet. You've just done the standard field test that soil scientists use.
