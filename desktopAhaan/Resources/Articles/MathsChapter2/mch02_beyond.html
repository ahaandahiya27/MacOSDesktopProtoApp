Beyond the Book — Who Invented the Signs?

The +, −, ×, and = you write every day are inventions with birthdays. Here is where they came from.

≈ 6 min read · curious-kid level

An arithmetic expression like 3 + 4 × 2 is really a tiny sentence. And like any language, its punctuation had to be invented by somebody. Read one idea at a time.

1. The plus and minus signs are about 500 years old

The symbols + and − first appeared in print in a German arithmetic book by Johannes Widmann in 1489. Before that, people wrote "and" or the Latin word plus in full. So for most of human history, no one had ever seen a "+" sign.

💡 Try this

Write 7 + 5 − 3 using only words, the way someone in the year 1400 would have to: "seven and five, less three." Now you can feel why a short symbol was such a good idea.

2. The equals sign was born from two parallel lines

In 1557, the Welsh mathematician Robert Recorde got tired of writing "is equal to" again and again. He drew two parallel lines — = — explaining that "no two things can be more equal" than a pair of parallel lines of the same length. We have used his sign ever since.

3. Times and divide came later

The multiplication cross × was popularised by William Oughtred around 1631. The division sign ÷ (called the obelus) came into common use a little after that. Notice that mathematicians often avoid the × sign in algebra, because it looks too much like the letter x — that is why we write 3n instead of 3 × n.

4. Why order of operations is a rule we agreed on

When you read 3 + 4 × 2, you do the multiplication first and get 11, not 14. That is not a law of nature — it is a convention, an agreement, so that everyone in the world reads the same expression the same way. Brackets exist to override the agreement: (3 + 4) × 2 = 14. The brackets are you telling the reader, "do this part first."

💡 Try this

Find a way to put brackets in 2 + 3 × 4 so the answer becomes 20 instead of 14. Then try to make it equal 14 with no brackets at all.
