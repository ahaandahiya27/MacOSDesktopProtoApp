Beyond the Book — The Cleverest Old Trick for Common Factors

Finding the HCF of two numbers has a 2,300-year-old shortcut — and LCM explains why some insects only appear once every 13 or 17 years.

≈ 6 min read · curious-kid level

Highest Common Factor and Least Common Multiple sound like dry vocabulary, but they answer real questions: what is the biggest tile that fits a floor exactly, and when will two repeating events line up again? Read one idea at a time.

1. Euclid's lightning-fast method for the HCF

To find the highest common factor of two numbers, Euclid (around 300 BCE) gave a trick still used in computers today: keep replacing the bigger number with its remainder when divided by the smaller, until the remainder is 0. The last non-zero number is the HCF. For 12 and 16: 16 leaves remainder 4 with 12; 12 leaves remainder 0 with 4 — so the HCF is 4.

💡 Try this

Use Euclid's method on 48 and 18. (48 → remainder 12, 18 → remainder 6, 12 → remainder 0.) The HCF is 6 — found in three quick steps, with no need to list every factor.

2. The HCF is the biggest tile that fits

If a room is 12 ft by 16 ft and you want the largest square tile that fits a whole number of times along both walls, the side of that tile is exactly the HCF of 12 and 16 — that is, 4 ft. HCF questions are really "what is the biggest equal piece that divides everything evenly?"

3. The LCM is when repeating events meet

If one bell rings every 6 minutes and another every 8, they ring together after the least common multiple of 6 and 8 — which is 24 minutes. LCM answers every "when will these line up again?" question, from traffic lights to planets to bus timetables.

4. Prime numbers and the cicadas

Some insects called periodical cicadas stay underground and emerge all at once only every 13 or 17 years — both prime numbers. Biologists think a prime-numbered cycle is hard for predators (with shorter cycles) to line up with, because the LCM of a prime and a smaller number is large. Nature appears to be using LCM to stay safe.

💡 Try this

Two friends jog a track: one finishes a lap every 4 minutes, the other every 6. After how many minutes do they cross the start line together? Find the LCM of 4 and 6 — it is 12 — and you have the answer.
