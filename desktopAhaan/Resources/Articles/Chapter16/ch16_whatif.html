Three "What Ifs?" About Water: A Precious Resource

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if you could turn salt water into fresh water cheaply?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Coastal water shortages would end. Israel and Saudi Arabia already do this with desalination plants — but it's energy-expensive (~3 kWh per cubic metre). If we found a cheap way (solar-powered or new technology), Chennai, Mumbai, Visakhapatnam, all coastal Indian cities could get unlimited water. India is building large desal plants in Chennai and Mumbai already. The problem is cost — saltwater desal is 5-10× more expensive than river water. Breakthrough technology (graphene membranes, solar-thermal desal) could change everything.

What if 2 — What if monsoons failed for 5 years in a row?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: India's economy would collapse. ~60% of agriculture is rain-fed; ~85% of rural drinking water comes from groundwater (which is recharged by monsoon). Food prices would spike; food security collapse; rural-urban migration would overwhelm cities. The Bengal Famine (1943) and the Maharashtra drought (1972) showed what just 1-2 bad monsoon years can do. Five years would be civilizational. Solution being built: India's National Water Mission, river-linking projects, drip irrigation subsidies, groundwater recharge mandates. Race against climate change.

What if 3 — What if every Indian home harvested its rainwater?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Indian urban roofs total ~50 billion sq m. At ~800 mm average rain, that's 40,000 billion litres of harvestable water per year. India's total annual water use is ~700,000 billion litres. So rooftop harvesting alone could supply ~6% of total water needs and 30% of urban needs. Tamil Nadu mandated rainwater harvesting in 2003; Chennai's groundwater levels stopped falling within years. If every Indian metro followed: Chennai's 2019-style crises eliminated. The math works. The policy needs to be enforced.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
