Drip Irrigation — Water at the Roots

How an Israeli idea revolutionised dry-region farming · Class 7 Science

The water-waste problem

Traditional irrigation methods waste enormous amounts of water:

  • Flood irrigation (the most common method worldwide): water poured over the entire field. Up to 50% evaporates before plants can absorb it.
  • Sprinkler irrigation: water sprayed over the field. Wind blows some away; sunlight evaporates more. Wastes 25–30%.
  • Drip irrigation: water dripped directly at each plant's roots. Wastes less than 10%.

The idea

In the 1950s, an Israeli engineer named Simcha Blass noticed that the largest tree on his arid plot grew next to a leaky pipe — getting just a tiny but steady drip of water. He realised: plants don't need a flood, they need a slow, continuous trickle right at their roots. He patented the system in 1959.

How drip irrigation works

  • A network of thin plastic pipes runs through the field.
  • At every plant, a small hole called an emitter drips water at a controlled rate — typically 1–4 litres per hour.
  • Water seeps slowly into the soil RIGHT at the root zone.
  • Almost zero evaporation. Almost zero runoff. Almost zero weed competition (since the area between plants stays dry).
A typical Indian farm switching from flood to drip irrigation cuts water usage by 30–60% AND increases crop yield by 20–50% (because plants don't suffer from waterlogging or drought stress). The same plot grows more crops with less water.

What drip systems can also deliver

Modern drip systems include fertigation — soluble fertilisers mixed into the water and delivered with the drip. So plants get water + nutrients precisely where and when they need them. Almost like a hospital IV drip for the field.

Where drip works best

  • Vegetables & orchard crops — tomatoes, chillies, grapes, mangoes, bananas, pomegranates.
  • Arid regions — Rajasthan, Gujarat, Israel, California. Where every drop counts.
  • Hilly or uneven land — flood irrigation doesn't work on slopes; drip works perfectly.

Why isn't it everywhere yet?

The pipe network and emitters cost money to install. For small Indian farmers, the initial cost is the barrier. Government subsidies under the Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY) now cover up to 55% of installation costs for smallholders.

Try this at home

Take an empty 1-litre plastic bottle. Punch one tiny pinhole near the bottom with a needle. Fill with water and place it next to a potted plant, hole touching the soil. The bottle slowly drips water — a homemade drip irrigation system that needs no electricity and uses less water than your watering can.
