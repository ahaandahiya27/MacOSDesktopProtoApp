The Water Table and Aquifers

What's beneath your feet — hidden water that wells tap · Class 7 Science

The hidden ocean under the ground

If you dig deep anywhere on land, you eventually hit water. The depth at which the soil becomes fully saturated is the water table. Above it, the soil has both air and some moisture; below, every pore is filled with water.

Three zones underground

  • Aeration zone (unsaturated) — surface to water table. Most pores have air; moisture seeps through after rain.
  • Capillary fringe — a thin band just above the water table where water rises by capillary action.
  • Saturation zone — below the water table. ALL pores are water-filled.

The boundary between unsaturated and saturated is the water table. It's not flat — it follows the surface topography but in a smoothed-out way, dipping where land is high and rising where land is low. Lakes and rivers are basically the water table peeking above the surface.

What's an aquifer?

An aquifer is a layer of rock or sediment in the saturation zone that holds AND releases groundwater easily. Sand, gravel, fractured rock, and limestone make good aquifers. Solid rock and dense clay are aquicludes — they hold water but don't release it.

The Indo-Gangetic plain — the alluvial belt from Punjab through Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and West Bengal — sits atop one of the largest aquifer systems in the world. Hundreds of millions of Indians depend on it for drinking water and irrigation. It's also one of the most rapidly depleting aquifers globally.

Two kinds of aquifers

  • Unconfined aquifer — open to the surface. Recharged directly by rain seeping in. Water table fluctuates seasonally. Most wells tap unconfined aquifers.
  • Confined aquifer — sandwiched between impermeable layers above and below. Water enters only at distant recharge zones (sometimes hundreds of km away). Often under pressure — drill into one and water gushes up on its own (artesian well).

How wells work

  • Open well (kuan) — a hand-dug shaft, 5-30 m deep. Reaches the unconfined aquifer. Pulled up by rope and bucket.
  • Tube well — a narrow pipe drilled with machinery, 30-300 m deep. Pumped electrically. Most modern irrigation uses these.
  • Artesian well — drilled into a confined aquifer with enough pressure that water flows up naturally without pumping.

The depletion crisis

India pumps more groundwater than any other country — about a quarter of the global total. In Punjab, Haryana, and parts of Tamil Nadu, the water table is dropping by 0.5-1 metre PER YEAR. Wells must be drilled deeper and deeper. Eventually, the aquifer collapses or becomes saline.

Counter-measures include rainwater harvesting (Ch 16 / Topic 2), recharge wells, the Atal Bhujal Yojana programme, and water-efficient crops.

Try this at home

Fill a clear jar half-way with sand. Slowly pour water into the side. Watch the water rise from the bottom — the visible boundary between dry sand on top and wet sand below IS the water table. Pour more, the table rises. Tilt the jar, the table on one side falls. Now drill a 'well' (a straw stuck in vertically) — water can be 'pumped' (sucked through the straw) only down to the level of the surrounding water table.
