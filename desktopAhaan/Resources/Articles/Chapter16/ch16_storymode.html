Story Mode — Water: A Precious Resource in Five Scenes

5 short scenes from real life that bring this chapter to a kitchen table, a garden, a classroom — somewhere you can picture.

≈ 10 min read · narrative-tier

Textbook examples can feel abstract. These five scenes don't happen in a textbook — they happen in places you go. Read them in order; each scene tightens the chapter's big idea by another notch.

Scene 1 — Cherrapunji — wettest place on Earth, yet thirsty in March

Cherrapunji in Meghalaya receives ~12,000 mm of rain a year (highest in the world). But by March, taps run dry. Why? All the rain falls in 4-5 monsoon months; the rest of the year is bone-dry. The water flows down steep slopes into Bangladesh before it can be stored. Even Khasi villagers have to walk hours to collect water in summer. Lesson: TOTAL rainfall isn't the same as USABLE water. Conservation is about TIMING — capturing the monsoon for the dry months.

Scene 2 — Chennai's Day Zero in 2019

In June 2019, Chennai (population 9 million) ran out of municipal water. The four reservoirs that supplied the city dried up after two failed monsoons. Tanker trucks queued for hours. Government schools shut. IT companies sent staff to work from home. The rich bought water from private tankers; the poor walked km to government supply trucks. This is a preview of the climate-change water crisis. Indian cities are not built for water scarcity — they need urgent rainwater harvesting and aquifer recharge.

Scene 3 — Chand Baori — Rajasthan's 1200-year-old water solution

Chand Baori (Abhaneri, Rajasthan), built in 800 CE, is a stepwell descending 13 storeys with 3500 narrow steps in a zigzag pattern. Rainwater is collected from a vast watershed, stored deep in the well, kept cool by being underground. Even in 45°C summers, the water at the bottom is cool. Built before electricity, before pumps — just by digging deep and trusting gravity. India built hundreds of such baolis (Rajasthan), tankas (Bikaner), and step-tanks (Gujarat) — pre-modern engineering masterpieces.

Scene 4 — Punjab tube wells running dry — the groundwater bank account

Punjab grows wheat and rice that need huge water — supported by deep tube wells pumping groundwater. Result: the water table has fallen from 2 m below ground (1970s) to 25 m below ground (today) in many districts. Farmers now drill deeper every few years. Soon, no water at any depth. This is OVERDRAFT — withdrawing faster than rain can recharge. Like a bank account you keep withdrawing from without depositing. Sustainable rate would be ~25% of current usage. Painful transition coming.

Scene 5 — Drip irrigation in Maharashtra grape orchards

Israeli engineers pioneered DRIP IRRIGATION — thin pipes delivering water DROP BY DROP to each plant's roots. Maharashtra grape farmers adopted it in the 1990s; now millions of hectares run on drip. Compared to flood irrigation (which loses 50% to evaporation/runoff), drip uses 30% of the water for the same yield. The plant gets exactly what it needs, no waste. Government's PMKSY scheme subsidises drip installation for farmers. A single technology shift can save thousands of crores of litres per year.

💬 What this is for

Concrete examples are how your brain anchors abstract ideas. Pick the scene you remember best after reading. The next time you see it in real life, you'll remember the chapter.
