Rainwater Harvesting Systems

Catching free water from the sky · Class 7 Science

Free water falls from the sky every monsoon

The Indian monsoon delivers 80% of India's annual rainfall in just 4 months (June–September). Without storage, most of that water just runs off into rivers and into the sea. Rainwater harvesting is the practice of catching that rain before it runs away — and using it for the dry 8 months that follow.

Two main forms of rainwater harvesting

  • Surface storage — rain is caught in tanks, reservoirs, ponds, or check dams. Used over the dry months.
  • Groundwater recharge — rain is directed into the soil, refilling underground aquifers that wells tap. Helps long-term water-table levels.

How rooftop harvesting works

  1. Rain falls on your roof.
  2. Gutters along the roof edge catch the runoff.
  3. Pipes carry the water down to either a storage tank or a recharge pit.
  4. A simple first-flush diverter throws away the first few litres (which carry the dirt and bird droppings off the roof).
  5. The clean rain is filtered through sand + gravel and stored.

A 100 sq.m rooftop in Mumbai (annual rainfall 2,200 mm) can collect about 220,000 litres of water per year — enough to supply a family of four for half a year, or to recharge a household well.

Tamil Nadu made rooftop rainwater harvesting MANDATORY for all buildings in 2001. Within 5 years, Chennai's water table rose by 4 metres in many neighbourhoods, and the city's chronic water shortage eased significantly.

Traditional Indian techniques (still working)

  • Khadins (Rajasthan) — earthen embankments across slopes that trap monsoon runoff for farming downstream.
  • Johads (Rajasthan) — village ponds that store rainwater and recharge surrounding wells.
  • Kuls (Himachal & Ladakh) — channels carrying glacial meltwater to farms.
  • Eris (Tamil Nadu) — cascading tank systems where each tank's overflow fills the next.
  • Bawdis / Vavs (Gujarat & Rajasthan) — stepwells that reach the water table even in drought.
  • Bhandaras (Maharashtra) — small check dams built across seasonal streams.

Many of these are 500–1,500 years old and still functional. They represent some of the oldest water-engineering knowledge in the world.

What you need at home (minimum)

  • Gutters along your roof edges.
  • Downpipes leading to ground level.
  • A first-flush diverter (a simple T-junction that wastes the first 20 litres).
  • A storage tank OR a recharge pit (gravel-filled hole in the ground).

Total cost for a typical Indian home: ₹15,000–40,000. Payback in 2–4 years from reduced municipal-water bills.

Try this at home

Next rain shower, place a clean wide-mouth bucket directly under the rain (clear of dust). Time it: 15 minutes of moderate rain typically fills it 5–10 cm. Multiply that by the area of your house's roof and the duration of your monsoon. The number is shockingly large.

Looking ahead

Class 10 Geography 'Resources and Development' tests rainwater harvesting traditions across India. Class 11 Geography goes deeper into the hydrological cycle and watershed management. Class 12 Economics covers India's Atal Bhujal Yojana (groundwater management programme launched 2019) and the Jal Jeevan Mission (piped water to all rural homes by 2024). CBSE boards consistently ask 'name traditional Indian water-harvesting structures' as a 3-mark question.
