Drip and Sprinkler — Modern Irrigation Methods

Saving water while growing more food · Class 7 Science

The case against flood irrigation

Traditional flood irrigation drowns entire fields. Up to 60% of the water evaporates or runs off without reaching plant roots. In a water-stressed country like India, this is unaffordable. Yet most Indian farms still use it because canal water is cheap or free.

Drip irrigation — water exactly where it's needed

A network of thin black pipes runs along plant rows. Each plant gets a tiny outlet (an emitter) that releases water drop-by-drop directly at the roots. Water savings: 30–70%. Yield increase: 20–90% (because plants are not water-stressed).

Sprinkler irrigation — rain on demand

Pipes feed rotating sprinkler heads that spray a fine mist. Suits sandy and uneven land where flood irrigation fails. Common for sugarcane, wheat, and lawns/parks. Water savings: 30–50% vs flood.

Why farmers don't all switch

Drip systems cost ₹40,000-₹80,000 per acre upfront. Small farmers can't afford this without subsidy. The Government's Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY) gives up to 80% subsidy for marginal farmers — adoption is growing fast in Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Gujarat.

Israel — most of the country is desert — feeds itself and exports food using almost entirely drip irrigation. Israeli drip technology is now in 100+ countries, including India through partnerships like the one with Maharashtra's Jain Irrigation.

Try this at home

Take a 2-litre plastic bottle. Make 4 pinholes near the bottom. Bury it next to a potted plant, leaving the cap visible. Fill the bottle every 2 days. Water drips out slowly — a homemade drip system.
