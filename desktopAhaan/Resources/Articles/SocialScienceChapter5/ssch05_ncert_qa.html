Q1. What are the features of an empire, and how is it different from a kingdom? Explain. (NCERT page 114)

An empire is a collection of many smaller kingdoms or territories ruled by one powerful emperor, while a kingdom is a single territory under one king. The word 'empire' comes from the Latin imperium, 'supreme power'. In an empire, the smaller kings usually keep their thrones as tributaries who pay tribute and accept the emperor's overlordship. An empire is held together by six features: it maintains an army; runs an administration with officials to collect taxes and keep order; makes laws and issues currency, weights and measures; controls access to resources such as mines, forests and farm produce; encourages art, literature, religions and centres of learning; and maintains communication networks like roads and river and sea routes. The key difference from a kingdom is the layered authority of one emperor over many tributary rulers, plus this elaborate machinery of control.

Q2. What are some important factors for the transition from kingdoms to empires? (NCERT page 114)

Several factors helped kingdoms grow into empires. First, abundant natural resources — fertile land, forests for timber and elephants, and minerals like iron ore — gave the foundation, as in resource-rich Magadha. Iron tools (ploughs) increased food production, creating a surplus that freed people for crafts, trade and soldiering, while iron weapons strengthened armies. Rivers such as the Ganga and Son provided cheap transport and boosted trade, which filled the treasury through taxes. Controlling trade routes and supporting guilds further increased wealth. A strong, well-funded army let a kingdom conquer or subdue its neighbours, and able rulers and advisers (like Chandragupta Maurya and Kauṭilya) combined these advantages with good strategy and administration. Together, surplus wealth, military strength, trade and capable leadership turned a kingdom into an empire.

Q3. In early Indian history, the Mauryas are considered important. State your reasons. (NCERT page 114)

The Mauryas are important because they created the first vast empire in Indian history, stretching from the northwest across the northern plains to the Deccan and, under Ashoka, covering almost the entire subcontinent. Founded by Chandragupta Maurya around 321 BCE with the help of Kauṭilya, the empire left a legacy that lasted for centuries: strong trade routes and economic systems, extensive use of coins, well-planned cities like Pāṭaliputra, and an elaborate administration with food-security measures such as granaries. They promoted art and architecture — the polished stone pillars, the Sarnath Lion Capital (now India's national emblem), and stūpas like the Great Stūpa at Sanchi. Ashoka spread the idea of dhamma through his rock and pillar edicts and helped carry Buddhism far beyond India. For all these reasons the Mauryas remain one of the greatest empires of Indian history.

Q4. What were some of Kauṭilya's key ideas? Which ones can you observe even today in the world around us? (NCERT page 114)

Kauṭilya, in his Arthaśhāstra ('the science of governance and economics'), set out how a kingdom should be run. His key ideas include the saptānga — the seven parts of a state (the king, ministers and officials, the territory and its people, forts, the treasury, the army and justice system, and allies) — all of which must work together. He stressed strong administration, law and order, laws against corruption with set punishments, careful management of the economy and trade, urban planning, agriculture, and above all the welfare of the people: 'In the happiness of his subjects lies the king's happiness.' Many of these ideas survive today: modern countries still have a head of state, ministers and a bureaucracy, defined territory and population, a treasury (finance ministry), armed forces and courts, and alliances with other nations. The emphasis on people's welfare, anti-corruption laws, and caring for the countryside and public works (roads, bridges) is clearly visible in modern government too.
