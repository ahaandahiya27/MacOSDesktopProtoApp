What is an Empire?

The meaning of 'empire', how it differs from a kingdom, the role of tributaries and emperors, and the six features that every empire must maintain to survive.

  1. Empire and Emperor: The Meaning of ImperiumAn empire is a collection of smaller kingdoms ruled by one powerful emperor, and the word comes from the Latin 'imperium', meaning 'supreme power'.
  2. Tributaries, Vassals and OverlordshipA tributary (or vassal) is a ruler who has submitted to an emperor and pays tribute as a sign of loyalty and respect.
  3. The Six Features of an EmpireEvery empire maintains an army, an administration, laws and currency, control over resources, support for art and learning, and communication networks.

Trade, Routes and Guilds

Why economic power is essential to an empire, what goods ancient India traded, the great trade routes like the Uttarapatha and Dakṣhiṇapatha, and the self-organising guilds (śhrenīs) of traders and craftsmen.

  1. Economic Power and Trade RoutesEmpires depended on trade and economic power, so controlling trade routes across and beyond their territory was hugely important.
  2. Guilds (Śhrenīs): India's Self-Organising SocietyGuilds, called śhrenīs, were powerful associations of traders, craftsmen, moneylenders or farmers that made their own rules and governed themselves.

The Rise of Magadha and the Nandas

How fertile land, iron, forests, elephants and river trade let Magadha grow into India's first great power, the role of King Ajātaśhatru, and the rise of Mahāpadma Nanda and the Nanda dynasty.

  1. Why Magadha Rose to PowerMagadha rose because of its fertile Ganga-plains land, iron ore and forests, war elephants, and the trade advantage of the Ganga and Son rivers.
  2. The Nanda Dynasty: Mahāpadma and Dhana NandaAround the 5th century BCE Mahāpadma Nanda founded the Nanda dynasty, unifying many kingdoms; its last ruler, Dhana Nanda, was rich but unpopular.

Alexander, Chandragupta and Kauṭilya

Alexander of Macedonia's campaign into northwest India and his clash with Porus, the rise of Chandragupta Maurya, and the genius of his mentor Kauṭilya — author of the Arthaśhāstra and the saptānga theory of the state.

  1. Alexander's Campaign in Northwest IndiaAlexander of Macedonia conquered the Persian Empire and reached northwest India around 326 BCE, defeating King Porus before his tired soldiers refused to go further.
  2. Chandragupta Maurya and Kauṭilya (Chāṇakya)Around 321 BCE Chandragupta Maurya founded the Maurya empire with the help of his brilliant mentor Kauṭilya, also called Chāṇakya or Viṣhnugupta.
  3. Kauṭilya's Arthaśhāstra and the SaptāngaIn his Arthaśhāstra, Kauṭilya described how to run a kingdom and named its seven essential parts, the saptānga.

Ashoka, Dhamma and the Fragile Nature of Empires

Ashoka's vast empire and the Kalinga war that changed him, his policy of dhamma and his famous edicts, the Sarnath lion capital that became India's national emblem, life in the Mauryan period, and why even great empires eventually decline.

  1. Ashoka and the Kalinga WarAshoka (268–232 BCE), Chandragupta's grandson, expanded the empire across almost all of India, but the horror of the Kalinga war turned him toward peace and non-violence.
  2. Dhamma and Ashoka's EdictsAshoka spread the idea of dhamma — moral and ethical duty — through edicts engraved on rocks and pillars, mostly in the Prakrit language and Brahmi script.
  3. The Fragile Nature of EmpiresEmpires are a paradox — they can bring unity and prosperity, yet because they rest on war and force, and distant regions tend to break away, they are fragile and eventually decline.
  4. Mauryan Art, the Lion Capital and Life in the PeriodThe Mauryas left magnificent art — including the Sarnath lion capital that became India's national emblem — and Mauryan cities were well-planned, prosperous and food-secure.
