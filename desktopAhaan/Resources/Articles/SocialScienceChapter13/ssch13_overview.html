Chapter Overview

Farming: India's Oldest and Most Important Work

What agriculture means, how much of India's economy and people depend on it, and the deep prehistoric and ancient roots of Indian farming.

  1. What Agriculture MeansAgriculture is the work of preparing the soil, growing crops and rearing animals, and in its broad sense it also covers forestry, fisheries and more.
  2. The Backbone of the Indian EconomyAgriculture and allied activities give a little over 18% of India's GDP and employ about 46% of its workers, and more than 75% of farm workers are women.
  3. Echoes from the Past: Ancient Roots of Indian FarmingIndian farming goes back to prehistory — rice in the Ganga Plain by the 7th–8th millennium BCE, barley and millets at Mehrgarh, and a rich record in ancient texts.

Climate, Monsoons and the Cropping Seasons

How India's diverse climates and the two monsoons decide what grows where and when, and how farmers organise the year into the kharif, rabi and zaid seasons.

  1. The Monsoons and AgricultureIndia's farming depends on two monsoons — the southwest monsoon (June–September) and the northeast monsoon (October–December).
  2. India's Climatic Diversity and Agroclimatic ZonesIndia has seven main climate types and is divided into 15 agroclimatic zones to plan what, when and how to grow crops.
  3. The Three Cropping Seasons: Kharif, Rabi and ZaidIndian farming runs on three cropping seasons — kharif (monsoon), rabi (winter) and zaid (summer) — each with its own crops.

Soil and Water: The Foundations of Cultivation

How soil forms, India's six major soil types and the crops they suit, the difference between rain-fed and irrigated farming, and India's rich heritage of water structures.

  1. How Soil Forms and India's Six Major SoilsSoil is the thin top layer of the crust that supports plants; India has six major soil types — alluvial, black, red, laterite, desert and mountain/alpine.
  2. Rain-Fed Versus Irrigated AgricultureRain-fed farming relies entirely on rainfall, while irrigated farming supplies water to crops by artificial means.
  3. India's Heritage of Water StructuresIndia has a very long history of irrigation, from the Indus civilisation to community systems like phad and bamboo drip irrigation and the ancient Kallanai dam.

Nurturing Soil and Seeds

How farmers keep soil healthy with traditional and modern conservation methods, and how seeds are passed down through families or bought from companies.

  1. Keeping Soil Healthy: Conservation MethodsFarmers protect and renew soil using traditional methods like crop rotation, multiple cropping, contour ploughing and organic manure, and modern ones like terracing and afforestation.
  2. Seeds: Saved Traditions and Bought VarietiesSeeds have long been saved and shared within families and communities, but today many farmers buy high-yielding seeds from companies, sometimes creating dependence.

Traditional Wisdom, Modern Innovations and Today's Challenges

The contrast between traditional and contemporary farming, the Green Revolution and its costs, the search for sustainable and organic farming, the government's support, and the challenges farmers face today.

  1. Traditional Versus Contemporary FarmingTraditional farming works with nature as a whole soil-plant system, while contemporary farming uses modern technology, machinery and new techniques.
  2. The Green Revolution and Its CostsThe Green Revolution of the 1960s–70s used high-yielding seeds, irrigation, chemicals and machinery to make India self-sufficient in food, but caused long-term environmental and health harm.
  3. Sustainable and Organic FarmingSustainable agriculture combines the productivity of modern methods with the eco-friendly wisdom of tradition, including organic farming that avoids chemicals.
  4. Challenges Farmers Face and Government SupportFarmers struggle with shrinking landholdings, costly machinery, climate change and debt, while the government provides seeds, advice, cheap power, crop insurance and crop purchase.
