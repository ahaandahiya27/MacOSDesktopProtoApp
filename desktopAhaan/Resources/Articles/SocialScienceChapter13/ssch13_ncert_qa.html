Farming, or agriculture, is one of humankind's oldest occupations and the backbone of the Indian economy. This chapter follows the long story of Indian farming

Q1. Why do farmers in Kerala grow rice while farmers in Punjab grow mostly wheat? What would happen if they swapped? (NCERT page 27)

Crops follow the local climate, soil and water. Kerala has a wet, tropical climate with abundant water, which suits rice (paddy), a crop that needs plenty of standing water. Punjab has a subtropical climate and very fertile alluvial plains well suited to wheat, the main rabi (winter) crop, supported by irrigation. If they swapped, each crop would do poorly: rice would lack the constant water it needs in Punjab's drier winter conditions, and wheat would struggle in Kerala's heavy, year-round wetness. This shows why certain crops thrive only in regions whose climate, soil and water match their needs.

Q2. Why do certain crops thrive in specific regions? (NCERT page 27)

Because the growth of a crop depends on several factors that vary from place to place: the climate (temperature, sunshine and rainfall), the type of soil, the availability of water, and the terrain. India has seven main climate types and six major soil types, and the government even divides the country into 15 agroclimatic zones. A crop thrives where these conditions match its needs — for example, cotton in moisture-holding black soil, rice in wet regions with fertile alluvial soil, and tea in the rainy hills. Where the conditions do not match, a crop either cannot grow or needs heavy irrigation and inputs.

Q3. How has modern technology helped farmers? (NCERT page 28)

Modern technology has helped farmers in many ways. High-yielding varieties (HYV) of seeds and chemical fertilisers, introduced by the Green Revolution, greatly increased food production, especially of wheat and rice. Machinery such as tractors and harvesters made ploughing, sowing and harvesting faster and easier. Modern irrigation methods like drip and sprinkler irrigation use water far more efficiently, which matters as groundwater falls. Drones can assess soil moisture, and digital technology and cold storage give farmers better access to information and markets, helping them get fair prices. Hydroponics even allows farming without soil.

Q4. Name some challenges that farmers face today. What might be their impact for people? (NCERT page 28)

Farmers face shrinking landholdings (the average is now about three-fourths of a hectare) as land is divided among family members, which lowers their income and makes machinery hard to use. They face climate change, with untimely rains, droughts and floods that destroy crops. Costly machinery, market difficulties, and the resulting burden of debt are further challenges; hardship has driven many farmers to abandon agriculture. The impact on people is serious: it threatens farmers' livelihoods and quality of life, and because farmers feed the whole country, it can endanger the nation's food supply and raise food prices for everyone.
