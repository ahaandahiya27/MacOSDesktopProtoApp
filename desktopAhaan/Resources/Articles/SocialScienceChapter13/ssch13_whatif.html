What If?

Farming, or agriculture, is one of humankind's oldest occupations and the backbone of the Indian economy. This chapter follows the long story of Indian farming

≈ 9 min · read-mode · 🌏 Social Science

History, geography and society are full of 'what if' turning points. Think about each question yourself first — then open the panel to compare your reasoning.

What if the southwest monsoon failed for two years in a row?

Think first, then open to compare

The kharif (monsoon) crops, which depend on the timely arrival of the southwest monsoon, would largely fail. Rain-fed farms would suffer most, and even irrigated farms would struggle as rivers, ponds and groundwater ran low. Food grain production, especially of rice, would fall sharply, and prices would rise. Many foods would become scarce or expensive in our meals, and rain-dependent farmers could face heavy losses and debt. This is exactly why farmers use drought-resistant crops, water harvesting and irrigation to reduce their dependence on the monsoon.

What if every farmer in India suddenly switched to fully organic farming overnight?

Think first, then open to compare

Organic farming protects the soil, water and creatures like bees, so in the long run the land would grow healthier. But in the short run yields would probably drop, because organic methods often produce less than chemical-fed high-yielding crops, and soil takes time to rebuild its natural fertility after years of chemicals. There could be a dip in food production during the transition. That is why the chapter suggests balancing modern productivity with traditional, sustainable practices rather than switching abruptly — and why Sikkim's planned, state-wide move to organic over years (not overnight) is so notable.

What if all farmers stopped saving seeds and bought new ones every year?

Think first, then open to compare

Saved, traditional seeds carry generations of local adaptation — to a region's pests, soil and climate — and they cost nothing. If everyone bought new seeds yearly, farmers would depend on companies and spend more, and many hardy local varieties could be lost forever, shrinking crop diversity. That diversity is an insurance policy: when a disease wipes out one variety, another may resist it. Losing it makes the whole food system more fragile.
