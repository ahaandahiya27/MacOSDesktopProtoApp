Beyond the Book — When Numbers Went Negative

For centuries the greatest mathematicians refused to believe a number could be less than nothing. Here is how negatives won.

≈ 6 min read · curious-kid level

Adding and subtracting positives feels natural. But the idea that −3 is a genuine number — not just a mistake — took a very long time for the world to accept. Read one idea at a time.

1. India wrote the first rules for negatives

In 628 CE, the Indian mathematician Brahmagupta wrote down clear rules for working with negative numbers in his book Brahmasphutasiddhanta. He pictured them as debts and positives as fortunes: a debt taken away from a fortune adds to it, two debts combine into a bigger debt. These are exactly the integer rules you are learning now.

💡 Try this

Read "subtract −3 from 5" as "take away a debt of 3 from a fortune of 5." Taking away a debt makes you richer — so the answer is 8. Brahmagupta would have agreed.

2. Ancient China used coloured rods

Long ago, Chinese mathematicians calculated with small counting rods laid out on a board. In the classic text known as the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, they used red rods for positive amounts and black rods for negative ones — a colour code for plus and minus, centuries before a "−" symbol existed.

3. Europe called them "absurd" numbers

Even as late as the 1600s and 1700s, many European mathematicians distrusted negatives, calling them "false" or "absurd" numbers because you cannot hold −3 apples in your hand. The idea only became fully respectable when the number line made it obvious: negatives are simply the numbers to the left of zero, perfectly mirroring the positives on the right.

4. Why a minus times a minus is a plus

This rule confuses everyone at first. Think of it as reversing a reversal. Walking backwards is negative; a film of you walking backwards, then played in reverse, shows you walking forwards again. Two reversals cancel out. That is why (−1) × (−1) = +1: undoing an "opposite" twice brings you back to the positive direction.

💡 Try this

A diver descends 4 m each minute (that is −4 per minute). Where was the diver 3 minutes ago compared to now? That is (−4) × (−3) — and the answer, +12 m higher, shows a minus times a minus going positive in real life.
