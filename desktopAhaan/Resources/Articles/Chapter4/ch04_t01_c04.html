Newton's Law of Cooling

Why your chai cools quickly at first, then seems to take forever

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

You pour a cup of piping hot tea and leave it on the table. Five minutes later it is warm. Twenty minutes later it is barely lukewarm. An hour later it is basically room temperature. But have you noticed that the biggest drop in temperature happens in those first few minutes?

Isaac Newton noticed the same pattern in 1701 and turned it into a rule that scientists still use today. His law of cooling explains the exact relationship between how hot something is and how fast it loses heat.

What this concept means

Newton's Law of Cooling states: the rate at which an object loses heat is proportional to the difference in temperature between the object and its surroundings.

In simpler words: the bigger the gap between an object's temperature and the room temperature, the faster it cools. As the object cools and the gap shrinks, the cooling slows down. That is why the last few degrees take the longest.

Mathematically, if a cup of tea is at 80 °C in a room at 25 °C, the temperature difference is 55 °C, and the tea cools rapidly. When the tea drops to 35 °C, the difference is only 10 °C, so cooling has slowed to a crawl.

Why it works this way

Heat always flows from hot to cold. The bigger the temperature difference, the stronger the "push" for heat to flow. Imagine two water tanks connected by a pipe — if one tank is much higher, water gushes through. If the levels are nearly equal, the flow is a trickle. Temperature difference is like that water-level difference.

As the hot object transfers heat to the cooler surroundings, its temperature drops, reducing the difference. With a smaller difference, there is less driving force, so the rate of heat loss decreases. The result is an exponential decay curve — steep at first, gradually levelling off.

This law works well when the temperature difference is not too extreme and there is no wind or forced airflow changing conditions. In real kitchens, a breeze from a fan speeds up cooling because it removes the warm air layer around the cup and replaces it with fresh cool air.

💡 Did you know?

Forensic scientists use Newton's Law of Cooling to estimate the time of death. By measuring a body's temperature and knowing the room temperature, they can work backward along the cooling curve to figure out when the person died — sometimes accurate to within an hour.

Real-world applications

  1. Serving chai at the right time — Indian tea stalls know hot chai cools fastest in the first minute; waiting just 2–3 minutes makes it drinkable.
  2. Forensic science — Detectives estimate time of death using body-temperature measurements and the cooling law.
  3. Cooling of engines — Car radiators are designed so that the large initial temperature gap drives rapid heat removal from the engine.
  4. Food safety — Cooked food must cool from 60 °C to below 20 °C within 2 hours; the law predicts this is feasible in normal kitchens.
  5. Ceramic kilns — Potters open kiln doors slowly because rapid cooling (when the gap is huge) can crack pottery.
  6. Electronics cooling — Heat sinks on computer chips exploit the large temperature difference to draw heat away quickly.
  7. Blast furnace management — Steel plants control cooling rates of molten metal to achieve desired crystal structures.
  8. Hot-water geysers — The insulation around a geyser tank reduces the effective temperature difference, slowing heat loss and keeping water warm longer.
  9. Cooking rice — After switching off the stove, the rice pot cools slowly because the thick walls maintain a smaller temperature gap with the food.
  10. Climate science — Earth's night-time cooling follows a pattern similar to Newton's Law — deserts cool fast (dry air, big gap) while coastal areas cool slowly (water retains heat).

🌍 In your kitchen tonight

Pour two cups of hot water — leave one in the open and wrap the other in a towel. Measure their temperatures every 5 minutes. The open cup cools faster because it has direct contact with cool air. The towel reduces the effective temperature difference at the cup's surface, slowing the cooling — just like an insulated flask.

🧪 Try this at home

Boil water and pour equal amounts into two identical cups. Leave one in the kitchen (say 25 °C) and place the other near an open window on a cooler evening (say 18 °C). Record temperatures every 5 minutes. The cup near the window will cool faster because its temperature gap with the surroundings is larger. You have just verified Newton's Law of Cooling!

🧠 Check your understanding (1 of 3)

Question: A cup of coffee at 90 °C is in a room at 25 °C. After some time it cools to 50 °C. Has the rate of cooling increased or decreased compared to when it was at 90 °C?

Answer: The rate of cooling has decreased. At 90 °C the temperature difference was 65 °C; at 50 °C the difference is only 25 °C. A smaller difference means a slower rate of heat loss.

🧠 Check your understanding (2 of 3)

Question: Why does a hot metal rod cool faster in cold water (10 °C) than in warm water (40 °C)?

Answer: Because the temperature difference between the rod and the cold water is larger, creating a stronger driving force for heat flow, as Newton's Law of Cooling predicts.

🧠 Check your understanding (3 of 3)

Question: Will a hot object ever cool to below room temperature if left alone?

Answer: No. According to Newton's Law, cooling stops when the object reaches the temperature of its surroundings (thermal equilibrium). It cannot go below that without an external cooling device.
