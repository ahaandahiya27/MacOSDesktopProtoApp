Cryogenics

The science of extreme cold — where nitrogen becomes a liquid and physics gets weird

≈ 9 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

We have spent most of this chapter talking about heat — adding it, measuring it, watching it move. But what happens when you go in the completely opposite direction and remove almost all the heat from a substance? Welcome to the world of cryogenics, where temperatures plunge below −150 °C and ordinary materials start behaving in extraordinary ways.

At −196 °C, the air you breathe would be a liquid. At −269 °C, helium becomes a liquid that can flow uphill. And at temperatures near absolute zero (−273.15 °C or 0 K), electrical resistance can vanish completely. Cryogenics is not science fiction — it is real science that powers space rockets, saves lives in hospitals, and might shape the future of computing.

What this concept means

Cryogenics is the branch of physics that deals with the production and effects of very low temperatures — typically below −150 °C (123 K). At these temperatures, gases we normally breathe become liquids or even solids.

Key cryogenic liquids include:

  • Liquid nitrogen (LN₂) — boils at −196 °C (77 K). It is cheap, widely available, and used in medicine, food processing, and science labs.
  • Liquid oxygen (LOX) — boils at −183 °C (90 K). Used as rocket fuel oxidiser by ISRO, NASA, and other space agencies.
  • Liquid helium — boils at −269 °C (4 K). Used to cool MRI magnets in hospitals and particle accelerators in physics labs.
  • Liquid hydrogen — boils at −253 °C (20 K). Used as rocket fuel by ISRO's GSLV rockets and NASA's Space Launch System.

Why it works this way

At extremely low temperatures, molecules move very slowly. With so little kinetic energy, the weak attractive forces between gas molecules — which are negligible at room temperature — become strong enough to pull them together into a liquid or solid. That is how nitrogen gas (78% of the air you breathe) becomes liquid nitrogen at −196 °C.

Some of the most remarkable phenomena appear near absolute zero:

  • Superconductivity — Certain materials lose all electrical resistance below a critical temperature (often near 4 K). Electric current can flow through them forever with zero energy loss. This is used in MRI machines and maglev trains.
  • Superfluidity — Liquid helium below 2.17 K flows with zero viscosity. It can climb up the walls of its container and leak through microscopically tiny gaps — a truly bizarre quantum effect.

Creating cryogenic temperatures requires removing heat from a substance faster than it gains heat from the environment. Scientists use compressors, expansion valves, and vacuum insulation (Dewar flasks) to achieve and maintain these extreme conditions.

💡 Did you know?

ISRO's GSLV Mk III rocket (which launched Chandrayaan-3 to the Moon) uses a cryogenic upper stage that burns liquid hydrogen (−253 °C) and liquid oxygen (−183 °C). Managing these super-cold liquids in a rocket is one of the toughest engineering challenges in space science — and India mastered it domestically after years of effort!

Real-world applications

  1. ISRO's cryogenic rockets — The GSLV and LVM3 rockets use liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen as fuel for their upper stages, giving them the power to reach geostationary orbit.
  2. MRI scanners in hospitals — The superconducting magnets in MRI machines are cooled with liquid helium to near absolute zero, producing the powerful magnetic fields needed for medical imaging.
  3. Preserving biological samples — Sperm banks, blood banks, and stem cell labs store samples in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C, where biological activity essentially stops.
  4. Food industry (flash freezing) — Liquid nitrogen freezes food almost instantly, preserving texture and nutrients better than slow freezing. Some Indian ice-cream parlours make "instant ice cream" using LN₂.
  5. Wart removal in dermatology — Doctors spray liquid nitrogen on warts to freeze and destroy the tissue — a common procedure called cryotherapy.
  6. Superconducting maglev trains — Trains in Japan use superconducting magnets cooled by liquid helium to levitate above the track, reaching speeds over 600 km/h.
  7. Particle physics (CERN) — The Large Hadron Collider uses 120 tonnes of liquid helium to cool its superconducting magnets to 1.9 K — colder than outer space.
  8. Liquefied natural gas (LNG) — Natural gas is cooled to −162 °C to become a liquid for efficient transport in ships from the Middle East to India.
  9. Cryopreservation of coral — Scientists freeze coral sperm and embryos in liquid nitrogen to save endangered reef species for future restoration.
  10. NASA deep-space missions — Infrared telescopes in space (like the James Webb Space Telescope) use cryogenic cooling to keep their detectors cold enough to sense faint heat signals from distant galaxies.

🌍 At a hospital near you

If you have ever had an MRI scan, you were lying inside a giant superconducting magnet cooled to about 4 K (−269 °C) by liquid helium — just a few degrees above absolute zero. The extreme cold is necessary because the magnet's coils must be superconducting (zero resistance) to produce the strong, stable magnetic field needed for clear images of your body.

🧪 Try this at home (thought experiment)

You cannot safely handle liquid nitrogen at home, but here is a thought experiment: if you could dip a rubber ball into liquid nitrogen (−196 °C), it would become so brittle that you could shatter it like glass by throwing it on the floor. At such low temperatures, the rubber molecules lose almost all their flexibility — they freeze into a rigid, fragile structure. This demonstrates how dramatically material properties change at cryogenic temperatures.

🧠 Check your understanding (1 of 3)

Question: What temperature range does cryogenics deal with?

Answer: Cryogenics deals with temperatures below −150 °C (123 K). At these temperatures, common gases like nitrogen and oxygen become liquids.

🧠 Check your understanding (2 of 3)

Question: Why is liquid hydrogen used as rocket fuel?

Answer: Liquid hydrogen is an extremely efficient fuel because when it reacts with liquid oxygen, it releases a huge amount of energy per kilogram. Storing it as a cryogenic liquid makes it compact enough to fit in a rocket's fuel tanks. ISRO uses this in its GSLV upper stages.

🧠 Check your understanding (3 of 3)

Question: What is superconductivity, and at what temperatures does it occur?

Answer: Superconductivity is the phenomenon where certain materials lose all electrical resistance, allowing electric current to flow without any energy loss. It typically occurs at very low temperatures near absolute zero (often below 20–30 K, depending on the material). It is used in MRI machines and maglev trains.
