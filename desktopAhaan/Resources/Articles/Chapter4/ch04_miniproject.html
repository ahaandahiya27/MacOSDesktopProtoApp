Two Mini Projects for Chapter 4

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🥄 Spoons in hot water — race the conductors

~10 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A wooden spoon, plastic spoon, steel spoon, and copper spoon (or any small piece of copper wire)
  • A heatproof mug of hot water (just boiled)
  • A small dab of butter on each handle

Steps

  1. Stand all spoons upright in the hot water — handles up, bowl ends in the water.
  2. Quickly stick a small dab of butter at the top of each handle (just below the upper edge).
  3. Watch which butter dab slides or drops first.
  4. Order from fastest to slowest: copper, steel, plastic, wood.

👀 What you should see

Copper handle's butter melts first, then steel's, then plastic's stays solid the longest (and wood's barely warms).

Why it works: Each material has a different thermal conductivity. Copper conducts heat ~400 W/m/K, steel ~50, plastic and wood under 0.2. The heat racing up the copper handle melts the butter quickly; the wooden handle barely warms even after 5 minutes. This is why every Indian kitchen has copper-bottom pans with wooden handles — chosen for the same physics you just demonstrated.

🌬️ Convection chimney — see hot air rise

~15 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A clean glass jar (mouth open)
  • Two short candles
  • A thin strip of newspaper (~10 cm × 2 cm)
  • Matches (adult supervision)

Steps

  1. Place the two candles side by side. Light them.
  2. Hold the jar upside down over them — the candles should burn for a moment, then start flickering.
  3. Twist the newspaper strip and light one end. As it gives smoke, hold the smoking end near the jar's mouth.
  4. Watch the smoke: it spirals around the rim, gets sucked into the jar, then puffs out as the candles run out of fresh air.

👀 What you should see

Smoke shows a clear circulation: cold smoke pushed down into the jar at one side, hot air rising out the other side.

Why it works: Burning candles heat the air above them. Hot air expands and rises, leaving low pressure behind. Cool air from outside the jar rushes down to fill the gap, and the cycle continues — this is convection in motion. The smoke just makes the invisible air movement visible. The same convection drives sea breezes, monsoons, and the air-conditioning in your home.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
