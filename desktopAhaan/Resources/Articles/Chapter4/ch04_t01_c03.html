Celsius and Kelvin

Two scales, one universe — why scientists need both

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

When someone in India says it is "40 degrees outside," everyone knows that is blazing hot. But a scientist working in a space-research lab might describe the same air temperature as "313 Kelvin." Same hotness, different number — why do we need two scales?

Understanding Celsius and Kelvin is like learning two languages that describe the same reality. Once you see how they connect, converting between them becomes as easy as adding or subtracting 273.

What this concept means

The Celsius scale (°C) was designed around water. Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius defined 0 °C as the point where water freezes and 100 °C as the point where it boils (at sea-level pressure). Every degree is one-hundredth of that gap, which is why it used to be called the "centigrade" scale.

The Kelvin scale (K) starts at the coldest temperature theoretically possible — absolute zero, where molecular motion virtually stops. Absolute zero is −273.15 °C, or 0 K. Each step of 1 K is the same size as 1 °C, so converting is simple:

K = °C + 273 and °C = K − 273

Scientists prefer Kelvin because it never goes negative, which makes many physics equations simpler and avoids confusing minus signs.

Why it works this way

Celsius chose water because it is everywhere — easy to freeze and boil in any laboratory. That made his scale practical for everyday life and simple experiments.

Lord Kelvin (William Thomson) took a deeper approach. He asked: "Is there a temperature where all thermal motion stops?" Theoretical physics said yes — at −273.15 °C. Kelvin set that point as zero and built his scale upward. Because 0 K is the true bottom of the temperature ladder, negative Kelvin temperatures do not exist in normal physics. This makes equations involving gas pressure, energy, and entropy much cleaner.

In India, Celsius is the standard for weather, cooking, and medicine. Kelvin is the standard in physics and chemistry labs. Both are part of the SI (International System of Units), but Kelvin is the official SI base unit for temperature.

💡 Did you know?

The surface of the Sun is about 5,778 K (5,505 °C). The centre is roughly 15 million K. Meanwhile, outer space between galaxies is only about 2.7 K — just above absolute zero, a faint glow left over from the Big Bang!

Real-world applications

  1. Weather reports in India — The India Meteorological Department reports temperature in Celsius, making it familiar to every citizen.
  2. Physics exams — Gas-law problems (like Boyle's and Charles's laws) require temperatures in Kelvin to work correctly.
  3. Space missions (ISRO) — Engineers use Kelvin to calculate heat shields and fuel requirements for satellites.
  4. Medical thermometers — Doctors read body temperature in Celsius (normal is 37 °C) for quick diagnosis.
  5. Chemistry labs — Reaction rates and equilibrium constants use Kelvin to avoid division-by-zero errors.
  6. Colour temperature of lights — LED bulbs are labelled in Kelvin (e.g., 2700 K warm, 6500 K daylight).
  7. Astrophysics — Star temperatures (thousands to millions of Kelvin) use the Kelvin scale for convenience.
  8. Cooking thermometers — Oven dials in India show Celsius so you can bake naan at 230 °C precisely.
  9. Air conditioning — AC units in India are set in Celsius; 24 °C is a common comfort setting.
  10. Cryogenic research — Liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K (−196 °C); Kelvin avoids confusing negative signs in calculations.

🌍 On your phone right now

Check your weather app — it shows the temperature in °C. If you switch to Kelvin, today's 35 °C afternoon becomes 308 K. Same heat, different number. Scientists doing climate modelling always convert to Kelvin before running their equations.

🧪 Try this at home

Write down five temperatures you encounter today (room, fridge, hot water, outside, your body). Convert each to Kelvin by adding 273. You will quickly see that Kelvin numbers are always positive and that the "size" of a degree is the same in both scales.

🧠 Check your understanding (1 of 3)

Question: Convert 25 °C to Kelvin.

Answer: 25 + 273 = 298 K.

🧠 Check your understanding (2 of 3)

Question: What is absolute zero in Celsius?

Answer: −273.15 °C (often rounded to −273 °C). It is the lowest possible temperature, where molecular motion nearly stops.

🧠 Check your understanding (3 of 3)

Question: Why do scientists prefer the Kelvin scale over Celsius for gas-law calculations?

Answer: Because the Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero and has no negative values, it prevents mathematical errors like dividing by zero when using formulas such as Charles's Law (V/T = constant).
