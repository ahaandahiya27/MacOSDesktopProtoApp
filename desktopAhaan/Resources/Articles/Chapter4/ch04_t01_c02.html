How a Thermometer Works

Mercury rises, and we read the temperature — but what is really going on inside?

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Before thermometers existed, people had no reliable way to say exactly how hot or cold something was. "Warm" and "cool" were the best they could do. Then, in the late 1500s, Galileo built a crude thermoscope, and the race to measure temperature precisely began.

Today, a simple glass thermometer can tell you your body temperature to within a tenth of a degree. How does a thin tube of silvery liquid accomplish that? The answer is beautifully simple: thermal expansion.

What this concept means

A mercury thermometer is a narrow glass tube with a small bulb at one end filled with mercury — a liquid metal. When the bulb touches something warm, the mercury molecules absorb energy and begin to move faster, pushing each other apart. The mercury expands and creeps up the tube. When it cools, the molecules slow down, the liquid contracts, and the level drops.

Printed alongside the tube is a scale — marks and numbers that let you read the temperature. The two most common scales are Celsius (°C) and Fahrenheit (°F). On the Celsius scale, 0 °C is the freezing point of water and 100 °C is its boiling point.

A clinical thermometer — the kind a doctor uses — has a small kink (a narrow bend) in the tube just above the bulb. This kink prevents mercury from falling back immediately after you remove the thermometer from your mouth, giving you time to read the temperature.

Why it works this way

Almost every substance expands when heated. This happens because adding energy makes molecules vibrate or move more vigorously, and they need more room. Liquids like mercury and alcohol expand at a very predictable rate, which makes them ideal for thermometers.

Mercury is especially popular because it has a wide useful range (−39 °C to 357 °C), it does not wet (stick to) the glass, and its silvery colour is easy to see. However, mercury is toxic, which is why many countries are phasing it out in favour of digital sensors.

The narrow bore (inner tube) of the thermometer acts like a magnifier: even a tiny expansion of mercury in the bulb translates to a visible change in the column height. That is why thermometer tubes are so thin — precision depends on it.

💡 Did you know?

Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit invented the first reliable mercury thermometer in 1714 in Amsterdam. He chose 96 °F for normal body temperature (it was later refined to 98.6 °F or about 37 °C). His scale is still used in the United States today!

Real-world applications

  1. Checking for fever — A clinical thermometer placed under the tongue uses mercury expansion to show if your body temperature is above 37 °C.
  2. Weather stations — Maximum-minimum thermometers record the highest and lowest temperatures of the day using two columns of mercury.
  3. Cooking — Kitchen thermometers ensure oil for deep-frying reaches the right temperature, around 170–180 °C.
  4. Incubators for newborns — Hospital incubators use precise thermometers to keep premature babies at the perfect 36.5 °C.
  5. Laboratory experiments — Scientists measure the temperature of chemical reactions to understand how energy changes occur.
  6. Aquariums — A thermometer inside a fish tank ensures tropical fish stay in their comfort zone of 24–28 °C.
  7. Greenhouses — Farmers monitor temperatures to protect plants from frost or excessive heat.
  8. Dairy industry — Milk must be pasteurised at 72 °C for 15 seconds; thermometers verify the process.
  9. Metalworking — Thermocouple thermometers (electrical cousins of mercury types) measure furnace temperatures above 1000 °C.
  10. Chocolate tempering — Chocolatiers use thermometers to track precise temperatures (27–32 °C) so chocolate sets with a glossy finish.

🌍 At your school today

If your school has a science lab, look for a lab thermometer in its protective case. Notice how thin the inner tube is compared to the bulb. That narrow tube is the secret to its accuracy — even a tiny expansion of mercury in the bulb pushes the column a visible distance upward.

🧪 Try this at home

Put a room thermometer in a cup of warm water and watch the mercury (or coloured alcohol) rise. Then move it to a cup of cold water. Time how long the column takes to drop. The delay happens because the liquid needs time to give up its heat to the cooler surroundings.

🧠 Check your understanding (1 of 3)

Question: What physical property of mercury allows a thermometer to measure temperature?

Answer: Mercury expands (increases in volume) when heated and contracts when cooled. This thermal expansion is predictable and proportional to temperature change.

🧠 Check your understanding (2 of 3)

Question: Why does a clinical thermometer have a kink (narrow bend) near the bulb?

Answer: The kink prevents mercury from flowing back into the bulb as soon as the thermometer is removed from the body, giving you time to read the temperature.

🧠 Check your understanding (3 of 3)

Question: Why is the bore (inner tube) of a thermometer made very narrow?

Answer: A narrow bore means that even a small expansion of mercury in the bulb produces a large, visible change in the column height, making the thermometer more sensitive and easier to read.
