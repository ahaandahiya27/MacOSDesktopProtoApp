Q1. Differentiate between conduction, convection, and radiation.

Model answer: All three transfer heat from hot to cold, but differently. Conduction needs a solid medium — heat passes by atoms bumping their neighbours (e.g., spoon in hot tea). Convection needs a fluid (liquid or gas) — heated fluid expands, rises, and cooler fluid replaces it (e.g., water boiling in a pot). Radiation needs no medium at all — heat travels as electromagnetic waves through empty space (e.g., the sun heating Earth, a bonfire warming your face). Conduction and convection move heat through material; radiation moves heat through nothing.

Q2. Why does sea breeze flow during the day and land breeze at night?

Model answer: Land heats up faster than the sea during the day because water has a much higher specific heat capacity. As land air heats, it expands and rises; cooler sea air rushes in to replace it — that's the sea breeze, blowing inland. At night the land cools faster than the sea (which retains its heat). Now the sea is warmer, the land cooler. Warm sea air rises, cool land air flows out to replace it — the land breeze. The whole pattern is convection driven by the heat-capacity difference between land and water.

Q3. Why do we wear cotton clothes in summer?

Model answer: Two reasons. (1) Cotton has tiny pores that let air pass — sweat from your skin evaporates faster, taking heat with it. (2) Cotton absorbs sweat and holds it close to the skin where it can keep evaporating. Synthetic clothes (polyester) don't absorb sweat, so it stays on your skin without evaporating — you feel sticky and hot. Wool is too thick and traps heat — bad in summer.

Q4. A thermos flask works by minimizing the three modes of heat transfer. How does it do this?

Model answer: A thermos has three smart features. (1) Double glass walls with vacuum between them — vacuum has no atoms to carry heat, so conduction and convection are nearly zero. (2) Silver coating on the inner glass surface — silver reflects radiation back into the flask instead of letting it escape. (3) A tight insulated cap — prevents convection of warm air out the top. Defeating all three modes is what lets your tea stay hot for 5+ hours.

Q5. Look at the figure of a kitchen vessel. Why is the handle made of plastic but the bottom made of copper?

Model answer: Different jobs need different materials. The bottom faces the flame — needs to conduct heat efficiently into the food. Copper is one of the best conductors of heat (almost as good as silver, much cheaper). The handle needs to NOT conduct heat — plastic is a poor conductor (insulator), so heat from the cooking bottom doesn't reach the cook's hand. Two opposite needs, two opposite materials, one vessel.

Q6. In places of hot climate, why is it advised that the outer walls of houses be painted white?

Model answer: White paint reflects most of the incoming sunlight before it can be absorbed and converted to heat. A dark wall absorbs sunlight, gets hot, and slowly transfers that heat through the wall into the room. A white wall stays cooler, and the room behind it stays cooler — sometimes by 5°C or more. In a Rajasthan summer this is the difference between sleeping comfortably and not sleeping at all.

Q7. One litre of water at 30°C is mixed with one litre of water at 50°C. The temperature of the mixture will be (i) 80°C (ii) more than 50°C but less than 80°C (iii) 20°C (iv) between 30°C and 50°C.

Model answer: (iv) between 30°C and 50°C — specifically, exactly 40°C. When equal masses at different temperatures mix, the final temperature is the average. The hotter water loses heat; the cooler water gains heat; they meet in the middle. Total heat is conserved, not temperature.

Q8. An iron ball at 40°C is dropped in a mug containing water at 40°C. The heat will (i) flow from iron ball to water (ii) not flow from iron ball to water or from water to iron ball (iii) flow from water to iron ball (iv) increase the temperature of both.

Model answer: (ii) Heat will not flow. Heat flows ONLY when there's a temperature difference — from hot to cold. Both objects are at 40°C; they're in thermal equilibrium. The iron ball doesn't 'feel' hot to the water (or vice versa) because there's no temperature gradient. The whole system stays at 40°C indefinitely (assuming no loss to surroundings).
