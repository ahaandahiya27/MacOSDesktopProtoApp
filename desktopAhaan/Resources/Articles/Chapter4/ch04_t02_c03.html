Radiation

Heat that travels without any medium — even through empty space

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

The Sun is about 150 million kilometres from Earth, with nothing but empty space in between — no air, no water, no solid. Yet we feel its warmth every single day. How does heat cross all that emptiness?

The answer is radiation — the transfer of heat through invisible electromagnetic waves. Unlike conduction and convection, radiation needs no material medium at all. It is the reason you feel warmth from a bonfire even from metres away, and it is how Earth receives almost all its energy.

What this concept means

Every object above absolute zero emits thermal radiation — energy in the form of electromagnetic waves (mainly infrared). Hotter objects emit more radiation and at shorter wavelengths. The Sun, at about 5,778 K, emits visible light and lots of infrared. Your body, at about 310 K, emits only infrared — invisible to your eyes but detectable by thermal cameras.

Radiation does not need molecules to travel. It moves at the speed of light (300,000 km/s) and can pass through a vacuum. When radiation hits an object, it can be absorbed (heating the object), reflected (bouncing off), or transmitted (passing through, like sunlight through glass).

Dark, rough surfaces are good absorbers and emitters of radiation. Light, shiny surfaces are good reflectors and poor absorbers — that is why emergency blankets are silver.

Why it works this way

All matter is made of charged particles — protons and electrons. When these particles vibrate (which they always do if the temperature is above 0 K), they create electromagnetic waves, just as shaking a charged rod creates radio waves. The faster the vibrations (higher temperature), the more intense the radiation and the shorter the wavelengths.

Because electromagnetic waves are self-propagating — they are oscillations of electric and magnetic fields that sustain each other — they do not need a medium. This is fundamentally different from conduction (which needs solid contact) and convection (which needs fluid flow).

Black and dark objects absorb more radiation because their surface molecules are better at capturing a wide range of wavelengths. White and shiny surfaces reflect most wavelengths, absorbing little energy. That is why people in hot places like Rajasthan traditionally wear light-coloured, loose clothing — it reflects the Sun's radiation away.

💡 Did you know?

Your body radiates about 100 watts of thermal energy right now — enough to power a light bulb! Most of it is infrared radiation, invisible to our eyes but clearly visible on a thermal camera. In a crowd of people, thermal cameras can spot someone with a fever because they radiate slightly more than everyone else.

Real-world applications

  1. Solar water heaters — Black-painted tubes on rooftops absorb the Sun's radiation and heat water for bathing — common across Indian cities.
  2. Emergency rescue blankets — Thin silver-coated plastic sheets reflect a person's body radiation back, preventing dangerous heat loss.
  3. White rooftops (cool-roof policy) — Cities like Ahmedabad paint rooftops white to reflect solar radiation and lower indoor temperatures by up to 5 °C.
  4. Thermal cameras — Security, firefighting, and medical screening all use cameras that detect infrared radiation from objects.
  5. Microwave ovens — Electromagnetic radiation (microwaves) is absorbed by water molecules in food, heating the food from within.
  6. Greenhouse effect — Sunlight passes through the atmosphere, warms Earth's surface, which then emits infrared radiation that is partly trapped by greenhouse gases.
  7. Thermos flask design — The silvery inner surface reflects radiation, reducing heat loss (or gain) by this mode.
  8. Spacecraft design — Satellites have reflective coatings and radiator panels to manage the intense radiation in space.
  9. Drying clothes in the Sun — Dark clothes dry faster because they absorb more solar radiation, evaporating water quickly.
  10. Desert survival — Light-coloured loose clothing reflects solar radiation, while dark clothing would absorb it and overheat the wearer.

🌍 On a sunny day

Park two toy cars in the sun — one black, one white. After 30 minutes, touch their roofs. The black car is noticeably hotter because it absorbed more solar radiation. This is exactly why real dark-coloured cars get scorching inside during Indian summers, while lighter cars stay a bit cooler.

🧪 Try this at home

Take two identical metal cans. Paint one black and leave the other shiny. Fill both with equal amounts of hot water. Place a thermometer in each and record the temperature every 5 minutes. The black can cools faster because it emits more radiation. You have just demonstrated that dark surfaces are better radiators!

🧠 Check your understanding (1 of 3)

Question: How is radiation different from conduction and convection?

Answer: Radiation transfers heat through electromagnetic waves and needs no medium — it works even in a vacuum. Conduction requires direct contact between solids, and convection requires fluid movement.

🧠 Check your understanding (2 of 3)

Question: Why do we wear white or light-coloured clothes in summer?

Answer: Light colours reflect most of the Sun's radiation rather than absorbing it, keeping us cooler. Dark colours absorb more radiation and heat up.

🧠 Check your understanding (3 of 3)

Question: Does a cold object emit any radiation?

Answer: Yes! Every object above absolute zero (0 K) emits thermal radiation. Cold objects emit less radiation and at longer wavelengths (far infrared) compared to hot objects, but they still radiate.
