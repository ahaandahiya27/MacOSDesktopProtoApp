Thermos Flask

One device that fights conduction, convection, and radiation all at once

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

You fill a thermos with hot chai in the morning, and at lunchtime — hours later — it is still steaming. How does a simple bottle keep liquids hot (or cold) for so long? The secret is that a thermos is designed to block all three modes of heat transfer at once.

The thermos flask (also called a vacuum flask or Dewar flask) is one of the cleverest inventions in the history of thermal engineering. Let us take it apart — layer by layer — and see how it works.

What this concept means

A thermos flask has a double-walled glass or steel container with a vacuum between the two walls. The inner surfaces are coated with a reflective silver layer. The flask is sealed with a tight insulating lid, often made of plastic or cork.

  • Vacuum layer — eliminates conduction and convection. Heat cannot conduct through nothing, and there is no fluid to carry convection currents.
  • Silver coating — reflects thermal radiation back into the flask, preventing heat from escaping (or entering) by radiation.
  • Insulating lid — plastic and cork are poor conductors, reducing heat loss through the opening.
  • Outer casing — typically plastic or stainless steel, providing a protective and insulating shell.

Why it works this way

Without the thermos, a hot liquid loses heat through all three modes: conduction through the walls, convection as warm air rises from the surface, and radiation from the hot surface outward. The thermos tackles each one systematically:

The vacuum is the key innovation. In the gap between the two walls, there are essentially no molecules — so conduction (molecule-to-molecule transfer) and convection (fluid flow) are both eliminated. The only way heat can cross a vacuum is by radiation.

To deal with radiation, the walls are coated with shiny silver. Silver reflects about 95–97% of infrared radiation back. So even radiation — the last escape route — is nearly blocked.

The lid must also be insulating, because the opening is the one spot where all three modes can act. A tight cork or plastic stopper greatly reduces heat loss from the top.

The result: a well-made thermos can keep water hot for 12–24 hours, losing only a few degrees per hour.

💡 Did you know?

The thermos flask was invented in 1892 by Scottish scientist Sir James Dewar. He did not patent it, so two German glass-blowers started a company called "Thermos" to sell it commercially. The name stuck, and today "thermos" is used generically worldwide — even though it started as a brand name, like "xerox" for photocopying!

Real-world applications

  1. Carrying hot chai to work — Millions of Indians use thermos flasks daily to carry hot tea or coffee for hours.
  2. Keeping cold drinks cold — The same design that keeps heat in also keeps heat out — ice stays frozen much longer inside a thermos.
  3. Vaccine transport (cold chain) — Health workers use vacuum-insulated carriers to keep vaccines at the right temperature during transport to remote villages.
  4. Storing liquid nitrogen — Dewar flasks (lab-grade versions) store cryogenic liquids at −196 °C for long periods.
  5. Space station food — Astronauts use insulated containers based on the thermos principle to keep food warm in zero gravity.
  6. Baby bottles on the go — Parents keep milk warm for hours in thermos-style baby bottles during travel.
  7. Tiffin carriers — Insulated Indian tiffin boxes use similar double-wall vacuum principles to keep lunch warm until noon.
  8. Blood transport — Blood banks use vacuum-insulated containers to transport blood at the correct temperature to hospitals.
  9. Antarctic research stations — Scientists use large thermos-style containers to keep water from freezing during fieldwork in −40 °C conditions.
  10. Industrial calorimeters — Devices that measure heat energy use vacuum-flask designs to minimise heat exchange with the environment.

🌍 In your school bag

If you carry a steel water bottle that keeps water cold for hours, it probably has a double-wall vacuum design — the exact same principle as a thermos flask. The vacuum between the walls is doing the heavy lifting, not the steel itself.

🧪 Try this at home

Pour equal amounts of hot water into a thermos flask and a regular glass. Measure the temperature of both after 1 hour. The thermos water will be much hotter. Then think about why: the glass loses heat by all three modes (conduction through glass, convection from the surface, radiation from the hot glass). The thermos blocks almost all of them.

🧠 Check your understanding (1 of 3)

Question: Which mode of heat transfer does the vacuum in a thermos flask prevent?

Answer: The vacuum prevents both conduction and convection. Heat cannot conduct through empty space (no molecules), and convection currents cannot form without a fluid.

🧠 Check your understanding (2 of 3)

Question: Why are the inner walls of a thermos coated with silver?

Answer: Silver is an excellent reflector of infrared radiation. The coating reflects thermal radiation back into the flask, minimising heat loss by radiation — the only mode that can cross a vacuum.

🧠 Check your understanding (3 of 3)

Question: Can a thermos keep cold things cold? Explain.

Answer: Yes! A thermos works by blocking heat transfer in both directions. For a cold liquid inside, the vacuum prevents outside heat from conducting or convecting in, and the silver coating reflects outside radiation away. So cold stays cold.
