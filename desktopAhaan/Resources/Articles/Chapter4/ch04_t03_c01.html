Heat in the Human Body

How your body keeps itself at exactly 37 °C — rain or shine

≈ 9 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Whether you are standing in the 45 °C heat of a Rajasthan summer or shivering through a 5 °C Delhi winter morning, your body's core temperature stays remarkably constant at about 37 °C. How does your body pull off this incredible balancing act?

Your body is a heat-producing machine — food is its fuel — and it has a built-in thermostat controlled by a part of your brain called the hypothalamus. When you get too hot, you sweat. When you get too cold, you shiver. Let us explore the science behind your personal temperature control system.

What this concept means

Your body constantly generates heat through metabolism — the chemical reactions that break down food for energy. Even when you are sleeping, your body produces about 80 watts of heat (like a bright light bulb). When you exercise, heat production can spike to 1,000 watts or more.

To maintain 37 °C, your body must balance heat production with heat loss. It uses several mechanisms:

  • Sweating — Sweat glands release water onto your skin. As sweat evaporates, it absorbs heat energy from your body, cooling you down. This is the body's most powerful cooling tool.
  • Vasodilation — Blood vessels near the skin widen, allowing more warm blood to flow near the surface, where heat radiates away.
  • Shivering — When you are cold, muscles contract rapidly and involuntarily, generating heat through friction and metabolic activity.
  • Vasoconstriction — Blood vessels near the skin narrow, reducing blood flow to the surface and keeping warm blood deeper inside.
  • Goosebumps — Tiny muscles pull your body hairs upright, trapping a thin layer of air for insulation (more effective in furry animals than in humans).

Why it works this way

Evaporation is a powerful cooling process because it requires energy. To change from a liquid to a vapour, water molecules must absorb a large amount of heat energy — about 2,260 kJ per kilogram (called the latent heat of vaporisation). This energy comes from your skin, which is why you feel cooler as sweat evaporates.

This is also why you feel colder when you step out of a swimming pool — water evaporating from your skin rapidly draws heat away. And it is why a fan makes you feel cooler even though it does not lower the room temperature: moving air speeds up evaporation of sweat.

In very humid conditions — like during the Mumbai monsoon — sweat does not evaporate easily because the air is already saturated with moisture. That is why humid heat feels far more oppressive than dry heat: your body's main cooling mechanism is crippled.

When it is cold, your body shifts strategy. Shivering generates heat by making muscles do rapid, tiny contractions. Vasoconstriction keeps warm blood away from the cold skin surface. Your body may even increase metabolic rate — burning more food calories to produce extra warmth.

💡 Did you know?

A healthy human can produce up to 2–4 litres of sweat per hour during intense exercise in hot conditions. Marathon runners in the Indian heat can lose 3–5% of their body weight in sweat during a single race. That is why hydration is absolutely critical — dehydration impairs the body's cooling system and can be dangerous.

Real-world applications

  1. Fever as a defence — Your body deliberately raises its thermostat during infection so that pathogens (which prefer 37 °C) are weakened by the higher temperature.
  2. Summer cooling vests — Workers in Indian steel plants wear vests with water-soaked pads; evaporation of the water cools them down.
  3. Hypothermia treatment — Rescue teams wrap cold patients in insulating blankets and give warm fluids to raise their core temperature back to 37 °C.
  4. Heatstroke prevention — Knowing that humidity blocks sweat evaporation, sports coaches track the wet-bulb temperature to decide if outdoor practice is safe.
  5. Terracotta coolers (matkas) — Work on the same evaporation principle as sweating: water seeps through porous clay and evaporates, cooling the water inside.
  6. AC design in Indian homes — Air conditioners cool and dehumidify air, helping sweat evaporate and making the body's cooling system more effective.
  7. Neonatal care — Premature babies cannot regulate body temperature well, so incubators maintain a steady 36–37 °C environment.
  8. Athletics in hot climates — Indian cricketers use ice vests and cold towels during drinks breaks to lower skin temperature and enhance heat dissipation.
  9. Antarctic expeditions — Explorers wear multiple insulating layers to trap air (a poor conductor), helping the body retain its metabolic heat.
  10. Thermal imaging for health — Infrared cameras detect body-surface radiation patterns to identify inflammation, poor circulation, or fever without any contact.

🌍 After your next PE class

After running laps, notice how your face is red (vasodilation — blood rushing to the skin to dump heat) and you are sweating heavily. Stand in front of a fan and feel the instant coolness — the fan speeds up sweat evaporation, pulling heat from your skin faster. Your body is using conduction, convection, radiation, and evaporation all at once!

🧪 Try this at home

Dab a few drops of water on the back of one hand and leave the other hand dry. Blow across both hands gently. The wet hand feels much cooler. The blowing speeds up evaporation of the water, which absorbs heat from your skin — the same principle your body uses when you sweat.

🧠 Check your understanding (1 of 3)

Question: Why does sweating cool your body down?

Answer: When sweat (water) evaporates from your skin, it absorbs a large amount of heat energy (latent heat of vaporisation) from your body. This removal of heat energy lowers your skin temperature.

🧠 Check your understanding (2 of 3)

Question: Why does humid heat feel worse than dry heat at the same temperature?

Answer: In humid conditions, the air is already saturated with water vapour, so sweat cannot evaporate efficiently. Since evaporation is the body's primary cooling mechanism, reduced evaporation means reduced cooling, making you feel much hotter.

🧠 Check your understanding (3 of 3)

Question: What is the role of shivering when you are cold?

Answer: Shivering is rapid, involuntary muscle contraction. Each contraction uses energy and produces heat as a by-product, helping to raise your body temperature when you are losing heat faster than your normal metabolism can replace it.
