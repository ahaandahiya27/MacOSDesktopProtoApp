Heat and Climate Change

How greenhouse gases trap heat and warm our planet

≈ 9 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Earth is the perfect temperature for life — not too hot, not too cold. But that comfort depends on a delicate balance: the right amount of heat from the Sun coming in, and the right amount escaping back to space. Greenhouse gases in our atmosphere act like a blanket, trapping some of that outgoing heat. Without them, Earth would be a frozen −18 °C on average.

The problem is that humans have been adding extra greenhouse gases — mainly carbon dioxide and methane — by burning fossil fuels, cutting forests, and farming livestock. The thicker blanket traps more heat, and Earth's average temperature is rising. This is climate change, and understanding the heat science behind it is one of the most important things you can learn.

What this concept means

The greenhouse effect is a natural process:

  1. The Sun sends energy (mostly visible light) to Earth.
  2. Earth's surface absorbs this energy and warms up.
  3. The warm surface emits infrared radiation (heat) back toward space.
  4. Greenhouse gases — CO₂, methane (CH₄), water vapour, nitrous oxide — absorb some of this outgoing infrared radiation and re-emit it in all directions, including back down to Earth.
  5. This "trapping" keeps Earth warm enough for liquid water and life.

The enhanced greenhouse effect happens when extra greenhouse gases are added to the atmosphere. More gas means more infrared radiation is trapped, and Earth's temperature rises beyond its natural balance. Since the Industrial Revolution (around 1850), CO₂ levels have risen from about 280 ppm to over 420 ppm, and global average temperature has increased by about 1.2 °C.

Why it works this way

Greenhouse gas molecules have a special structure: they can absorb and re-emit infrared radiation. When an infrared photon (a packet of heat energy) hits a CO₂ or CH₄ molecule, the molecule vibrates at a specific frequency, absorbs the energy, and then re-emits it in a random direction. About half the re-emitted radiation goes back down to Earth, effectively "recycling" heat.

Visible light from the Sun passes right through these gases without being absorbed — that is why sunlight reaches the ground. But the ground re-emits this energy as longer-wavelength infrared, which the gases can absorb. This wavelength selectivity is the heart of the greenhouse effect.

The more CO₂ and methane we add (by burning coal, petrol, and natural gas, and by deforestation), the more infrared radiation is re-absorbed. The result is a slow but steady increase in Earth's average temperature — about 0.2 °C per decade currently.

India is especially vulnerable: rising temperatures affect monsoon patterns, glacier melt in the Himalayas, heatwaves in northern plains, and sea-level rise threatening coastal cities like Mumbai and Kolkata.

💡 Did you know?

India experiences some of the world's most intense heatwaves. In May 2024, several Indian cities recorded temperatures above 49 °C. Scientists have calculated that climate change made these extreme events about 45 times more likely than they would have been without the extra greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.

Real-world applications

  1. Solar energy adoption — India's massive solar parks (like the Bhadla Solar Park in Rajasthan) help reduce CO₂ by replacing coal-fired electricity.
  2. Electric vehicles — India's push toward electric scooters and buses reduces fossil fuel burning and greenhouse gas emissions.
  3. Afforestation programmes — Trees absorb CO₂ during photosynthesis, acting as "carbon sinks" that slow down global warming.
  4. Energy-efficient buildings — Green building codes in India mandate insulation and efficient cooling to reduce electricity use and associated emissions.
  5. Rice paddy management — Flooded rice fields produce methane; new farming techniques (like alternate wetting and drying) reduce these emissions.
  6. Glacier monitoring in the Himalayas — Scientists track how rising temperatures melt glaciers that feed rivers supporting hundreds of millions of people.
  7. Coastal flood planning — Cities like Mumbai are planning for rising sea levels caused by thermal expansion of warmer ocean water.
  8. Carbon footprint labelling — Some Indian companies now label products with their carbon footprint, helping consumers make climate-friendly choices.
  9. International climate agreements — India participates in the Paris Agreement, committing to reduce emissions intensity and increase renewable energy use.
  10. Weather prediction — Climate scientists use supercomputers to model how extra heat in the atmosphere will change rainfall, cyclone frequency, and extreme weather.

🌍 In your daily choices

Every time you switch off a light you are not using, take a bus instead of asking for a car ride, or eat locally grown food instead of imported products, you are reducing the amount of fossil fuel burned and therefore the amount of CO₂ added to the atmosphere. Climate action starts with understanding the heat science — and you have just done that.

🧪 Try this at home

Place two identical glass jars in sunlight. In one, put a small open container of vinegar and baking soda (which produces CO₂ gas) and seal the jar. Leave the other jar with just regular air, also sealed. After 30 minutes, measure the temperature inside each jar with a thermometer. The jar with extra CO₂ will be slightly warmer — you have just demonstrated the greenhouse effect!

🧠 Check your understanding (1 of 3)

Question: What is the greenhouse effect, and is it entirely harmful?

Answer: The greenhouse effect is the trapping of infrared (heat) radiation by gases like CO₂ and water vapour in the atmosphere. It is not entirely harmful — the natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth warm enough (about 15 °C average) for life. The problem is the enhanced greenhouse effect, caused by extra human-made emissions, which is making Earth too warm too fast.

🧠 Check your understanding (2 of 3)

Question: Why does visible sunlight pass through greenhouse gases but infrared radiation does not?

Answer: Greenhouse gas molecules can only absorb and re-emit radiation at specific wavelengths — mainly infrared. Visible light has shorter wavelengths that these molecules cannot absorb, so it passes through. Earth's surface absorbs the visible light and re-emits it as longer-wavelength infrared, which the gases then trap.

🧠 Check your understanding (3 of 3)

Question: Name two ways India is trying to reduce greenhouse gas emissions.

Answer: (1) Building large solar energy parks (like Bhadla in Rajasthan) to replace coal power. (2) Promoting electric vehicles to reduce petrol and diesel burning. Other valid answers include afforestation, energy-efficient building codes, and improved rice farming techniques.
