Conduction

How heat flows through solid materials — molecule by molecule

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Hold one end of a metal spoon and dip the other end into a cup of hot soup. Within seconds, the handle starts to feel warm and then hot. The heat travelled from the soup end all the way up to your fingers — without the spoon moving at all. This is conduction.

Conduction is the transfer of heat through a material without the material itself moving. It is the main way heat travels through solids, and understanding it explains everything from why we use wooden handles on cooking pans to why igloos keep people warm.

What this concept means

In conduction, heat energy passes from one molecule to the next in a chain. The molecules at the hot end vibrate vigorously. They bump into their neighbours, transferring energy, which those neighbours pass along to their neighbours, and so on. No molecule actually travels — they stay in place, just passing the energy along like a relay race.

Materials that conduct heat well are called conductors — metals like copper, aluminium, and iron are excellent conductors. Materials that conduct heat poorly are called insulators — wood, plastic, rubber, and air are good insulators.

Why it works this way

In metals, conduction is especially fast because metals have free electrons — electrons that are not tightly bound to any single atom. These free electrons can zip through the metal carrying energy much faster than the atom-to-atom vibration alone. That is why metals feel cold to the touch on a winter morning: they rapidly conduct heat away from your warm hand.

Insulators like wood and plastic lack free electrons. Heat can only travel through them via slow molecular vibrations, so they feel warmer to touch even at the same temperature as a piece of metal.

Air is one of the best insulators because its molecules are far apart, making it hard for vibrations to pass from one molecule to the next. That is why materials full of trapped air — wool, feathers, Styrofoam — are excellent insulators.

💡 Did you know?

Diamond is one of the best thermal conductors in nature — even better than copper! Its carbon atoms are locked in a perfectly rigid crystal, allowing vibrations to travel through at incredible speed. That is why a diamond feels ice-cold to the touch — it pulls heat from your skin extremely fast.

Real-world applications

  1. Cooking pan handles — Metal pans have wooden or plastic handles because these insulators prevent heat from reaching your hand by conduction.
  2. Woollen clothing in winter — Wool fibres trap air, and air is a poor conductor. The trapped air insulates your body, keeping warmth in.
  3. Igloo construction — Blocks of snow trap air pockets. Despite being made of ice, igloos stay warmer inside because snow is a poor conductor.
  4. Double-walled glass cups — Two layers of glass with air between them reduce conduction, keeping your coffee hot and your fingers cool.
  5. Copper-bottomed pans — Copper conducts heat evenly, preventing hot spots that burn food. Many Indian pressure cookers have copper or aluminium bases for this reason.
  6. Thermal paste in computers — A thin layer of thermally conductive paste between the CPU and its heat sink ensures heat conducts away from the chip efficiently.
  7. Building insulation — Foam panels in walls trap air, reducing heat conduction from the hot exterior into air-conditioned rooms.
  8. Railway track gaps — Gaps are left between rail sections because metal expands when heated by conduction from the sun, and without gaps the tracks would buckle.
  9. Blacksmith tongs — Long iron tongs keep the blacksmith's hand far from the hot end; by the time heat conducts along the full length, it has dissipated.
  10. Thermos flask walls — The vacuum between the double walls of a thermos eliminates conduction entirely — heat cannot conduct through nothing.

🌍 In your kitchen right now

Look at any cooking pot in your home. The body is likely aluminium or steel (good conductors to spread heat evenly across the food) but the handles are plastic or wood (insulators to protect your hands). Every pot is a mini-lesson in conduction!

🧪 Try this at home

Gather a metal spoon, a wooden spoon, and a plastic spoon. Stick a small blob of butter on the handle of each. Stand all three in a cup of hot water so the blobs are above the water line. The butter on the metal spoon melts first — proving metal conducts heat fastest. Wood and plastic take much longer.

🧠 Check your understanding (1 of 3)

Question: Why does a metal bench feel colder than a wooden bench on a winter morning, even though both are at the same temperature?

Answer: Metal conducts heat away from your warm skin much faster than wood. The rapid heat loss makes the metal bench feel colder, even though its actual temperature is the same as the wooden bench.

🧠 Check your understanding (2 of 3)

Question: Why is air a good insulator?

Answer: Air molecules are far apart, so collisions that transfer vibrational energy happen rarely. This makes it difficult for heat to conduct through air.

🧠 Check your understanding (3 of 3)

Question: Name three good conductors and three good insulators of heat.

Answer: Conductors: copper, aluminium, iron. Insulators: wood, plastic, air (or wool, rubber, glass).
