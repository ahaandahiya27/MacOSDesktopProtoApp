Chapter 4 · Overview

Heat

Understanding the energy that warms our world

≈ 6 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Have you ever wondered why a metal spoon left in hot soup burns your fingers while a wooden spoon stays cool? Or why the breeze near a beach reverses direction between day and night? The answer to all of these questions lies in a single word: heat.

Heat is a form of energy that flows from hotter objects to cooler ones. It shapes our weather, cooks our food, keeps our bodies alive, and drives some of the biggest machines on the planet. In this chapter, you will explore what heat really is, how it travels, and why understanding it matters more than ever.

From ancient blacksmiths forging iron to modern scientists tackling climate change, humans have been learning about heat for centuries. Let us trace that journey and see what science has uncovered.

A brief history of heat science

  • 1592 — Galileo Galilei builds an early thermoscope, one of the first devices to detect temperature changes using air expansion in a glass tube.
  • 1701 — Isaac Newton publishes his Law of Cooling, describing how an object loses heat to its surroundings at a rate proportional to the temperature difference.
  • 1843 — James Prescott Joule demonstrates the mechanical equivalent of heat, proving that mechanical work and heat are interchangeable forms of energy.
  • 1872 — Ludwig Boltzmann develops the kinetic theory of gases, explaining heat as the motion of tiny molecules — a revolutionary idea at the time.
  • 1988 — Modern climate science takes centre stage as the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) is established, bringing heat and global warming into worldwide focus.
