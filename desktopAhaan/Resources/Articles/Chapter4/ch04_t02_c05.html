Cooking Vessels

Why pans have copper bottoms and wooden handles — the science of smart design

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Walk into any Indian kitchen and pick up a cooking vessel. Chances are the bottom is made of aluminium, steel, or copper, while the handle is plastic, wood, or Bakelite. Have you ever wondered why the two parts are made of different materials?

The answer is pure heat science. Every part of a cooking vessel is chosen based on how well — or how poorly — it conducts heat. Understanding this turns a simple pan into a masterclass in thermal engineering.

What this concept means

A good cooking vessel must do two opposite things at the same time: it must conduct heat efficiently to the food through its base and walls, and it must block heat from reaching your hand through its handle.

The base is made of a material with high thermal conductivity — copper (401 W/m·K), aluminium (237 W/m·K), or stainless steel (16 W/m·K). Copper is the best but expensive, so many pans have a thin copper layer on an aluminium or steel body. This ensures heat from the flame spreads evenly across the base, preventing hot spots that burn food.

The handle is made of an insulator — wood (0.15 W/m·K), Bakelite, or silicone — so heat does not conduct from the pot to your fingers.

Why it works this way

The flame under a pan is concentrated in a small area. If the base were a poor conductor, only the food directly above the flame would get hot, and the rest would stay cold. A copper or aluminium base spreads the heat sideways, ensuring the entire base reaches a uniform temperature. This is critical for cooking rice, making chapatis, or simmering dal evenly.

Meanwhile, the handle is attached to the rim, far from the flame. An insulating material ensures that even after 30 minutes on the stove, the handle stays cool enough to grip. Bakelite — one of the first synthetic plastics, invented in 1907 — became hugely popular for Indian pressure cooker handles because it is heat-resistant up to about 300 °C and does not soften.

Some premium pans use multi-layer (clad) construction: an aluminium core sandwiched between stainless steel layers. The aluminium provides fast, even heat spreading, while the steel gives durability and a non-reactive cooking surface — important for acidic foods like tomato-based curries.

💡 Did you know?

Traditional Indian brass and copper vessels (called pital and tamba) have been used for centuries not just because they conduct heat well, but also because copper has natural antibacterial properties. Water stored in a copper vessel overnight can kill certain harmful bacteria — a practice backed by modern science!

Real-world applications

  1. Indian pressure cookers — Aluminium body for fast heating, Bakelite handle and pressure knob for safe grip.
  2. Tawa (flat griddle) — Cast iron tawas heat evenly for perfect rotis; the thick metal distributes heat across the whole surface.
  3. Copper handi — Traditional copper pots used in slow-cooking Mughlai dishes; copper's excellent conductivity ensures gentle, even simmering.
  4. Non-stick pans — Aluminium base coated with PTFE (Teflon); aluminium heats fast, and the coating prevents food from sticking.
  5. Kadhai (wok) — Often made from iron or stainless steel; the rounded shape lets convection currents move food during deep-frying.
  6. Double boilers — An inner pot sits in an outer pot of boiling water, using water's even temperature to gently melt chocolate or make sauces.
  7. Clay pot cooking — Earthen pots heat slowly and retain warmth (like a mild insulator), perfect for slow-cooked dishes like biryani dum.
  8. Electric kettles — A concealed stainless steel heating element conducts heat directly into water; the plastic body stays cool.
  9. Silicone baking moulds — Silicone is a mild insulator that heats evenly in ovens but stays cool enough to touch briefly.
  10. Camping cookware — Titanium pans are light and conduct heat reasonably well, making them ideal for trekking in the Himalayas.

🌍 In your kitchen tonight

Pick up a pan and look at the bottom — you may see a different colour or layer. That is the conductor doing its job. Now feel the handle while the pan is hot (carefully!) — it should be cool. Two materials, two opposite jobs, one safe cooking experience.

🧪 Try this at home

With an adult's help, place a metal spoon and a wooden spoon in a pot of boiling water. After 2 minutes, touch the exposed ends. The metal spoon is hot (good conductor), the wooden spoon is barely warm (insulator). This is exactly why we use wooden or plastic handles on cookware.

🧠 Check your understanding (1 of 3)

Question: Why is copper preferred for the base of cooking vessels?

Answer: Copper has very high thermal conductivity (401 W/m·K), which means it spreads heat from the flame evenly across the entire base, preventing hot spots and ensuring uniform cooking.

🧠 Check your understanding (2 of 3)

Question: Why are handles made of wood or Bakelite instead of metal?

Answer: Wood and Bakelite are thermal insulators — they conduct heat very poorly. This prevents the heat from the pot body from reaching your hand, keeping the handle safe to touch.

🧠 Check your understanding (3 of 3)

Question: What advantage does a multi-layer (clad) pan have over a single-material pan?

Answer: A clad pan combines the best properties of multiple materials — for example, aluminium for fast heat spreading and stainless steel for durability and food safety — giving better performance than any single material alone.
