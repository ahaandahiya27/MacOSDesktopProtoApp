✂️ Mini Project — Congruence by Cut-and-Fold

A hands-on activity that turns this chapter into something you actually do, not just read.

≈ 35 min · do-it-yourself level

Reading about maths and doing maths are two different things. This short project lets you see the ideas of "Geometric Twins" at work with your own hands.

What you'll need

  • Paper
  • Scissors
  • A ruler and pencil

Steps

  1. Draw any triangle and cut it out. Trace around it, then cut a second copy — the two should be congruent.
  2. Stack them: do they match exactly when slid, turned or flipped? If yes, they're congruent.
  3. Now draw an isosceles triangle (two equal sides) and cut it out.
  4. Fold it along the line from the apex to the middle of the base. Do the two halves land exactly on each other?
  5. Mark the two base angles before folding; after folding, check that they coincide — proving they're equal.

🔍 What you should notice

The two cut copies match exactly (congruent). The isosceles triangle's two halves coincide on folding, and its two base angles land on top of each other — showing equal sides really do face equal angles.

Why it works

Folding the isosceles triangle along the apex altitude maps one half onto the other — a reflection that is a congruence. Because the halves are congruent, the base angles are corresponding parts and must be equal. The scissors-and-fold makes Euclid's proof physical.
