Question 1 — The warm-up

What does it mean for two figures to be congruent?

🧠 Answer in your head first

Work it out before you read on — no peeking.

Answer: They have the same shape AND the same size, so one can be placed exactly on top of the other (by sliding, turning, or flipping). Congruence is written with the symbol ≅.

Question 2 — The check

Meera says the three side lengths are enough to make a triangle congruent to a given one, with no angles needed. Is she right?

🧠 Answer in your head first

Work it out before you read on — no peeking.

Answer: Yes. By the SSS criterion, three equal sides determine a triangle uniquely up to congruence. Constructing with the three lengths gives exactly one triangle shape.

Textbook page 4.

Question 3 — The stretch

Why don't two sides alone determine a unique triangle?

🧠 Answer in your head first

Work it out before you read on — no peeking.

Answer: Drawing the base and swinging arcs of the two given side-lengths from its ends produces two intersection points (one above and one below the base), giving two different triangles. So two sides are not enough.

Question 4 — The trap

List the valid congruence criteria for triangles.

🧠 Answer in your head first

Work it out before you read on — no peeking.

Answer: SSS (three sides), SAS (two sides and the included angle), ASA (two angles and the included side), and RHS (right angle, hypotenuse, one side). SSA is NOT a valid criterion.

Textbook page 6.

Question 5 — The finisher

Two triangles have two pairs of equal sides with equal included angles. Are they congruent? By which rule?

🧠 Answer in your head first

Work it out before you read on — no peeking.

Answer: Yes, by SAS (Side-Angle-Side). Two sides and the angle between them being equal forces the triangles to be congruent.
