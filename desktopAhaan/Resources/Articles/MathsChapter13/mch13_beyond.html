Beyond the Book — How One Average Can Fool You

Mean, median, and mode are three different "typical values" — and choosing the wrong one can completely change the story the data tells.

≈ 6 min read · curious-kid level

Data handling is really the art of summarising a big pile of numbers into something a person can understand. But every summary leaves things out — and a clever (or careless) summary can mislead. Read one idea at a time.

1. The "average" has three different meanings

When someone says "average," they might mean the mean (add everything, divide by how many), the median (the middle value when sorted), or the mode (the most common value). For most groups these three are close — but not always, and that gap is where the surprises hide.

💡 Try this

Find the mean, median, and mode of: 2, 2, 3, 4, 9. Mean = 4, median = 3, mode = 2. One small list, three different "typical" answers. Which one best describes the group? It depends on what you want to know.

2. When the mean lies

Imagine nine people earning ₹10,000 a month and one earning ₹1,000,000. The mean income looks like about ₹1,09,000 — but nobody actually earns near that! The single huge value drags the mean up. Here the median (₹10,000) tells the honest story. This is why news about incomes usually reports the median.

3. India's father of statistics

Modern statistics in India owes a huge amount to Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis, who founded the Indian Statistical Institute in 1931. He pioneered large-scale surveys and a famous measure of "distance" between data groups now called the Mahalanobis distance. His work helped shape India's early national planning.

4. A graph can mislead too

Bar graphs are honest only if the scale is honest. If a graph's vertical axis starts at 90 instead of 0, a tiny difference between 95 and 100 can look like a giant jump. Always check where the scale begins before believing what a graph seems to "show."

💡 Try this

Collect the shoe sizes of ten classmates. Work out the mean, median, and mode. Then decide which single number you would tell a shoemaker who could only stock one size — and explain why.
