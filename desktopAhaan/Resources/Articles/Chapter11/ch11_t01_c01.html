The Four-Chambered Heart

Two pumps in one organ · Class 7 Science

Why two pumps, not one

Your heart isn't one pump — it's two side-by-side. The right half pumps blood to the lungs to pick up oxygen. The left half pumps the oxygen-rich blood to the rest of your body. Both halves beat in perfect rhythm, but they handle completely different blood.

Four chambers, four jobs

  • Right atrium (RA) — receives oxygen-poor blood from the body (via the superior and inferior vena cava).
  • Right ventricle (RV) — pumps that blood up to the lungs through the pulmonary artery.
  • Left atrium (LA) — receives oxygen-rich blood back from the lungs through the pulmonary veins.
  • Left ventricle (LV) — the strongest chamber. Pumps the freshly-oxygenated blood to every cell in your body through the aorta.

One-way valves between each atrium and its ventricle (tricuspid on the right, bicuspid/mitral on the left), plus valves at the exit of each ventricle, ensure blood flows only forward.

The left ventricle wall is about 3× thicker than the right's. It has to push blood out to your feet (a 1.5 metre journey), while the right ventricle only has to push to the lungs (right next door).

The cardiac cycle

Each heartbeat has two phases:

  1. Systole — the ventricles contract, pushing blood out. Atrium-ventricle valves slam shut ("lub" sound).
  2. Diastole — the ventricles relax and fill. Exit valves slam shut ("dub" sound).

That's the "lub-DUB" you hear through a stethoscope. A normal heart rate is 60-100 beats per minute for a Class 7 child, about 100,000 beats per day, and around 2.5 billion beats in a lifetime.

Try this at home

Make a paper-tube stethoscope: roll a sheet of A4 into a tight tube. Press one end firmly against a friend's chest (just below the left collarbone) and the other end to your ear. Listen carefully — you'll hear "lub-DUB...lub-DUB". The two sounds are the valves snapping shut at the end of each phase.
