Story Mode — Transportation in Animals and Plants in Five Scenes

5 short scenes from real life that bring this chapter to a kitchen table, a garden, a classroom — somewhere you can picture.

≈ 10 min read · narrative-tier

Textbook examples can feel abstract. These five scenes don't happen in a textbook — they happen in places you go. Read them in order; each scene tightens the chapter's big idea by another notch.

Scene 1 — Heart rate during a 100m sprint

At rest, your heart beats ~72 times per minute. During a 100m sprint, it spikes to ~180+. Each beat pushes about 70 mL of blood; in a sprint that's 12+ litres per minute being pumped through your body. The heart is a fist-sized muscle doing the work of an industrial pump for the duration of the run. The dramatic recovery — heart returning to 72 in 5 minutes — is the cardiac system showing how robust it is.

Scene 2 — Blood donation camps and the gift of red cells

Every drop of donated blood gets used. A 350 mL bag from a donor in Bengaluru is separated into three parts: red blood cells (for accident victims and anaemia), plasma (for burn victims and clotting disorders), and platelets (for cancer patients). One donation can save up to 3 lives. India needs ~14 million units of blood per year — and currently collects only ~9 million. Indian Red Cross, RBC drives at colleges, and Rotary blood banks are the network keeping hospitals supplied.

Scene 3 — Sweating on a Delhi May afternoon — kidney + skin teamwork

On a 45°C day, you can lose 1–2 litres of sweat per hour. Your kidneys notice the drop in blood volume and produce concentrated urine (saving water). Your sweat glands pour water out of the skin to cool you. The body is constantly rebalancing — kidneys hold water in, skin lets water out — depending on temperature. Drink less water in heat, and the kidneys will eventually fail to keep up; that's heatstroke.

Scene 4 — Why a Rajasthan keekar tree drinks 200 litres a day

A mature mango or keekar (acacia) tree in Rajasthan loses ~200 litres of water through transpiration on a hot summer day. The water is sucked up from roots → trunk → branches → leaves → evaporates from stomata. This 'transpiration pull' is so strong it can lift water 100+ metres up a tall tree without any pump. The same physics: water column under tension acts like a stretched rope.

Scene 5 — Mango grafting — joining two transport systems

Indian mango farmers don't grow mangoes from seed (slow, unpredictable). They graft: a healthy alphonso branch (scion) is joined to a sturdy native mango rootstock. The cut surfaces are tied together with tape. Within weeks, the xylem and phloem of both halves fuse — sap can now flow continuously from root to leaf. One year later, the grafted tree fruits true alphonso despite having different roots. Plumbing engineering of plant transport.

Concrete examples are how your brain anchors abstract ideas. Pick the scene you remember best after reading. The next time you see it in real life, you'll remember the chapter.
