Xylem — The Water Pipeline

How a tree pulls water 100 metres up · Class 7 Science

Plants drink without a pump

A tall tree must move water from its roots up through 50, 80, even 100 metres of trunk to its leaves. There's no heart, no muscle, no obvious pump. Yet water flows continuously, all day, every day. How?

Xylem — a system of tiny straws

Inside the stem and roots are xylem vessels — narrow tubes made of dead, hollow cells stacked end-to-end. There are millions of them in a tree trunk, each only a fraction of a millimetre across. Water rises through these tubes like through a packed bundle of drinking straws.

Three forces pull water up

  • Transpiration pull (the main force). Water evaporates out through tiny pores (stomata) on the leaf surface. As each molecule leaves, it tugs the next molecule up — like sipping through a straw, but the 'suck' is powered by sunlight evaporating water.
  • Cohesion — water molecules stick to each other via hydrogen bonds. So the column of water in the xylem behaves as a continuous chain — pull at the top and the whole column rises.
  • Adhesion — water sticks to the xylem walls, helping it climb against gravity.
The cohesion-tension theory was first proposed by Henry Dixon in 1894. It predicts that water columns inside xylem can support tensions equivalent to several MEGAPASCALS (atmospheric pressure is 0.1 MPa) — and they DO hold together, because hydrogen bonds are surprisingly strong in narrow tubes.

How fast?

In a hot sunny day, water can flow up a tree's xylem at 1–4 metres per HOUR (slow by human standards but enormous for plants). A large oak tree pulls up about 400 litres of water per day. The Amazon rainforest's trees together transpire so much water they effectively act as a continent-scale pump moving moisture inland.

What xylem also carries

Not just water — dissolved minerals too. Nitrogen (as nitrate), potassium, phosphorus, calcium, magnesium, sulphur, and a dozen micronutrients are absorbed by roots from the soil and travel up the xylem with the water. That's how the leaves get their fertiliser.

Try this at home

Put a stick of celery (with leaves still attached) into a glass of water dyed with red or blue food colouring. Check after 1 hour, 4 hours, overnight. The dye climbs up the xylem visibly. Cut the stalk in cross-section after a day — you'll see red dots where the dye has stained the xylem vessels.

Looking ahead

Class 12 'Transport in Plants' adds the famous Münch hypothesis for phloem (sugar transport via osmotic pressure flow).
