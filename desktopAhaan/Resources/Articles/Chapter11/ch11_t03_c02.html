Xylem, Phloem and How Plants Move Water 100 m Up

Pumping sap to the top of a sequoia · Class 7 Science

Two pipelines, two directions

Plants have two long-distance transport tissues:

  • Xylem carries water and minerals from roots UP to leaves.
  • Phloem carries sugars made in leaves DOWN to roots, fruits, and storage organs.

Mnemonic: 'X-up, P-down'.

How water reaches the top — the cohesion-tension story

Water molecules stick to each other (cohesion). As leaves lose water through transpiration, they pull on the water column below. The pull travels all the way down to the roots, sucking up more water — like sipping through a very long, very thin straw.

The world's tallest plumbing

This pull is so strong that sequoia trees lift water 110 metres from root to canopy — without any pump, just leaf transpiration and water's cohesion. Engineers are still inspired by this passive plumbing.

Phloem — actively pumped

Unlike xylem, phloem requires energy. Sugar is loaded into phloem at leaves and pulled out at fruits/roots. The sugar concentration drives water in, creating pressure that pushes the sap. This is why phloem stops in dead bark — it needs living cells to run.

Tap a maple tree in spring and ~50 litres of sweet xylem sap flows out before the tree even realises. Boiling that sap concentrates it into maple syrup — a kind of liquid summer-energy harvested from the tree's plumbing.

Try this at home

Put a celery stalk in a glass of water mixed with food colouring. Wait 2-3 hours. Slice it across. You will see the xylem vessels stained — a beautiful proof of upward transport.
