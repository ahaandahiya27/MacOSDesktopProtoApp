India: A Home to Many

For thousands of years India has welcomed people of many faiths and lands, guided by values like vasudhaiva kutumbakam ('the whole world is family') and celebrated by Vivekananda in 1893. Newcomers came either seeking refuge from persecution or seeking opportunity through trade, and over time kept their traditions while becoming Indian. The ancient Jewish communities - the Bene Israel and the Cochin Jews - were among the first, granted land and freedom to worship.

  1. India's Ethos of AcceptanceFor thousands of years India has welcomed people of many faiths and lands, guided by values like vasudhaiva kutumbakam - 'the whole world is family'.
  2. Two Kinds of Newcomers: Refuge and OpportunityPeople came to India for two main reasons - to escape persecution at home, or to seek trade and a better life - and over time they became part of Indian society.
  3. The Jews of IndiaJewish communities like the ancient Bene Israel (Konkan coast) and the Cochin Jews found a safe haven in India, where the Raja of Kochi even granted them land 'as long as the world endures'.

Escaping Persecution: Christians and Parsis

Two communities fled religious persecution to find freedom in India. The Syriac (Syrian) Christians, persecuted in the Roman and Persian empires from the 4th century CE, travelled along trade routes to Kerala's Malabar coast. The Parsis, followers of Zoroastrianism fleeing the Islamic conquest of Persia, reached Gujarat between the 8th and 10th centuries - where the famous legend of milk and sugar shows how they promised to enrich, not burden, their new home.

  1. The Syriac Christians of KeralaFrom the 4th century CE, Syriac Christians persecuted in West Asia travelled along trade routes to the Malabar coast of Kerala, where they could live and worship freely.
  2. The Parsis and ZoroastrianismThe Parsis - followers of Zoroastrianism - fled the Islamic conquest of Persia and reached Gujarat (8th-10th centuries), where the legend of milk and sugar shows how they promised to enrich, not burden, their new home.

Trade and New Communities

Trade brought new communities to India. Arab merchants came peacefully from the 7th century, forming the Mappila Muslims and helping build India's oldest mosque - showing Islam first arrived by trade, not conquest. The Siddis, of African origin, were brought enslaved between the 7th and 19th centuries and created a unique African-Indian culture. And Armenian merchants, trading in spices and muslin, settled from the Malabar coast to Mughal Agra and made Kolkata a major hub, integrating even into the Mughal court.

  1. The Arab Merchant CommunityArab merchants came peacefully to India's west coast from the 7th century onward, trading spices, marrying locally, forming the Mappila Muslim community and helping build India's oldest mosque.
  2. The African Connection: The SiddisThe Siddis, of African origin, were brought to India enslaved between the 7th and 19th centuries; they created a unique African-Indian culture, are recognised as a scheduled tribe, and include achievers like Padma Shri awardee Hirabai Lobi.
  3. The Armenians in IndiaArmenian merchants traded in Indian spices and muslin for centuries, settling from the Malabar coast to Mughal Agra (where Akbar let them build a church) and making Kolkata a major Armenian hub.

Modern Refuge and the Unity of Humankind

India's tradition of refuge continued into modern times. The Baha'is, persecuted in 19th-century Persia, found a home (and most Baha'is here today are Indian). During World War II, the 'Good Maharaja' of Nawanagar sheltered around a thousand Polish orphans. And from 1959, the 14th Dalai Lama and many Tibetans took refuge in India, which helped preserve their culture and medicine. All these stories reflect India's core value of vasudhaiva kutumbakam - the unity of humankind - a gift the chapter offers to a troubled world.

  1. The Baha'i Community in IndiaThe Baha'i faith, founded in 19th-century Persia and persecuted there, found refuge in India; most Baha'is here today are Indians, and the Lotus Temple in Delhi welcomes people of all religions.
  2. The 'Good Maharaja' and the Polish ChildrenDuring World War II, Maharaja Digvijaysinhji of Nawanagar (Jamnagar) sheltered around a thousand orphaned Polish children, earning a memorial in Warsaw and the title the 'Good Maharaja'.
  3. Tibetan Refugees in IndiaWhen China annexed Tibet, the 14th Dalai Lama and many Tibetans took refuge in India from 1959; India helped them build settlements, monasteries and preserve their culture and medicine (Sowa Rigpa).
  4. Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam: India's Gift to the WorldThe many communities India welcomed reflect its core value of the fundamental unity of humankind - 'the whole world is family' - a civilisational ideal that can help solve today's global crises.
