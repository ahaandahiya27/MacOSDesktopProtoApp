History, geography and society are full of 'what if' turning points. Think about each question yourself first — then open the panel to compare your reasoning.

What if India had turned away the persecuted communities instead of welcoming them?

Think first, then open to compare

India would be far less diverse, and the world would have lost much. The Parsis - who became great industrialists and philanthropists - might never have flourished; ancient Jewish and Syriac Christian traditions might have vanished; Tibetan Buddhist learning might have had no safe home. And India would have lost the cultural richness, ideas and contributions these communities brought. It shows how a culture of acceptance benefits both the newcomers and the host.

What if every country practised vasudhaiva kutumbakam today?

Think first, then open to compare

If every country truly treated the whole world as one family, refugees fleeing war or disaster would always find shelter, and cooperation on shared problems like climate change and inequality would be far easier. There would be less discrimination and fewer conflicts driven by 'us versus them' thinking. The chapter suggests this ancient Indian value is exactly what a divided, crisis-hit world needs - which is why it calls the unity of humankind India's gift to the world.

What if India had turned away refugees instead of welcoming them?

Think first, then open to compare

Communities like the Parsis, the Jews of Kerala, and Tibetan refugees found safety in India and, in return, enriched it with crafts, trade, learning and faith. Had India shut its doors, it would be poorer in culture and would not embody vasudhaiva kuṭumbakam — 'the world is one family'. The willingness to give refuge has long been part of India's identity. Acceptance made the country more varied and more humane.
