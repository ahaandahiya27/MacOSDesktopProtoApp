"What If?" — द्वीपोऽण्डमानः

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook teaches what IS. Real understanding comes from asking what IF? Pick a question below, pause and think for a minute, then read one possible discussion.

What if 1 — the Cellular Jail had never been built?

The question: Imagine the British had instead exiled freedom-fighters within the mainland.

🧠 Think first

Would Indian independence have happened sooner, later, or at the same pace?

One discussion path: The Cellular Jail's brutality became powerful propaganda for the freedom movement — its existence galvanised opposition. A less spectacular exile might have created less moral pressure on British rule but also broken fewer revolutionary leaders. History rarely answers such counterfactuals cleanly; both directions are plausible.

What if 2 — India had opened the Sentinelese island to tourism?

The question: Imagine the law protecting the Sentinelese were lifted and visitors allowed.

🧠 Think first

What would happen?

One discussion path: Almost certainly catastrophe. The Sentinelese have no immunity to common diseases — even a single visitor's flu virus could be lethal to the tribe. India's protection law exists for this exact reason. The few attempts at contact (a documented 2018 case) ended badly. Sometimes the most respectful policy is distance.

What if 3 — Veer Savarkar had not survived the ten years in the Cellular Jail?

The question: Imagine he had died in confinement.

🧠 Think first

What would Indian history have lost?

One discussion path: His writing alone — poetry composed on prison walls, later political works — was substantial. He became a controversial figure post-independence, but the Cellular Jail's role as a National Memorial owes much to his eventual fame as a survivor. The chapter respects him for his ten years; history beyond the chapter is more complicated.

📝 Stretch your thinking

None of these has one 'correct' answer — they are for arguing about. Try one on a friend or a parent and see if their discussion path differs from yours.
