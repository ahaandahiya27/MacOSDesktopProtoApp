Cloning Plants in the Lab — Tissue Culture

From one cell to a million identical seedlings · Class 7 Science

What is tissue culture?

Take a tiny piece of a plant — even a single cell — and grow it in a sterile nutrient gel. Each cell can divide and eventually develop into a whole new plant. This is tissue culture, the laboratory version of asexual reproduction.

Why it matters

From one parent plant you can grow thousands of identical copies in months — much faster than waiting for seeds. Useful for:

  • Saving endangered orchids and medicinal plants.
  • Producing disease-free banana, pineapple and sugarcane stocks.
  • Mass-producing high-yield rice or vegetable varieties.

How it's done

  1. A small bit of stem or leaf is sterilised with bleach.
  2. It's placed on agar gel with sugar, vitamins and plant hormones.
  3. The hormone balance is shifted to trigger root growth, then shoot growth.
  4. The tiny plantlet is moved to soil — and grows into a full plant.

Indian success stories

India's banana plantations are now almost entirely from tissue-cultured stock — yields jumped 30–50 % once farmers switched. The National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources (NBPGR) maintains tissue cultures of rare medicinal plants like Picrorhiza kurroa.

Because all tissue-culture plants from one parent are genetically identical, a single new disease can wipe them all out. That's why scientists still keep wild seed banks — diversity is insurance.

Try this at home

Try the easiest 'mini tissue culture': cut a stem of money plant, place in a glass of water in light. New roots emerge in 7-10 days. That single stem can grow into an entire plant — asexual reproduction by cutting.
