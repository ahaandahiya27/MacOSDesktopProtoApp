One Curious Person Behind Chapter 12

The scientist most tied to reproduction in plants — their question, their experiment, and what changed because of them.

≈ 4 min read · biography

Every chapter has a person behind it — someone who first asked the right question, ran the right experiment, or wrote down the answer. This is a one-page portrait of the scientist most tied to this chapter's content.

Edavaleth Kakkat Janaki Ammal

1897–1984 · Indian

🎧 In one line

First Indian woman to receive a PhD in Botany; pioneered sugarcane hybridisation that made India self-sufficient in sugar.

E.K. Janaki Ammal was born in Tellicherry, Kerala, in 1897. She studied botany at Presidency College Madras, then went to the University of Michigan in 1925 — at a time when Indian women rarely studied abroad. She returned to India and joined the Sugarcane Breeding Station in Coimbatore. In the 1930s, Indian sugarcane gave low yields and was dependent on Java imports. Janaki Ammal cross-bred Indian sugarcane (high sugar but low yield) with wild Saccharum spontaneum (low sugar but disease-resistant). Her hybrid varieties tripled India's sugar yields and ended import dependence. She also did pioneering work on chromosomes of magnolia plants (her cytology textbook is still cited). When Prime Minister Nehru asked her to help build Indian science, she returned from England to lead the Botanical Survey of India. She lived simply in a small house in Madras, growing magnolias she'd bred herself. She used cross-pollination — Class 7's sexual reproduction — to literally re-engineer Indian sugar supply. The Janaki Ammal National Award for Plant Taxonomy is given in her honour every year.
