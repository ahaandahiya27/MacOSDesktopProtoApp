Q1. Fill in the blanks: (a) Production of new individuals from the vegetative part of a parent is called ______. (b) A flower may have either the male or the female reproductive parts. Such a flower is called ______. (c) The transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of the same or another flower is called ______. (d) The fusion of male and female gametes is termed as ______. (e) Seed dispersal takes place by means of ______, ______, and ______.

Model answer: (a) vegetative propagation (b) unisexual (c) pollination (d) fertilisation (e) wind, water, and animals (some also accept explosion).

Q2. Describe the different methods of asexual reproduction. Give examples.

Model answer: Five main asexual methods. (1) Budding — a small bud grows on parent, separates as new individual. Example: yeast, hydra. (2) Fragmentation — body breaks into pieces; each piece grows into a new individual. Example: spirogyra. (3) Vegetative propagation — new plants from roots/stem/leaves of parent. Examples: potato (tuber), onion (bulb), bryophyllum (leaf), ginger (rhizome), sugarcane (stem cuttings). (4) Spore formation — parent produces spores in tough containers; each spore can grow into new individual. Example: bread mould (rhizopus), fern. (5) Binary fission — single cell splits into two. Example: amoeba.

Q3. Explain what you understand by sexual reproduction.

Model answer: Sexual reproduction is when TWO gametes (sex cells) fuse to produce a new individual. In plants, the male gamete is inside the pollen grain (produced in anthers); the female gamete is the egg (inside the ovule in the ovary). Pollination transfers pollen to stigma; the pollen grows a tube down through the style to reach the ovary; the male and female gametes fuse — that's fertilisation. The fertilised egg becomes a seed. The seed contains genetic material from BOTH parents — so the offspring is similar but not identical to either parent. This genetic mixing is the great advantage of sexual reproduction.

Q4. State the main difference between sexual and asexual reproduction.

Model answer: Sexual reproduction needs TWO parents (or two sex cells). Asexual needs only ONE. Sexual produces offspring that are GENETICALLY DIFFERENT from either parent (mix of both). Asexual produces clones — exact copies of the parent. Sexual is slow and energetically expensive; asexual is fast and cheap. Sexual generates variation (helps species adapt to change); asexual maintains a successful blueprint exactly. Most plants and animals use BOTH, depending on conditions.

Q5. Sketch the reproductive parts of a flower.

Model answer: A typical flower has: (1) Petals — coloured, attract pollinators. (2) Sepals — green, protect bud. (3) Stamens (male) — each has a stalk (filament) topped by an anther where pollen forms. (4) Pistil/Carpel (female) — has stigma (sticky top), style (stalk), ovary (base, containing ovules with female gametes). A bisexual flower has both stamens and pistil. After pollination and fertilisation, the ovary swells into a fruit and ovules become seeds.

Q6. Explain the difference between self-pollination and cross-pollination.

Model answer: Self-pollination: pollen from a flower's own anther lands on its own stigma (or another flower of the same plant). The offspring is genetically very similar to the parent. Found in peas, wheat, rice. Cross-pollination: pollen from one plant's anther reaches another (genetically different) plant's stigma. The offspring has mixed genes from two different parents — more variation. Found in mango, papaya, cucumber. Cross-pollination requires a vector (insect, bird, wind, water); self-pollination doesn't. Most cultivated cereal crops self-pollinate (predictable yield); fruit trees cross-pollinate (better fruit set).

Q7. How does the process of fertilisation take place in flowers?

Model answer: After pollen reaches the stigma, it absorbs sticky stigma fluid and germinates: a long thin pollen tube grows down through the style toward the ovary. The pollen tube carries two male gametes inside it. When it reaches an ovule, it releases the gametes. One male gamete fuses with the egg cell — that's fertilisation. The fertilised egg (zygote) divides repeatedly to form the embryo of a new plant, which is packaged into a seed. The other male gamete fuses with two female cells to form the food storage tissue (endosperm) inside the seed. Both fusions together: 'double fertilisation' — unique to flowering plants.

Q8. Discuss how seeds get dispersed by wind, water, and animals.

Model answer: WIND: lightweight, often with wings or parachutes. Drumstick seeds have papery wings; dandelion seeds have fluffy parachutes; cotton seeds have cotton fibres. Wind carries them far. WATER: floating seeds with waterproof coats. Coconut floats in seawater; lotus seeds float on ponds. They can travel rivers, oceans, then germinate where they land. ANIMALS: some seeds are STICKY or have hooks (e.g., the Xanthium 'cocklebur') and cling to animal fur. Others are inside FRUITS that animals eat — animal digests the fruit but the seed passes through intact, gets deposited elsewhere with natural manure. Mango seeds eaten by elephants this way travel kilometres.
