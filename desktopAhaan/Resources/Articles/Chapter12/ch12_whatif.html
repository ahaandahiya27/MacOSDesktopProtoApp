Three "What Ifs?" About Reproduction in Plants

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if all bees disappeared from Earth?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: About 1/3 of all human food (by weight) depends on bee pollination — apples, mangoes, almonds, cucumber, coffee, cocoa, watermelon. Without bees, these crops would fail. Wind-pollinated cereals (wheat, rice, maize) would survive. We'd be left with bread and rice but no fruit. Wild flowering plants (millions of species) would also collapse, taking down the animals that feed on them. Albert Einstein supposedly said 'if bees disappear, humans have 4 years left.' Whether or not he said it, the ecological collapse would be severe. India has lost ~30% of its honey bee population to pesticides in the last decade — a quiet crisis.

What if 2 — What if a flower had no petals?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Many flowers DO lack petals — and they're all wind-pollinated. Petals exist to attract animal pollinators; without animals, they're wasted energy. Grasses, oaks, willows, rice, wheat, maize — no petals (or tiny green ones). Such flowers must produce HUGE amounts of pollen, because wind dispersal is wasteful (most pollen lands on the wrong place). One maize tassel releases millions of pollen grains; only a few hundred actually reach a silk. Petals are an investment in efficiency — fewer pollen grains, but each one delivered.

What if 3 — What if a banana could reproduce sexually?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: It would have hard seeds you'd have to spit out (like wild bananas still do). But: genetic variation. Cultivated banana right now is a single clone (Cavendish variety, replaced Gros Michel after a fungus wiped Gros Michel out in the 1960s). If a new fungus attacked the Cavendish (and one is currently spreading — Panama Tropical Race 4), every banana on Earth could disappear. Sexual reproduction = different genes = different vulnerabilities = species survival. Cloned crops are a hidden agricultural risk. India is breeding new banana varieties at IIHR Bangalore for exactly this reason.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
