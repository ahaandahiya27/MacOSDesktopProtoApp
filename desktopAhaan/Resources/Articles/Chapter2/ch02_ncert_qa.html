Q1. Fill in the blanks: (a) The main steps of nutrition in humans are ______. (b) The largest gland in the human body is ______. (c) The stomach releases hydrochloric acid and ______ juices, which act on food. (d) The inner wall of the small intestine has many finger-like outgrowths called ______. (e) Amoeba digests its food in the ______.

Model answer: (a) ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation, egestion (b) liver (c) digestive (or pepsin) (d) villi (e) food vacuole.

Q2. Mark true or false: (a) Digestion of starch starts in the stomach. (b) The tongue helps in mixing food with saliva. (c) The gall bladder temporarily stores bile. (d) The ruminants bring back swallowed grass into their mouth and chew it for some time.

Model answer: (a) False — digestion of starch starts in the mouth with salivary amylase. (b) True. (c) True. (d) True — this is called rumination.

Q3. Why do we need to chew food?

Model answer: Chewing has three purposes. (1) It breaks food into smaller pieces so digestive enzymes can reach more surface area. (2) It mixes food with saliva, which both moistens it for swallowing and begins starch digestion (amylase). (3) It signals the rest of the digestive tract to prepare — stomach starts releasing acid, intestines start releasing enzymes. Eating without chewing properly means more work for the stomach and slower, less efficient digestion.

Q4. Why do you think buffaloes and cows graze grass and return to chew it later?

Model answer: Grass is mostly cellulose — a tough fibre that's hard to break down. Cows have evolved a four-chambered stomach to handle it. They graze quickly during the day to fill the first chamber (rumen), where bacteria begin softening the cellulose. Later, while resting safely, they bring small balls of partly-digested grass (cud) back to the mouth, chew them thoroughly, and re-swallow. This two-step process maximises cellulose breakdown that one chewing couldn't achieve.

Q5. What is the function of the digestive enzymes?

Model answer: Digestive enzymes break large food molecules into small ones that can be absorbed into the blood. They act like specialised scissors — each enzyme cuts only one kind of food: amylase cuts starch into sugar, pepsin and trypsin cut proteins into amino acids, lipase cuts fats into fatty acids and glycerol. Without enzymes, food would pass through the body undigested and unused.

Q6. How is the small intestine designed to absorb digested food?

Model answer: The small intestine has three design features that maximise absorption. (1) It is very long (~6 metres in an adult), giving food a long contact time. (2) Its inner wall is covered with millions of tiny finger-like projections called villi, and each villus has even tinier projections (microvilli) on its surface. Together they multiply the absorption surface to roughly the size of a tennis court. (3) Each villus has a rich blood supply, so absorbed nutrients are quickly carried away.

Q7. Match the items of Column I with those of Column II. (food → primary organ of digestion)

Model answer: Starch → mouth (saliva amylase) and small intestine. Protein → stomach (pepsin) and small intestine (trypsin). Fat → small intestine (bile + lipase). Water → large intestine (re-absorption). Glucose / amino acids → small intestine (villi absorption).

Q8. Write one similarity and one difference between the nutrition in Amoeba and human beings.

Model answer: Similarity: Both are heterotrophs that follow the basic steps of ingestion → digestion → absorption → assimilation → egestion; both digest food using enzymes. Difference: Amoeba is a single cell — it engulfs food with pseudopodia (fake feet) and digests it inside a food vacuole; humans are multicellular and have a dedicated digestive system with many specialised organs (mouth, stomach, intestines).
