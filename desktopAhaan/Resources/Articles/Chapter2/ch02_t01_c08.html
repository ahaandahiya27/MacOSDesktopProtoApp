The liver and bile — breaking down fats

The liver makes bile, a greenish fluid that breaks fats into tiny droplets so digestive enzymes can attack them.

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Your liver is your body's biggest internal organ. One of its many jobs is making bile — a greenish-yellow liquid that flows into your small intestine when you eat fatty food. Bile doesn't have enzymes; instead, it acts like detergent, breaking fat globules into tiny droplets (emulsification).

The liver evolved as a 'chemical factory' that sits between the gut and the rest of the body — a filter to detoxify and process everything eaten before it reaches the bloodstream. Its 500+ functions make it the body's most multi-tasking organ. This concept explains how your digestive system accomplishes this, step by step.

What this concept means

The liver synthesises 800 mL to 1 L of bile daily. Bile contains bile salts (derived from cholesterol), bilirubin (a pigment from haemoglobin breakdown), cholesterol, and phospholipids. It is stored in the gall bladder and released via the bile duct into the duodenum in response to the hormone cholecystokinin (CCK).

Why it works this way

💡 Did you know?

The ancient Egyptians believed the liver was the seat of emotion and intelligence — not far wrong, given how critical the liver is to thought and mood (via glucose regulation and detoxification).

Real-world applications

  1. Why bacon grease solidifies when cold — Hot bacon fat is liquid; cold bacon fat is solid. Bile acts like a liquid detergent to keep dietary fats emulsified in the warm, watery intestine.
  2. Jaundice from liver disease — When the liver is damaged, bilirubin backs up into the blood → yellow skin and eyes. A sign that the liver isn't processing bile properly.
  3. Gallstones blocking bile — If the gall bladder develops stones, they can plug the bile duct → no fat digestion, yellow stools, abdominal pain.
  4. Fatty liver disease from obesity — Too much sugar and alcohol → excess glucose converted to fat and stored in the liver → fatty infiltration → liver damage.
  5. Alcohol and the liver — The liver detoxifies alcohol by oxidising ethanol to acetaldehyde, then acetic acid. Chronic drinking overwhelms this → cirrhosis.
  6. Bile recycling — About 95% of bile salts are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and recycled back to the liver — the same bile molecules used 6-8 times daily.
  7. Egg yolk emulsifier — Egg yolk contains lecithin, a natural emulsifier. Chefs use it to make mayonnaise — mimicking what bile does in your intestine.
  8. Hepatitis A from contaminated water — The virus infects liver cells → inflammation → jaundice, fatigue. Usually recovers, but liver damage is real.
  9. Cholestyramine drug — This drug binds bile in the intestine and removes it → more bile must be made from cholesterol → cholesterol levels drop. Indirect lipid control.
  10. Liver transplant necessity — Severely damaged livers (end-stage cirrhosis, acute failure) cannot recover → the only option is a liver transplant from a donor.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: What would happen if your stomach didn't produce hydrochloric acid?

Answer: Food wouldn't be broken down chemically. Only the mechanical churning would happen, leaving larger particles that are harder for the small intestine to absorb. Proteins wouldn't be denatured by pepsin, and you'd lose a major source of nutrition.

🧠 Try another

Question: Why is the small intestine much longer than the large intestine?

Answer: The small intestine is where most digestion finishes and absorption happens — it needs maximum surface area (about 6 meters long). The large intestine is only for water reabsorption and waste storage, so it doesn't need to be long.

🧠 One more

Question: Does the liver digest food, or does it do something else?

Answer: The liver doesn't digest food directly. Instead, it produces bile (which breaks down fats), stores glucose, detoxifies poisons, and processes amino acids. It's more of a chemical factory and warehouse than a digestive organ.
