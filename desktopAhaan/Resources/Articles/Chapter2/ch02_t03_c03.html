Smell, Taste and the Science of Flavour

What you call 'taste' is mostly smell — your tongue detects only 5 basic tastes; the nose decodes the other 9000 flavours.

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Pinch your nose and bite into an apple. Now bite into a raw onion. They taste shockingly similar — sweet, watery, mildly sour. Why? Your tongue only knows 5 tastes (sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami). Everything else — strawberry vs raspberry, mango vs banana — is your nose recognising the smell that rises from your mouth into the nasal cavity. Lose your sense of smell and you lose 90 % of flavour.

Evolution gave us this combination as a safety system: bitter detects poisons, sour detects spoilage, sweet detects energy. Smell adds the discrimination needed to recognise specific foods, predators, and mates. The brain merges them into one experience because survival depends on accurate food evaluation. This concept explains how your digestive system accomplishes this, step by step.

What this concept means

Taste is the perception of soluble chemicals by taste buds on the tongue, detecting five primary qualities: sweet (sugars), salty (Na⁺), sour (H⁺), bitter (alkaloids and toxins), and umami (glutamate). Flavour is the brain's integration of taste with retronasal olfaction (volatile compounds rising from the mouth to the olfactory epithelium during chewing), texture (mouthfeel), temperature, and pain (capsaicin, menthol). Studies show 75–95 % of perceived flavour is attributable to smell.

Why it works this way

Evolution gave us this combination as a safety system: bitter detects poisons, sour detects spoilage, sweet detects energy. Smell adds the discrimination needed to recognise specific foods, predators, and mates. The brain merges them into one experience because survival depends on accurate food evaluation.

💡 Did you know?

Plants and animals exploit our flavour wiring — fruits use sweet smells to advertise ripeness for seed dispersal; flowers like the corpse flower mimic rotting meat to attract fly pollinators.

Real-world applications

  1. Pinch-nose apple test — Pinch your nose, eat an apple slice and an onion slice — they taste almost identical. Release nose, smell rushes in, flavour returns.
  2. Cold and bland food — When you have a stuffy cold, food 'has no taste'. Your tongue still works; your blocked nose doesn't.
  3. COVID anosmia — A common symptom of COVID-19 is sudden loss of smell — patients describe coffee as 'just hot bitter water'.
  4. Wine sommelier training — Wine experts train their olfactory cortex to distinguish 1000+ aromas — the tongue alone can't.
  5. Spicy food and 'taste' — Chilli's heat is detected by pain receptors (TRPV1), not taste buds — the tongue interprets it as taste because of co-activation.
  6. Hot vs cold food — Cold foods release fewer volatile aroma molecules; cold soup tastes blander than hot soup of identical recipe.
  7. Mango variety chemistry — Each mango cultivar has a unique volatile signature — Alphonso, Kesar, Langra differ chemically more than they differ on the tongue.
  8. Aromatic biryani layer — The fragrant rice in biryani works because saffron, cardamom, and bay leaf release retronasal aromas with each bite.
  9. Bitter-coffee tolerance — Coffee tastes bitter to children and pleasant to adults — repeated exposure rewires the brain's bitter response.
  10. Food labelled 'natural flavour' — On a packaging label, 'natural flavour' usually means a precise volatile compound (e.g. ethyl butyrate for pineapple) extracted from real food.

🌍 In your body right now

The science of smell, taste and the science of flavour is working in your body every moment. Pay attention to the physical sensations: your digestive system is one of the few body systems you can feel actively working.

🧪 Try this at home

Look in a mirror and identify your incisors, canines, and molars by shape. Feel with your tongue which teeth are sharper (canines) and which are flatter (molars). Match the shape to the function you learned.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: What would happen if your stomach didn't produce hydrochloric acid?

Answer: Food wouldn't be broken down chemically. Only the mechanical churning would happen, leaving larger particles that are harder for the small intestine to absorb. Proteins wouldn't be denatured by pepsin, and you'd lose a major source of nutrition.

🧠 Try another

Question: Why is the small intestine much longer than the large intestine?

Answer: The small intestine is where most digestion finishes and absorption happens — it needs maximum surface area (about 6 meters long). The large intestine is only for water reabsorption and waste storage, so it doesn't need to be long.

🧠 One more

Question: Does the liver digest food, or does it do something else?

Answer: The liver doesn't digest food directly. Instead, it produces bile (which breaks down fats), stores glucose, detoxifies poisons, and processes amino acids. It's more of a chemical factory and warehouse than a digestive organ.
