Chapter 2

Nutrition in Animals

How animals take in food, break it down, and convert it into energy and growth

≈ 45 min read · 3 topics · 20 concepts

Animals can't make their own food, so every animal — from a snake that swallows a whole egg to a cow that chews cud — has evolved its own way to take food in and break it down. This chapter walks through the five steps of nutrition (ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation, egestion), the human alimentary canal, and special tricks like rumination and Amoeba's pseudopodia.

Unlike plants that cook their own meals from sunlight and air, animals must eat. But eating is just the first step. Food must be broken down, absorbed, transported, and finally transformed into energy, growth, repair, and reproduction. Each step is a marvel of chemistry and mechanics.

This chapter walks you through the five steps of nutrition, the human alimentary canal, the specialized digestive systems of ruminants and single-celled organisms, and finally how cells extract usable energy from absorbed nutrients. By the end, you'll understand why you get hungry, what happens when you swallow, and why your body feels energized after a meal.

Why this chapter matters

Survival depends on nutrition. Every animal must find food, eat it, and extract its nutrients. How an animal eats shapes its entire body — a lion's sharp teeth for tearing meat, a cow's flat molars for grinding grass, an Amoeba's ability to engulf prey with pseudopodia.

Your health depends on digestion. Digestive disorders, food allergies, malnutrition, and metabolic diseases are failures of the digestive system. Understanding how digestion works helps you understand how to keep yours healthy.

This chapter is the foundation for Class 10 and 12 biology. Enzyme action, cellular respiration, and photosynthesis all depend on understanding nutrition first. The energy captured by plants flows through herbivores to carnivores — a concept that shapes all of ecology.

Timeline: How we learned about nutrition

  • 200 AD — Galen: Roman physician. Performed public animal dissections and mapped the digestive organs. Believed in "humours" but correctly identified the stomach, intestines, and liver.
  • 1770s — Spallanzani: Italian naturalist. Experimented with falcons and tubes. First to prove that digestion was a chemical process, not just mechanical grinding.
  • 1810s-1822 — William Beaumont: Army surgeon. Treated a musket wound in Alexis St. Martin's stomach. Had unique access to observe digestion directly. Documented acid secretion and food breakdown.
  • 1908 — Élie Metchnikoff: Russian biologist. Discovered white blood cells (phagocytes) that fight infection. Won the Nobel Prize. Promoted yogurt as a health food.
  • 2007-Present — Human Microbiome Project: International collaboration. Sequenced thousands of bacterial species in the human gut. Revealed that "you" are part human, part bacterial colony — 38 trillion bacterial cells for every human cell.
