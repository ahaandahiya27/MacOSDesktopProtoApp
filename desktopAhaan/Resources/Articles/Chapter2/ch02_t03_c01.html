How your cells use absorbed nutrients — the energy factory

Glucose enters cells and is broken down in the mitochondria to release energy that powers all life activities.

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Once glucose crosses into the blood from the small intestine, it travels to cells everywhere. Inside each cell is a tiny power plant called the mitochondrion. Here, glucose is broken down step-by-step to release energy (ATP).

Cells need ATP to power every function. This concept explains how your digestive system accomplishes this, step by step.

What this concept means

Cellular respiration occurs in the mitochondria in three stages: glycolysis, Krebs cycle, and electron transport chain. This process converts glucose into ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the cell's energy currency.

Why it works this way

Cells need ATP to power every function.

💡 Did you know?

Mitochondrial dysfunction is implicated in aging and many diseases.

Real-world applications

  1. Tired after skipped breakfast — No glucose → mitochondria run on fat reserves → ATP output is slower → you feel sluggish.
  2. Caffeine wakefulness — Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, masking the 'I need rest' signal that builds up as ATP is used.
  3. Exercise builds mitochondria — Endurance training tells muscle cells to make more mitochondria → more energy capacity → faster recovery.
  4. Insulin resistance — Type-2 diabetes happens when cells stop responding to insulin → glucose can't enter → mitochondria starve.
  5. Lactic acid burn — Sprinting outpaces oxygen delivery; mitochondria switch to anaerobic mode → lactic acid builds → muscles burn.
  6. Cyanide poisoning — Cyanide blocks mitochondrial electron transport — cells suffocate even with full oxygen in the blood.
  7. Statin muscle pain — Some cholesterol-lowering drugs interfere with mitochondrial CoQ10 → muscle aches in some patients.
  8. Altitude sickness — Low oxygen at high altitude limits mitochondrial output → headache, fatigue, breathlessness until acclimatisation.
  9. Brown fat heating — Brown adipose tissue contains specialised mitochondria that burn fat directly to heat — babies have lots of it.
  10. Ageing and mitochondria — Mitochondrial DNA accumulates damage with age → cells produce less ATP → tissues weaken → ageing.
  11. Mitochondrial disease — Mutations in mitochondrial DNA cause severe diseases where cells can't produce enough ATP for organ function.
  12. Cold sensitivity and heat production — Brown adipose tissue (brown fat) has extra mitochondria that burn glucose specifically to generate heat, protecting newborns and hibernating animals.
  13. Endurance training adaptations — Aerobic exercise increases mitochondrial density in muscle cells, allowing athletes to produce more ATP and exercise longer.

🌍 In your body right now

The science of how your cells use absorbed nutrients — the energy factory is working in your body every moment. Pay attention to the physical sensations: your digestive system is one of the few body systems you can feel actively working.

🧪 Try this at home

Place some bread in your mouth without chewing for 30 seconds. Notice it begins to taste sweet — that's salivary amylase breaking down starch into maltose. This proves digestion starts in the mouth.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: What would happen if your stomach didn't produce hydrochloric acid?

Answer: Food wouldn't be broken down chemically. Only the mechanical churning would happen, leaving larger particles that are harder for the small intestine to absorb. Proteins wouldn't be denatured by pepsin, and you'd lose a major source of nutrition.

🧠 Try another

Question: Why is the small intestine much longer than the large intestine?

Answer: The small intestine is where most digestion finishes and absorption happens — it needs maximum surface area (about 6 meters long). The large intestine is only for water reabsorption and waste storage, so it doesn't need to be long.

🧠 One more

Question: Does the liver digest food, or does it do something else?

Answer: The liver doesn't digest food directly. Instead, it produces bile (which breaks down fats), stores glucose, detoxifies poisons, and processes amino acids. It's more of a chemical factory and warehouse than a digestive organ.
