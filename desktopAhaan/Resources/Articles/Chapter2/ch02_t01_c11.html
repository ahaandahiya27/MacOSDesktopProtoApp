Real-world applications

  1. Coeliac disease starvation — Gluten flattens the villi; nutrients pass through the gut without being absorbed → weight loss.
  2. Diarrhoea damages villi — Severe gut infection blunts villi for days — even after symptoms go, nutrient absorption stays poor.
  3. Iron and B12 absorption sites — Iron is absorbed in the duodenum; B12 in the ileum — different villi specialise in different nutrients.
  4. Gastric bypass surgery — Surgeons skip past most of the small intestine — patients lose weight because absorption area is shorter.
  5. Probiotics protect villi — Beneficial bacteria help maintain villi integrity; this is why yogurt is recommended after antibiotics.
  6. Fasting and atrophy — Long fasts cause villi to shrink; gentle re-feeding (broth → soft food → solids) helps them rebuild.
  7. Lactose intolerance — Lactase enzyme lives on the microvilli tips; gut infections that damage tips cause temporary lactose intolerance.
  8. Chemotherapy side effects — Chemo kills fast-dividing cells, including villi cells — leading to mucositis, nausea, and weight loss.
  9. Surface area magic — If straightened out, your small intestine's villi cover the area of a tennis court — packed into a 6-metre tube.
  10. Crohn's disease — Patches of small intestine become inflamed; villi in those patches can't absorb properly → vitamin deficiencies.
  11. Gastric bypass surgery — Surgeons reduce intestinal surface area to treat obesity, but this means fewer villi for nutrient absorption, requiring careful dietary management.
  12. Celiac disease — The autoimmune attack damages villi, flattening them and reducing absorption — causing malabsorption even when eating adequate food.
  13. Tropical diseases (intestinal parasites) — Worms and parasites damage villi, reducing nutrient absorption and causing stunted growth in affected children.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: What would happen if your stomach didn't produce hydrochloric acid?

Answer: Food wouldn't be broken down chemically. Only the mechanical churning would happen, leaving larger particles that are harder for the small intestine to absorb. Proteins wouldn't be denatured by pepsin, and you'd lose a major source of nutrition.

🧠 Try another

Question: Why is the small intestine much longer than the large intestine?

Answer: The small intestine is where most digestion finishes and absorption happens — it needs maximum surface area (about 6 meters long). The large intestine is only for water reabsorption and waste storage, so it doesn't need to be long.

🧠 One more

Question: Does the liver digest food, or does it do something else?

Answer: The liver doesn't digest food directly. Instead, it produces bile (which breaks down fats), stores glucose, detoxifies poisons, and processes amino acids. It's more of a chemical factory and warehouse than a digestive organ.
