Question 3 — from Chapter 2

A friend says: 'Brushing teeth twice a day is enough — sugar doesn't really cause cavities, bacteria do.' Evaluate this claim. Is your friend right, wrong, or partly correct? Justify with the biology of how cavities actually form.

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: Partly correct — bacteria are the proximate cause, sugar is the necessary fuel. Mouth bacteria (mainly Streptococcus mutans) feed on sugar leftovers and excrete acids. The acids dissolve tooth enamel, creating cavities. Without sugar, the bacteria starve and can't make enough acid; without bacteria, sugar alone wouldn't dissolve enamel. So 'sugar doesn't cause cavities, bacteria do' misses that sugar IS the trigger that lets bacteria be destructive. Brushing twice removes both bacteria AND sugar — that's why it works.

Why: Name the actors: Streptococcus mutans bacteria + dietary sugar + tooth enamel.

Question 5 — from Chapter 2

An amoeba and a paramecium both ingest food by surrounding it. Compare HOW each one engulfs food and which method seems more efficient for a single-celled organism.

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: Amoeba: extends pseudopodia (false feet) — temporary projections of cytoplasm — around the food particle, eventually meeting on the far side to form a food vacuole. Slow, opportunistic, works for any food size up to the cell. Paramecium: has a fixed oral groove with cilia (tiny hairs) that beat to sweep food particles into a permanent cytostome (cell mouth). Fast, specialised, but only works for small particles. Paramecium is more efficient PER UNIT TIME for small food; amoeba is more flexible across food sizes. Different strategies for different niches.

Why: Compare on TWO axes: speed (paramecium wins) and flexibility (amoeba wins).
