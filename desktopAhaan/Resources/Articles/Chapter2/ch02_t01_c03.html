The mouth and your 32 teeth

An adult human has 32 teeth in four kinds — incisors for cutting, canines for tearing, premolars and molars for grinding.

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Open your mouth and count your teeth — an adult has 32 of them, each one shaped for a specific job. The flat ones in front (8 incisors) cut food like scissors. The pointy ones next (4 canines) tear meat. The bumpy ones at the back (8 premolars and 12 molars) grind food into a paste. Together with saliva from glands under your tongue, your mouth turns roti into a soft ball ready to swallow.

Food enters the body through the mouth, so the mouth must do four things at once: cut, tear, grind, and moisten. Four tooth shapes plus saliva is nature's elegant solution. Different jobs, different tools, all in one place. This concept explains how your digestive system accomplishes this, step by step.

What this concept means

Adult humans have 32 permanent teeth: 8 incisors (cutting), 4 canines (tearing), 8 premolars and 12 molars (grinding). Each tooth has a crown (the visible part above the gum), a neck (at the gum line), and a root (anchored in the jaw). The crown is covered with enamel — the hardest substance in the human body. Inside is dentine, and at the centre is the pulp cavity containing nerves and blood vessels. The mouth also contains the tongue and three pairs of salivary glands that secrete saliva, which moistens food and begins starch digestion through the enzyme salivary amylase (ptyalin).

Why it works this way

Food enters the body through the mouth, so the mouth must do four things at once: cut, tear, grind, and moisten. Four tooth shapes plus saliva is nature's elegant solution. Different jobs, different tools, all in one place.

💡 Did you know?

Wisdom teeth (the back-most molars) are leftovers from our ancestors who ate tougher, less-cooked food. Modern jaws are smaller because cooking made chewing easier — so wisdom teeth often don't fit and need removal. Evolution in real time, visible in your dentist's chair.

Real-world applications

  1. Why babies are toothless — Babies start without teeth because they only need milk. Around 6 months, milk teeth (20 of them) start growing in time for solid food. By age 12-13, all 32 permanent teeth are usually in place.
  2. A cracker in your mouth — Bite — incisors do the cutting. Move it back — molars start crushing. Saliva covers it. After 30 chews you've already started digesting the starch (it tastes slightly sweet — that's amylase at work).
  3. A lion's teeth at the zoo — Compare a lion's huge canines and missing flat molars with your teeth. Their jaw is built for ripping flesh, yours for a mixed diet.

🌍 In your body right now

The science of the mouth and your 32 teeth is working in your body every moment. Pay attention to the physical sensations: your digestive system is one of the few body systems you can feel actively working.

🧪 Try this at home

Look in a mirror and identify your incisors, canines, and molars by shape. Feel with your tongue which teeth are sharper (canines) and which are flatter (molars). Match the shape to the function you learned.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: What would happen if your stomach didn't produce hydrochloric acid?

Answer: Food wouldn't be broken down chemically. Only the mechanical churning would happen, leaving larger particles that are harder for the small intestine to absorb. Proteins wouldn't be denatured by pepsin, and you'd lose a major source of nutrition.

🧠 Try another

Question: Why is the small intestine much longer than the large intestine?

Answer: The small intestine is where most digestion finishes and absorption happens — it needs maximum surface area (about 6 meters long). The large intestine is only for water reabsorption and waste storage, so it doesn't need to be long.

🧠 One more

Question: Does the liver digest food, or does it do something else?

Answer: The liver doesn't digest food directly. Instead, it produces bile (which breaks down fats), stores glucose, detoxifies poisons, and processes amino acids. It's more of a chemical factory and warehouse than a digestive organ.
