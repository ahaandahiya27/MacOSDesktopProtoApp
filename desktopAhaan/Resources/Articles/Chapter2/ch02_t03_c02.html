Assimilation — growth, repair, and building tissue

The amino acids and glucose absorbed by the body are used to build proteins, grow muscles, repair wounds, and replace old cells.

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Not all absorbed nutrients are burned for energy. A big fraction is used to build new tissue. Amino acids are assembled into proteins: collagen (skin), haemoglobin (blood), antibodies (immunity), muscle.

Without assimilation, the body would always be shrinking. This concept explains how your digestive system accomplishes this, step by step.

What this concept means

Protein synthesis on ribosomes follows DNA instructions. Different genes encode different proteins for different functions. Growth requires net positive protein synthesis.

Why it works this way

Without assimilation, the body would always be shrinking.

💡 Did you know?

Mitochondrial DNA is inherited only from the mother.

Real-world applications

  1. Kids grow during sleep — Growth hormone peaks during deep sleep — amino acids are assembled into new bone and muscle while you rest.
  2. Wound healing protein — A surgical wound needs ~30 % more dietary protein for collagen synthesis to rebuild damaged tissue.
  3. Sickle cell mutation — A single amino-acid swap in haemoglobin makes red cells deform — assimilation goes wrong because the recipe is broken.
  4. Building muscle — Resistance training breaks muscle fibres slightly; protein-rich meals fuel the rebuild — fibres grow back thicker.
  5. Hair and nail growth — Both are mostly keratin — a protein assimilated from amino acids; deficiency shows as brittle hair and ridged nails.
  6. Pregnancy assimilation — A growing foetus draws ~80 g protein/day from the mother; her body recycles tissue protein if intake is low.
  7. Bone remodelling — Osteoblasts assimilate calcium and protein into new bone every day; bones replace ~10 % of their mass per year.
  8. Chemotherapy and protein — Cancer treatment damages fast-dividing cells; high-protein diets help patients rebuild during recovery weeks.
  9. Puberty growth spurt — Adolescents assimilate enormous amounts of protein and minerals — appetite spikes for a reason.
  10. Anorexia tissue loss — Severe undernutrition forces the body to break down its own muscle and organ tissue for amino acids — reverse assimilation.
  11. Protein deficiency (kwashiorkor) — Lack of amino acids for assimilation causes muscle wasting, swelling, hair loss, and stunted growth in malnourished children.
  12. Calcium absorption issues — Assimilation of calcium requires vitamin D; without it, bones become weak (rickets in children, osteoporosis in adults).
  13. Iron deficiency anemia — Despite eating iron, poor absorption or blood loss means insufficient iron for assimilation into hemoglobin, causing fatigue and weakness.

🌍 In your body right now

The science of assimilation — growth, repair, and building tissue is working in your body every moment. Pay attention to the physical sensations: your digestive system is one of the few body systems you can feel actively working.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: What would happen if your stomach didn't produce hydrochloric acid?

Answer: Food wouldn't be broken down chemically. Only the mechanical churning would happen, leaving larger particles that are harder for the small intestine to absorb. Proteins wouldn't be denatured by pepsin, and you'd lose a major source of nutrition.

🧠 Try another

Question: Why is the small intestine much longer than the large intestine?

Answer: The small intestine is where most digestion finishes and absorption happens — it needs maximum surface area (about 6 meters long). The large intestine is only for water reabsorption and waste storage, so it doesn't need to be long.

🧠 One more

Question: Does the liver digest food, or does it do something else?

Answer: The liver doesn't digest food directly. Instead, it produces bile (which breaks down fats), stores glucose, detoxifies poisons, and processes amino acids. It's more of a chemical factory and warehouse than a digestive organ.
