Two Mini Projects for Chapter 2

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🍞 Saliva amylase — taste it work

~5 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A small bite of plain roti or white bread (no jam, no butter)
  • A working tongue, a quiet 3 minutes
  • Water to rinse afterwards

Steps

  1. Take one small bite of plain roti — keep it on your tongue.
  2. Don't swallow yet. Just chew slowly.
  3. At the 30-second mark, notice the taste.
  4. At 1 minute, notice again.
  5. At 2 minutes, the bread tastes noticeably sweet.

👀 What you should see

By 2 minutes the plain starchy bread tastes sweet.

Why it works: Saliva contains an enzyme called amylase. Amylase breaks the long starch chains in wheat into a sweet sugar called maltose. You're watching digestion happen on your own tongue. The starch was tasteless; the maltose is sweet — and the change is literally enzyme-driven chemistry, no cooking required.

🍍 Pineapple enzyme — see protein digestion in a glass

~60 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A small bowl of cooled, just-set unflavoured jelly / gelatin (or sweet plain dahi)
  • A piece of fresh raw pineapple
  • A piece of canned (cooked) pineapple
  • Two saucers and an hour

Steps

  1. Spoon a small heap of jelly onto each saucer.
  2. Place a slice of raw pineapple on top of one saucer's jelly.
  3. Place a slice of canned pineapple on top of the other.
  4. Wait 30–60 minutes.
  5. Look at both: under the RAW pineapple the jelly has melted into a puddle. Under the canned pineapple it's still firm.

👀 What you should see

Raw pineapple breaks down the jelly. Canned pineapple doesn't.

Why it works: Raw pineapple contains a protein-digesting enzyme called bromelain. It breaks the protein gelatin into liquid — exactly the same way pepsin in your stomach breaks meat protein. Canned pineapple has been cooked, which destroys (denatures) the enzyme. This is also why fresh pineapple makes your tongue tingle — bromelain is mildly digesting the cells of your tongue.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
