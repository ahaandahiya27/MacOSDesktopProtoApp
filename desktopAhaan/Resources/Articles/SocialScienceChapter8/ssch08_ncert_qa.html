Q1. Why do you think natural elements like rivers, mountains and forests are considered sacred for the people? How do they contribute to our lives? (NCERT page 184)

Rivers, mountains and forests are considered sacred because people sense a divine presence in Nature and depend on these elements for their very lives. Rivers like the Ganga and Kaveri are worshipped as devīs (goddesses) because they give water, food and a means of travel and are the lifelines of civilisation; mountains are seen as gateways from earth to heaven; forests are revered as the abodes of deities. They contribute to our lives by providing water, fertile land, food, medicine, shelter and biodiversity — for example, sacred groves shelter rich plant and animal life and conserve water. Treating them as sacred also protects them, so the reverence and the benefit reinforce each other.

Q2. Why do people visit a tīrtha or other sacred sites? (NCERT page 184)

People visit a tīrtha for both an outer and an inner reason. Outwardly, a tīrtha is a sacred 'crossing place', often beside a river or on a hill, associated with a deity, a great spiritual figure or an important event, and visiting it is an act of devotion. Inwardly, a pilgrimage is a spiritual journey requiring a code of conduct, by which the pilgrim hopes to cross from ordinary worldly life toward a higher, spiritual life. Some people also travelled to sacred sites to trade, to learn from eminent teachers, or to debate and spread their beliefs — but the central purpose is religious and spiritual growth.

Q3. How did the ancient pilgrimage routes help in fostering trade during those times? Do you think the sacred sites help in developing the economy of the region? (NCERT page 184)

Pilgrims constantly met traders and merchants on their journeys, and the two helped each other: pilgrims needed food, supplies and goods, which traders provided, so pilgrimage routes and trade routes often overlapped (some traders even doubled as pilgrims). Great routes like the Uttarapatha and Dakṣhinapātha carried both devotees and goods such as pearls, gold, cotton, spices and sandalwood. Yes, sacred sites help develop a region's economy: the steady flow of pilgrims creates demand for food, lodging, transport and offerings, supporting traders, artisans and local businesses, so towns around major tīrthas often prosper.

Q4. What is the two-fold significance of a tīrthayātrā or a pilgrimage? (NCERT page 184)

A pilgrimage (tīrthayātrā) has a two-fold significance. First, it is an outer or physical journey across real geography to a sacred site, which in India also meant covering the country and meeting its diverse languages, customs and foods — strengthening cultural integration. Second, it is an inner or spiritual journey, requiring a specified code of conduct, by which the pilgrim seeks to cross from ordinary worldly life to a higher, spiritual life. So a tīrthayātrā transforms both the land the pilgrim travels and the person the pilgrim becomes.
