Air Pressure and How We Measure It

What makes wind blow from one place to another · Class 7 Science

Air has weight — and that weight pushes

Air feels weightless, but a 1 m³ box of air at sea level weighs about 1.2 kg. Above your head right now is a column of air ~100 km tall, weighing about 10 tonnes per square metre — pressing in from all sides equally. That's atmospheric pressure.

At sea level, atmospheric pressure is approximately 1.013 × 10⁵ pascals (101,300 Pa), or 1 atm, or about 1,013 millibars (mb). You feel none of it because your body pushes back with equal force.

Why pressure varies

Two things change air pressure:

  • Altitude — higher up means less air above, so less pressure. On Mount Everest's summit (8,848 m), pressure is only ~30% of sea level. That's why mountaineers need oxygen tanks.
  • Temperature — warm air expands and becomes less dense, lowering pressure at the ground. Cold air contracts and increases pressure.

How meteorologists measure pressure

The instrument is the barometer. Two main types:

  • Mercury barometer (Torricelli, 1643). A glass tube of mercury sealed at one end and inverted into a dish. Atmospheric pressure pushes mercury up the tube to about 760 mm at sea level. Higher pressure → higher column.
  • Aneroid barometer — a sealed metal can with a partial vacuum. As outside pressure rises, the can flexes inward; a needle attached to it shows pressure on a dial. This is what's inside altimeters and most home barometers.

High pressure vs low pressure

Weather maps show pressure as contour lines called isobars. Closely-spaced isobars = sharp pressure gradient = strong wind. Wind always blows from HIGH to LOW pressure. The bigger the difference, the faster the wind.

  • A typical strong cyclone has a central pressure of 950 mb (vs the surrounding 1,013 mb) — a 60-mb difference packed into a few hundred kilometres. That's why cyclone winds reach hundreds of km/h.
  • A gentle breeze corresponds to a difference of only 1-3 mb across the same distance.

Try this at home

Half-fill a glass with water and slide a flat playing card over the top. Press the card flat. Now flip the glass upside down (over a sink, just in case) and let go of the card. The card stays! Atmospheric pressure (10 tonnes per m²) is pushing UP on the card more strongly than the small column of water inside is pushing down.
