Inside a Cyclone — Eye, Wall, and Wind Bands

Why the centre is calm but the wall is deadly · Class 7 Science

Anatomy of a tropical cyclone

A mature cyclone is a giant rotating system 500–1500 km wide. From the centre outward:

  • Eye — a 30–60 km wide circular zone where wind is light, sky often clear, pressure lowest.
  • Eye wall — a ring of towering thunderstorm clouds with the strongest winds (200+ km/h) and heaviest rain.
  • Spiral rainbands — curved arms of thunderstorms feeding moisture into the eye wall.

Why the eye is calm

Inside the eye, air sinks instead of rising. Sinking air warms and dries, dissolving clouds. People caught in a cyclone's eye often think the storm has passed — then the back wall hits with full force. Always wait for an official 'all clear' before leaving shelter.

How cyclones are tracked

India's Cyclone Warning Centres in Chennai, Bhubaneswar, Mumbai and others use Doppler radar, INSAT-3D satellites, and ocean buoys. Cyclone tracks are now predicted ~5 days in advance with ~100 km accuracy — enough to evacuate millions before landfall (as in Cyclone Phailin 2013).

The Coriolis force (caused by Earth's rotation) is what makes cyclones spin. North of the Equator they rotate anticlockwise; south of the Equator clockwise. There are no cyclones at the Equator itself because the Coriolis force is zero there.

Try this at home

On a windy day, hold a wet finger up — the cold side faces the wind. Now imagine that same wind multiplied by 50 and circling a 50-km eye. That is a Category-3 cyclone.

Looking ahead
