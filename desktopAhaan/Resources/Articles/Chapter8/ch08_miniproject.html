Two Mini Projects for Chapter 8

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🥤 Can crusher — see air pressure in action

~15 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • An empty aluminium soda can
  • A small amount of water (1 tablespoon)
  • A pair of tongs
  • A bowl of cold water
  • A stove or burner (adult help essential)

Steps

  1. Pour 1 tablespoon water into the empty can.
  2. Place the can on the stove with tongs. Heat until water boils and steam rushes out of the top for 30 seconds.
  3. Quickly invert the can into the bowl of cold water — opening down.
  4. Watch what happens.

👀 What you should see

The can collapses inward with a sharp crack. Outside air pressure has crushed it instantly.

Why it works: Boiling water filled the can with steam, pushing air out. When you inverted the can in cold water, the steam condensed back to liquid (which has much less volume). This created near-vacuum inside the can. Atmospheric pressure outside (~10,000 kg/m²) had nothing pushing back — so it crushed the can. The same principle works at planetary scale: any region with sudden pressure drop draws everything around it. Cyclones are exactly this effect.

🎈 Convection chimney — bottle storm in a jar

~20 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A glass jar (mouth open)
  • Hot water
  • Cold blue food coloured ice cube
  • A pipette or small dropper

Steps

  1. Fill the jar 3/4 with hot tap water. Wait 30 seconds for it to settle.
  2. Gently lower a blue food-coloured ice cube using tongs.
  3. Watch what happens immediately — cold blue water sinks and spreads at the bottom, hot clear water above. Within 30 seconds, the blue rises in plumes.
  4. Time the convection cycle — how long until the whole jar is uniformly mixed?

👀 What you should see

Cold blue water sinks first (denser), then plumes rise back up as warm water carries the dye upward. The pattern visualises convection cells.

Why it works: Hot water is less dense than cold water. As the ice melts, cold blue water sinks; that cold water displaces warm clear water upward. The warm water rises, cools, and sinks again — a convection cell. This is exactly how a thunderstorm cloud forms: hot ground air rises, cool upper air sinks, and a vertical circulation builds up. Replace 'cold water' with 'cold polar air' and 'hot water' with 'tropical air' and you have monsoon dynamics.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
