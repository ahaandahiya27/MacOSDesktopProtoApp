Why Sea Breeze Blows During the Day

Land heats faster than water · Class 7 Science

The single fact that explains coastal weather

Land warms up faster than water. On a sunny morning, by 10 am the beach sand is too hot to walk on while the sea water is still cool. By 2 pm, the temperature difference between land and water can be 10–15 °C. That single fact creates the breeze that has shaped fishing, sailing, and coastal life for thousands of years.

What happens during the day

  1. Sun heats the land much faster than the sea.
  2. Air over the hot land warms up, expands, and rises.
  3. That rising column leaves a low-pressure pocket at ground level over the land.
  4. Cooler, higher-pressure air over the sea rushes in to fill the gap — flowing FROM sea TO land.
  5. That horizontal flow is the sea breeze. It typically peaks between 1 pm and 4 pm.

What happens at night

After sunset, land cools faster than the sea. Within a few hours the situation reverses: the sea is now warmer than the land. Now air over the sea rises, and the breeze flows FROM land TO sea — the land breeze. Smaller in magnitude than the daytime sea breeze, but still real.

The water's slow heating and cooling come from its high specific heat capacity — water needs about 5× more energy than sand to heat the same amount. Class 11 Physics formalises this as the heat capacity per unit mass: c = Q / (m·ΔT).

Try this at home

On a hot afternoon, place one hand on a window facing the sun and another on the floor under it. The glass is warmer than the floor. Sit in the same spot at 9 pm — the floor is now warmer. Same uneven-heating principle, just at room scale.

Looking ahead

Class 9 Geography extends sea/land breezes into the Indian monsoon — a continent-scale land–sea breeze that operates over 6 months instead of 12 hours. The Indian subcontinent heats up dramatically in May–June, drawing in moist sea-air from the Indian Ocean — the SW monsoon. In October–December, the cooling landmass reverses the flow — the NE monsoon. Class 11 Geography goes into the monsoon's economic and agricultural importance.
