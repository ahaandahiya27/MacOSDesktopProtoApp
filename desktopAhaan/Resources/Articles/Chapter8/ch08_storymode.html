Story Mode — Winds, Storms and Cyclones in Five Scenes

5 short scenes from real life that bring this chapter to a kitchen table, a garden, a classroom — somewhere you can picture.

≈ 10 min read · narrative-tier

Textbook examples can feel abstract. These five scenes don't happen in a textbook — they happen in places you go. Read them in order; each scene tightens the chapter's big idea by another notch.

Scene 1 — Cyclone Amphan crashes into Bengal coast, May 2020

Amphan formed in the central Bay of Bengal, intensified within 36 hours into a super cyclone with winds of 240 km/h, and slammed the Sundarbans on 20 May 2020. Trees uprooted, electricity gone for weeks, mangroves destroyed across 1500 km². Death toll: ~100 (would have been thousands without IMD's 7-day advance warning). Damage: ₹1.02 lakh crore. Cyclones are the Bay of Bengal's defining feature — warm water + low wind shear = perfect formation zone. Every year, India watches this corner of the ocean.

Scene 2 — Tauktae batters Mumbai and Gujarat, May 2021

Most Indian cyclones hit the east coast. Tauktae was the rare one that formed in the Arabian Sea and tracked north along the west coast — Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Gujarat. Mumbai recorded 230 km/h gusts. The Tauktae oil-rig disaster cost 86 lives. The Arabian Sea has been warming faster than the global average; cyclones there are getting more frequent. Tauktae was a warning that the west coast can't relax.

Scene 3 — Why the monsoon arrives every year (almost)

Summer (March-May) heats the Indian subcontinent more than the Indian Ocean. Hot air rises over land; low pressure forms over north India. Cool moist air from the Indian Ocean rushes in to fill the gap, carrying water vapour. As it rises over land, it cools, condenses, and rains. The monsoon is just a giant sea breeze on a continental scale. The same physics as Mumbai's afternoon breeze, but across a whole subcontinent and four months.

Scene 4 — Why a balloon pops when you sit on it

Inside the balloon, air is at higher pressure than outside. The rubber wall holds it in. When you push on it, you compress the inside air further; pressure climbs until the rubber can't take it and bursts. Same with cyclones at planetary scale — air pressure differences drive wind from high-pressure regions to low-pressure regions. The bigger the difference, the faster the wind. A cyclone's eye is the lowest-pressure spot on Earth at that moment.

Scene 5 — A thunderstorm over Delhi in pre-monsoon May

On a hot May afternoon in Delhi, air at ground level is 45°C. It rises rapidly as a tower of warm air. Higher up, it meets cold air, condenses, and forms cumulus clouds that stack 10 km high. Water droplets and ice crystals in the cloud rub against each other, building up electric charge. Eventually the charge difference between cloud and ground gets so big that lightning bridges it — a billion-volt arc. The thunder is the air superheated by the lightning expanding faster than the speed of sound.

💬 What this is for

Concrete examples are how your brain anchors abstract ideas. Pick the scene you remember best after reading. The next time you see it in real life, you'll remember the chapter.
