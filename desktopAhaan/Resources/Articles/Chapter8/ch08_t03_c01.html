Inside a Cyclone

Why the eye is calm and the wall is deadly · Class 7 Science

A cyclone is a giant low-pressure storm

Imagine a column of warm, moist air rising fast off a hot ocean. As it rises, it cools and dumps its moisture as rain. The rising column leaves a low-pressure region at sea level. Air from all directions rushes in to fill the gap — but Earth's rotation forces the inflow to spiral, not flow straight. The result: a giant rotating storm, hundreds of kilometres across, called a cyclone (hurricane in the Atlantic, typhoon in the Pacific — same physics, different names).

The eye and the wall

Three concentric zones, from inside out:

  • Eye — the calm centre, 20–60 km across. Air actually sinks here, so the sky is often clear. Wind speed is near zero. Step outside during the eye's passage and you'd think the storm was over — until the back wall arrives.
  • Eye wall — the ring of towering thunderstorms around the eye. This is where the most violent winds and heaviest rain live. Wind speeds in a category-5 cyclone can exceed 250 km/h.
  • Rain bands — spiral arms of clouds and rain extending outward for hundreds of km from the eye wall.
The eye is calm because the spiralling winds can't easily turn the corner inward — they orbit the centre instead. Above the eye, air sinks and warms by compression, which is why eye-photos often show blue sky in the middle of the storm.

How cyclones form (briefly)

Need three things: warm sea-surface temperature (over 26 °C), moist atmosphere, and rotation (Earth's spin via the Coriolis force). That's why cyclones form near the equator but not exactly at it (no Coriolis at zero latitude), and only in summer (when oceans are warmest).

Try this at home

Stir a tall glass of water vigorously with a spoon in one direction. Pull the spoon out. A miniature 'eye' forms in the middle — a low-pressure column. The same physics as a real cyclone, just made of water.

Looking ahead

Class 11 Physics covers the Coriolis force — the apparent force that makes anything moving across a rotating planet curve sideways. In the Northern Hemisphere, cyclones spin counter-clockwise; in the Southern Hemisphere, clockwise. JEE Physics asks Coriolis problems on rotating-frame mechanics. Class 9 Geography covers cyclone tracking, evacuation protocols, and the IMD warning system — directly applicable as India sees roughly 5–6 cyclones a year from the Bay of Bengal.
