Three "What Ifs?" About Winds, Storms and Cyclones

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if the Earth stopped rotating?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Wind patterns would collapse. The Coriolis effect (which makes cyclones spin) would vanish, so cyclones would lose their rotation and become weaker. The big jet streams that move weather across continents would slow down. Day-night temperature differences would become extreme — half the planet roasting, half freezing. Eventually a new wind system would emerge: hot equator-to-pole circulation, but with no daily cycle. Most life as we know it depends on Earth's rotation; weather is just one casualty of stopping it.

What if 2 — What if a cyclone with the strength of Amphan hit Mumbai?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Mumbai is geographically less cyclone-prone than the east coast — the Arabian Sea is cooler and shears more wind. But warming oceans are changing this (Tauktae 2021 was a preview). If a 240 km/h cyclone hit Mumbai: skyscrapers would have ~half the wind stress they're rated for; old chawls and slums would be levelled; storm surge would flood coastal Mumbai (Marine Drive, BKC, Bandra reclamation) by 3-5 metres; the airport would be unusable for weeks. Mumbai's cyclone resilience is far below east-coast cities like Visakhapatnam.

What if 3 — What if monsoon failed two years in a row?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: This has happened — the back-to-back failures of 1965-66 and 1972-73 caused mass food shortages in India. Today, with greater rice and wheat stockpiles (Food Corporation of India holds ~50 million tonnes buffer), a single failed monsoon doesn't cause famine. But two failures: groundwater depletion accelerates, summer crops collapse, prices rise sharply, rural distress spreads, electricity (hydroelectric) declines. The Indus and Ganges rivers run very low. The 1965-66 failure pushed India to launch the Green Revolution. A repeat today would similarly force big policy shifts.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
