Beyond the Book — A Practice Older Than the Asana

The Sun Salutation is famous as a yoga practice. The chapter taught its mantras. Here is what surrounds them — twelve names, ancient temples, and a 20th-century shaping you may not know.

≈ 6 min read · curious-kid level

Every line you learn in Sanskrit class often turns out to be one piece of a much bigger story. The Sun Salutation is a perfect example.

1. Twelve names — and not by accident

The chapter named ten of the Sun's traditional names; the full set is twelve. The number is not casual: twelve names match the twelve months of the solar year and the twelve आदित्याः (sons of Aditi) named in the Vedas.

When the Sun Salutation is performed with mantra, each of its twelve postures is paired with one of these twelve names — मित्राय नमः at sunrise, then रवये नमः, सूर्याय नमः, भानवे नमः, खगाय नमः, पूष्णे नमः, हिरण्यगर्भाय नमः, मरीचये नमः, आदित्याय नमः, सवित्रे नमः, अर्काय नमः, भास्कराय नमः.

2. The Sun has its own temple

At Konark in Odisha stands the great सूर्य-मन्दिर — built in the 13th century in the shape of Surya's twelve-wheeled chariot, drawn by seven horses (the seven days of the week, the seven colours of light). Each wheel is also a sundial, accurate enough to read by the shadow even today.

Konark Sun Temple is a UNESCO World Heritage site — one of India's most spectacular pieces of architecture, dedicated to exactly the deity whose names you are reciting in class.

3. The Gāyatrī's hidden subject

The most famous Sanskrit prayer of all — the Gāyatrī Mantra from the Rig Veda — is addressed to सवितृ, the same Savitṛ named in the Sun's twelve. ‘तत् सवितुर्वरेण्यं भर्गो देवस्य धीमहि…’ literally means ‘we meditate on the radiant glory of that divine Savitṛ’.

The Sun in the Sanskrit tradition is not merely a star — it is the visible image of consciousness itself, named, addressed, saluted, and revered.

4. The modern shape of the practice

The full सूर्यनमस्कार sequence as a 12-posture flow was systematised in the early 20th century — Bhavanrao Pant Pratinidhi, raja of Aundh, published a popular book on the practice in 1928, and it spread from there into modern yoga.

So the practice you learn today is a beautiful weave: ancient mantras + ancient deity-names + a relatively recent 20th-century arrangement of postures. Old and new together.
