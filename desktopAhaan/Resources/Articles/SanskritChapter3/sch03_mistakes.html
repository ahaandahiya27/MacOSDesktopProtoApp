Common Mistakes

Five errors students often make in this chapter — and the gentle corrections that fix them.

≈ 5 min · honest-with-yourself level

Sanskrit makes precise distinctions that English glosses over — case endings, gender, tense, prefix vs augment. The mistakes below are not personal failings — they are signals that a specific detail of the language needs another look.

Mistake 1 — Saying 'मित्रः नमः' instead of 'मित्राय नमः'

What students often write/say: Students use nominative मित्रः (the subject form).

The correct form: नमः takes its object in the DATIVE: मित्राय (the चतुर्थी विभक्ति).

💡 Why

नमः is one of a small set of Sanskrit words (also स्वाहा, स्वस्ति) that idiomatically govern the dative. The chapter teaches this explicitly — the dative ending -आय makes the difference.

Mistake 2 — Counting eleven Sun-names instead of twelve

What students often write/say: Students sometimes miss one when reciting.

The correct form: Twelve: मित्र, रवि, सूर्य, भानु, खग, पूष, हिरण्यगर्भ, मरीचि, आदित्य, सवितृ, अर्क, भास्कर.

💡 Why

The twelve mirror the twelve postures and the twelve months. The full sequence is canonical — losing one breaks the symmetry.

Mistake 3 — Saying 'सूर्यः नमस्कारः' instead of 'सूर्यनमस्कारः'

What students often write/say: Treating it as two words with case agreement.

The correct form: It's a COMPOUND: सूर्य-नमस्कार (sun-salutation) → सूर्यनमस्कारः.

💡 Why

Sanskrit compounds (समास) glue words together. The first member loses its case ending; the second takes the case of the whole compound. Two separate words would be 'सूर्याय नमस्कारः' = 'salutation to the Sun' — grammatically different from the compound name of the practice.

Mistake 4 — Translating 'आचार्या' (feminine) as 'a teacher' without gender

What students often write/say: आचार्य looks like a generic word for teacher.

The correct form: आचार्या (with -आ) = female teacher; आचार्यः (with -ः) = male teacher.

💡 Why

Sanskrit, like Hindi, marks teacher's gender at the noun level. The chapter's dialogue specifically uses आचार्या (feminine) — students refer to a female teacher. Ignore this and the dialogue flips gender.

Mistake 5 — Confusing 'स्वस्थ' with 'स्व-स्थ'

What students often write/say: Looks like just an adjective 'healthy'.

The correct form: It IS 'healthy', but the etymology reveals it as स्व ('self') + स्थ ('standing/abiding') = 'self-abiding'.

💡 Why

Sanskrit's etymology is alive: स्वस्थ literally means 'centred in oneself'. Knowing the parts gives you the LESSON: health is being at ease within yourself. Hindi स्वस्थ carries the same root.

📝 Self-check

Re-read the chapter once with these five mistakes in mind. Notice the places where the textbook has DELIBERATELY chosen one form over another — that's often where the trap lies.
