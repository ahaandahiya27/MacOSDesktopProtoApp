Federalism: governments within a government [Class 8]

The chapter notes that Indian states such as Rajasthan or Kerala have their own governments, yet are not completely independent — they are governed within the framework of the Union Government of India. This arrangement is called federalism: power is shared between a central (Union) government and state governments, each with its own areas of responsibility, all bound by one Constitution. Federalism lets a huge, diverse country be governed both at the national level (defence, foreign affairs, currency) and at the local level (police, agriculture, local schools), so decisions can be made closer to the people while the nation stays united. You will study how this division of powers works, and the role of the Constitution in settling disputes between the Union and the states.

How a constitution limits power [Class 9]

A constitution is the book of fundamental rules of a country, and in most democracies it is what decides how the government will work. It does far more than organise the government: it limits power. By guaranteeing fundamental rights, fixing the terms of elected bodies, separating the legislature, executive and judiciary, and making everyone — including the most powerful officials — subject to the law, a constitution turns the abstract principles of democracy into enforceable rules. This is the idea of 'constitutionalism': the belief that government power should always be limited by law. It is precisely what a dictatorship lacks, since a dictator faces no limits from any constitution or law. Studying the Indian Constitution shows how its Preamble, Fundamental Rights and structure of government put these limits into practice.
