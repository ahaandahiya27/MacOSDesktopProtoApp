Challenge 1 [Level 5 — hardest]

A fast-growing town decides it needs a speed limit on its roads. Show how ALL THREE functions of government would each play a part — from creating the rule, to carrying it out, to dealing with people who break it. Name the function (legislative, executive, judicial) at each step.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

Step 1 — LEGISLATIVE: the law-making body discusses and passes the rule, e.g. 'the speed limit in town is 40 km/h.' Making rules is the legislative function. Step 2 — EXECUTIVE: the part of government that carries out rules puts up the signs, runs the speed cameras and sends the police to patrol and catch speeders. Carrying out and enforcing rules is the executive function. Step 3 — JUDICIAL: when someone is accused of breaking the limit, a court decides whether they really did and what the penalty should be, making sure the rule is applied fairly. Ensuring rules are followed and judging disputes is the judicial function. So one simple speed limit needs all three functions — make it, enforce it, and judge it — which is exactly why every government has these three jobs.

How to crack it

  1. Identify the three functions: legislative (make rules), executive (carry out), judicial (ensure followed).
  2. Step 1: the legislature passes the speed-limit law.
  3. Step 2: the executive (police, signs, cameras) enforces it.
  4. Step 3: the judiciary tries offenders and decides penalties fairly.
  5. Conclude all three are needed for a single rule to work.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Mixing up the executive (enforces) with the judiciary (judges).
  • Listing the functions without tying each to a step in the speed-limit example.

🚀 Now try this twist

Which function of government actually PUNISHES someone who breaks a law, and which one MADE the law?

Open the answer

The judiciary (courts) decides guilt and punishment; the legislature made the law in the first place.

  1. Recall judicial = ensures rules are followed / judges.
  2. Recall legislative = makes the rules.

Challenge 2 [Level 5 — hardest]

In a class of 10 students, everyone can vote on every single decision. But a school of 2,000 students instead elects a small student committee to take decisions. Use this contrast to explain the difference between DIRECT democracy and REPRESENTATIVE democracy, and why large countries almost always use the representative form.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

The class of 10 is like a DIRECT democracy: the group is small enough that every member can take part in every decision themselves. The school of 2,000 is like a REPRESENTATIVE democracy: there are far too many people for everyone to debate and vote on everything, so they ELECT a few representatives (the committee) to decide on their behalf. Large countries — with crores of citizens — work the same way: it would be impossible for everyone to gather and vote on every law, so people elect leaders to make decisions for them, and replace them at the next election if they are unhappy. So direct democracy suits small groups, while representative democracy is what makes 'rule of the people' practical at the scale of a whole country.

How to crack it

  1. Map the small class to DIRECT democracy: everyone decides everything.
  2. Map the large school to REPRESENTATIVE democracy: elect a few to decide.
  3. Explain why scale forces the representative form (too many people to vote on everything).
  4. Conclude: direct for small groups, representative for large countries.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Swapping the two — direct democracy is the all-vote one; representative uses elected leaders.
  • Not explaining WHY size makes direct democracy impractical for a country.

🚀 Now try this twist

Give one real situation where DIRECT democracy could actually work well.

Open the answer

Any small group deciding together by everyone voting — for example a village gram sabha meeting, a small club, or a class of students voting on a class trip.

  1. Recall direct democracy needs a small enough group.
  2. Give a small-group example where all can vote.

Challenge 3 [Level 4 — stretch]

Explain the main difference between a parliamentary democracy and a presidential democracy, giving one example of each.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

In a parliamentary democracy, the members of the executive are also part of the legislature, and the executive stays in power only while it has the confidence of the legislature. For example, in India the prime minister and the council of ministers are members of Parliament and continue working only while they have the confidence of the Lok Sabha. In a presidential democracy, the executive works independently of the legislature: the president is elected directly by the people and does not need the confidence of the legislature to keep his or her position. The USA is an example of a presidential democracy.

How to crack it

  1. Identify the key test: the relationship between executive and legislature.
  2. Describe the parliamentary form: executive is part of the legislature and needs its confidence; give India as the example.
  3. Describe the presidential form: executive is elected separately and independent of the legislature; give the USA as the example.
  4. Conclude by contrasting the two.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Saying India is a presidential democracy because it has a President — India is parliamentary; real executive power lies with the prime minister.
  • Forgetting to mention that in a parliamentary system the executive needs the legislature's confidence.

🚀 Now try this twist

In India, the council of ministers can continue working only while it has the confidence of which house?

Open the answer

The Lok Sabha (the Lower House).

  1. Recall that India is a parliamentary democracy.
  2. The council of ministers needs the confidence of the Lok Sabha.

Challenge 4 [Level 5 — hardest]

For EACH description below, name the form of government and give the KEY reason for your choice: (a) a small group of wealthy, influential families makes all the important decisions; (b) the country is ruled by religious leaders according to the principles of one religion; (c) one person seizes total power with no legal limits on what they can do.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

(a) This is an OLIGARCHY — the key feature is that a small, powerful group (often wealthy families) holds the decision-making power, rather than the people or a single ruler. (b) This is a THEOCRACY — the key feature is that religion and religious leaders rule, and the law is based on religious principles. (c) This is a DICTATORSHIP — the key feature is that one person (or a tiny group) holds absolute power with no legal limits and is not accountable to the people. The trick to telling them apart is to ask 'WHO holds the power and what limits it?': a few rich families (oligarchy), religious authority (theocracy), or one unchecked person (dictatorship).

How to crack it

  1. (a) Small wealthy group decides → OLIGARCHY.
  2. (b) Religious leaders rule by religious law → THEOCRACY.
  3. (c) One person, absolute power, no limits → DICTATORSHIP.
  4. State the deciding question for each: who holds power and what limits it?

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Confusing oligarchy (a small GROUP) with dictatorship (usually ONE person).
  • Calling any harsh government a 'dictatorship' without checking the defining feature.

🚀 Now try this twist

What is the main difference between an oligarchy and a dictatorship?

Open the answer

An oligarchy is ruled by a small GROUP (often wealthy or influential families), while a dictatorship concentrates absolute power in ONE person (or a very tiny group) with no legal limits.

  1. Oligarchy = rule by a small group.
  2. Dictatorship = absolute power in one person.
  3. The difference is how many hold the power.

Challenge 5 [Level 4 — stretch]

Give three reasons why an independent judiciary is important in a democracy.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

First, an independent judiciary protects every citizen's fundamental rights, so that even powerful people or the government itself cannot take them away. Second, it ensures that laws are followed by everyone — by ordinary citizens and by all parts of the government alike — so the law applies equally to all. Third, because it is independent and free from the control of the rulers, it can act as a fair check on the legislature and the executive, striking down unjust laws or actions and helping maintain the separation of powers that keeps any one organ from becoming too powerful.

How to crack it

  1. Recall the role of an independent judiciary from the principles of democracy.
  2. Reason one: it protects citizens' fundamental rights, even against the government.
  3. Reason two: it ensures laws are followed equally by citizens and by the government.
  4. Reason three: being independent, it checks the other organs and upholds the separation of powers.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Giving only one reason instead of three.
  • Saying the judiciary makes the laws — it does not; the legislature makes laws, while the judiciary ensures they are followed.

🚀 Now try this twist

What does 'separation of powers' mean?

Open the answer

It means that the three organs of government — the legislature, the executive and the judiciary — work independently and do not interfere in each other's functions.

  1. Recall the definition of separation of powers.
  2. State that the three organs work independently.

Challenge 6 [Level 4 — stretch]

The ancient Vajji mahājanapada chose its leaders by merit and election, and the 10th-century Uttaramerur inscriptions in Tamil Nadu describe a careful village election system. Why is this historical evidence important, and what does it tell us about the idea that 'the ruled can choose their rulers'?

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

It is important because it shows that the idea of people choosing and replacing their leaders is NOT a recent or purely foreign invention — it has deep roots in India itself. The Vajji republic selected leaders by merit and vote more than 2,000 years ago, and the Uttaramerur inscriptions, carved in stone in the 10th century, set out detailed rules for electing village representatives (even with qualifications and ways to remove the unfit). This evidence tells us that 'the ruled choosing their rulers' is an ancient human idea that India practised long ago, so modern Indian democracy can be seen as reviving and building on its own old traditions, not simply copying others. It also reminds us that democracy must be actively organised with rules and elections — something people understood even centuries ago.

How to crack it

  1. Note the two pieces of evidence: the Vajji republic and the Uttaramerur inscriptions.
  2. Explain why they matter: they show democratic ideas are ancient and rooted in India.
  3. Draw the lesson: 'the ruled choosing rulers' is an old idea India practised, not a modern import.
  4. Add that they show democracy needs deliberate rules and elections, understood even then.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Treating democracy as a purely modern or Western invention, ignoring India's early examples.
  • Just naming the examples without explaining WHY the evidence matters.

🚀 Now try this twist

What do the Uttaramerur inscriptions describe?

Open the answer

A detailed village-level election system from 10th-century Tamil Nadu, including how representatives were chosen (and could be removed) — early evidence of organised local self-government in India.

  1. Recall the Uttaramerur inscriptions record a village election system.
  2. Note they show organised early local democracy in India.
