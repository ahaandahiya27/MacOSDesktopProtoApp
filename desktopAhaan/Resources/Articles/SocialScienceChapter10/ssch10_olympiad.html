Challenge 1 [Level 5 — hardest]

Imagine a brand-new country that has NO constitution at all. Describe THREE specific problems that would soon arise, and explain how having a constitution prevents each one.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

Problem 1: No one would agree on WHO has the power to make decisions, so different leaders or groups would fight over control. A constitution prevents this by setting out in advance how the government is formed and who has which powers. Problem 2: The strong could trample the weak — there would be no guaranteed rights, so powerful people could take away others' freedoms. A constitution prevents this by promising Fundamental Rights that even the government cannot take away. Problem 3: There would be no agreed, fair way to settle disputes, so quarrels could turn into chaos or violence. A constitution prevents this by laying down laws and an independent judiciary to judge disputes fairly. In short, a constitution works like a rulebook everyone accepts BEFORE problems arise, so the country runs by fair agreed rules instead of by force.

How to crack it

  1. Problem 1 — disputes over who holds power → constitution defines how government is formed and powers shared.
  2. Problem 2 — the strong oppress the weak → constitution guarantees Fundamental Rights.
  3. Problem 3 — no fair way to settle disputes → constitution provides laws and an independent judiciary.
  4. Conclude: a constitution is an agreed rulebook set BEFORE problems arise.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Listing problems without saying how a constitution solves each one.
  • Giving only vague 'there would be chaos' instead of specific problems.

🚀 Now try this twist

In one sentence, why do we need a constitution?

Open the answer

So that everyone agrees in advance on fair rules for running the country, sharing power and settling disputes, rather than leaving it to force or chance.

  1. Recall the purpose of a constitution.
  2. State it provides agreed fair rules in advance.

Challenge 2 [Level 5 — hardest]

A critic says: 'India's Constitution borrowed ideas from other countries — like liberty-equality-fraternity from France and the independent-judiciary idea from the USA — so it is unoriginal and not truly Indian.' Write a reasoned argument AGAINST this view.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

The critic is mistaken. First, the makers of the Constitution did not blindly copy — they studied many democracies and chose only the ideas that suited India, adapting them to Indian conditions. Choosing wisely from the world's best experience is a sign of good judgement, not weakness; a doctor who learns from many teachers is not 'unoriginal'. Second, the Constitution is rooted just as deeply in things that ARE distinctly Indian — the ideals of the freedom movement, and age-old Indian values like respect for differing views, the importance of duties, and 'the world is one family'. Third, the way all these influences were combined into one coherent document suited to India's huge diversity is itself original work. So the Constitution is best seen as a thoughtful blend: Indian at its core, and wisely enriched by the world's best ideas — which makes it stronger, not weaker.

How to crack it

  1. Reject the premise: borrowing wisely is good judgement, not copying.
  2. Point out the makers adapted foreign ideas to Indian conditions.
  3. Show the Constitution's distinctly Indian roots: freedom movement + civilisational values.
  4. Argue that blending many sources into one coherent document is itself original and a strength.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Agreeing that borrowing = unoriginal, instead of arguing that wise borrowing is a strength.
  • Forgetting the strong Indian sources (freedom movement, heritage) that also shaped it.

🚀 Now try this twist

Name one idea India adapted from another country, and one that came from India's own heritage.

Open the answer

From abroad: 'liberty, equality, fraternity' (France) or the independent judiciary (USA). From India's heritage: the emphasis on duties, or respect for differing views ('the world is one family').

  1. Recall a borrowed idea (France/USA).
  2. Recall an indigenous influence (duties / heritage values).

Challenge 3 [Level 4 — stretch]

What is the difference between a Fundamental Right and a Directive Principle of State Policy?

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

A Fundamental Right is a promise that must be kept: if someone treats you unfairly or denies your right, you can go to court and a judge can help you enforce it. Examples include the Right to Equality and the Right to Freedom. A Directive Principle of State Policy (DPSP) is a goal or guideline that the government should try to achieve, such as ensuring a good standard of living for everyone. But if the government does not follow it, you cannot go to court and complain — DPSP are guidelines rather than strict, enforceable rules. So Rights can be demanded immediately, while Directive Principles are bigger goals to be reached over time.

How to crack it

  1. Define a Fundamental Right as an enforceable promise.
  2. State that one can go to court if a right is denied.
  3. Define DPSP as goals/guidelines for the government.
  4. State that DPSP cannot be enforced in court.
  5. Conclude with the key difference: rights are immediate, DPSP are long-term goals.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Saying both can be enforced in court — only Fundamental Rights can.
  • Confusing Directive Principles with Fundamental Duties (which apply to citizens, not the government).

🚀 Now try this twist

Give one example each of a Fundamental Right and a Directive Principle.

Open the answer

A Fundamental Right: the Right to Equality (Article 14) or the Right to Education (Article 21-A). A Directive Principle: ensuring a good standard of living for all, or welfare of the people (Article 41).

  1. Recall the listed Fundamental Rights.
  2. Recall the example DPSP from the chapter.
  3. Give one of each.

Challenge 4 [Level 5 — hardest]

The Constitution is called a 'living document' because it can be changed by AMENDMENTS. Explain why this power to amend is both NECESSARY and potentially RISKY, and what this tells us about why amendments are not made too easily.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

Amendment is NECESSARY because society changes: new problems, technologies and ideas of fairness appear that the original writers in 1949 could not foresee. A constitution that could never change would slowly become out of date and unable to serve the people — so the power to amend lets it stay alive and relevant (for example, Fundamental Duties were added in 1976). But the power is also RISKY: the Constitution protects citizens' most important rights and the basic shape of the government, so if it could be changed too easily or carelessly, a powerful group might strip away rights or upset the balance of power for their own benefit. This is why amendments are deliberately NOT made too easily — they usually need large special majorities and careful debate, so that change happens only with wide agreement. A living document must be able to grow, but its roots must be protected from hasty damage.

How to crack it

  1. Necessary: society changes, so the Constitution must adapt to stay relevant (e.g. Fundamental Duties added 1976).
  2. Risky: it guards vital rights and the structure of government, which careless change could damage.
  3. Therefore amendments require special majorities and debate, not a simple easy vote.
  4. Conclude: a living document must grow but its roots must be protected.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Only saying amendments let it 'change with time' without noting the risk and the safeguards.
  • Thinking amendments are made as easily as ordinary laws — they need special majorities.

🚀 Now try this twist

Give one example of a change made to the Constitution by amendment.

Open the answer

The Fundamental Duties were added to the Constitution by amendment in 1976.

  1. Recall an example of an amendment.
  2. Cite the addition of Fundamental Duties in 1976.

Challenge 5 [Level 5 — hardest]

The Preamble promises every citizen four great goals: Justice, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity. Choose any TWO of these and, with an everyday example each, explain what that goal actually promises a citizen.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

Take EQUALITY and FRATERNITY as an example. EQUALITY promises that every citizen is treated the same by the law and the state, regardless of caste, religion, gender or wealth — for instance, every child has the same right to sit in a government school, and everyone must draw water from the same village well without anyone being kept out. FRATERNITY promises a spirit of brotherhood and unity among all Indians, so that despite our many languages, religions and regions we treat one another as members of one family — for instance, neighbours of different faiths celebrating each other's festivals together. (LIBERTY promises freedoms such as speaking, believing and moving freely within the law; JUSTICE promises fair treatment in social, economic and political life.) Together these four goals describe the kind of society the Constitution is trying to build for every citizen.

How to crack it

  1. Pick two of the four goals (Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity).
  2. For each, state what it promises a citizen in plain words.
  3. Give an everyday example for each chosen goal.
  4. Note that together the four describe the society the Constitution seeks.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Just listing the four words without explaining what any of them promises.
  • Confusing Equality (same treatment) with Liberty (freedoms).

🚀 Now try this twist

What is the difference between Liberty and Equality as Preamble goals?

Open the answer

Liberty is about FREEDOMS — being free to think, speak, believe and move within the law. Equality is about being treated the SAME as everyone else by the law and the state, with no discrimination.

  1. Liberty = freedoms within the law.
  2. Equality = equal, non-discriminatory treatment.
  3. State the contrast.
