Mini Project

From the fading of the Gupta Empire (c.600 CE) to about 1200 CE, India had no single empire but many strong regional kingdoms - a creative, not declining, age.

≈ 25 min · read-mode · 🌏 Social Science

Reading teaches; doing sticks. Try this hands-on activity at home — it turns the chapter's idea into something you can see, build, or trace for yourself.

🗺️ Map the dynasties of 600-1000 CE (about 40 min)

What you need

  • A blank outline map of India
  • Coloured pencils
  • The chapter's dynasty list

Steps

  1. Mark Kannauj at the centre of north India.
  2. Colour the three Tripartite powers in their home regions: Palas (east), Gurjara-Pratiharas (west), Rashtrakutas (Deccan).
  3. Mark the southern powers: Chalukyas (Badami), Pallavas (Kanchi), Cholas (Tanjavur), Pandyas (Madurai), Cheras (Kerala).
  4. Draw arrows from each Tripartite power toward Kannauj to show the struggle.

👀 What you should see

You will see why Kannauj, sitting between east, west and Deccan, became the prize of three powers, while the south developed its own set of dynasties.

🧠 Why it works

Placing dynasties on a map turns a list of names into a clear picture of who was where and why they clashed.

📜 Be a source detective (about 35 min)

What you need

  • A notebook
  • The quotations in the chapter

Steps

  1. Pick two sources from the chapter (e.g. the Aihole inscription praising Pulakeshin II, and Xuanzang's description of a city).
  2. For each, write down who made it and why (to praise a king? to record a journey?).
  3. Decide which parts might be exaggerated and which seem reliable.
  4. Write one sentence on how you would check each claim against another source.

👀 What you should see

You will notice that praise-inscriptions exaggerate while a traveller's account, though useful, also reflects the writer's own viewpoint.

🧠 Why it works

It practises source criticism - the core skill historians use to turn old texts into trustworthy history.
