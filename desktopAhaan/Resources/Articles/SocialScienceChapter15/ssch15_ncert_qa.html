NCERT Q&A

From the fading of the Gupta Empire (c.600 CE) to about 1200 CE, India had no single empire but many strong regional kingdoms - a creative, not declining, age.

Q1. What does the term 'Tripartite Struggle' refer to? (NCERT page 67)

It refers to the long, indecisive contest after Harsha's death (647 CE) among three powers - the Palas (east), the Gurjara-Pratiharas (west) and the Rashtrakutas (Deccan) - for control of the city of Kannauj on the Ganga, through the 8th and 9th centuries, with no lasting victor.

Q2. Why is the post-Gupta period not really a 'dark age'? (NCERT page 62)

Because, despite the absence of one large empire, the period saw strong regional kingdoms, magnificent temples (Ellora, Mamallapuram), great universities (Vikramashila, Nalanda), flourishing trade and towns, advances in mathematics (Brahmagupta) and the spread of the Bhakti tradition - all signs of creativity, not decline.

Q3. How do we know about this period? Name some sources. (NCERT page 65)

From inscriptions (e.g. Harsha's copper-plate, the Aihole inscription, the Chola Uttaramerur inscriptions, the Gwalior inscription), coins, court literature (Harshacharita, Kadambari), foreign travelogues (Xuanzang) and Arab accounts (Al-Masudi, Sulaiman, al-Istakhri, the Chachnama). Historians cross-check these because some, like the Aihole inscription, exaggerate.

Q4. What was special about education at Vikramashila? (NCERT page 69)

Founded by Dharmapala in the late 8th century, Vikramashila was a great monastery-university with six colleges, lecture halls and a vast library, and nearly 3,000 scholars studying grammar, logic and philosophy. Admission was selective - each college had a dvarapandita ('scholar gatekeeper') who tested students before letting them in. It was especially linked with Tibet.

Q5. Did foreign invasions change Indian society in this period? Compare the Hunas and the Arabs. (NCERT page 90)

The Hunas were defeated and then absorbed into Indian society, adopting Sanskrit, Indian coins, titles and deities. The Arabs, who raided from 637 CE and conquered Sindh in 712 CE under Muhammad bin Qasim, brought a new religion and did not assimilate the same way, but their political control stayed limited for centuries because Indian rulers such as the Gurjara-Pratiharas and Kashmir's Lalitaditya resisted strongly.
