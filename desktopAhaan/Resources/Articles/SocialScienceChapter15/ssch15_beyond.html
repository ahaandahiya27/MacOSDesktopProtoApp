Was this 'Indian feudalism'? A historians' debate [Class 10]

Historians have argued for decades about how to describe the early-medieval economy. One school called it 'Indian feudalism': they pointed to the spread of land grants, the rise of powerful landholding intermediaries between king and peasant, and (they claimed) a decline of towns, trade and coins - a picture resembling feudal Europe. A second school disagreed, showing that towns mostly survived and new ones appeared, that long-distance sea trade boomed along both coasts, and that powerful merchant guilds and temple economies kept money and commerce alive. Your textbook leans toward the second view, noting that 'the evidence paints a different and more complex picture' than simple urban decline. The debate is a great example of how history is not just a list of facts but an argument about what the evidence means - and how new evidence can overturn an old theory.

How a king becomes legitimate: titles, temples and gifts [Class 10]

Why did kings build giant temples, take grand titles, and give away wealth? In an age without modern elections or constitutions, rulers had to prove they deserved to rule - this is called legitimation. They did it in several ways: by taking divine or cosmic titles (Bhoja called himself 'Adi Varaha' after Vishnu's world-rescuing boar; Amoghavarsha was 'peak of kings'); by sponsoring spectacular temples (the Rashtrakutas' Kailasanatha, the Pallavas' Mamallapuram) that displayed both wealth and devotion; by patronising many religions at once to win the loyalty of all communities; and by grand public gift-giving, like Harsha's five-yearly ceremony at Prayaga where he gave away his treasure. Reading inscriptions, historians can decode these strategies - so a temple or a coin is not just art, but a political message carved in stone or metal.
