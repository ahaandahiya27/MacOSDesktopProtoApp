Was this 'Indian feudalism'? A historians' debate [Class 10]

Historians have argued for decades about how to describe the early-medieval economy. One school called it 'Indian feudalism': they pointed to the spread of land grants, the rise of powerful landholding intermediaries between king and peasant, and (they claimed) a decline of towns, trade and coins - a picture resembling feudal Europe. A second school disagreed, showing that towns mostly survived and new ones appeared, that long-distance sea trade boomed along both coasts, and that powerful merchant guilds and temple economies kept money and commerce alive. Your textbook leans toward the second view, noting that 'the evidence paints a different and more complex picture' than simple urban decline. The debate is a great example of how history is not just a list of facts but an argument about what the evidence means - and how new evidence can overturn an old theory.

How a king becomes legitimate: titles, temples and gifts [Class 10]

Why did kings build giant temples, take grand titles, and give away wealth? In an age without modern elections or constitutions, rulers had to prove they deserved to rule - this is called legitimation. They did it in several ways: by taking divine or cosmic titles (Bhoja called himself 'Adi Varaha' after Vishnu's world-rescuing boar; Amoghavarsha was 'peak of kings'); by sponsoring spectacular temples (the Rashtrakutas' Kailasanatha, the Pallavas' Mamallapuram) that displayed both wealth and devotion; by patronising many religions at once to win the loyalty of all communities; and by grand public gift-giving, like Harsha's five-yearly ceremony at Prayaga where he gave away his treasure. Reading inscriptions, historians can decode these strategies - so a temple or a coin is not just art, but a political message carved in stone or metal.

An election a thousand years ago, written on a wall [Class 10]

The chapter's village self-government is not a vague idea — we can read its actual rules, because they were carved on a temple wall at Uttaramerur in the Chola country. The inscription describes how the village ran itself: it was divided into wards, candidates for committees had to meet real qualifications (owning some land, knowing the Vedas or administration, being honest and not in debt), and members were chosen by the kudavolai or 'pot-ticket' method — names written on palm leaves were drawn from a pot by a young child, a kind of lottery to keep it fair. Committees looked after tanks, gardens, justice and gold, and a member who misused funds, or whose close relatives did, could be disqualified. This is astonishing: a careful, written system of local elections, qualifications and accountability, running in Tamil villages while most of the world was ruled by kings alone. It reminds us that the idea of ordinary people governing themselves has deep roots in India.

You'll want to know first: Knowing that Chola villages had self-government through assemblies (sabhas).

Bhakti: when God was praised in the mother tongue [Class 10]

The Bhakti tradition that this chapter says began in the Tamil south was a quiet revolution in how people worshipped. Its saint-poets — the Nayanars who sang of Shiva and the Alvars who sang of Vishnu — did something new: they poured out love for a personal god not in scholarly Sanskrit but in everyday Tamil, the language ordinary people actually spoke. Because the path was simple, heartfelt devotion rather than complicated ritual, it was open to all — men and women, rich and poor, and people of every jati. Among the most loved saints were a woman, Andal, and people from humble communities, showing how Bhakti could cut across the hardening social divisions of the age. Over the following centuries this devotional movement travelled north and reshaped religious life across India. It is a powerful example of how using the people's own language can open up something that was once closed to a few.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the Bhakti tradition began with the Nayanars and Alvars in the south.
