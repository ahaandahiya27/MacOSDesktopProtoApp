The Age of Regional Kingdoms

After the Gupta Empire faded around 600 CE, India entered the 'early medieval' centuries (c.600-1200 CE) of many strong regional kingdoms rather than one empire - a creative, not declining, age. Harshavardhana of Kannauj (from 606 CE) was the north's last great king, and the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang's travelogue, read alongside inscriptions and coins, lets historians reconstruct the period.

  1. From One Empire to Many KingdomsAfter the Gupta Empire faded around 600 CE, India entered six centuries (c.600-1200 CE) of many strong regional kingdoms rather than one giant empire.
  2. Harshavardhana, the 'Great King of Kings'Harshavardhana ruled north India from Kannauj from 606 CE; a poet-king of the Pushyabhuti dynasty, he was the last great power of the north before the Tripartite Struggle.
  3. Xuanzang and Reading History from SourcesThe Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang travelled India (630-644 CE), and his travelogue - alongside inscriptions and coins - is a key, but carefully weighed, source for this period.

The Tripartite Struggle for Kannauj

After Harsha's death in 647 CE, three great powers fought for over a century to control the prized Gangetic city of Kannauj: the Palas of Bengal (patrons of Mahayana Buddhism and the universities Vikramashila and Nalanda), the Gurjara-Pratiharas of the west (who blocked Arab expansion under King Bhoja), and the Rashtrakutas of the Deccan (who carved the colossal Kailasanatha temple at Ellora). None won lasting victory, keeping the age multipolar.

  1. The Tripartite Struggle for KannaujAfter Harsha's death in 647, three great powers - the Palas, the Gurjara-Pratiharas and the Rashtrakutas - fought for over a century to control the prized city of Kannauj, with no lasting victor.
  2. The Palas of Bengal and Their UniversitiesThe Palas, beginning with Gopala (chosen king in 750 CE), ruled eastern India, championed Mahayana Buddhism, and funded great universities like Vikramashila and Nalanda.
  3. The Gurjara-Pratiharas, Defenders of the North-WestThe Gurjara-Pratiharas, founded by Nagabhata I in the mid-8th century, ruled much of north India under King Bhoja and became famous for pushing back Arab invasions.
  4. The Rashtrakutas and the Wonder of ElloraThe Rashtrakutas, from Dantidurga (mid-8th c.) to the 64-year reign of Amoghavarsha I, dominated the Deccan, carved the colossal Kailasanatha temple at Ellora, and patronised all faiths.

The Southern Powers

While three powers fought over Kannauj, the south flourished. The Chalukyas of Vatapi (Badami) under Pulakeshin II checked Harsha and built famous temples at Badami and Aihole. The Pallavas of Kanchi under Narasimhavarman I 'Mamalla' carved the rock-cut wonders of Mamallapuram. And the ancient Tamil Cholas, Pandyas and Cheras revived - the Cholas, reborn under Vijayalaya at Tanjavur, growing into one of south India's greatest empires.

  1. The Chalukyas of VatapiThe Chalukyas, founded by Pulakeshin I (mid-6th c.) and expanded by Pulakeshin II from their capital Vatapi (Badami), dominated the Deccan and left famous temples at Badami and Aihole.
  2. The Pallavas of Kanchi and the Stone Temples of MamallapuramThe Pallavas of Kanchi, peaking under Narasimhavarman I 'Mamalla' in the 7th century, defeated the Chalukyas and carved the famous rock-cut and monolithic temples at Mamallapuram.
  3. The Revival of the Cholas, Pandyas and CherasThe ancient Tamil dynasties revived: the Pandyas (Madurai) and Cheras (Kerala) re-emerged, and the Cholas, reborn under Vijayalaya (9th c., Tanjavur), grew into one of south India's greatest empires.

Society, Administration and Economy

Beneath the dynasties, society and economy were dynamic. Kings ruled their core directly but governed outer regions through subordinate samantas, while villages largely governed themselves - the Chola Uttaramerur inscriptions reveal democratic village assemblies. Land grants, irrigation tanks and powerful merchant guilds kept agriculture, trade and towns flourishing. Society grew complex: jatis multiplied yet stayed flexible, women took varied roles, and some regions saw ruling queens.

  1. Kings, Samantas and Village Self-GovernmentKingdoms ruled their core directly but governed outer regions through subordinate rulers (samantas), while villages largely governed themselves through assemblies like the Chola sabha.
  2. Land Grants, Trade and Flourishing TownsThe land-grant system expanded, irrigation and agriculture grew, and busy merchant guilds traded along both coasts, keeping towns and ports flourishing rather than declining.
  3. A More Complex Society: Jatis, Women and QueensSociety grew complex: jatis multiplied yet the varna-jati system stayed flexible, women took varied roles, and some regions even saw ruling queens.

Faith, Learning and Foreign Encounters

This was a golden age of faith and learning. From around 650 CE the Bhakti tradition spread from the Tamil south through the 12 Alvars (Vishnu) and 63 Nayanars (Shiva), offering devotion open to all. Brahmagupta (b.598 CE) gave the world the rules of zero. Kashmir under Lalitaditya was strong (recorded in Kalhana's Rajatarangini), the older Huna invaders were absorbed into India, and the Arabs - repulsed from 637 CE - conquered Sindh in 712 CE under Muhammad bin Qasim, though their rule stayed limited as Indian rulers resisted.

  1. The Bhakti Tradition Begins in the SouthFrom around 650 CE the Bhakti tradition spread from the Tamil south - the 12 Alvars (devotees of Vishnu) and 63 Nayanars (devotees of Shiva) - offering direct, personal devotion open to everyone.
  2. A Galaxy of Mathematicians and AstronomersThis period produced brilliant scientists - above all Brahmagupta (born 598 CE), who gave the world the rules of arithmetic with zero and negative numbers.
  3. Kashmir, the Hunas, and the End of an Older ThreatKashmir under Lalitaditya Muktapida was a strong kingdom recorded in Kalhana's Rajatarangini, while the earlier Huna invaders were defeated and then peacefully absorbed into Indian society.
  4. The Arabs Arrive: The Conquest of SindhArab naval raids from 637 CE were repulsed until Muhammad bin Qasim conquered Sindh in 712 CE, but Arab rule stayed limited and short-lived as Indian rulers resisted for centuries.
