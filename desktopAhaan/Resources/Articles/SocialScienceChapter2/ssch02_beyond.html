Why pressure (and oxygen) falls with altitude [Class 9]

Atmospheric pressure equals the weight of the entire column of air above a point, so it must fall as you climb, because more of the air ends up below you. But the fall is not steady — it is roughly exponential, dropping fastest near the ground where the air is densest. That is why pressure goes from about 1013 mb at sea level to around 650 mb at Khardung La (over 5,600 m): more than a third of the air is already below you. Crucially, the share of oxygen in air stays about 21% everywhere, but because the whole air is thinner, each breath contains fewer oxygen molecules. The body responds over days by making more red blood cells to carry the scarce oxygen — which is exactly why mountaineers acclimatise instead of climbing straight to the top.

You'll want to know first: Knowing that atmospheric pressure is the weight of the air above us.

Relative humidity, dew point and why clouds form [Class 9]

Air can hold only a limited amount of water vapour, and that limit rises sharply as the air gets warmer — warm air is 'roomier' for vapour. Relative humidity is the ratio of the vapour actually present to that maximum, as a percentage. The clever consequence: if you cool a parcel of air without adding any water, its relative humidity climbs, because the 'room' shrinks. Cool it enough to reach 100% — the 'dew point' — and vapour starts condensing into tiny droplets, forming dew, fog or clouds. This is why dew appears on cold mornings, why fog forms in cold valleys, and why rising air (which cools as it goes up) makes clouds. It also explains evaporative cooling: sweat and wet clothes dry slowly when the air is already near 100%, leaving you hot and sticky.

You'll want to know first: Understanding humidity as the amount of water vapour in the air.

How a weather forecast is actually made [Class 10]

A modern forecast is not a guess. Thousands of weather stations, balloons, ships, aircraft and satellites measure temperature, pressure, humidity and wind across the whole globe at the same moments. These numbers are fed into giant computer programs that divide the atmosphere into a 3-D grid and use the physics of how air, heat and moisture move to calculate the next few hours, then the next, and so on. The catch is that the atmosphere is 'chaotic': a tiny error in today's measurements grows bigger the further ahead you compute — the famous 'butterfly effect'. That is why a forecast for tomorrow is usually reliable, but one for ten days away is much less certain, and why forecasters now run many slightly different calculations and report the odds (a '60% chance of rain') rather than a single answer.

Why warm air rises: convection and the sea breeze [Class 9]

Heat a parcel of air and it expands, becomes less dense than the air around it, and floats upward — just like a hot-air balloon. Cooler, denser air then flows in along the ground to take its place, and that horizontal flow is wind. This 'convection' explains the sea breeze: by day the land heats faster than the sea, so air rises over the warm land and cooler air blows in from the sea, giving coastal afternoons their refreshing breeze; at night it reverses. The same rising-and-sinking links straight back to pressure — air rises where pressure is low and sinks where it is high — so convection ties together temperature, pressure and wind, three of the five weather elements, into one moving picture.

You'll want to know first: Knowing that wind is moving air and that warm air is lighter than cold air.

Why a thermometer lives in a white box in the shade [Class 9]

If you leave a thermometer in direct sunshine it does not measure the air at all — it measures how hot the sun has made the glass, and can read many degrees too high. So a real weather station never does that. Its thermometers sit inside a white, louvred wooden box (a 'Stevenson screen') raised about a metre and a quarter above short grass. White paint reflects sunlight, the slatted sides let air flow freely past the bulb, and the standard height keeps the bulb away from heat radiating off the ground. The point is fairness: only if every station in the country measures the air the same way — in shade, at the same height, with free-flowing air — can a temperature taken in Delhi be honestly compared with one taken in Chennai. Good measurement is not just about the instrument; it is about measuring the same thing, the same way, everywhere.

You'll want to know first: Knowing temperature is measured with a thermometer.

What a rain-gauge reading really means [Class 10]

When a forecast says '25 mm of rain fell today', what does that number mean? A rain gauge catches rain through a funnel of a known opening and channels it into a narrow measuring tube. The reading is a DEPTH: 25 mm means that if the rain had not drained away, soaked in or evaporated, it would have covered flat ground to a depth of 25 millimetres — everywhere, not just in the gauge. The funnel matters because it concentrates the catch into the thin tube, so a tiny depth becomes an easy-to-read height of water. That is also why rainfall does not depend on the size of your gauge: a wide bucket and a narrow tube standing side by side both fill to the same depth. Turn the depth into volume and the numbers get dramatic — 25 mm over a single hectare is 250,000 litres of water.

You'll want to know first: Knowing rainfall is measured with a rain gauge.
