Challenge 1 [Level 4 — stretch]

On a summer day a town recorded a maximum temperature of 38°C and a minimum of 26°C. What is that day's MEAN (average) daily temperature, in °C?

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

32

How to crack it

  1. Recall that the mean daily temperature = (maximum + minimum) ÷ 2.
  2. Add the two readings: 38 + 26 = 64.
  3. Divide by 2: 64 ÷ 2 = 32.
  4. So the mean daily temperature is 32°C.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Confusing the MEAN (38+26)÷2 = 32 with the RANGE (38−26) = 12.
  • Forgetting to divide the sum by 2.

🚀 Now try this twist

For the same day (max 38°C, min 26°C), what is the temperature RANGE in °C?

Open the answer

12

  1. Range = maximum − minimum.
  2. 38 − 26 = 12.
  3. The range is 12°C.

🚀 Now try this twist

Over three days the maximum/minimum temperatures were Mon 30/18, Tue 33/19, Wed 28/20 (°C). On which day was the temperature range the GREATEST?

Open the answer

Tuesday — its range was 33 − 19 = 14°C, larger than Monday's 12°C and Wednesday's 8°C.

  1. Find each day's range (max − min): Mon 12, Tue 14, Wed 8.
  2. Compare the three ranges.
  3. Tuesday's 14°C is the largest.

Challenge 2 [Level 5 — hardest]

A weather map shows a region of HIGH atmospheric pressure lying right next to a region of LOW pressure. (a) Which way will the wind blow between them, and why? (b) If the difference in pressure is very large, how will the wind be different? Explain using how wind forms.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

(a) Wind will blow FROM the high-pressure region TO the low-pressure region. This is because air is a fluid: where the air is squashed harder (high pressure) it pushes out toward where it is pressing less hard (low pressure), and that moving air is what we call wind. (b) The bigger the difference between the two pressures, the harder the air is pushed, so the wind will be STRONGER and faster. A small pressure difference gives a gentle breeze; a very large difference gives strong, gusty winds.

How to crack it

  1. Recall that wind is moving air, and air moves from high pressure to low pressure.
  2. Part (a): name the direction — high → low — and explain the 'push' from squashed air to less-squashed air.
  3. Part (b): link the SIZE of the pressure difference to wind strength.
  4. Conclude: bigger pressure gap → stronger wind; small gap → gentle breeze.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Saying wind blows from low to high pressure — it is the other way round.
  • Forgetting that a bigger pressure difference makes the wind stronger, not just change its direction.

🚀 Now try this twist

Two days have the same weather except the pressure difference across the town is much larger on Tuesday. On which day is the wind likely stronger, and why?

Open the answer

Tuesday. A larger difference in pressure pushes the air harder from the high to the low side, so the wind blows faster and stronger.

  1. Recall wind strength depends on the size of the pressure difference.
  2. Bigger difference → stronger push → stronger wind.
  3. So Tuesday's wind is stronger.

Challenge 3 [Level 4 — stretch]

On a hot afternoon a hygrometer shows the relative humidity climbing higher and higher. Explain why this makes the air feel 'sticky' and uncomfortable, and why your wet clothes on the line would dry more slowly.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

Relative humidity tells us how much water vapour the air already holds compared to the most it could hold. When it climbs high, the air is nearly 'full' of water vapour. That makes it sticky because the sweat on your skin cannot evaporate easily into already-moist air, so it stays on you instead of cooling you down. For the same reason wet clothes dry slowly: drying means water evaporating from the cloth into the air, and air that is already nearly full of moisture cannot take in much more, so the water leaves the clothes only very slowly.

How to crack it

  1. Recall that relative humidity = how full of water vapour the air is.
  2. High humidity → air is nearly saturated, so it can absorb little extra moisture.
  3. Sweat can't evaporate → skin stays wet → feels 'sticky' and hot.
  4. Clothes dry by evaporation, which is slow when the air is already moist.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Thinking high humidity means it is raining — it just means the air holds a lot of water vapour.
  • Saying clothes dry FASTER in humid air — evaporation is actually slower.

🚀 Now try this twist

Would clothes dry faster on a dry, breezy day or a humid, still day? Why?

Open the answer

On the dry, breezy day. Dry air can absorb more water vapour and the breeze keeps replacing the moist air near the cloth with fresh dry air, so evaporation — and drying — is much faster.

  1. Recall drying is evaporation into the air.
  2. Dry air can take more moisture; a breeze removes the moist air.
  3. So a dry, breezy day dries clothes faster.

Challenge 4 [Level 5 — hardest]

Over a few hours a weather station records: the barometer FALLING steadily, the hygrometer reading RISING, the anemometer showing the wind SPEEDING UP, and the sky CLOUDING over. As the meteorologist on duty, what kind of weather would you forecast for the next few hours, and name the instrument that gave you each clue?

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

I would forecast that wet, stormy weather is on the way — most likely rain, with possibly strong winds. The clues: the BAROMETER (pressure) is falling, and falling pressure usually means an area of low pressure with unsettled, rainy weather is approaching. The HYGROMETER shows humidity rising, meaning the air is filling with water vapour that can condense into clouds and rain. The ANEMOMETER shows the wind speeding up, which fits air rushing toward the low-pressure area. And the thickening clouds (seen by eye) are where the rain will form. Reading all the instruments together, not just one, is what lets a meteorologist forecast confidently.

How to crack it

  1. Link each reading to the instrument: falling pressure → barometer; rising humidity → hygrometer; rising wind → anemometer.
  2. Falling barometer → low pressure approaching → unsettled, rainy weather.
  3. Rising humidity → more water vapour → clouds and rain likely.
  4. Speeding wind → air rushing toward the low pressure.
  5. Combine the clues into one forecast: rain and possibly strong winds soon.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Reading only ONE instrument — a good forecast combines several clues.
  • Thinking a RISING barometer signals rain; it is usually FALLING pressure that warns of bad weather.

🚀 Now try this twist

Another day the barometer is steady and high, humidity is low and the sky is clear. What weather would you forecast?

Open the answer

Fine, dry, settled weather. High steady pressure, low humidity and clear skies all point to calm, sunny conditions with little chance of rain.

  1. High steady pressure → settled weather.
  2. Low humidity + clear sky → no rain expected.
  3. Forecast fine, dry weather.
