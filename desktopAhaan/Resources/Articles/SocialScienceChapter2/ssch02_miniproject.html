🌧️ Build your own rain gauge (about 45 min)

What you need

  • A clear plastic bottle (1 to 2 litres)
  • Scissors (use with an adult)
  • A ruler and a waterproof marker
  • Some small stones or sand
  • A little water

Steps

  1. With an adult's help, cut off the top third of the bottle.
  2. Put a few stones or some sand in the bottom to keep it steady, then add water up to the top of the stones and mark this as your '0' line.
  3. Stick a ruler scale (in mm) on the side, starting from the 0 line.
  4. Turn the cut-off top upside down and place it into the bottle like a funnel.
  5. Put the gauge in open ground, away from walls, trees and roofs, on a flat surface where it will not topple in the wind.
  6. At the same time each day for a few weeks, read and record the depth of water above the 0 line, then empty it back to 0.
  7. If snow falls, let it melt before reading. Calculate the average rainfall for each week and compare the weeks.

👀 What you should see

On rainy days the water rises by several millimetres; on dry days it stays at 0. You will see how much rainfall changes from day to day and week to week.

🧠 Why it works

The funnel catches rain over a wide mouth and channels it into the bottle, while the scale measures the depth — exactly how a real rain gauge reports rainfall in millimetres. Open siting stops walls and trees from blocking the rain and spoiling the reading.

🌬️ Make a simple wind vane (about 40 min)

What you need

  • A drinking straw
  • A stiff paper or card (for an arrow and a tail)
  • A pin and a pencil with an eraser
  • A small lump of clay
  • A compass (or a phone compass app)

Steps

  1. Cut a small triangular pointer (arrow) and a larger square tail from the card.
  2. Make a slit in each end of the straw and slide the pointer into one end and the bigger tail into the other.
  3. Push a pin through the exact middle of the straw and into the eraser of a pencil, so the straw can spin freely.
  4. Stand the pencil upright in a lump of clay as a base.
  5. Take it outdoors to an open spot; use the compass to mark North, South, East and West around the base.
  6. Watch which way the pointer turns when the wind blows, and note the direction the wind is coming from.
  7. Record the wind direction at the same time each day for a week and look for any pattern.

👀 What you should see

The big tail is pushed by the wind, swinging the pointer to face into the wind, so the arrow shows where the wind is coming from — and it turns even in a light breeze.

🧠 Why it works

The tail has a larger surface for the wind to push, so it is always blown downwind, automatically turning the slender pointer to face the wind's source — the same principle as a real wind vane or weather vane.
