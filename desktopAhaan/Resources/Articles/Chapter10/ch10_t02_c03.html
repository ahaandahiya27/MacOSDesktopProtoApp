Anaerobic respiration — without oxygen

Anaerobic respiration breaks down glucose without oxygen, producing less energy (2 ATP) and by-products like alcohol or lactic acid.

≈ 2 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Sometimes oxygen is in short supply — and yet cells still need energy. Yeast in bread dough and bacteria in your gut have evolved to extract energy WITHOUT oxygen. This is anaerobic respiration. It produces just 2 ATP per glucose (compared to 38 with oxygen) and leaves behind by-products: alcohol + CO₂ in yeast, lactic acid in bacteria. Even your own muscles do this when you sprint hard — that 'burn' you feel is lactic acid building up because oxygen can't keep up.

The textbook version

Anaerobic respiration is the type of respiration in which glucose is broken down in the absence of oxygen. It produces less energy (about 2 ATP per glucose) and different by-products depending on the organism. In yeast: Glucose → Alcohol (ethanol) + Carbon Dioxide + Energy. In animal muscles during heavy exercise: Glucose → Lactic Acid + Energy. Many bacteria, fungi, and some animal cells perform anaerobic respiration. Anaerobic respiration is also observed in human muscles when there is a temporary shortage of oxygen during intense exercise.

Why does this happen?

Glycolysis (the first stage of respiration) doesn't need oxygen. It breaks glucose into pyruvate, releasing 2 ATP. Without oxygen, the cell cannot run the rest of respiration, so it just modifies pyruvate into something it can excrete (alcohol or lactate). Less efficient, but life continues.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Bread rising — Yeast in dough does anaerobic respiration — splits sugar into CO₂ (which puffs up the dough) and alcohol (which bakes off). Two ancient discoveries: bread and beer use the exact same chemistry. [Kitchen]
  • Curd from milk — Lactobacillus bacteria do lactic acid fermentation in milk, lowering pH and curdling proteins. The curd's tang is the lactic acid you can taste. [Kitchen]
  • Burning legs after running fast — When you sprint, your muscles work faster than oxygen delivery. Cells switch to anaerobic respiration, building up lactic acid. The familiar burn means muscles ran low on oxygen. [Human body]

Going deeper

Two main anaerobic pathways: alcoholic fermentation (yeast, used in bread, beer, wine) and lactic acid fermentation (Lactobacillus, used in curd, cheese, pickles, also human muscle during sprints). The accumulation of lactic acid in muscles isn't actually the cause of soreness (despite the popular belief) — recent research suggests soreness is from microscopic muscle tears repaired by inflammation. Industrial anaerobic respiration powers wastewater treatment, biogas production, and bio-fuel manufacturing — humans now domesticate microbes' anaerobic energy strategy at industrial scale.

Beyond the book

Some deep-sea organisms NEVER use oxygen. Tube worms near hydrothermal vents host symbiotic bacteria that respire hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) instead — chemoautotrophic respiration. An entire food web exists at the bottom of the ocean independent of sunlight or oxygen, fundamentally challenging assumptions about what's needed for life.
