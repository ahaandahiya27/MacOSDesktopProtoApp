Beyond the Book — Three Mind-Stretchers About Respiration in Organisms

Three ideas that don't show up in the NCERT textbook but will change how you see this chapter.

≈ 9 min read · curious-kid level

The chapter teaches you every living thing — from a bacterium to a blue whale — needs energy. This article goes a step further — three stretch topics that lead naturally into Class 8 and beyond. Read one a day; don't try to absorb them all at once.

1. Cellular respiration — the steps inside the mitochondrion

Class 7 says aerobic respiration: glucose + O₂ → CO₂ + water + energy. Class 8 reveals this happens in stages, mostly inside MITOCHONDRIA (cell powerhouses). Stage 1 GLYCOLYSIS: in the cytoplasm, glucose (6-carbon) splits into 2 pyruvate (3-carbon) molecules + a little ATP energy. Stage 2 KREBS CYCLE: pyruvate enters mitochondria, gets stripped of CO₂ (the CO₂ you exhale!), produces NADH + a little more ATP. Stage 3 ELECTRON TRANSPORT CHAIN: NADH dumps electrons to oxygen — that's where most ATP (~34 per glucose) is made. Without oxygen for stage 3, only glycolysis runs — that's anaerobic respiration, with just 2 ATP. That's why aerobic is so much more efficient than anaerobic.

2. Glycolysis to ATP — the energy currency in detail

Class 7 says respiration makes 'energy'. Class 11 specifies: ENERGY in cells = ATP (adenosine triphosphate). Each glucose makes ~38 ATP total via aerobic respiration. ATP stores energy in its third phosphate bond — when the cell uses energy (muscle contraction, nerve firing, building proteins), it 'cracks' ATP → ADP + Pi (inorganic phosphate) + energy. The whole human body 'turns over' your bodyweight worth of ATP per day — but only ~250g exists at any moment. ATP is constantly made and broken. Subbarao's 1929 discovery (Yellapragada Subbarao at Harvard — Chapter 10's scientist) was what ATP actually IS. Modern medicine — chemotherapy, antibiotics — often works by disrupting ATP production in fast-growing cells.

3. Lung volumes and breathing efficiency — what fitness actually means

Class 7 introduces the respiratory system. Class 10 quantifies lung function. TIDAL VOLUME (normal breath): ~500 mL in/out. VITAL CAPACITY (max forceful breath): ~4500 mL adult, can reach 7000+ in trained athletes. INSPIRATORY RESERVE (extra you can inhale above tidal): ~3000 mL. RESIDUAL VOLUME (always trapped): ~1200 mL — lungs never empty completely. Olympic swimmers like India's Sajan Prakash train to expand vital capacity — efficient breathing = better endurance. A fit Class 7 student already has vital capacity ~70-80% of adult range. As you age, vital capacity declines ~25 mL per year after age 25 — exercise slows this decline by half. Lung function is one of the few biomarkers you can directly improve with consistent training.
