Plants Breathe Too — Just Differently

Stomata, lenticels and root respiration · Class 7 Science

Plants need oxygen 24×7

It's a common mistake to think plants only 'breathe out' oxygen. In fact, every plant cell needs oxygen to release energy from sugar — exactly like animal cells. Plants also make oxygen during the day via photosynthesis, but they consume oxygen day and night for respiration.

Stomata — leaf breathing pores

The underside of a leaf has microscopic pores called stomata, each guarded by two kidney-shaped cells. Stomata open and close to control gas exchange and water loss. A single tomato leaf may have 50,000+ stomata.

Lenticels — stem breathing pores

Woody stems have a corky outer layer that won't let air through. So they breathe through tiny rough patches called lenticels. Look at the bark of a guava or mango tree — those little brown spots are lenticels.

Roots and the root-zone problem

Roots need oxygen too. They get it from air spaces in the soil. Waterlogged soil has no air pockets, so roots suffocate. That's why over-watered houseplants die — not from too much water, but from no oxygen at the roots.

Mangrove trees grow in waterlogged saline mud where oxygen barely reaches the roots. They evolved pneumatophores — pencil-thick roots that stick up out of the mud like snorkels — to bring air to the buried roots.

Try this at home

Place a fresh leaf in a glass of water with the underside up. Look at the underside through a magnifier — you can sometimes see tiny silvery bubbles forming. Those are gases escaping through the stomata.
