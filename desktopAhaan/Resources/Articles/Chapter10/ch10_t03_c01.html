How Fish Use Their Gills

Pulling oxygen out of water · Class 7 Science

Water has oxygen — just not much

A litre of air contains about 200 mL of oxygen. A litre of water contains about 6–10 mL of dissolved oxygen — twenty to thirty times less. Yet fish thrive entirely on this dissolved oxygen. How?

The answer is gills

A fish has 4 pairs of gills tucked under flap-like covers (operculum) on each side of its head. Each gill is a feathery comb of thin red filaments, each filament made of even thinner lamellae. The total surface area of the gills, unfolded, is enormous — many times the fish's body surface area.

The gill cycle

  1. The fish gulps water through its open mouth.
  2. Closes the mouth and lowers the floor of its mouth — forcing the water through the gills.
  3. Each gill lamella has thousands of capillaries running through it. Water flows OUTWARD past the lamellae while blood flows INWARD through them — opposite directions. This is called counter-current flow.
  4. Oxygen diffuses from the water into the blood. CO₂ goes the other way.
  5. Used water leaves through the gill cover slits.
  6. Operculum closes; fish gulps the next mouthful.
Counter-current flow is what makes the system efficient: ~80% of the dissolved oxygen in passing water gets extracted, compared to only ~25% in your mammalian lungs (where blood and air flow parallel). The same idea is now used in industrial heat exchangers.

Why fish die out of water

Not because they can't get oxygen — there's plenty in air. The problem is that gills only work when wet. Out of water, the delicate gill filaments collapse and stick to each other. No diffusion, no breathing. Within minutes, the fish suffocates.

Some fish are exceptions

  • Mudskippers can survive on land for hours because they trap water in their gill chambers.
  • Lungfish have a primitive lung in addition to gills.
  • Walking catfish can absorb oxygen through their skin and accessory air-breathing organs.

Try this at home

At your nearest aquarium (home or pet shop), watch a fish closely for 30 seconds. You'll see its mouth opening and closing rhythmically; its gill covers flap in counter-time. Every cycle pulls a mouthful of water past the gills. You're watching counter-current respiration in action.

Looking ahead

Class 11 Biology 'Breathing and Exchange of Gases' compares the four respiratory strategies — cutaneous (skin, in earthworms and frogs), tracheal (insects), branchial (gills, in fish), pulmonary (lungs, in mammals/birds/reptiles). NEET frequently asks why fish gills are more efficient than mammalian lungs (counter-current). Class 12 'Excretory Products' notes that fish also use gills for ammonia excretion.
