Aerobic Respiration in Detail

How cells release energy from glucose with oxygen · Class 7 Science

The food → energy reaction

Every cell in your body burns glucose with oxygen to make energy. The overall reaction:

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + energy (38 ATP)

That's aerobic respiration — 'aerobic' because oxygen is required. The energy is released gradually in 38 small steps, captured into 38 high-energy molecules called ATP (adenosine triphosphate), which cells use like a chemical battery.

Where in the cell?

Inside organelles called mitochondria. Each cell has hundreds to thousands of them — more in active cells (muscle, brain, liver). Mitochondria are sometimes called 'the powerhouse of the cell' for this reason.

Why oxygen is essential

Oxygen accepts the electrons stripped off glucose at the end of the chain. Without oxygen, the chain backs up, ATP production stops at just 2 molecules (instead of 38), and the cell starves of energy. That's why holding your breath for too long ends in unconsciousness within minutes.

What if oxygen runs short?

Some cells can switch briefly to anaerobic respiration — getting just 2 ATP per glucose but without oxygen. In humans, this happens in skeletal muscles during a sprint. The waste product, lactic acid, accumulates and causes cramps. After exercise, you keep breathing deeply to oxidise that lactic acid back — that's the 'oxygen debt' you're repaying.

Yeast — a tiny single-celled fungus — uses anaerobic respiration as its main strategy. It produces alcohol and CO₂ instead of lactic acid. That CO₂ inflates bread dough; that alcohol becomes wine, beer, and rum. Same chemistry, different end products.

Try this at home

Mix a spoon of fresh dahi (yogurt starter) into warm milk in a covered bowl. Leave it overnight in a warm spot. The Lactobacillus bacteria respire anaerobically on milk sugar, producing lactic acid that curdles the milk into fresh yogurt — same chemistry as muscle cramps, just turned into food.

Looking ahead

Class 11 Biology 'Respiration in Plants' (don't be fooled by the name — same cellular chemistry applies to animals) covers the four stages of aerobic respiration in formal detail:

  1. Glycolysis — glucose → 2 pyruvate, in cytoplasm, no oxygen needed, yields 2 ATP.
  2. Link reaction — pyruvate → Acetyl-CoA, in mitochondrial matrix.
  3. Krebs cycle (Citric acid cycle) — Acetyl-CoA → CO₂, generating high-energy NADH and FADH₂.
  4. Electron transport chain — NADH/FADH₂ feed electrons; O₂ accepts them at the end. ATP synthase makes ~34 more ATP.

NEET asks 'where in the mitochondrion does X happen' and 'how many ATP from Y' questions every year. Class 12 connects to disorders of mitochondrial function.
