Q1. Why does an athlete breathe faster and deeper than usual after finishing the race?

Model answer: During a fast run, muscles need energy faster than oxygen can reach them. They temporarily switch to anaerobic respiration, producing lactic acid as a byproduct. After the race, the athlete must (1) supply enough oxygen to all muscle cells to switch back to aerobic respiration, and (2) clear the lactic acid that built up. Both require extra oxygen — hence the fast deep breathing for several minutes after stopping. This is called 'oxygen debt' and is paid back at rest.

Q2. List the similarities and differences between aerobic and anaerobic respiration.

Model answer: Similarities: Both break down glucose to release energy. Both happen in living cells. Both produce some energy + a waste product. Differences: Aerobic uses oxygen, anaerobic doesn't. Aerobic happens in mitochondria; anaerobic happens in cytoplasm. Aerobic produces CO₂ + water + LOTS of energy (~38 ATP per glucose). Anaerobic produces lactic acid (in animals) OR alcohol + CO₂ (in yeast) + LITTLE energy (~2 ATP per glucose). Aerobic is the everyday process; anaerobic is the emergency backup.

Q3. Why does breathing rate increase when one does heavy exercise?

Model answer: Heavy exercise means muscles work harder, demand more energy. To meet that demand, the muscles need more oxygen and produce more CO₂. The brain's breathing centre detects rising CO₂ in blood and signals the diaphragm to contract faster and deeper. Inhalation rate increases from ~16/minute (rest) to ~50/minute (exercise). Breath volume also increases. Together, these supply 6-10× more oxygen per minute. The body's hardware automatically scales to match the engine's demand.

Q4. Take three test tubes. Fill 3/4 of each with water. Label them A, B, C. Keep a snail in A, a plant in B and both in C. Which test tube contains more amount of CO₂ in the air above water after a few hours?

Model answer: Test tube A (snail only) accumulates the most CO₂ over a few hours. A snail respires continuously (producing CO₂) but doesn't photosynthesise. B has a plant in water — during the day, photosynthesis (using CO₂) dominates respiration; little net CO₂ output. C has both — the plant uses some of the snail's CO₂ for photosynthesis, balancing partially. So A > C > B in CO₂ accumulation. This demonstrates how plants and animals balance the atmosphere.

Q5. Tick the correct answer: In cockroaches, air enters the body through (a) lungs (b) gills (c) spiracles (d) skin.

Model answer: (c) spiracles. Cockroaches and other insects have spiracles — small holes along the sides of their bodies — connected to a network of tubes called trachea. Air enters through spiracles, flows through trachea, and reaches every cell directly. No lungs, no blood-carried oxygen. This works because insects are small enough that trachea can reach every cell. (Why insects can't be much bigger than your palm: trachea wouldn't reach distant cells fast enough.)

Q6. During heavy exercise, we get cramps in the legs due to the accumulation of (a) carbon dioxide (b) lactic acid (c) alcohol (d) water.

Model answer: (b) lactic acid. When muscle cells run short of oxygen during heavy exercise, they switch to anaerobic respiration: glucose → lactic acid + a little energy. Lactic acid builds up in the muscle tissue, interfering with muscle contraction and causing the burning + cramping feeling. Rest allows oxygen to return and the body to clear the lactic acid (it's converted back to glucose by the liver).

Q7. Take two glass jars and put equal amounts of fresh germinating seeds in both. Cover one jar tightly with a plastic sheet. Leave for 1 hour. Then add lime water to each jar. What do you observe?

Model answer: The covered jar's lime water turns milky white; the uncovered jar's lime water stays mostly clear. The germinating seeds were respiring — using oxygen and releasing CO₂. In the covered jar, CO₂ accumulated; lime water (Ca(OH)₂) reacts with CO₂ to form chalky calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) — that's the milky white. In the uncovered jar, CO₂ dispersed into the air; less to react. This experiment proves seeds (plants) respire.

Q8. Why is yawning natural and contagious?

Model answer: Yawning is the body's way of taking a deeper-than-normal breath to refresh oxygen supply and possibly cool the brain. We yawn when tired, bored, or just after waking — all times when shallow breathing might leave the brain slightly under-oxygenated. Why contagious? Mirror neurons in the brain make us copy the behaviour of others we see. Seeing or even hearing a yawn triggers similar brain activity in observers. It's been observed in dogs and chimpanzees too — a primitive social-physiological behaviour.
