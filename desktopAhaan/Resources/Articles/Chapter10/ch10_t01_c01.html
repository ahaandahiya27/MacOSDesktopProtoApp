Inhalation and Exhalation

How a sheet of muscle moves all the air in your lungs · Class 7 Science

The diaphragm — the muscle you forget about

Your lungs don't have any muscles of their own. They're passive bags. Every breath you take is actually powered by a sheet of muscle below the lungs called the diaphragm, plus the muscles between your ribs (intercostals).

What happens when you inhale

  • Your diaphragm contracts and pulls itself DOWN, flattening from its usual dome shape.
  • The intercostal muscles contract and pull the rib cage UP and OUT.
  • Together, these movements increase the volume of the chest cavity.
  • Larger volume → lower pressure inside the lungs (gas law: P × V = constant).
  • Outside air, which is now at higher pressure, rushes IN through the nose and trachea to equalise.

What happens when you exhale

The reverse, but you don't have to work for it: the diaphragm relaxes back to its dome shape, the rib cage drops, chest volume decreases, internal pressure rises, and air pushes OUT. At rest, exhalation is passive — driven only by the elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall.

You take about 20,000 breaths a day. Each one moves around 500 mL of air in and out — totalling 10,000 L (~13,000 of those 1L bottles) of air passing through your lungs daily. All without conscious effort.

Inside the lungs — alveoli

Inside each lung, the bronchi branch into smaller bronchioles, ending in tiny grape-like sacs called alveoli. Each lung holds about 300 million of them. Their combined surface area is about 70 square metres — roughly the size of a tennis court, all packed into your chest. That's where oxygen passes into the blood and CO₂ passes out.

Try this at home

Sit still for one minute, then count your breaths for 30 seconds. Multiply by 2 to get your resting breath rate. For a Class 7 child, the normal range is 12–20 breaths per minute. Now run up and down a flight of stairs three times and count again. The rate doubles or triples — your body's way of saying "I need more O₂".

Looking ahead

Class 11 Biology 'Breathing and Exchange of Gases' covers this whole mechanism in formal detail — tidal volume (500 mL), inspiratory and expiratory reserve volumes, residual volume, and total lung capacity (≈ 6 litres). It also introduces partial pressures of O₂ and CO₂, the oxygen dissociation curve of haemoglobin, and the famous Bohr effect (CO₂ shifts the curve right — releases O₂ where tissues need it). All of this is NEET high-yield, with multiple questions every year.
