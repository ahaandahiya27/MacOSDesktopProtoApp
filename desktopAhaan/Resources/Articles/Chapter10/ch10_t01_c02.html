Gas Exchange in the Alveoli

Where oxygen actually moves from air to blood · Class 7 Science

The lungs are 300 million tiny balloons

The trachea (windpipe) splits into two bronchi (one per lung). Each bronchus splits into smaller and smaller bronchioles. At the tips of the smallest bronchioles are clusters of microscopic air sacs called alveoli. Each lung holds roughly 150 million of them — 300 million total.

Why so many?

Because diffusion (the process that moves O₂ from air into blood) depends entirely on SURFACE AREA. The 300 million alveoli, if flattened out and joined side by side, would cover about 70 square metres — roughly the floor area of a tennis court, all packed into your chest cavity.

That 70 m² is what lets you absorb enough oxygen in a single breath to keep all 37 trillion of your cells alive. Without this surface-area trick, animals would have to be tiny — which is why the largest land animals (elephants, blue whales) have proportionally HUGE lungs.

The diffusion wall

Each alveolus is wrapped in a dense network of capillaries — the smallest blood vessels. The wall between the alveolus's air and the capillary's blood is incredibly thin — only ONE cell thick on each side, so total wall thickness is ~0.5 micrometres. Oxygen and CO₂ diffuse across this wall by simple gas-pressure differences:

  • Fresh air in the alveolus: ~21% O₂, 0.04% CO₂.
  • Used blood arriving: low O₂, high CO₂ from cellular respiration.
  • Net result: O₂ flows alveolus → blood; CO₂ flows blood → alveolus.

Within a fraction of a second, the blood is reoxygenated. It leaves the lungs and travels back to the heart's left side, ready to be pumped to every cell.

Why surfactant matters

If the alveoli were just thin films, surface tension would collapse them like soap bubbles. The alveolar lining produces a chemical called surfactant that reduces surface tension and keeps the alveoli from sticking shut. Premature babies are sometimes born before their lungs make enough surfactant — a serious condition called Respiratory Distress Syndrome.

What can go wrong

  • Smoking destroys alveoli (emphysema). Surface area drops, gas exchange suffers, the smoker becomes breathless even climbing stairs.
  • Pneumonia fills alveoli with fluid, blocking gas exchange.
  • COVID-19 pneumonia damages the alveolar lining and causes inflammation that blocks diffusion.

Try this at home

Inflate a balloon as full as you can in one breath. The volume you blew out is roughly your tidal volume (~500 mL at rest, up to ~3 L during deep breathing). Now keep blowing past that point — you've gone into your expiratory reserve volume. Most adults can push out 4-5 L total — that's their vital capacity.

Looking ahead

Class 11 Biology 'Breathing and Exchange of Gases' covers alveolar physiology in extreme detail — partial pressures (PO₂ ≈ 100 mmHg in alveoli, PCO₂ ≈ 40 mmHg), the oxygen dissociation curve of haemoglobin, the Bohr effect (CO₂ shifts the curve right, releasing O₂ where tissues need it).
