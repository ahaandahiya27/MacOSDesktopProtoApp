🪙 Run a no-money classroom barter market (about 45 min)

What you need

  • 10–15 small objects or paper 'goods' (toy, eraser, sticker, drawing, etc.)
  • Slips of paper for each student's 'wants' list
  • A notebook to record trades
  • A timer

Steps

  1. Give each student one 'good' to own and a slip listing two things they want.
  2. Announce that NO money may be used — only direct swaps.
  3. Start the timer and let students walk around trying to trade for what they want.
  4. Each time a trade happens, both traders record it in the notebook.
  5. After 10 minutes, count how many students actually got what they wanted.
  6. Discuss who got stuck and why (no one wanted their good, or no fair ratio could be agreed).
  7. Now repeat the game, but give everyone equal 'money' tokens they all accept.
  8. Compare how much faster and easier the second round was.

👀 What you should see

In the barter round many students get stuck because of the double coincidence of wants and no common measure of value; with money, almost everyone quickly gets what they want.

🧠 Why it works

Living through barter's frustration and then experiencing money makes the abstract problems — double coincidence of wants, divisibility, no common measure of value — real and memorable, exactly the way money solved them in history.

🔍 Old-coin detective (about 60 min)

What you need

  • A handful of old coins collected from family, neighbours or shopkeepers
  • A magnifying glass
  • A notebook and pen
  • Optional: access to a library or trusted website

Steps

  1. Collect as many different old coins as you can.
  2. For each coin, write down the year inscribed on it.
  3. Look closely at the obverse (head) and reverse (tail) and sketch or note the symbols and motifs.
  4. Try to find out what each coin is made of (which metal or alloy).
  5. Group the coins by decade or by denomination.
  6. Make a guess about which is oldest and why, using the year and how worn it looks.
  7. Check your guesses against a library book or trusted website.
  8. Present your 'coin file' to the class and explain what the symbols might mean.

👀 What you should see

You will notice coins changed in metal, size, language (Hindi and English) and symbols over time, and special coins mark national events — a hands-on view of money's evolution.

🧠 Why it works

Examining real coins turns the chapter's facts about minting, alloys, obverse/reverse and rūpa symbols into direct observation, and matching guesses to evidence teaches how historians actually use coins.
