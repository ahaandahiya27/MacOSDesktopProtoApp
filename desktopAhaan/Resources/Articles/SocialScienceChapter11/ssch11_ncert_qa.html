Q1. How does the barter system take place and what kinds of commodities were used for exchange under the system? (NCERT page 244)

Under the barter system, people exchanged goods or services directly for other goods or services, without using money. For example, a person needing a pencil who had a spare eraser could swap the eraser for a classmate's spare pencil. To carry out exchanges, people used various commodities as money, including cowrie shells, salt, tea, tobacco, cloth, cattle (cows, goats, horses, sheep) and seeds. In some parts of the world, unusual items served as money too — Rai stones (giant stone discs) on Yap Island, the copper tajadero in Central Mexico, and Tevau feather coils in the Solomon Islands.

Q2. What were the limitations of the barter system? (NCERT page 244)

Barter had several limitations. (1) It needed a double coincidence of wants — each person had to want exactly what the other offered at the same time, which was hard to find. (2) There was no common standard measure of value, so it was difficult to decide a fair proportion for an exchange. (3) Divisibility was a problem — you cannot split an ox to pay for a small item. (4) Portability was a problem — goods like an ox are hard to carry around to trade. (5) Durability was a problem — goods like wheat rot or get eaten and cannot be stored for long. These difficulties led to the invention of money.

Q3. What were the salient features of ancient Indian coins? (NCERT page 244)

Ancient Indian coins were issued and controlled entirely by rulers, so each kingdom had its own coinage; over time, the coins of powerful rulers were accepted across kingdoms, helping trade. They were made from precious metals like gold, silver and copper, or their alloys, which made them strong. They were called kārṣhāpaṇas or paṇas and had symbols punched on them called rūpas. Their two sides — the obverse (head) and reverse (tail) — bore motifs of animals, trees, hills, kings, queens and deities; for example, Chalukya coins showed a Varaha (avatar of Vishnu) and a three-tiered royal parasol.

Q4. How has money as a medium of exchange transformed over time? (NCERT page 244)

Money has evolved through several forms. It began with the barter system (no money), then commodity money such as cowrie shells. Next came metallic money — coins of iron, silver, gold and copper issued by rulers. As carrying many coins became difficult, paper money (currency) was adopted; it was first used in China and introduced in India in the late 18th century, and today only the RBI may issue it. Most recently, technology has brought digital money — debit and credit cards, net banking, UPI and QR-code payments — which moves electronically between bank accounts. This evolution continues as we create new and easier ways to make and receive payments.
