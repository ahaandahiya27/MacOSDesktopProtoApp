Why money needs to be 'generally accepted' [Class 10]

The single most important property of money is that everyone is willing to accept it. A ₹500 note has almost no value as paper; you take it only because you are confident the next shopkeeper will take it from you too. This is a 'self-fulfilling' belief: money works because everyone expects everyone else to use it. Governments strengthen this by declaring official currency 'legal tender' (it must be accepted to settle debts) and by giving sole issuing power to a central bank like the RBI, which guarantees uniformity and fights counterfeiting. When that trust breaks — say, through runaway inflation or floods of fake notes — money can stop working even if it is physically the same. This is why protecting the trust behind money is treated as seriously as printing it.

Inflation: when money becomes a weaker store of value [Class 10]

Money is supposed to store value so you can spend it later — but over time, prices tend to rise, a process called inflation. If a chocolate that cost ₹10 a few years ago now costs ₹20, then ₹10 saved has lost half its buying power, even though the coin is unchanged. So money is a convenient but imperfect store of value. This is why people often keep larger savings in things expected to hold value better, like gold, land, or bank deposits that earn interest. It also explains why central banks like the RBI try hard to keep inflation low and steady: stable prices keep money trustworthy both as a store of value and as a standard for deferred payments like loans.

A coin is a tiny history book [Class 10]

The chapter shows ancient coins with their obverse and reverse designs — and to a historian each old coin is a packed little document. The branch of study is called numismatics. From a single coin a scholar can read a surprising amount: the ruler's name or portrait tells us who was in power; the script and language fix roughly when and where it was made; the symbols (a bull, a goddess, a bow) reveal which gods and ideas a kingdom honoured; and the metal — gold, silver or copper — and its purity hint at how rich or troubled the economy was, since rulers in hard times often mixed cheaper metal into 'gold' coins. Best of all, where coins are found maps how far a kingdom's trade and power reached — Roman coins in south India, for instance, prove sea trade with Rome. So the earliest purpose of coins was to make buying easy, but a happy side effect is that they quietly recorded history for us to read two thousand years later.

What really happens when you scan a QR code [Class 9]

When you pay by scanning a shop's QR code on a UPI app, no note or coin moves at all — yet money truly changes hands. Behind the scan, your app reads the shop's unique payment address, you approve the amount with a secret PIN, and within seconds instructions travel between your bank and the shop's bank so that your account is reduced and theirs is increased by the same amount. The 'money' here is just numbers updated in two banks' records, made trustworthy by the same idea as a banknote: everyone agrees these records count. India's UPI system now handles billions of such payments a month, letting even a tiny street vendor accept money without a card machine. Digital money does not replace what money is — a generally accepted medium of exchange — it only changes how the message is carried, from coins in a hand to data over a network.

You'll want to know first: Knowing that UPI and QR codes are forms of digital money.
