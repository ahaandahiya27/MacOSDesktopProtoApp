Three "What Ifs?" About Wastewater Story

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if all sewage in India was treated before discharge?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Yamuna, Ganga, Krishna, Cauvery — all rivers would become drinkable in their middle stretches. Diarrhoea deaths in children would drop another 60% on top of Swachh Bharat gains. Coastal fisheries would recover (currently devastated by Mumbai/Chennai/Kolkata sewage outflows). Tourism would boom — clean Ganga is worth lakhs of crores in spiritual + tourism value alone. Cost: ~3 lakh crore for nationwide WWTP infrastructure + ongoing operation. ROI: probably 10× over 30 years. This is achievable — Singapore and South Korea have done it. India is decades behind but moving.

What if 2 — What if every Indian household had a biogas digester?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: India produces ~3 lakh crore kg of organic waste yearly (cow dung + food waste + sewage). At realistic conversion, that's ~150 billion kWh of biogas annually — about 15% of India's total energy use. Replaces fuelwood (saving forests), kerosene (reducing imports), and coal (reducing emissions). India already has ~5 million biogas plants (mostly cow dung based); scaling 50× is feasible. Plus: the residual slurry is excellent organic fertiliser. Two birds — energy + fertiliser — with one stone.

What if 3 — What if rivers gave us legal status as 'persons'?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: In 2017, Uttarakhand High Court declared the Ganga and Yamuna LEGAL PERSONS — they have rights, can sue, and have legal guardians (state officials). The idea: a river polluted is a person harmed. The order was stayed by Supreme Court for technicalities. But the legal precedent is being copied (New Zealand's Whanganui River, Colombia's Atrato). If implemented: factories dumping into rivers could be sued for personal injury. Activists could legally fight river pollution. India has not yet finalised the model — but Class 7 students today may see rivers as legal persons in their adult lifetimes.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
