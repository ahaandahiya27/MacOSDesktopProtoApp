From Open Drains to Modern Sanitation — How Cities Changed

London 1854, Mumbai 2024 · Class 7 Science

Before sanitation

For most of human history, cities dumped sewage in the street. Rome had the Cloaca Maxima (a sewer) by 600 BCE but most other cities did not. London in 1850 had open gutters running with raw sewage. Cholera epidemics killed thousands every summer.

John Snow's 1854 breakthrough

Doctor John Snow mapped cholera deaths in Soho, London and traced them to a single water pump on Broad Street. He removed the pump handle and the outbreak stopped. It was the world's first proof that sewage in drinking water spreads disease.

The sewage revolution

By 1870 London had built 1,800 km of brick sewers — designed by Joseph Bazalgette. Within 20 years cholera disappeared from the city. Other capitals copied the design. Sanitation is now one of the biggest reasons for the doubling of human life expectancy since 1900.

India's sanitation journey

India in 2014 still had over 600 million people defecating in the open. The Swachh Bharat Abhiyan built 100+ million toilets in 5 years. Rural open defecation dropped sharply, child diarrhoea deaths fell, and women's safety improved. Work continues on greywater management and sewage treatment.

Public health experts often say that 'the toilet has saved more lives than the doctor'. Sanitation, clean water, and vaccines — in that order — are the top three reasons for falling child mortality worldwide.

Try this at home

Visit your school's washroom. Count: are there enough taps and soap? Is the sewage line working? Sanitation isn't just toilets — it's the full system from tap to sewer to treatment plant.

Looking ahead
