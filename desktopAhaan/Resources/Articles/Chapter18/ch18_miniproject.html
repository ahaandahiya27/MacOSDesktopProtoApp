Two Mini Projects for Chapter 18

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

💩 Build a 3-stage water filter from sand, gravel, and charcoal

~45 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A 2-litre plastic bottle (clean)
  • Sand (fine, washed)
  • Small gravel
  • Activated charcoal (or charcoal pieces from a barbeque, crushed)
  • A coffee filter or piece of cotton cloth
  • Muddy water (or grey water from washing vegetables)

Steps

  1. Cut the bottom off the bottle and invert it (mouth down) into a glass.
  2. Place a coffee filter at the mouth (catches small particles).
  3. Layer (bottom to top): 4 cm gravel → 4 cm sand → 3 cm charcoal → 3 cm fine sand → 2 cm gravel.
  4. Pour your muddy water in slowly from the top.
  5. Watch what comes out the bottom — and what stays in the filter.

👀 What you should see

The water emerging at the bottom is MUCH clearer than what went in. Mud particles caught in sand layers; some smell removed by charcoal. NOT safe to drink — but visibly cleaner. Real WWTPs work on similar principles plus biological + chemical treatment.

Why it works: Each layer does a specific job: GRAVEL catches large debris; SAND traps suspended particles; CHARCOAL adsorbs dissolved organic matter and smells. Same physics WWTPs and home RO filters use, scaled down to a bottle. Builds intuition for what 'filtration' physically means.

♻️ Conduct a household waste audit — what goes into your sewage

~60 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A small notebook + pencil
  • A measuring jug (1 L)
  • Family cooperation

Steps

  1. For one week, track everything that goes down: (a) the kitchen sink, (b) the bathroom sink, (c) the toilet, (d) the laundry drain.
  2. Note daily: any oils poured down? Any solid food bits? Any chemicals (cleaners)? Any plastic accidentally dropped (e.g., wrappers)?
  3. Estimate volumes weekly: cooking oil down sink? Detergent? Bath water? Flush water (~6 L per flush × number of flushes)?
  4. Calculate per-person + total household weekly volume of sewage produced.
  5. Identify TOP 3 things you could change.

👀 What you should see

Typical 4-person Indian household produces ~600-1000 L of sewage daily. ~25% from flushing, ~30% from bathing, ~25% from kitchen, ~20% laundry. Most surprising: how much oil goes down kitchen sinks (often unmeasured). Top changes: collect oil in bottle instead of pouring, dual-flush, bucket-bathe instead of shower, fix leaks, use less detergent.

Why it works: You can only manage what you measure. The audit makes the invisible sewage system tangible. Then the changes — small individual actions × millions of households = saving rivers. Many Indian families have never thought about where their wastewater goes; this project starts that thinking.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
