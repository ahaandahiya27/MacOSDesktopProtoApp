Bacteria as Water Cleaners — Aeration Tanks and Activated Sludge

How a sewage plant turns black water back into clear · Class 7 Science

Why bacteria matter for sewage

Sewage is mostly water mixed with organic waste — food scraps, faeces, soap, detergent. Bacteria love this. In a treatment plant they're given exactly the right conditions: lots of oxygen, lots of food, and lots of space. In hours, they convert messy organic waste into carbon dioxide, water, and more bacteria.

The aeration tank

The heart of any sewage plant is the aeration tank. Compressed air bubbles up through the dirty water, providing oxygen for aerobic bacteria. The bacteria multiply rapidly, eating the dissolved waste. After 4–8 hours, the water is mostly clean.

Activated sludge — the bacterial workforce

The bacteria-rich mass at the bottom of the tank is called activated sludge. Plant operators recycle most of it back into the aeration tank — keeping the bacterial population strong and active. A bit is removed and sent for further dewatering.

After the bacteria — chlorination

Even after biological treatment, some disease-causing microbes survive. The water is chlorinated (or UV-treated) before being released into rivers or used for non-potable purposes.

1 mL of activated sludge contains roughly 1 billion bacteria. A medium-sized city's sewage treatment plant houses several thousand tonnes of these microbes working 24×7. Without them, every Indian river would be black with sewage.

Try this at home

Pour a glass of water from a fishbowl into a clear jar. Bubble air through it with a straw for 5 minutes. The water will smell fresher — oxygen-loving bacteria break down dissolved waste. That's exactly the principle behind sewage aeration.

Looking ahead

Class 12 Biology covers Microbes in Human Welfare — biofertilisers and biological treatment of sewage. NEET asks ~1–2 questions per year on activated sludge. Environmental engineering students design treatment plants and study their kinetics in detail.
