Sources of Wastewater

Domestic, industrial, agricultural — and what each one carries · Class 7 Science

Not all wastewater is the same

The dirty water that needs treatment comes from THREE different sources, and each carries a different pollution mix. Knowing which is which matters because each requires different treatment.

1. Domestic wastewater (from homes)

This is the wastewater you generate at home every day. About 200 litres per person per day in urban India.

Split into two sub-types:

  • Greywater — water from bathing, washing, dishwashing. Relatively clean. Soap, hair, oil, food particles. Can be reused for gardens after light filtering.
  • Blackwater — water from toilets. Contains faeces, urine, toilet paper. Carries dangerous pathogens (cholera, typhoid, dysentery bacteria). MUST be treated before release.

2. Industrial wastewater (from factories)

Vastly more concentrated and more dangerous than domestic. Different industries produce different pollutants:

  • Textile dyeing — coloured water full of dyes and bleaches.
  • Paper mills — high BOD, sulphur compounds, chlorine residues.
  • Tanneries (leather) — chromium salts, sulphides, organic waste from animal hides.
  • Pharmaceutical plants — drug residues, organic solvents, antibiotics.
  • Mining and metallurgy — heavy metals (lead, mercury, cadmium, arsenic), acidic drainage.
  • Petroleum refineries — hydrocarbons, phenols, hot water.
India has strict regulations on industrial effluent — every factory must treat its wastewater BEFORE releasing it. The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) monitors and fines violators. The Ganga (Namami Gange) and Yamuna (Yamuna Action Plan) cleanup missions specifically target untreated industrial effluent.

3. Agricultural runoff (from farms)

When it rains on farmland, water washes off carrying:

  • Pesticides — most modern pesticides are toxic to aquatic life even at parts-per-billion levels.
  • Fertiliser runoff — nitrate and phosphate fertilisers. Cause eutrophication in lakes (algae bloom → oxygen drops → fish die).
  • Animal waste from livestock — high BOD, pathogens.
  • Eroded soil — clogs rivers, smothers fish eggs.

Agricultural runoff is HARDER to control than industrial waste because it doesn't come from a single pipe — it spreads diffusely from millions of fields.

The combined challenge

Most rivers receive all three at once. The Yamuna in Delhi, for example, gets:

  • Domestic sewage from 20+ million people.
  • Industrial effluent from thousands of factories.
  • Agricultural runoff from upstream Haryana.

By the time it leaves Delhi, the Yamuna's BOD is 30-50× the safe limit. It's biologically dead — no fish can survive in it.

What proper treatment looks like

  • Domestic wastewater → secondary biological treatment (the bacterial aeration tank you saw in Topic 2).
  • Industrial wastewater → primary chemical pretreatment (neutralise pH, precipitate heavy metals) + secondary biological + tertiary advanced (UV, ozone, membrane filtration).
  • Agricultural runoff → wetland filtration (use natural plants to absorb nitrates and phosphates) + soil conservation (terracing, cover crops, reduced pesticide use).

Try this at home

Walk around your colony with paper and a pencil. Identify the wastewater sources visible in 10 minutes: kitchen sinks, bathroom drains, washing machine outlets (domestic). Factories, workshops, repair garages with oil staining (industrial). Gardens, lawns, agricultural plots (with runoff after rain). Most urban neighbourhoods have all three types within walking distance.

Looking ahead

Class 12 Biology 'Environmental Issues' covers water-pollution sources in detail — including specific case studies of Ganga and Yamuna cleanup. Quantitative measures: BOD (Biological Oxygen Demand) and COD (Chemical Oxygen Demand), permissible limits in different water-use categories (drinking, bathing, irrigation, industrial). Class 12 Chemistry 'Environmental Chemistry' covers biomagnification (DDT in food chain, mercury in fish), eutrophication, and oxygen-depletion zones. NEET asks BOD-curve questions every year.
