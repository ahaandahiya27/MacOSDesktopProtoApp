Inside a Sewage Treatment Plant

How dirty water becomes safe · Class 7 Science

A factory that runs on bacteria

A modern wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) doesn't filter sewage with chemicals or expensive technology. The heart of the process is a billion microscopic bacteria that EAT the organic pollutants and turn them back into water, CO₂, and harmless sludge. Engineering's job is to keep those bacteria happy.

The five stages, in order

1. Bar screen (mechanical)

Vertical metal bars catch large solids — rags, plastic bags, twigs, the occasional toy. These are removed and sent to landfill. Without this step, pumps further down the line would clog within hours.

2. Grit chamber (settling)

Sewage flows slowly through a long tank. Heavy sand, gravel, and grit settle to the bottom by gravity. They're scraped out and trucked to construction sites. This protects the next-stage equipment from being abraded by sand particles.

3. Primary clarifier (more settling)

A larger, calmer tank. The remaining suspended solids (organic matter, fine particles) sink to the bottom as primary sludge. About 30% of the original pollution is removed at this point — by gravity alone, no chemicals or biology yet.

4. Aeration tank (biology takes over)

This is where the magic happens. Air is bubbled vigorously through the sewage. Aerobic bacteria, which were already floating in the water, multiply explosively in the oxygen-rich environment and EAT the dissolved organic pollutants. The bacterial population doubles every 20–30 minutes.

This single tank reduces pollution by another 60–70%. The bacteria become so dense that the sewage now looks like brownish soup — that's the bacterial biomass at work.

5. Secondary clarifier (final separation)

The aerated sewage flows into another calm tank. The bacterial mass settles as secondary sludge. The clear water on top — now 95% cleaner than the raw sewage — flows out, gets chlorinated to kill any remaining pathogens, and returns to rivers or the sea.

The sludge isn't waste. It goes into anaerobic digesters where different bacteria break it down further, producing methane (biogas) that powers the plant itself. Many WWTPs are energy-positive — they generate more power than they use.

Two key numbers you'll meet in Class 12

  • BOD (Biological Oxygen Demand) — how much oxygen the bacteria need to digest the organics in 1 L over 5 days. Drinking water: BOD < 1 mg/L. Heavily polluted river: BOD > 20 mg/L.
  • COD (Chemical Oxygen Demand) — total oxygen needed to chemically oxidise ALL the organic matter (including parts bacteria can't eat). Always higher than BOD.

What WWTPs CAN'T remove

  • Heavy metals (mercury, lead, cadmium) — require separate chemical treatment.
  • Some pharmaceutical residues — pass through and contaminate downstream waters.
  • Hormone-disrupting chemicals — an active research area.
  • Microplastics — too small to filter, but trapped in sludge.

Try this at home

Build a primary-treatment filter: cut a 1-litre bottle in half, invert the top into the bottom. Layer: cotton at the very top, then sand, then fine gravel, then coarse gravel. Pour muddy water from above. Clear water drips out the bottom — much cleaner, but not yet drinkable (no bacterial step here). You've demonstrated stages 1–3 of a WWTP.
