What Is Sewage?

The journey of every drain in your home · Class 7 Science

The word for the dirty stuff

Anything that flows down your drains — from the kitchen sink, the bathroom basin, the toilet, the washing machine — is called wastewater or sewage. Don't think of it as just dirty water. Sewage is actually 99.9% water and only 0.1% solids, dissolved chemicals, and microbes — but that tiny 0.1% is what makes it dangerous if not treated.

What's in sewage

Wastewater contaminants split into three categories:

  • Organic matter — vegetable peels, food scraps, soap residue, hair, paper, faeces. Made of carbon-based molecules. Bacteria love eating these.
  • Inorganic matter — sand, detergent chemicals, salts, metal traces, plastic bits. Doesn't get eaten by bacteria; has to be filtered out physically.
  • Pathogens — disease-causing microbes. Cholera (Vibrio cholerae), typhoid (Salmonella typhi), dysentery (Entamoeba histolytica), hepatitis A virus. These are the real danger.
An average Indian household generates around 200 litres of sewage per day. Multiply by a billion households and you get a sense of the scale. Treating that water before it returns to rivers is one of the biggest engineering challenges of modern civilisation.

The flow path: from your sink to the sea

  1. House drain. The pipe under your sink leads down through the wall to a vertical waste stack.
  2. Building sewer. The waste stack connects to a horizontal pipe under your house's foundation.
  3. Lateral sewer line. Joins the main municipal sewer running under your street.
  4. Main trunk sewer. Carries the combined waste of an entire neighbourhood, kilometres long.
  5. Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP). Where the sewage is cleaned in multiple stages.
  6. Receiving water. A river, lake, or ocean. By the time the water gets here it should be clean enough not to harm aquatic life.

Why this matters

Cholera outbreaks killed millions in 19th-century London until the city built a proper sewer system in the 1860s. India's Swachh Bharat Mission (launched 2014) is a similar national-scale push — over 100 million toilets built, drastically cutting open defecation and the water-borne disease that comes with it. But less than half of India's sewage actually reaches a treatment plant — the rest still flows into rivers untreated, which is why the Ganga has had so many cleanup missions.

Try this at home

Open the cabinet under your kitchen sink. Follow the pipe out of the wall as far as you can. It goes through the floor, into the wall, joins the main sewer at your street, and from there travels kilometres to your city's treatment plant. The water you use to wash a single plate has a longer journey than you do most days.

Looking ahead

Class 12 Biology 'Environmental Issues' covers wastewater in detail — the three treatment stages (primary, secondary, tertiary), plus quantitative pollution measures:

  • BOD (Biological Oxygen Demand): how much oxygen microbes need to digest the organic matter in 1 L of water over 5 days. High BOD = high organic pollution. Clean rivers: BOD < 3 mg/L. Heavily polluted: BOD > 20 mg/L.
  • COD (Chemical Oxygen Demand): how much oxygen would be needed to chemically oxidise all the organic matter. Includes the parts microbes can't eat.

NEET asks BOD-curve questions every year. Class 12 'Microbes in Human Welfare' covers the helpful bacteria used in aeration tanks. Class 12 Civics covers the legal framework — the Water Act 1974, the National Green Tribunal, and the Polluter Pays principle.
