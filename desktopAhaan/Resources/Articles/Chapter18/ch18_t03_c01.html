Compost Pits and Soak Pits

Two low-cost sanitation upgrades for any home · Class 7 Science

Not every home can afford a sewer line

In urban India, sewers reach about 30% of households. For the other 70% — in rural areas, peri-urban slums, small towns — sanitation has to work without industrial treatment plants. Two cheap, decentralised solutions: compost pits for solid organic waste, and soak pits for liquid grey water.

Compost pit basics

A compost pit is just a hole in the ground (or a simple bin) where you put kitchen and garden organic waste — peels, leaves, weeds, food scraps. Inside, the same decomposers that work in a forest floor (fungi, bacteria, worms) turn the waste into dark, crumbly humus that you can spread on plants.

What you need

  • Roughly a 1m × 1m × 1m pit dug in a garden corner — or a sealable bin with air holes.
  • Daily kitchen waste: vegetable peels, fruit scraps, tea leaves, eggshells.
  • 'Browns': dry leaves, cardboard, sawdust, straw — about equal volume to the greens.
  • A pinch of garden soil to kickstart the bacterial community.
  • Occasional moisture (sprinkle of water) and weekly stirring.

In 6–8 weeks: dark, crumbly compost that smells like fresh earth, not garbage. Spread it on potted plants or in the garden.

About half of a typical Indian household's daily waste is organic. Composting at home halves what gets sent to landfills — and gives you free fertiliser. India generates over 60 million tonnes of garbage a year; widespread home composting could divert 30 million tonnes away from overflowing landfill sites.

What NOT to put in a compost pit

  • Meat, fish, dairy — attract rats and dogs, smell bad.
  • Cooking oil — coats everything and slows decomposition.
  • Plastic, glass, metal — don't decompose; remove before composting.
  • Diseased plant material — pathogens can survive composting and infect new plants.

Soak pit basics

A soak pit handles GREY water — the relatively clean liquid waste from bathing, dishwashing, and washing clothes (not toilet sewage). The grey water seeps through layers of gravel and sand into the surrounding soil, where natural biological filtration cleans it before it reaches groundwater.

How to build one

  1. Dig a pit ~2 metres deep and 1 metre across.
  2. Fill the bottom half with coarse gravel.
  3. Then a layer of medium gravel.
  4. Then sand at the top.
  5. Cover with a perforated lid (concrete slab with holes).
  6. Pipe in the grey water from your kitchen and bathroom drains.

The grey water trickles down through the layers. Bacteria living in the gravel digest the organic matter. By the time the water seeps into the surrounding soil, it's mostly clean.

Critical rule

Soak pits must be at least 15 metres from any drinking-water well. Closer than that, contaminants might reach the well before the soil has fully filtered them.

What these can replace

A home with a properly-sized compost pit + soak pit + a basic septic tank for toilet waste can be fully sanitation-self-sufficient — no need for a centralised sewer connection. India's Swachh Bharat Mission (2014–2019) used this exact combination to build over 100 million toilets in rural areas.

Try this at home

For composting: get a clean small bucket with a lid. Layer 5 cm dry brown leaves at the bottom, then 5 cm fresh kitchen peels, sprinkle of garden soil. Repeat. Stir weekly. In 6–8 weeks the smell goes away and you have dark, crumbly compost — free fertiliser for plants.

For soak pit: build a small demonstration with a 1-litre bottle: 10 cm coarse gravel at bottom, 5 cm fine sand, 5 cm garden soil on top. Pour dirty greywater (used dishwashing water) into the top. Water seeps out the bottom much cleaner.
