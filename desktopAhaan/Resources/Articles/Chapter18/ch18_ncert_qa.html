Q1. Fill in the blanks. (a) Cleaning of water is a process of removing pollutants before it enters a water body or is reused. This process of wastewater treatment is commonly known as ______. (b) Wastewater released by houses is called ______. (c) Dried ______ after treatment is used as manure. (d) Drains get blocked by ______ and ______.

Model answer: (a) sewage treatment (b) sewage (c) sludge (d) cooking oil/fats and other solid waste (plastic, hair, sanitary napkins, etc.).

Q2. What is sewage? Explain why it is harmful to discharge untreated sewage into rivers or seas.

Model answer: Sewage is wastewater from homes (kitchen, bathroom, toilet, laundry), offices, factories, and hospitals. It contains: harmful bacteria and viruses (E. coli, cholera, typhoid pathogens); organic waste (food, faeces); chemicals (detergents, oil, heavy metals); plastics. Discharging untreated sewage into rivers/seas: (1) Spreads diseases (cholera, dysentery, hepatitis A) when people drink/bathe in that water. (2) Kills aquatic life by depleting dissolved oxygen (bacteria consume O₂ digesting waste). (3) Contaminates groundwater used for drinking. (4) Algal blooms from nutrients destroy fish habitats. (5) Coastal pollution kills coral reefs and fisheries.

Q3. Why should oils and fats be not released in the drain? Explain.

Model answer: Oils and fats cool inside pipes and turn solid, sticking to pipe walls. Over time, layer upon layer narrows the pipe diameter — eventually blocking it entirely. They also: (1) Trap other waste (food bits, hair) forming massive 'fatbergs'. (2) Don't biodegrade easily — bacteria in WWTPs struggle to digest them. (3) Coat fish gills in rivers, suffocating them. (4) Form a thin film on water surface that blocks oxygen exchange. Correct disposal: cooled oil → bottle → dustbin or oil-recycling collection where available.

Q4. Describe the steps involved in getting clear water from wastewater.

Model answer: Step 1 — SCREENING: wastewater passes through metal bars to remove large items (rags, sticks, plastic). Step 2 — GRIT REMOVAL: water flows slowly through chambers; sand and small stones settle. Step 3 — SETTLING (primary clarifier): water sits in large tanks; solid matter settles to bottom as primary SLUDGE. Step 4 — AERATION: air is pumped through the water; bacteria multiply and digest dissolved organic matter. Step 5 — SECONDARY CLARIFIER: bacteria-laden particles settle as secondary sludge. Step 6 — DISINFECTION: chlorine or UV light kills remaining pathogens. Step 7 — DISCHARGE: treated water now safe to release into river. Step 8 — SLUDGE DIGESTION: collected sludge is treated separately (anaerobic, producing biogas) and dried for manure.

Q5. What is sludge? Explain how it is treated.

Model answer: Sludge is the SETTLED SOLID matter collected at the bottom of clarifiers in a WWTP — faecal matter, food bits, and bacterial bodies. Treatment: (1) Sludge is pumped into a closed digester tank. (2) Anaerobic bacteria (working without oxygen) digest organic matter for ~30 days. (3) During digestion, methane (biogas) is released — collected and used as fuel for the WWTP itself or sold. (4) Digested sludge is removed and DRIED in open beds. (5) Dried sludge is rich in nitrogen and phosphorus — used as MANURE in agriculture, or as soil conditioner. Two valuable byproducts from waste: energy + fertiliser.

Q6. Untreated human excreta is a health hazard. Explain.

Model answer: Human excreta contains: (1) Pathogenic bacteria — E. coli, salmonella, cholera vibrio. (2) Viruses — hepatitis A, polio. (3) Worms — roundworms, hookworms, tapeworms. (4) Protozoa — Giardia, Entamoeba. When excreta contaminates drinking water (via groundwater seepage or surface runoff), diseases spread fast. Children are most vulnerable — diarrhoea kills ~150,000 Indian children annually (mostly from poor sanitation). The 2009 Bihar cholera outbreak and the 2017 Delhi dengue surge had similar root causes. Proper toilets + functioning sewage systems = saving lives, not just convenience.

Q7. Name two chemicals used to disinfect water.

Model answer: (1) CHLORINE — used in most municipal water plants. Cheap, effective, leaves trace amounts that kill pathogens in distribution pipes too. Smell of pool water is chlorine. (2) OZONE (O₃) — stronger oxidiser, no residual taste, used in some advanced plants. (3) UV LIGHT — not a chemical but a disinfection method, used by some Indian plants. Also: bleaching powder (calcium hypochlorite — releases chlorine in water), iodine tablets (for emergency drinking water), KMnO₄ (potassium permanganate — for well disinfection in villages).

Q8. Explain the relationship between sanitation and disease.

Model answer: Direct: where sanitation is poor, sewage contaminates drinking water → people drink/cook with pathogen-laden water → diseases like cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A spread. Indirect: poor sanitation means more flies and mosquitoes (which breed in stagnant sewage) → spread additional diseases (typhoid, dengue, malaria). Childhood diarrhoea (mostly preventable with sanitation) is the biggest killer of children under 5 in poor countries. The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme has shown: every $1 spent on sanitation generates $5-15 in health benefits. India's massive sanitation push (Swachh Bharat, Jal Jeevan) is one of the highest-ROI public health investments in modern history.
