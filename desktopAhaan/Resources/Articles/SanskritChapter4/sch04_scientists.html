Minds Behind न लभ्यते चेत् आम्लं द्राक्षाफलम्

Sanskrit's living tradition was built by real people. Here is one of them — whose work the chapter quietly stands on.

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Every Sanskrit chapter you read is the visible tip of a much longer history. Behind each verse, each word-table, each story is at least one person who made the next step possible. This article names one of them.

Viṣṇu Śarmā (around 3rd–5th century CE (traditional dates uncertain))

Indian. The traditional author of the Pañcatantra — India's most-translated book of animal fables.

Tradition says Viṣṇu Śarmā was a learned brahmin asked by a king to educate his three rather slow-witted princes. Within six months, the story goes, he taught them everything a king needs to know — through animal fables. The result was the Pañcatantra ('five treatises'), a collection of stories in five books, told by animals, that became one of the most-translated books in world history. The Pañcatantra was translated into Pahlavi in the 6th century, into Arabic as Kalīla wa Dimna in the 8th century, then into Hebrew, Spanish, German, English — over 200 translations in 50+ languages. The Sanskrit Fox and the Grapes shares ancestry with this great tradition.

💡 What they gave us

A book of children's stories has shaped the moral imagination of more cultures than almost any other Indian work. Story is the longest-travelling medium.

📝 Stretch your thinking

The figures behind Sanskrit's tradition rarely worked alone — Pāṇini built on Yāska, Patañjali built on Pāṇini, Śaṅkara built on the Upaniṣads, Bhartṛhari built on centuries of prior verse. Every great mind sits on top of another. Whose 'shoulders' would you most want to stand on?
