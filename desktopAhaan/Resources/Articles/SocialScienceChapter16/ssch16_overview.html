The Turkic Invasions Begin: The Ghaznavids

The 11th and 12th centuries were a turning point: Turkic invaders penetrated far deeper than earlier Arab raids. The Hindu Shahis guarded the Khyber Pass - India's great north-western gateway - until the Ghaznavids overran them. Mahmud of Ghazni then led 17 plundering campaigns, destroying rich temples like Somnath, but he only raided and withdrew, never settling to rule India.

  1. A Turning Point in Indian HistoryThe 11th and 12th centuries were a turning point: Turkic invasions from the north-west penetrated far deeper than the earlier Arab raids, yet Indian kingdoms also flourished and resisted.
  2. The Hindu Shahis and the Khyber PassThe Hindu Shahis guarded the Khyber Pass - the great gateway from Central Asia into India - resisting invaders until the Ghaznavids overran them in the early 11th century.
  3. Mahmud of Ghazni and His RaidsMahmud of Ghazni, the first great Turkic invader, led 17 plundering campaigns into north India (1000s-1020s), destroying rich temples like Somnath but never settling to rule.

Scholars Amid the Storms

Even amid invasions, learning thrived. Al-Biruni, a Persian polymath who came with Mahmud around 1017, learned Sanskrit and wrote an encyclopaedic survey of Indian knowledge - while honestly recording how Mahmud's raids drove Indian science to refuges like Kashmir. And Bhaskaracharya (born 1114), working in the Deccan, produced mathematical masterpieces like the Lilavati, proving Indian science kept flourishing far from the invaded north.

  1. Al-Biruni, the Scholar Who Studied IndiaAl-Biruni, a Persian polymath who came with Mahmud around 1017, learned Sanskrit and wrote an encyclopaedic survey of Indian religion, philosophy and science - while honestly recording the damage Mahmud's raids did.
  2. Bhaskaracharya and the Endurance of Indian ScienceBhaskara II 'Bhaskaracharya' (born 1114) was one of India's greatest mathematicians, whose works Lilavati, Bijaganita and Siddhantashiromani show that learning kept flourishing far from the invaded north.

The Southern and Deccan Powers Flourish

While the north faced invasions, the south and Deccan flourished. The Kakatiyas (Warangal) and Hoysalas (Belur, Halebidu) built kingdoms famous for self-governance and breathtaking temples. The Cholas reached their peak as masters of the seas: Rajaraja I (985-1014) conquered the Maldives and northern Sri Lanka and built the Brihadishvara temple, while his son Rajendra I earned the title 'Gangaikondachola' and sent a navy across the Bay of Bengal to defeat the Srivijaya Empire.

  1. The Kakatiyas and the HoysalasWhile the north faced invasions, the Kakatiyas (Warangal) and Hoysalas (Belur, Halebidu) built flourishing Deccan kingdoms famous for self-governance, irrigation and breathtaking temple architecture.
  2. Rajaraja Chola I, Master of the SeasRajaraja Chola I (985-1014) made the Cholas a great sea power, conquering northern Sri Lanka and the Maldives and building the magnificent Brihadishvara temple at Thanjavur.
  3. Rajendra Chola I and the Naval Expedition to SrivijayaRajendra Chola I extended the empire to the Ganga (earning the title 'Gangaikondachola') and sent a famous navy across the sea to defeat the Srivijaya Empire over control of the Strait of Malacca.

The Northern Kingdoms and the Ghurid Turning Point

In the north, the Paramaras of Malwa produced the scholar-king Bhoja (from 1010), famous for learning and public works like the Bhojtal lake. Then came the true turning point: the Ghurids under Muhammad Ghuri, who - after an early defeat by the Chaulukyas - beat Prithviraj Chauhan at the Second Battle of Tarain (1192). Unlike Mahmud, Ghuri sought to rule; his generals Qutb-ud-din Aibak (founder of the Delhi Sultanate) and Bakhtiyar Khilji (destroyer of Nalanda and Vikramashila) turned conquest into lasting foreign rule.

  1. The Paramaras and the Scholar-King BhojaThe Paramaras of Malwa, especially the scholar-king Bhoja (ruled from 1010), combined good governance and great public works (like the Bhojtal lake) with deep learning, authoring treatises on architecture, poetics and more.
  2. The Ghurids and the Battles of TarainThe Ghurids under Muhammad Ghuri seized power from the Ghaznavids; after an early defeat, Ghuri beat Prithviraj Chauhan at the Second Battle of Tarain (1192), unlike Mahmud, aiming to conquer and rule.
  3. From Plunder to Rule: Aibak and Bakhtiyar KhiljiMuhammad Ghuri's generals - Qutb-ud-din Aibak (who founded the Delhi Sultanate) and Bakhtiyar Khilji (who destroyed Nalanda and Vikramashila) - turned conquest into lasting foreign rule in north India.

Faith, Art and the Lesson of Resilience

Amid the invasions, faith and art flourished. Thinker-saints reshaped Indian religion: Ramanujacharya taught a philosophy of devotion (vishishtadvaita), and Basavanna founded the Lingayat movement rejecting caste for equality. Magnificent temples rose at home (Khajuraho) and India's culture reached abroad, seen in Cambodia's Angkor Wat. Taking stock, the period brought profound change (Turkic conquest, foreign rule, Islam as a new creed) yet deep continuity (a free south, surviving trade, thriving learning) - its lesson is resilience.

  1. New Thinkers: Ramanujacharya and BasavannaTwo great thinker-saints reshaped Indian faith: Ramanujacharya developed a bhakti philosophy (vishishtadvaita) of devotion and surrender, while Basavanna founded the Lingayat movement, rejecting caste and ritual for equality and devotion.
  2. Temples at Home and India's Reach AbroadMagnificent temples rose across India (like Khajuraho's by the Chandellas), and India's religious and artistic traditions reached far abroad - seen in Cambodia's Angkor Wat, the world's largest religious monument.
  3. Taking Stock: Change and ContinuityThe period brought profound change (Turkic conquest, the start of foreign rule, Islam as a new creed) but also deep continuity (free south, surviving trade, thriving art and learning) - its lesson is resilience.
