The Pole Star (Dhruv Tara) and finding north

The Pole Star (Polaris/Dhruv Tara) indicates true north and can be located using the pointer stars of Ursa Major (Saptarishi).

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine you're lost in a forest at night with no phone and no compass. Look up! Find the Saptarishi — seven bright stars shaped like a big ladle. Now look at the two stars that form the outer edge of the ladle's bowl. Draw an imaginary line through them and extend it about 5 times the distance between them. You'll hit a star that barely moves all night while everything else wheels around it. That's Dhruv Tara — the Pole Star — and it always points NORTH. Face it, and east is on your right, west on your left, and south behind you. Sailors, soldiers, and travellers have used this trick for thousands of years!

What this concept means

Polaris, also known as Dhruv Tara (the Pole Star), is located very close to the north celestial pole and therefore appears almost stationary in the sky. To locate it, identify the constellation Ursa Major (Saptarishi). The two stars forming the outer edge of the 'bowl' (Dubhe and Merak) are called 'pointer stars'. An imaginary line drawn through them and extended about five times the distance between them points to Polaris. Since Polaris indicates true north, it has been used for navigation for centuries. It is visible only from the Northern Hemisphere.

Why it works this way

Polaris appears fixed because it's almost exactly aligned with Earth's rotational axis extended into space. All other stars appear to rotate around this point. The pointer-star method works because Dubhe and Merak are geometrically positioned so that a line through them extended northward reliably points toward the celestial pole.

Beyond the book

Here's a fun challenge: on a clear night, use the Saptarishi pointer method to find Dhruv Tara. Then use a compass app on your phone to verify — Polaris should be within 1 degree of true north. Ancient Indian texts refer to Dhruva as a prince so devoted in meditation that Lord Vishnu granted him an eternal, unmoving place in the sky. In Vastu Shastra (Indian architectural science), the north direction is considered auspicious partly because of Dhruv Tara's fixed, guiding presence. Try photographing the north sky with a 2-minute exposure — you'll see star trails arcing around a central still point: Polaris!

Real-world applications

  1. Night navigation without GPS — If your phone dies while trekking in the Himalayas, finding Dhruv Tara gives you reliable north — it hasn't moved for thousands of years.
  2. Scout and NCC training — Indian Scouts and NCC cadets learn to find Polaris using Saptarishi — it's a core wilderness navigation skill.
  3. Ancient Polynesian navigators — Pacific islanders navigated thousands of kilometres of open ocean using star positions — Polaris guided Northern Hemisphere voyagers.
  4. Latitude from Polaris — Polaris's angle above the horizon equals your latitude — from Delhi (~28 degrees N), it's ~28 degrees above the northern horizon.
  5. Star trail photography — In long-exposure photos, all star trails are circles centred on Polaris — a beautiful proof of Earth's rotation.

Expert depth

Polaris (Alpha Ursae Minoris) is actually a triple star system. The primary, Polaris Aa, is a classical Cepheid variable with a pulsation period of ~3.97 days and an amplitude of ~0.03 magnitudes. It lies 0.66 degrees from the true celestial pole (as of 2025) and will be closest to the pole around 2105. Due to precession of the equinoxes (period ~25,772 years), the celestial pole traces a circle among the stars. Around 3000 BCE, Thuban (Alpha Draconis) was the pole star — the Great Pyramid of Giza's descending passage was aligned to Thuban. In ~12,000 years, Vega will assume the role.
