The Moon — Earth's natural satellite

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite, orbiting at an average distance of 384,400 km and held in place by gravity.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

If Earth were a football, the Moon would be a tennis ball circling about 7 metres away. It's our closest neighbour in space — and the only other world humans have ever walked on. The Moon has no air, no water, no weather — just grey rocky plains, mountains, and craters from billions of years of asteroid impacts. It's about one-quarter the size of Earth, and its gravity is only one-sixth of ours. If you weigh 60 kg on Earth, you'd weigh just 10 kg on the Moon — you could jump six times higher!

What this concept means

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite, with a diameter of 3,474 km (about one-quarter of Earth's). It orbits Earth at an average distance of 384,400 km. The Moon has no atmosphere, no liquid water, and a surface covered with craters, maria (dark basaltic plains), and highlands. Its surface gravity is about 1/6th of Earth's (1.62 m/s squared compared to 9.8 m/s squared). The Moon does not produce its own light — it shines by reflecting sunlight. The Moon's gravitational influence causes tides on Earth.

Why it works this way

The Moon is bound to Earth by gravitational attraction. It formed from the same collision that tilted Earth's axis, and the two bodies have been locked in a gravitational dance for 4.5 billion years. The Moon reflects sunlight rather than producing its own, which is why its appearance changes as its position relative to the Sun changes.

Beyond the book

India's Chandrayaan-1 (2008) made a groundbreaking discovery: water molecules on the Moon's surface, particularly in permanently shadowed craters near the poles. This changed our entire understanding of the Moon from a bone-dry world to one with potential water resources for future bases. Chandrayaan-3's Pragyan rover (2023) confirmed sulphur and other elements in lunar south pole soil. ISRO is now planning Chandrayaan-4, a sample-return mission. Try looking at the Moon through binoculars — you'll see the dark maria (ancient lava plains) and bright crater rims with stunning clarity.

Real-world applications

  1. Chandrayaan-3 landing — India's ISRO successfully landed Chandrayaan-3 near the Moon's south pole on August 23, 2023 — the first country to achieve this.
  2. Moon weight calculator — Multiply your weight by 0.165 to find your Moon weight — a 60 kg student would weigh just 9.9 kg and could dunk a basketball easily.
  3. Moonlight photography — The 'moonlight' illuminating your night walk is actually reflected sunlight — the Moon reflects only 12% of the light hitting it.
  4. Craters visible with binoculars — With simple binoculars, you can see lunar craters — each one records an ancient asteroid impact millions of years ago.
  5. Werewolf myths worldwide — Many cultures have moon-related myths (werewolves, Chandra Dev) because the Moon is the most dramatic object in the night sky.
  6. Apollo 11 footprint still there — Neil Armstrong's 1969 footprint is still on the Moon — with no wind or rain, nothing erases it.

Expert depth

The Moon is believed to have formed ~4.5 billion years ago from debris ejected when a Mars-sized body (Theia) impacted proto-Earth — the Giant Impact Hypothesis. This origin explains the Moon's composition (similar to Earth's mantle), its lack of a large iron core, and its initial close orbit that has been slowly spiralling outward (currently receding at 3.8 cm/year due to tidal interaction). India's Chandrayaan-1 mission (2008) confirmed water ice in permanently shadowed lunar craters near the poles, and Chandrayaan-3 (2023) achieved a soft landing near the lunar south pole — making India the first nation to land at such high latitude.
