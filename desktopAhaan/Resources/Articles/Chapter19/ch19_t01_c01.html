Earth's shape and the tilted axis

Earth is a slightly flattened sphere (oblate spheroid) whose axis is tilted at 23.5 degrees to its orbital plane.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine holding a basketball and tilting it slightly to one side — about the angle your head tilts when you're curious. That's Earth! It isn't a perfect ball; it's a tiny bit squished at the top and bottom, like an orange you pressed gently between your palms. And that tilt — 23.5 degrees — never changes direction as Earth moves around the Sun. This one little lean is responsible for giving us summer holidays, winter sweaters, and the beautiful colours of autumn. Without the tilt, every day on Earth would feel exactly the same.

What this concept means

The Earth is an oblate spheroid — slightly flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator due to its rotation. Its rotational axis is tilted at approximately 23.5 degrees relative to the perpendicular of its orbital plane (the ecliptic). This axial tilt, also called obliquity, remains nearly constant in direction as Earth orbits the Sun. The tilt is the primary cause of seasonal variations on Earth, because it determines how directly sunlight strikes different latitudes at different times of the year.

Why it works this way

Gravity pulls matter into a sphere, but spinning creates a centrifugal bulge at the equator. The tilt exists because of a giant collision during Earth's formation, and it persists due to angular momentum conservation — like a spinning top that keeps leaning the same way.

Beyond the book

Here's a mind-blowing home experiment: at solar noon on the equinox (around March 21 or September 23), plant a straight stick vertically in the ground and measure its shadow. Then do the same on the summer solstice (June 21). The shadow is much shorter in summer — proving the Sun is higher, which proves the tilt. Eratosthenes did exactly this in 240 BCE to calculate Earth's circumference, and got it right to within 2%! Aryabhata independently calculated Earth's circumference as 39,968 km — astonishingly close to the modern value of 40,075 km.

Real-world applications

  1. Globe in geography class — Every classroom globe is mounted tilted at 23.5 degrees — that's not a design choice, it's scientifically accurate.
  2. Satellite photos from ISRO — Images from Chandrayaan and other satellites confirm Earth's slightly squished shape from space.
  3. Summer sun through windows — In summer the Sun is higher in the sky and light pours deeper into your room — because your hemisphere tilts toward the Sun.
  4. Tropic of Cancer through India — The Tropic of Cancer at 23.5 degrees N passes through 8 Indian states — it's literally the tilt line on the map.
  5. Midnight Sun in Norway — Near the Arctic Circle, the Sun doesn't set for weeks in summer — only possible because of the axial tilt.
  6. Uttarayan and Dakshinayan — Indian astronomy divides the year into Uttarayan (Sun moves north) and Dakshinayan (Sun moves south) — both caused by the tilt.
  7. GPS accuracy corrections — GPS satellites must account for Earth's oblate shape; assuming a perfect sphere would cause errors of hundreds of metres.

Expert depth

Earth's oblateness (flattening factor ~1/298.3) results from the centrifugal effect of rotation — the equatorial radius exceeds the polar radius by about 21 km. The axial tilt of 23.44 degrees is believed to have been set by a Mars-sized body (Theia) colliding with proto-Earth ~4.5 billion years ago — the same impact that created the Moon. In Class 11 Geography, you'll learn that the tilt also defines the Tropics of Cancer (23.5 degrees N) and Capricorn (23.5 degrees S) and the Arctic/Antarctic Circles (66.5 degrees N/S). Aryabhata, the 5th-century Indian mathematician, was among the first to propose that Earth rotates on its axis.
