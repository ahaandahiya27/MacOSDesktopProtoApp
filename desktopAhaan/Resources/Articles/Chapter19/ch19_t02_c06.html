Tides — how the Moon pulls on Earth's oceans

Tides are the regular rise and fall of ocean water levels caused mainly by the Moon's gravitational pull on Earth.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine a rubber sheet stretched flat with a marble in the middle (that's Earth's ocean). Now bring a magnet close to one side — the sheet bulges toward the magnet. That's what the Moon's gravity does to our oceans! The water on the side nearest the Moon gets pulled toward it, creating a bulge (high tide). Surprisingly, there's also a bulge on the opposite side because the Moon pulls Earth's solid body more than the distant water. As Earth rotates, coastlines pass through these two bulges, giving most places TWO high tides and TWO low tides every day.

What this concept means

Tides are periodic rises and falls in sea level caused primarily by the gravitational attraction of the Moon (and to a lesser extent, the Sun) on Earth's oceans. The Moon's gravity creates two tidal bulges: one on the side of Earth nearest the Moon, and one on the opposite side. As Earth rotates, any given coastal point passes through both bulges, resulting in approximately two high tides and two low tides every 24 hours and 50 minutes. The 50-minute daily delay corresponds to the Moon's orbital motion. Spring tides (extra high) occur during Full and New Moon; neap tides (moderate) occur at Quarter Moons.

Why it works this way

Gravity weakens with distance. The Moon pulls the nearest ocean more strongly than Earth's centre, and Earth's centre more strongly than the far ocean. This gradient (tidal force) stretches the ocean into an ellipsoidal shape with two bulges. Earth's rotation carries coastlines through both bulges daily, creating the tidal rhythm.

Beyond the book

The Moon is slowly stealing Earth's rotational energy through tidal friction — each century, the day gets 2.3 milliseconds longer. That energy goes into pushing the Moon further away (3.8 cm/year). Laser retroreflectors left on the Moon by Apollo astronauts let us measure this drift with millimetre precision. In the distant past, the Moon was much closer (and tides were enormous!), and days were much shorter. 620 million years ago, a day was only about 21 hours long and there were ~400 days in a year.

Real-world applications

  1. Gujarat's extreme tides — The Gulf of Khambhat in Gujarat has tides exceeding 10 metres — so dramatic that India is exploring tidal energy generation there.
  2. Fishing with the tides — Indian coastal fishermen plan their trips around tides — fish are more active and accessible during tidal changes.
  3. Mumbai's Marine Drive at high tide — During monsoon spring tides, waves crash over Marine Drive's sea wall — a vivid demonstration of tidal forces plus wind.
  4. Tidal energy plants — Tidal power plants use the rise and fall of tides to generate electricity — the tidal barrage at La Rance, France, has produced power since 1966.
  5. Ships entering harbours — Large cargo ships at Mumbai and Chennai ports time their entry to high tide, when water depth is greatest in the harbour channel.
  6. Chandrabhaga beach Konark — At Konark's Chandrabhaga beach, the tidal range creates a wide flat beach at low tide that nearly disappears at high tide.

Expert depth

The tidal force is the differential gravitational force — the difference between the Moon's gravitational pull at the nearest point, the centre, and the farthest point of Earth. It falls off as the cube of the distance (1/r cubed), not the square, which is why the Moon (closer but less massive) produces larger tides than the Sun (factor ~2.2x stronger). When the Sun and Moon align (syzygy — Full/New Moon), their tidal forces add, creating spring tides ~20% above average. At quadrature (Quarter Moons), they partially cancel, creating neap tides ~20% below average. India's Gulf of Khambhat (Gujarat) has some of the highest tides in Asia, reaching 10+ metres.
