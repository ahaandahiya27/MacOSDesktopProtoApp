Spring tides and neap tides

Spring tides (extra high) occur when Sun, Moon, and Earth align at Full and New Moon; neap tides (moderate) occur at Quarter Moons when Sun and Moon pull at right angles.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine two friends pulling you from the same side — you move a lot. That's a spring tide! During Full Moon and New Moon, the Sun and Moon line up and pull the oceans together. Now imagine two friends pulling you from different sides at a right angle — you barely move. That's a neap tide! During Half Moons (Quarters), the Sun and Moon are at 90 degrees, and their pulls partly cancel out. Spring tides happen twice a month and are about 20% higher than average. The word 'spring' doesn't mean the season — it comes from an old word meaning 'to leap up'.

What this concept means

Spring tides occur during Full Moon and New Moon when the Sun, Moon, and Earth are approximately aligned (syzygy). The gravitational forces of the Sun and Moon reinforce each other, producing tides of greater-than-average range. Neap tides occur during the First Quarter and Third Quarter phases when the Sun and Moon are at right angles relative to Earth. Their gravitational forces partially counteract each other, producing tides of smaller-than-average range. Spring tides and neap tides alternate approximately every 7 days, corresponding to the progression of lunar phases.

Why it works this way

Tides result from the combined gravitational pull of Moon and Sun. When both pull along the same line (Full and New Moon), their effects add. When they pull at 90 degrees (Quarter Moons), the resultant is less than either alone. Coastal geography then amplifies or dampens this forcing.

Beyond the book

The highest tides on Earth occur at the Bay of Fundy in Canada — up to 16 metres (a five-storey building!) between low and high tide. This happens because the bay's funnel shape and natural resonance period (~12.5 hours) nearly matches the tidal cycle, amplifying the spring tide enormously. In India, the Gulf of Khambhat (Gujarat) sees spring tides of 10+ metres for similar resonance reasons. Future tidal power projects in Gujarat aim to harness this energy — imagine powering cities with the Moon's gravity!

Real-world applications

  1. Full Moon fishing in Kerala — Kerala's fishermen know spring tides (Full/New Moon) bring stronger currents and more fish activity — they plan big catches accordingly.
  2. Coastal flooding risk — Extreme spring tides combined with storm surges during cyclones can cause devastating coastal flooding — disaster planners watch the lunar calendar.
  3. Low tide shell collecting — Neap tide low points expose more seabed — the best time to collect shells and explore tide pools at Goa's beaches.
  4. Bridge construction timing — Engineers building sea bridges (like the upcoming Mumbai Trans Harbour Link) schedule foundation work during neap tides when tidal range is smallest.
  5. Rann of Kutch flooding — The Great Rann of Kutch floods seasonally, and spring tides push salt water further inland, affecting the landscape and flamingo habitats.

Expert depth

The Sun's tidal force is about 46% of the Moon's (despite the Sun being far more massive, tidal force scales as M/r cubed, and the Sun's much greater distance reduces its effect). During spring tides, these forces add: total = Moon's + Sun's tidal force. During neap tides, the perpendicular components partially cancel: net force = sqrt(Moon squared + Sun squared - 2*Moon*Sun*cos(90 degrees)), yielding a range roughly 20% below average. The actual tidal range at any coast depends heavily on local geography — funnel-shaped bays amplify tides (Bay of Fundy: 16 m) while open oceans show minimal range (~0.5 m).
