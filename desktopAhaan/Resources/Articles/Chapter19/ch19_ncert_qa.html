Q1. Why do we have day and night on Earth?

Model answer: Earth rotates on its axis (an imaginary line from North Pole to South Pole) once every 24 hours. At any moment, half of Earth faces the Sun (that's daytime) and the other half faces away (nighttime). As Earth keeps rotating eastward, the sunlit side becomes night and the night side becomes day — for every place. Day length varies with latitude and season because of Earth's 23.5° tilt; near equator, days and nights are roughly equal year-round; near poles, days can last months in summer or be absent in winter.

Q2. What causes the change in seasons?

Model answer: Earth's 23.5° AXIAL TILT plus its revolution around the Sun. As Earth orbits, the Northern Hemisphere is sometimes tilted TOWARD Sun (summer there — direct sunlight, long days), sometimes AWAY (winter — slanted sunlight, short days). Southern Hemisphere is opposite at any moment. Equator: roughly equal year-round (slight changes). At poles: extreme — six months continuous daylight in summer, six months darkness in winter. The 23.5° tilt + 365.25-day orbit = the season cycle we all live by.

Q3. Explain solar eclipse and lunar eclipse.

Model answer: SOLAR ECLIPSE: occurs at new moon, when Moon comes between Sun and Earth. Moon's shadow falls on Earth; people in shadow see Sun partially or fully blocked. Total solar eclipse: lasts ~7 minutes max, visible only on a narrow track. LUNAR ECLIPSE: occurs at full moon, when Earth comes between Sun and Moon. Earth's shadow falls on Moon — observers on Earth's night side see Moon darken (often turning reddish — 'blood moon' — because some sunlight refracts through Earth's atmosphere onto the Moon). Total lunar eclipse: lasts ~1.5 hours, visible from anywhere Moon is up. Eclipses are RARE because Moon's orbit is tilted 5° to Earth's orbital plane; alignment doesn't happen every month.

Q4. What is a leap year? Why do we have it?

Model answer: Earth's revolution around the Sun takes 365.25 days, not exactly 365. If we used a 365-day calendar, the seasons would slowly drift — winter would gradually creep into summer dates over centuries. To fix this, we add one extra day (Feb 29) every 4 years — a LEAP YEAR — making that year 366 days. Refinement: years divisible by 100 are NOT leap years, EXCEPT those also divisible by 400. So 1900 wasn't a leap year, but 2000 was. Result: calendar stays aligned with seasons within 1 day per 3300 years.

Q5. Describe the Moon's phases through one month.

Model answer: Start with NEW MOON (Moon between Earth and Sun; sun-side facing away from us; invisible). Days 1-7: thin CRESCENT grows. Day ~7: FIRST QUARTER (half-lit, growing). Days 8-14: GIBBOUS phase (more than half). Day ~14: FULL MOON (Moon opposite Sun in sky; sun-side fully facing us). Days 15-21: WANING GIBBOUS shrinks. Day ~21: LAST QUARTER (half-lit, shrinking). Days 22-29: thin waning crescent. Day 29: back to NEW MOON. Cycle: 29.5 days (synodic month). Indian festivals follow this: Diwali = new moon; Holi = full moon; Karwa Chauth = waning moon. Three quarters of Indian culture is built around moon phases.

Q6. Distinguish between stars and planets.

Model answer: STARS: huge, hot balls of gas (mostly hydrogen) that produce their own light through nuclear fusion. Examples: Sun, Sirius, Polaris, Vega. They twinkle (because their light point passes through Earth's atmosphere). Billions of km away. PLANETS: cold, solid or gaseous bodies that orbit a star and reflect its light. They don't produce light themselves. Examples: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter. They don't twinkle (because we see them as small discs, not points). Much closer than stars (within solar system). Planets are visible only because they reflect sunlight; stars shine on their own.

Q7. How do astronomers know about distant stars and planets?

Model answer: Through (1) Light analysis — spectroscopy reveals composition (each element has a unique fingerprint in light); (2) Brightness vs distance calculations using known 'standard candle' stars; (3) Observing motion patterns — wobble in a star reveals an orbiting planet; (4) Direct imaging — modern telescopes can see large planets directly; (5) Eclipses — when a planet passes in front of its star, it dims slightly; (6) Radio waves, X-rays, gamma rays — invisible to eyes but reveal hot processes. India's GMRT (Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope, Pune) and AstroSat (orbiting telescope, launched 2015) contribute to global astronomy.

Q8. What are constellations? Name two that you can see in the sky.

Model answer: Constellations are PATTERNS we see when we mentally connect stars. The stars in a constellation are NOT actually grouped — they may be at vastly different distances from Earth. But from our viewpoint, they form recognisable shapes. Examples: ORION (Hunter) — visible in winter, contains three bright stars in a row called Orion's Belt. URSA MAJOR (Big Bear, also called Saptarishi or Big Dipper) — visible most of the year, contains 7 bright stars in a saucepan shape. The two end stars of the saucepan point to POLARIS (Pole Star). Indian astronomy has 27 Nakshatras (lunar mansions) — different patterns than Western constellations.
