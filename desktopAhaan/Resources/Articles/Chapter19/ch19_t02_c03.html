Tidal locking — why the Moon shows only one face

The Moon rotates on its axis in exactly the same time it takes to orbit Earth, so the same side always faces us.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine walking in a circle around your friend, but always facing them. You'd have to slowly turn your body as you walk — by the time you complete one circle, you've also made exactly one full turn. The Moon does this around Earth! It takes 27.3 days to go around Earth AND 27.3 days to spin once on its axis. The result? We always see the same face. The 'far side' (often wrongly called the 'dark side') actually gets the same amount of sunlight — we just never see it from Earth. China's Chang'e 4 mission was the first to land on the far side in 2019.

What this concept means

The Moon is tidally locked to Earth, meaning its rotational period equals its orbital period (both approximately 27.3 days). As a result, the same hemisphere of the Moon always faces Earth. This synchronous rotation is not a coincidence — it was caused over billions of years by tidal forces from Earth's gravity, which gradually slowed the Moon's rotation until it matched its orbital period. The far side of the Moon was first photographed by the Soviet Luna 3 probe in 1959.

Why it works this way

Gravity isn't uniform across a body — the side closer to Earth feels stronger pull. This difference creates a tidal bulge. If the Moon spun faster than it orbited, the bulge would be dragged forward, and Earth's gravity would act like a brake. Over billions of years, this braking effect synchronised the Moon's rotation with its orbit.

Beyond the book

Most large moons in the Solar System are tidally locked to their planets — including Jupiter's four Galilean moons and Saturn's Titan. Earth itself is slowly becoming tidally locked to the Moon, but it would take tens of billions of years (far longer than the Sun's remaining lifetime). Fun fact: because the Moon's orbit is slightly elliptical, it appears to 'rock' slightly (libration), letting us peek around the edges and see about 59% of its total surface over time. You can observe this by comparing Moon photos taken a few weeks apart!

Real-world applications

  1. Same craters every night — Every time you look at the Moon, you see the same craters (like Tycho and Copernicus) — you're looking at the same face.
  2. Pink Floyd's Dark Side of the Moon — The famous album title is technically wrong — the far side gets the same sunlight. It should be 'far side', not 'dark side'.
  3. China's Chang'e 4 mission — In 2019, China landed Chang'e 4 on the far side — the first-ever far-side landing, needing a relay satellite because radio signals can't reach directly.
  4. Pluto and Charon — Pluto and its moon Charon are BOTH tidally locked to each other — they always show the same face to one another.
  5. Moon libration reveals 59% — Due to slight wobbles (libration), we actually see about 59% of the Moon's surface over time, not just 50%.

Expert depth

Tidal locking occurs because Earth's gravitational field creates a tidal bulge on the Moon. If the Moon's rotation were faster than its orbit, the bulge would be slightly ahead of the Earth-Moon line, and Earth's gravity would pull it back, slowing the rotation. This process continued until the rotation period exactly matched the orbital period — a stable equilibrium. The same mechanism is slowly locking Earth to the Moon (Earth's rotation is slowing by 2.3 ms/century). In the far future, Earth will be tidally locked to the Moon, and a 'day' will be ~47 current days long.
