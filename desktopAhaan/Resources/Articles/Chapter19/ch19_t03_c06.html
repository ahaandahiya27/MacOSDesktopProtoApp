Inner rocky planets vs outer gas giants

The four inner planets (Mercury-Mars) are small and rocky; the four outer planets (Jupiter-Neptune) are massive gas or ice giants.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

The Solar System has two teams. Team Rocky (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) are small, dense planets with solid surfaces you could stand on. They're close to the Sun where it was too hot for gases to stick around when the planets formed. Team Giant (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) are enormous balls of gas and ice — Jupiter alone could swallow all the other planets combined! They're far from the Sun where it was cold enough for gases like hydrogen and helium to clump together. You couldn't stand on Jupiter — there's no solid surface, just thicker and thicker gas all the way down!

What this concept means

The inner planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) are terrestrial — small, dense, rocky, with thin or no atmospheres. They lie within the frost line (~3 AU from the Sun). The outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) are much larger, less dense, and composed primarily of hydrogen, helium, and ices (water, ammonia, methane). Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants; Uranus and Neptune are ice giants. The asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter roughly marks the boundary. This division reflects conditions in the protoplanetary disc: inner regions were too warm for volatiles to condense, so only rock and metal accreted.

Why it works this way

The frost line divided the early Solar System into a hot inner zone (where only rock and metal could condense) and a cold outer zone (where ices also condensed). More solid material in the outer zone meant larger cores, which could then capture gas and grow massive. This temperature gradient during planet formation explains the two distinct types.

Beyond the book

Here's a thought experiment: what if Jupiter had formed at Earth's distance from the Sun? It would have prevented Earth from forming at all — its gravity would have scattered or absorbed all the material. We owe our existence to Jupiter forming beyond the frost line. Conversely, 'hot Jupiters' — gas giants orbiting close to their stars — have been discovered around other stars, showing that our Solar System's neat division isn't universal. In Class 11, you'll study how Kepler's laws connect orbital distance to orbital speed — inner planets move faster than outer ones.

Real-world applications

  1. Mars missions vs Jupiter probes — We can land rovers on Mars (rocky surface) but only fly probes through Jupiter's atmosphere — there's nothing solid to land on.
  2. Venus as Earth's evil twin — Venus is nearly Earth's size but has a 470 degrees C surface (runaway greenhouse) — showing how a rocky planet can go terribly wrong.
  3. Saturn would float in water — Saturn's average density (0.687 g/cm cubed) is less than water — if you had a bathtub big enough, it would float!
  4. Earth's uniqueness — Earth is the only rocky planet with liquid surface water, plate tectonics, and a protective magnetic field — a rare combination that enables life.
  5. Neptune's blue colour — Neptune appears blue because methane in its atmosphere absorbs red light — it's an ice giant, not a gas giant like Jupiter.

Expert depth

The frost line (or snow line) at ~3 AU in the protoplanetary disc was the critical boundary. Inside it, temperatures exceeded ~170 K and water ice sublimated, leaving only silicates and metals to form rocky protoplanets. Beyond it, water ice was stable and vastly increased the available solid mass, enabling rapid core growth. When cores reached ~10 Earth masses, they could gravitationally capture the surrounding hydrogen-helium gas envelope, growing into gas giants via runaway accretion (the core accretion model). This explains why no gas giant exists inside 3 AU in our system. Jupiter's core is estimated at 10-25 Earth masses of rock/ice, surrounded by ~300 Earth masses of gas.
