The Moon landing — Neil Armstrong and Apollo 11

On July 20, 1969, Apollo 11 astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin became the first humans to walk on the Moon.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine strapping yourself to a rocket the size of a 36-storey building, blasting off at 40,000 km/h, flying for 3 days through empty space, and then landing on a world where there's no air, no sound, and you weigh one-sixth of normal. That's exactly what Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin, and Michael Collins did on July 20, 1969! Armstrong's first words as he stepped onto the Moon were: 'That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.' They spent 21 hours on the surface, collected 22 kg of moon rocks, planted a flag, and came home safely. About 600 million people watched on TV — the largest audience ever at that time.

What this concept means

The Apollo 11 mission, launched by NASA on July 16, 1969, achieved the first crewed Moon landing on July 20, 1969. Commander Neil Armstrong and Lunar Module Pilot Buzz Aldrin landed the Lunar Module Eagle in the Sea of Tranquility, while Command Module Pilot Michael Collins orbited above. Armstrong became the first human to walk on the Moon. The Apollo programme (1969-1972) conducted six successful Moon landings. India's Chandrayaan programme (Chandrayaan-1 in 2008, Chandrayaan-2 in 2019, and Chandrayaan-3 in 2023) represents India's major contributions to lunar exploration.

Why it works this way

The Moon landing was possible because of Newton's laws (rocket propulsion), orbital mechanics (transfer orbits), and immense engineering. It proved that space travel beyond Earth orbit is achievable. India's Chandrayaan missions continue this legacy, demonstrating that even developing nations can contribute cutting-edge space science.

Beyond the book

Here's an amazing India connection: the tracking station at Honeysuckle Creek in Australia, which received the first TV signals from the Moon landing, was supported by tracking data from India's ISRO predecessor — the Indian National Committee for Space Research. And in 2008, Chandrayaan-1's Moon Impact Probe (MIP) — the first Indian object on the Moon — carried the Indian tricolour painted on it. Chandrayaan-3's success in 2023 made India only the 4th country to soft-land on the Moon, and the only one to land near the south pole. ISRO achieved this at a budget of about $75 million — less than the production cost of many Hollywood movies!

Real-world applications

  1. Chandrayaan-3's south pole landing — India became the 4th country to land on the Moon and the 1st to land near the south pole — a historic achievement for ISRO.
  2. Moon rocks in museums — Apollo astronauts brought back 382 kg of lunar samples — some are displayed in museums worldwide, including the Nehru Planetarium in Delhi.
  3. Tang and Velcro myths — Contrary to popular belief, Tang and Velcro weren't invented for the Moon missions — but memory foam, water purifiers, and scratch-resistant lenses were!
  4. Astronaut training in India — ISRO's Gaganyaan programme is training Indian astronauts (vyomanauts) for crewed spaceflight — building on Apollo's legacy.
  5. Footprints lasting millions of years — Armstrong's footprints will last millions of years on the Moon — no wind, rain, or erosion to erase them.

Expert depth

The Apollo programme was a Cold War achievement requiring innovations in rocket propulsion (Saturn V: 7.5 million pounds of thrust), navigation (the onboard computer had less processing power than a modern calculator), and life support. The total cost was approximately $25.4 billion (1973 dollars, ~$200 billion today). Lunar samples returned by Apollo missions revealed that the Moon has a similar composition to Earth's mantle, supporting the Giant Impact Hypothesis. India's Chandrayaan-3 (August 23, 2023) soft-landed near the lunar south pole at 69.37 degrees S — the highest-latitude lunar landing ever — and its Pragyan rover detected sulphur, aluminium, iron, and other elements in the polar regolith.
