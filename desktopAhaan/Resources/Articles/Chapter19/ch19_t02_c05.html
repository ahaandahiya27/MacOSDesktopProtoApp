Lunar eclipse — when Earth's shadow falls on the Moon

A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth comes between the Sun and Moon, and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, turning it dark or reddish.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine standing between a lamp and a wall. Your shadow falls on the wall, blocking the light. During a lunar eclipse, Earth is the person, the Sun is the lamp, and the Moon is the wall! Earth's shadow creeps across the Moon's face over a couple of hours. But here's the cool part — the Moon doesn't go completely dark. It turns a beautiful blood-red colour! This happens because Earth's atmosphere bends red sunlight (the same light that makes sunsets red) into the shadow, painting the Moon coppery red.

What this concept means

A lunar eclipse occurs when the Earth is positioned between the Sun and the Moon, and the Moon passes through Earth's shadow. This can only happen at Full Moon. In a total lunar eclipse, the Moon enters the darkest part of Earth's shadow (umbra) and often appears reddish because Earth's atmosphere refracts and filters sunlight, allowing red wavelengths to reach the Moon. In a partial lunar eclipse, only part of the Moon enters the umbra. Unlike solar eclipses, lunar eclipses are visible from anywhere on the night side of Earth and last much longer (up to ~1 hour 40 minutes of totality).

Why it works this way

The geometry is straightforward: when Sun, Earth, and Moon align with Earth in the middle, Earth blocks sunlight from reaching the Moon. The reddish colour arises because Earth's atmosphere acts as a lens and a filter — it bends (refracts) sunlight around the planet while scattering out blue wavelengths, letting only red light reach the Moon.

Beyond the book

Here's something poetic: during a total lunar eclipse, the red light hitting the Moon is literally the combined light of every sunrise and sunset happening on Earth at that moment. If you were standing on the Moon during a lunar eclipse, you'd see Earth as a dark disc surrounded by a brilliant ring of red-orange light — Earth's atmosphere glowing like a halo. The next total lunar eclipse visible from India is on September 7, 2025 — mark your calendar! No special glasses needed for lunar eclipses — they're perfectly safe to watch with the naked eye.

Real-world applications

  1. Blood Moon viral photos — Every total lunar eclipse trends on social media as 'Blood Moon' — the red colour comes from every sunrise and sunset on Earth projected onto the Moon simultaneously.
  2. Visible from half the Earth — Unlike solar eclipses (visible from a narrow strip), anyone on the night side of Earth can see a lunar eclipse — no travel needed.
  3. Ancient eclipse fear — Many ancient cultures feared lunar eclipses — in Hindu mythology, the demon Rahu was believed to swallow the Moon (Chandra Grahan).
  4. Columbus and the 1504 eclipse — Christopher Columbus used his knowledge of a coming lunar eclipse to intimidate Jamaican natives into providing food — he predicted the Moon would 'disappear'.
  5. Proving Earth is round — Aristotle noted that Earth's shadow on the Moon is always circular — only a sphere casts a circular shadow from every angle.

Expert depth

The reddish colour during a total lunar eclipse (sometimes called a 'Blood Moon') is caused by Rayleigh scattering in Earth's atmosphere — the same physics behind red sunsets. Blue light is scattered away, while longer red wavelengths are refracted around Earth into the umbral shadow. The brightness and colour of the eclipsed Moon depends on atmospheric conditions (volcanic ash makes it darker — the Danjon scale rates this from L=0 (very dark) to L=4 (bright copper-red)). Earth's umbral shadow at the Moon's distance is about 2.6 times the Moon's diameter, which is why total lunar eclipses can last over 100 minutes.
