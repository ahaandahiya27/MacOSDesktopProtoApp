Solar eclipse — when the Moon hides the Sun

A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes directly between the Sun and Earth, casting a shadow on Earth and blocking sunlight.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Hold a coin at arm's length and close one eye. You can block out a distant streetlight even though the light is much bigger — the coin is close enough to cover it. That's exactly what happens during a solar eclipse! The Moon is 400 times smaller than the Sun, but it's also 400 times closer — an amazing cosmic coincidence that makes them appear the same size in our sky. When the Moon slides exactly between the Sun and Earth, it blocks the Sun, turning day into an eerie twilight for a few minutes. Animals think it's night and birds stop singing!

What this concept means

A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon comes between the Sun and Earth, and the Moon's shadow falls on Earth's surface. In a total solar eclipse, the Moon completely covers the Sun's disc, revealing the solar corona. In a partial eclipse, only part of the Sun is covered. An annular eclipse occurs when the Moon is too far from Earth (near apogee) to fully cover the Sun, leaving a ring of sunlight visible. Solar eclipses can only occur at New Moon, but not every New Moon produces an eclipse because the Moon's orbital plane is tilted about 5 degrees to Earth's orbital plane.

Why it works this way

A solar eclipse requires three bodies to align precisely: Sun-Moon-Earth. The Moon's shadow is narrow (~270 km for totality), so total solar eclipses are rare at any specific location (average once every 375 years). The 5-degree tilt of the Moon's orbit means perfect alignment only occurs along the 'line of nodes' where the two orbital planes intersect.

Beyond the book

Never look directly at a solar eclipse without proper ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses — regular sunglasses are NOT safe. But here's a safe, fun way to watch: poke a tiny hole in a card and hold it above the ground. The light passing through the hole projects an image of the eclipsed Sun on the ground — a pinhole camera! During the 2009 Indian total solar eclipse, millions of people across Surat and Varanasi witnessed totality lasting up to 6 minutes. The next total solar eclipse visible from India will be on March 20, 2034.

Real-world applications

  1. Aditya-L1 studying the corona — ISRO's Aditya-L1 (2023) studies the Sun's corona from space — this layer is normally visible only during total solar eclipses.
  2. Eclipse-safe glasses — Special ISO-certified eclipse glasses are essential — looking at a partial eclipse without protection can permanently damage your retina in seconds.
  3. Eddington's 1919 eclipse — Arthur Eddington used a total solar eclipse in 1919 to photograph stars near the Sun, proving Einstein's General Relativity — light bends around massive objects.
  4. Birds roosting mid-day — During total solar eclipses, birds return to their nests and crickets start chirping — they think night has arrived.
  5. Eclipse tourism industry — People travel thousands of kilometres to stand in the path of totality — eclipse tourism is a multi-million-dollar industry.

Expert depth

The remarkable near-equal apparent sizes of the Sun (angular diameter ~0.53 degrees) and Moon (~0.52 degrees) as seen from Earth is a pure coincidence of the current epoch — the Moon is slowly receding, and in ~600 million years, total solar eclipses will no longer be possible. During totality, the corona (temperature ~1-2 million K) becomes visible, along with prominences and the chromosphere. India's solar eclipse of July 22, 2009 had a totality path crossing Surat, Varanasi, and Patna. ISRO's Aditya-L1 mission (launched September 2023) studies the solar corona continuously from the L1 Lagrange point.
