Moon phases — from New Moon to Full Moon and back

The Moon's phases are caused by the changing angle between the Sun, Moon, and Earth as the Moon orbits us every 29.5 days.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Grab a white ball and a torch. Stand in a dim room, hold the ball at arm's length, and slowly spin around. When the torch is behind you, the whole ball lights up — that's a Full Moon. When the torch is in front of you (behind the ball), the lit side faces away — that's a New Moon. Between these, you see half-lit, crescent, and gibbous shapes. The Moon does this around Earth every 29.5 days. It doesn't actually change shape — we just see different amounts of its sunlit half as it orbits us!

What this concept means

The Moon's phases are the different appearances of the Moon as seen from Earth, caused by the changing relative positions of the Sun, Earth, and Moon. The lunar cycle (synodic period) is approximately 29.5 days. The sequence is: New Moon (not visible) → Waxing Crescent → First Quarter (half lit) → Waxing Gibbous → Full Moon (fully lit) → Waning Gibbous → Third Quarter (half lit) → Waning Crescent → New Moon again. The Moon always has one half illuminated by the Sun; the phases arise because we see different portions of this illuminated half from Earth.

Why it works this way

The Moon reflects sunlight but orbits Earth, so the angle between Sun-Moon-Earth constantly changes. When the Moon is between Earth and Sun, the lit side faces away from us (New Moon). When Earth is between the Moon and Sun, the fully lit side faces us (Full Moon). All other phases are intermediate viewing angles.

Beyond the book

Here's a beautiful experiment: draw the Moon's shape every night for a month in a notebook. Date each drawing. After 29-30 nights, you'll have a complete visual record of the lunar cycle — and you'll notice the Moon rises about 50 minutes later each night (because it moves ~13 degrees along its orbit daily). Ancient Indian astronomers did exactly this and calculated the lunar month to within a few minutes of the modern value. The Surya Siddhanta (an ancient Indian astronomical text) gives the synodic month as 29.530588 days — the modern value is 29.530589 days!

Real-world applications

  1. Diwali on Amavasya — Diwali is celebrated on the New Moon night (Amavasya) of Kartik — the darkest night, which is why we light diyas.
  2. Holi on Purnima — Holi falls on the Full Moon (Purnima) of Phalgun — the brightest moonlit night of early spring.
  3. Karva Chauth moon sighting — On Karva Chauth, women watch for the Moon after fasting — it appears as a waxing gibbous, four days after Full Moon.
  4. Islamic calendar months — The Islamic calendar begins each month with the sighting of the thin Waxing Crescent (hilal) after New Moon.
  5. Fishing by moon phase — Many Indian fishermen prefer to fish during New Moon or Full Moon when tides are strongest and fish feed more actively.
  6. Photography tip for moonrise — The best time to photograph the Moon with landscape is at the Full Moon rising — it appears biggest near the horizon (Moon illusion).
  7. Panchang and farming — Indian farmers traditionally consult the Panchang (lunar calendar) for planting and harvesting dates tied to moon phases.

Expert depth

The synodic period (29.53 days — New Moon to New Moon) differs from the sidereal period (27.32 days — one orbit relative to stars) because Earth moves along its own orbit during each lunar cycle, so the Moon must travel slightly more than 360 degrees to return to the same Sun-Moon-Earth alignment. The Hindu calendar (Panchang) is lunisolar — months are defined by the Moon's phases (Shukla Paksha = waxing fortnight, Krishna Paksha = waning fortnight). Diwali falls on Amavasya (New Moon) in Kartik month, and Holi falls on Purnima (Full Moon) in Phalgun month.
