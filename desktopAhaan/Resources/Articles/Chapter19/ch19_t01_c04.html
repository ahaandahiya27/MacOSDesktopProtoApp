Seasons — why tilt plus revolution creates summer and winter

Seasons occur because Earth's tilted axis causes each hemisphere to receive more direct sunlight for part of the year and less for the other part.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Here's a puzzle: India has summer in June even though Earth is farthest from the Sun in July! So distance doesn't cause seasons — the tilt does. Imagine holding a torch above a sheet of paper. Shine it straight down — you get a small, bright circle (summer: direct rays, more heat). Now tilt the paper — the light spreads into a big, dim oval (winter: slanting rays, less heat). When the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun, India gets direct rays and long days — hello summer! Six months later, it tilts away — slanting rays, short days — winter sweaters come out.

What this concept means

Seasons are caused by the combined effect of Earth's axial tilt (23.5 degrees) and its revolution around the Sun. When the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun (around June), it receives more direct sunlight and experiences summer; simultaneously, the Southern Hemisphere is tilted away and experiences winter. Six months later, the situation reverses. The solstices (June 21 and December 21) mark maximum tilt toward/away from the Sun, while equinoxes (March 21 and September 23) occur when neither hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun, and day and night are approximately equal.

Why it works this way

The tilt means one hemisphere gets more concentrated sunlight (higher Sun angle = more energy per square metre) AND longer days simultaneously. This double effect — intensity plus duration — creates the large temperature swings we call seasons. Distance from the Sun varies only 3.3% and has minimal effect.

Beyond the book

What if Earth had no tilt at all? There would be NO seasons anywhere — every day would be a permanent equinox. The equator would always be hot, the poles always frozen, and the moderate climate band would be very narrow. Most of Earth's biodiversity exists because seasonal changes create ecological niches. Uranus is tilted at 98 degrees — it essentially rolls around the Sun on its side, giving it the most extreme seasons in the solar system: 42 years of continuous sunlight followed by 42 years of darkness at each pole!

Real-world applications

  1. Summer vacation timing — Indian schools have summer holidays in May-June because that's when the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun and temperatures peak.
  2. Winter crops in India — Rabi crops like wheat are sown in October and harvested in March — timed to the cooler season when the Northern Hemisphere tilts away.
  3. Christmas in Australia — Australians celebrate Christmas on the beach in summer because December is their summer — opposite hemisphere, opposite season.
  4. Makar Sankranti celebration — Makar Sankranti (January 14) marks the Sun's northward journey (Uttarayan) — days start getting longer in India after this festival.
  5. AC sales peak in April-May — Air conditioner sales in India peak before summer because people anticipate the heat from direct sunlight — a billion-dollar industry driven by axial tilt.
  6. Bird migration to India — Siberian cranes fly to India in winter because Siberia tilts away from the Sun and becomes too cold — India is warmer.
  7. Ice cream vs hot chocolate — Ice cream shops boom in summer and chai stalls boom in winter — all because Earth's axis points different hemispheres toward the Sun.

Expert depth

The seasonal variation in solar irradiance depends on two factors: (1) the angle of incidence — direct rays deliver more energy per unit area (Lambert's cosine law: I = I0 * cos(theta)), and (2) the length of daylight — more hours of sunshine means more total energy. At the summer solstice, a location at 28 degrees N (Delhi) receives ~14 hours of daylight with a solar elevation up to 85 degrees. At the winter solstice, the same location gets ~10 hours with maximum elevation ~38 degrees. The energy difference is roughly 3:1. Note that the Southern Hemisphere's seasons are exactly opposite — when Delhi shivers, Sydney sizzles.
