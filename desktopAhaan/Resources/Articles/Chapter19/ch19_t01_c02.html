Rotation — what causes day and night

Earth spins on its axis once every 24 hours; the side facing the Sun has day, and the opposite side has night.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine you're standing in a dark room with a single torch. Now slowly spin in a circle. Your face lights up when it points at the torch — that's daytime. When your back faces the torch — that's night. Earth does exactly this, spinning like a slow merry-go-round. It takes 24 hours for one full spin, which is why we have 24 hours in a day. The Sun doesn't move across our sky — we do! We're the ones spinning, and the Sun just sits there, 150 million km away, shining steadily.

What this concept means

The Earth rotates on its axis from west to east, completing one rotation in approximately 24 hours (23 hours 56 minutes to be precise — a sidereal day). This rotation causes the apparent movement of the Sun across the sky and produces the cycle of day and night. At any given moment, the hemisphere facing the Sun experiences daylight while the opposite hemisphere is in darkness. The speed of rotation at the equator is about 1,670 km/h, decreasing toward the poles where it reaches zero.

Why it works this way

Earth inherited angular momentum from the spinning cloud of gas and dust that formed the solar system. Once spinning, it continues (Newton's first law) with almost no friction in space. The Sun illuminates only the hemisphere facing it, creating the day-night boundary called the terminator.

Beyond the book

Try this tonight: go outside and watch a bright star for 15 minutes. You'll notice it has moved slightly westward — that's not the star moving, it's you rotating eastward on Earth. If you could watch for a full 24 hours, that star would appear to make a complete circle. The ancient Indian astronomer Aryabhata wrote in 499 CE: 'Just as a man in a boat moving forward sees the stationary objects on the shore as moving backward, so are the stationary stars seen by the people on Earth as moving westward.' He figured out Earth's rotation 1,000 years before Copernicus!

Real-world applications

  1. Sunrise in Arunachal first — Arunachal Pradesh sees sunrise before Gujarat because Earth rotates west to east — eastern India faces the Sun first.
  2. Cricket day-night matches — Day-night cricket matches use floodlights because Earth's rotation brings darkness mid-game — the schedule is built around rotation.
  3. Calling relatives abroad — When it's afternoon in India, it's early morning in the USA — because Earth's rotation puts them in darkness while we're in daylight.
  4. Solar panels tracking the Sun — Solar panels on rooftops rotate to follow the Sun's apparent path — they're really compensating for Earth's rotation.
  5. Shadows moving in the playground — Your shadow is long in the morning, short at noon, and long again in the evening because Earth is rotating you relative to the Sun.
  6. Foucault pendulum in science museum — A Foucault pendulum appears to change its swing direction over hours — it's actually the floor (Earth) rotating underneath it.
  7. Jet lag after long flights — Flying from India to the US causes jet lag because you've crossed time zones created by Earth's rotation faster than your body can adjust.

Expert depth

The sidereal day (one full 360-degree rotation relative to distant stars) is 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds. The solar day is ~4 minutes longer because Earth has moved ~1 degree along its orbit, so it must rotate slightly more to face the Sun again. In Class 11 Physics, you'll calculate the Coriolis force (F = 2m * omega cross v) that arises from this rotation, deflecting winds and ocean currents. Interestingly, Earth's rotation is slowing by about 2.3 milliseconds per century due to tidal friction from the Moon — 600 million years ago, a day was only 21 hours long!
