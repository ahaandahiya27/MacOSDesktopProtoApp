Revolution — Earth's orbit around the Sun

Earth orbits the Sun in a slightly elliptical path, completing one revolution in 365.25 days — one year.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine tying a ball to a string and swinging it around your head. The ball is Earth, your head is the Sun, and the invisible string is gravity. Earth zooms around the Sun at 107,000 km/h — 30 km every second! That's so fast you could travel from Delhi to Mumbai in 40 seconds. One full lap takes 365 days and about 6 extra hours, which is why we call it a year. The path isn't a perfect circle — it's a slightly squished oval called an ellipse. Surprisingly, Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January (winter in India!) and farthest in July.

What this concept means

The Earth revolves around the Sun in an elliptical orbit with the Sun at one focus of the ellipse. One complete revolution takes approximately 365.25 days, which defines a year. The average distance from Earth to Sun is about 149.6 million km (1 Astronomical Unit). Earth's orbital speed is approximately 29.8 km/s. The point where Earth is closest to the Sun is called perihelion (about 147 million km, in early January), and the farthest point is aphelion (about 152 million km, in early July).

Why it works this way

Earth moves in an orbit because the Sun's gravity constantly pulls it inward while its tangential velocity tries to carry it straight ahead. The balance between gravitational pull and inertial motion creates the stable elliptical orbit, as described by Newton's law of gravitation and Kepler's laws.

Beyond the book

Here's a stunning fact: right now, as you read this, you are hurtling through space at 107,000 km/h around the Sun. You can't feel it because everything around you — the air, your chair, your house — is moving at exactly the same speed (just like you don't feel the speed inside a smooth aeroplane). In Class 11 Physics, you'll use Kepler's third law (T squared is proportional to a cubed) to calculate the orbital period of any planet if you know its distance from the Sun.

Real-world applications

  1. Birthday = one full orbit — Every birthday means you've completed another 940-million-km journey around the Sun — congratulations, space traveller!
  2. Calendar and farming seasons — Indian farmers plan sowing and harvesting around the calendar year — which is defined by one complete revolution.
  3. New Year celebrations worldwide — January 1st marks an arbitrary point on Earth's orbit — the planet doesn't stop or start, it just keeps going.
  4. Satellite launch windows — ISRO plans launch dates partly based on Earth's orbital position to minimise fuel needed to reach Mars or the Moon.
  5. The zodiac and birth signs — Astrology signs correspond to which constellation the Sun 'appears' in front of during each month of the orbit — it's really about Earth's position.
  6. Perihelion in winter — Earth is closest to the Sun in January (Indian winter!) — proving distance isn't what causes seasons, the tilt is.

Expert depth

Kepler's first law (1609) states that planetary orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus. Earth's orbital eccentricity is 0.0167 — nearly circular, but enough to cause a 3.3% variation in solar intensity between perihelion and aphelion. This distance variation is NOT what causes seasons (a common misconception). In Class 11 Physics, you'll derive the orbital velocity using v = sqrt(GM/r) and prove that Earth moves faster at perihelion (Kepler's second law — equal areas in equal times). The 0.25-day excess per year accumulates to one extra day every four years — hence the leap year.
