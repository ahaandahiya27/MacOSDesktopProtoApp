The Coriolis effect — why winds and cyclones curve

Earth's rotation deflects moving objects — winds curve right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern Hemisphere.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine sitting on a spinning merry-go-round and trying to throw a ball straight to your friend across from you. The ball seems to curve sideways! That's because you're both spinning, but the ball isn't glued to the merry-go-round. Earth does the same thing to winds and ocean currents. In the Northern Hemisphere, moving air curves to the right. In the Southern Hemisphere, it curves to the left. This is why cyclones (like the ones that hit Odisha) spin counterclockwise in India but clockwise in Australia. Same physics, opposite directions!

What this concept means

The Coriolis effect is an apparent deflection of moving objects (winds, ocean currents, projectiles) caused by Earth's rotation. In the Northern Hemisphere, moving objects are deflected to the right of their direction of motion; in the Southern Hemisphere, to the left. The Coriolis effect is zero at the equator and maximum at the poles. It is responsible for the rotation of cyclonic systems — counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere. The Coriolis effect does not create wind but changes its direction.

Why it works this way

Different latitudes on Earth move at different speeds (fastest at the equator, zero at the poles). When air moves from one latitude to another, it retains its original east-west speed, which doesn't match the ground speed at its new latitude. This mismatch appears as a sideways curve from the ground observer's perspective.

Beyond the book

A common myth is that toilets and sinks drain in opposite directions in the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. This is false! The Coriolis effect is far too weak at the scale of a sink — the direction depends on the basin's shape and how the water was moving before. You'd need a perfectly circular pool tens of metres wide, perfectly still water, and many hours to see the effect. However, a famous 1962 experiment by Ascher Shapiro at MIT did demonstrate Coriolis in a large, carefully controlled tank — it took 24 hours of settling before draining!

Real-world applications

  1. Cyclone Fani spinning — Cyclone Fani (2019, Odisha) spun counterclockwise — a direct result of the Coriolis effect in the Northern Hemisphere.
  2. Trade winds and sailing routes — European explorers relied on northeast trade winds (deflected by Coriolis) to sail to India — Vasco da Gama used them in 1498.
  3. Weather maps in the news — Every TV weather forecast showing low-pressure systems with swirling winds is showing the Coriolis effect in action.
  4. Sniper long-range shots — Military snipers shooting beyond 1 km must correct for Coriolis deflection — a bullet can drift several centimetres over long distances.
  5. Drain water myth — Contrary to popular belief, the Coriolis effect does NOT determine which way water drains in a sink — the scale is too small. Only large-scale systems like cyclones are affected.

Expert depth

The Coriolis acceleration is given by a = -2 * omega cross v, where omega is Earth's angular velocity (7.29 x 10^-5 rad/s) and v is the velocity of the moving object. The effect scales with both speed and latitude (since the vertical component of omega varies as sin(latitude)). At the equator, sin(0) = 0, so there is no Coriolis deflection — this is why tropical cyclones never form exactly on the equator. The Coriolis effect also explains trade winds, westerlies, and the formation of the Hadley, Ferrel, and polar circulation cells studied in Class 11 Geography.
