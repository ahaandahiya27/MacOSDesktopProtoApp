Asteroids, comets, and meteors

Asteroids are rocky leftovers orbiting the Sun, comets are icy bodies with spectacular tails, and meteors are streaks of light from space debris burning in Earth's atmosphere.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

The Solar System isn't just planets — it's full of space rocks and ice balls! Asteroids are rocky chunks (some as big as small countries) that mostly orbit between Mars and Jupiter in the 'asteroid belt'. Comets are giant dirty snowballs from the outer Solar System — when they approach the Sun, the heat vaporises their ice, creating a beautiful glowing tail millions of kilometres long. Meteors are the shooting stars you see at night — tiny bits of space rock (often from comets) that burn up in Earth's atmosphere at incredible speeds, creating a streak of light. If a piece survives and hits the ground, it's called a meteorite!

What this concept means

Asteroids are small, rocky bodies orbiting the Sun, primarily concentrated in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. The largest asteroid is Ceres (now classified as a dwarf planet, ~940 km diameter). Comets are icy bodies from the outer Solar System (Kuiper Belt or Oort Cloud) that develop a coma and tail when heated by the Sun. Halley's Comet, visible every ~76 years (last in 1986, next in 2061), is the most famous periodic comet. Meteoroids are small particles in space; when they enter Earth's atmosphere and burn up, they produce meteors (shooting stars). A meteoroid that survives to reach the ground is a meteorite.

Why it works this way

Asteroids are remnants from the early Solar System that never formed a planet because Jupiter's gravity constantly stirred them. Comets preserved ancient ices from the Solar System's outer edges. Meteors occur when small particles hit Earth's atmosphere at 11-72 km/s — friction with air molecules superheats them to thousands of degrees, creating the visible glow.

Beyond the book

India's Lonar Lake in Maharashtra is a stunning impact crater — about 1.8 km across and 150 m deep, created ~50,000 years ago by a meteorite. It's one of the few impact craters in basaltic rock and has both saline and fresh water, supporting a unique ecosystem including flamingos. Try watching the Geminid meteor shower (mid-December) or the Perseid shower (mid-August) from a dark location away from city lights. You might see 60-100 meteors per hour! Each streak is a grain of space dust, often smaller than a grain of sand, burning up at 60 km/s — about 2 lakh km/h.

Real-world applications

  1. Shooting stars on Diwali night — The Taurid meteor shower peaks near Diwali some years — look for extra 'shooting stars' alongside fireworks on a clear Amavasya night.
  2. Halley's Comet in 2061 — Students reading this today will be in their 40s-50s when Halley's Comet returns in 2061 — mark your calendar for a once-in-a-lifetime spectacle.
  3. Dinosaur extinction asteroid — A 10 km asteroid hit Mexico's Yucatan Peninsula 66 million years ago and wiped out the dinosaurs — it left a 180 km crater called Chicxulub.
  4. Meteorites in museums — The Lonar Lake in Maharashtra (50,000 years old) was created by a meteorite impact — it's one of only four known impact craters in basaltic rock.
  5. Perseid meteor shower — Every August, the Perseid meteor shower produces 60-100 shooting stars per hour — caused by debris from comet Swift-Tuttle.
  6. NASA's DART mission — In 2022, NASA's DART spacecraft deliberately crashed into asteroid Dimorphos to test planetary defence — it successfully changed the asteroid's orbit.

Expert depth

The asteroid belt contains millions of bodies but their total mass is only ~4% of the Moon's mass — Jupiter's gravity prevented them from accreting into a planet. Near-Earth Asteroids (NEAs) are tracked by NASA's Planetary Defense Coordination Office and ISRO's Space Situational Awareness programme. The Chicxulub impactor (~10 km asteroid) caused the K-Pg mass extinction 66 million years ago, wiping out the dinosaurs. Comets are classified as short-period (< 200 years, from the Kuiper Belt) or long-period (> 200 years, from the Oort Cloud at ~50,000 AU). India successfully tested a planetary defence concept: Chandrayaan-2's orbiter tracked near-Moon objects as a pathfinder for future asteroid monitoring.
