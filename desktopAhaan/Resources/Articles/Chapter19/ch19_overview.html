Earth, Moon and the Sun

Our spinning, tilted planet, its faithful companion the Moon, and the star that powers it all.

≈ 3 topics · 23 core concepts · for curious 12-year-olds

Our Earth is a spinning top tilted at 23.5 degrees, racing around the Sun while the Moon faithfully circles us — and from these three simple motions come day and night, the seasons, eclipses, tides, and the changing face of the Moon. This chapter explores Earth's rotation and revolution, the lunar cycle from New Moon to Full Moon, solar and lunar eclipses, and the stars and constellations that ancient Indians mapped thousands of years ago.

For thousands of years, humans have looked up at the sky and wondered: why does the Sun rise and set? Why does the Moon change shape? Why do we have seasons? Indian astronomers like Aryabhata answered many of these questions over 1,500 years ago. This chapter follows their footsteps and yours.

Why this chapter matters

Understanding Earth, Moon and Sun motions is the foundation of timekeeping, navigation, agriculture, and space exploration. Every calendar, every season, every eclipse, every tide traces back to these three celestial bodies dancing together. India's ISRO missions to the Moon (Chandrayaan) and the Sun (Aditya-L1) are built on this exact science.

When you finish this chapter, you'll never look at a sunrise, a full moon, or a starry night the same way again. You'll understand the clockwork of the cosmos.

The history of understanding Earth and sky

  • ~500 CE Aryabhata

    The Indian mathematician proposed that the Earth rotates on its axis, causing the apparent motion of stars. He calculated Earth's circumference as 39,968 km — astonishingly close to the modern value of 40,075 km.

  • ~150 CE Ptolemy

    The Greek-Egyptian astronomer created an Earth-centred model of the cosmos that dominated Western science for 1,400 years.

  • 1543 Copernicus

    Published the heliocentric model placing the Sun at the centre, revolutionising astronomy.

  • 1609 Galileo

    Used a telescope to observe the Moon's craters, Jupiter's moons, and Venus's phases — proving the Sun-centred model.

  • 1687 Newton

    Published the law of universal gravitation, explaining why the Moon orbits Earth, why Earth orbits the Sun, and why tides happen.

  • 1969 Apollo 11

    Humans walked on the Moon for the first time. Neil Armstrong's "one small step" proved centuries of astronomical calculations correct.

  • 2008–2023 ISRO

    India's Chandrayaan-1 discovered water on the Moon (2008). Chandrayaan-3 soft-landed near the lunar south pole (2023). Aditya-L1 launched to study the Sun (2023).
