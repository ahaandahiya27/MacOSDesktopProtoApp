Time zones — why different places have different times

Earth is divided into 24 time zones because it rotates 360 degrees in 24 hours — each 15-degree slice has a one-hour difference.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine slicing an orange into 24 equal wedges. Each wedge is one time zone — a 15-degree-wide strip of Earth that shares the same clock time. When the Sun is directly overhead for your wedge, it's noon for you. But someone 15 degrees to the east saw noon one hour ago, and someone 15 degrees to the west won't see it for another hour. India is so wide (68 degrees E to 97 degrees E) that Gujarat and Arunachal Pradesh have almost 2 hours of Sun-time difference, but we all use one time — IST (Indian Standard Time) — set at 82.5 degrees E, which passes near Prayagraj.

What this concept means

The Earth rotates 360 degrees in 24 hours, which means it rotates 15 degrees every hour. This is the basis for dividing the world into 24 standard time zones, each spanning 15 degrees of longitude. The Prime Meridian (0 degrees, Greenwich, London) is the reference for Greenwich Mean Time (GMT) or Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). India maintains a single time zone: Indian Standard Time (IST) = UTC + 5 hours 30 minutes, calculated at 82.5 degrees E longitude. Countries spanning many degrees (like Russia or the USA) use multiple time zones.

Why it works this way

Since Earth is a sphere rotating at a constant rate, different longitudes face the Sun at different moments. Time zones formalise this into one-hour blocks so that clocks roughly match the Sun's position. Without time zones, scheduling any long-distance communication, travel, or broadcast would be chaotic.

Beyond the book

Here's an experiment: call a friend or relative in a different city (say Mumbai vs Guwahati) and both look at your shadows at exactly 12:00 IST. In Guwahati, the shadow will be longer and angled differently because solar noon already passed. In Mumbai, it'll be closer to shortest because solar noon is nearer to IST noon. Same clock, different Sun positions! In 2014, ISRO's Mars Orbiter Mission team had to coordinate across IST, UTC, and Mars solar time — imagine scheduling a meeting across three planets' clocks.

Real-world applications

  1. IST and the 82.5 degree meridian — India's single time zone is set at 82.5 degrees E, passing near Prayagraj (Allahabad) — so noon IST means the Sun is roughly overhead there.
  2. IPL matches for overseas fans — IPL starts at 7:30 PM IST, which is 2 PM in London and 6 AM in Los Angeles — all because of time zones.
  3. International Space Station clock — The ISS orbits Earth every 90 minutes, seeing 16 sunrises a day — astronauts use GMT to avoid confusion.
  4. New Year's celebrations — Kiribati (UTC+14) celebrates New Year first, while Baker Island (UTC-12) is last — a 26-hour difference!
  5. Northeast India's early dark — In winter, it gets dark by 4:30 PM in Guwahati but only by 6 PM in Mumbai — same IST, different solar times.
  6. IT outsourcing advantage — India's IST is perfectly placed for the 'follow the Sun' model — Indian engineers hand off work to US colleagues as their day begins.

Expert depth

The concept of standardised time zones was proposed by Sir Sandford Fleming in 1879 after missing a train due to time confusion. Before railways, every town kept its own solar time. India chose a single time zone for national unity, despite spanning nearly 30 degrees of longitude (about 2 hours of solar time difference). China, spanning 62 degrees, also uses a single zone (Beijing Time). In contrast, Russia uses 11 time zones. The International Date Line at 180 degrees longitude is where the calendar date changes — cross it westward and you skip ahead a day; cross it eastward and you repeat a day.
