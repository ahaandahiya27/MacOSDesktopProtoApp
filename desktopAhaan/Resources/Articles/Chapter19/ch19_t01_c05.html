Leap year — why we add a day every four years

Earth takes 365.25 days to orbit the Sun, so every four years we add February 29 to keep the calendar aligned with the seasons.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine your alarm clock gains 6 hours every year. After one year, it's 6 hours off. After four years, it's a whole day off! That's exactly what happens with our calendar. Earth takes 365 days AND about 6 extra hours to go around the Sun. Our calendar only counts 365 days per year, so those extra hours pile up — 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24 hours = 1 full day. So every 4 years, we add February 29 to catch up. People born on Feb 29 are called 'leaplings' — they technically have a birthday only once every 4 years!

What this concept means

The Earth's orbital period is approximately 365.2422 days (a tropical year). Since the calendar year has 365 days, the extra 0.2422 days accumulate. To correct this, a leap year with 366 days (adding February 29) occurs every 4 years. However, adding a day every 4 years slightly overcorrects (4 x 0.2422 = 0.9688, not 1.0), so century years (1900, 2100) are NOT leap years unless divisible by 400 (2000 was a leap year). This is the Gregorian calendar system, adopted worldwide.

Why it works this way

A calendar must stay synchronised with Earth's orbit so that seasons fall at the same dates each year. Since the orbital period isn't a whole number of days, periodic corrections (leap days) are needed. Without them, seasons would slowly drift through the calendar — eventually having summer in December.

Beyond the book

The year 2000 was a special case — it was a century year (normally NOT a leap year) BUT it was divisible by 400, so it WAS a leap year. The year 1900 was not a leap year, and 2100 won't be either. This triple rule (divisible by 4: yes; by 100: no; by 400: yes) is what separates the Gregorian calendar from the simpler Julian calendar. In competitive programming and coding interviews, leap year calculation is one of the most common beginner problems!

Real-world applications

  1. February 29 birthdays — Leaplings like Indian cricketer Ajay Jadeja (born Feb 29, 1971) celebrate 'real' birthdays only every 4 years.
  2. Payroll and software bugs — Many computer systems have crashed on February 29 because programmers forgot to account for leap years — it's a classic software bug.
  3. Olympics every leap year — The Summer Olympics happen every 4 years, always in a leap year (2024, 2028) — a coincidence, but easy to remember.
  4. School calendar planning — In a leap year, the school year is one day longer — 366 days means one extra day of classes (or holiday!).
  5. Agricultural season drift — Without leap years, the monsoon season would slowly drift to a different month over centuries, confusing farmers.

Expert depth

The Julian calendar (introduced by Julius Caesar in 46 BCE) added a leap day every 4 years, assuming a year was exactly 365.25 days. But the tropical year is 365.2422 days — an error of 0.0078 days/year. By 1582, the calendar had drifted 10 days from the astronomical seasons. Pope Gregory XIII corrected this with the Gregorian reform: century years are leap years only if divisible by 400. This reduces the average year to 365.2425 days — an error of just 1 day in 3,236 years. India adopted the Gregorian calendar after independence, though the Indian National Calendar (Saka calendar) also accounts for tropical year length.
