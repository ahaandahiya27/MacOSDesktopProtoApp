Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar

1910–1995 · Indian (worked in USA)

🎧 In one line

Discovered (at age 19, on a ship) the mathematical limit beyond which a dying star MUST collapse into a black hole or neutron star — won Nobel Prize in Physics 1983.

Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar was born in Lahore in 1910, in a family of Indian intellectuals (his uncle was Sir C.V. Raman, Ch.15's scientist). He studied at Presidency College Madras, then in 1930 sailed to Cambridge for graduate work. On the 18-day sea voyage, with no instruments, just paper and pen and his physics background, he calculated something astonishing: a star MORE MASSIVE than 1.44 times our Sun's mass CANNOT die as a white dwarf — it MUST collapse further, into a neutron star or black hole. This number — the CHANDRASEKHAR LIMIT — was so revolutionary that famous physicist Arthur Eddington publicly ridiculed it at a 1935 Royal Astronomical Society meeting. Chandrasekhar was crushed. He moved to the University of Chicago in 1937 and spent his life expanding stellar physics. He wrote 10+ definitive textbooks. He WAS RIGHT — by the 1960s, X-ray observations confirmed black holes; his limit became foundational. In 1983, at age 73, he received the Nobel Prize in Physics. He died in 1995. NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory (still operating 2026) is named after him. His niece, Lalita Chandrasekhar, oversaw his estate; his papers are at University of Chicago. The chapter on Earth, Moon, and Sun that Class 7 reads — the same physics, scaled up to stellar collapse, is what one Tamil scientist worked out alone on a ship at age 19.
