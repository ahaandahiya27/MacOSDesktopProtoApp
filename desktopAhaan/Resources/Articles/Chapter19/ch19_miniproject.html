Two Mini Projects for Chapter 19

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🌑 Track the Moon's phases for one full month

~14400 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A notebook + pencil
  • A clear view of the sky each evening
  • A calendar

Steps

  1. Starting on any day, look at the Moon (or sky if it's New Moon) at the same time each evening — say 7 PM.
  2. Sketch what you see: full circle? Half? Crescent? Thin sliver?
  3. Mark the date.
  4. Repeat for 29-30 days.
  5. Note: which side (left or right) is the lit part on?
  6. End of month: arrange sketches in a row. You'll see the full lunar cycle.

👀 What you should see

Crescent → first quarter → gibbous → full → gibbous waning → last quarter → crescent waning → new (invisible) → and back to crescent. Cycle takes ~29.5 days exactly. The lit side faces the Sun — so right after sunset, the lit side is to the WEST.

Why it works: You're observing the same phenomenon Indians have used for 4000 years to plan agriculture, festivals, fasts. The Hindu calendar runs on this cycle (Tithi = 1/30th of a lunar month). Your sketch becomes a personal record of cosmic mechanics — the Moon orbiting Earth, Sun lighting one side, your viewing angle changing every night. Astronomy made personal.

🌅 Build a working sundial out of cardboard

~720 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A piece of stiff cardboard (~25 cm × 25 cm)
  • A piece of straight stiff wire or stick (~10 cm — chopstick works)
  • Tape
  • Pencil and compass for marking
  • A sunny outdoor space

Steps

  1. Mark the centre of the cardboard.
  2. Fix the wire/stick VERTICALLY at the centre, perpendicular to the cardboard. Tape securely.
  3. Take to a sunny outdoor flat spot at 6 AM. Tape the cardboard down so it doesn't blow away.
  4. Every hour (6, 7, 8, ... 5, 6 PM), mark where the wire's shadow falls. Label each mark with the hour.
  5. Now you have a permanent sundial — bring it out on any sunny day, line it up with cardinal directions, and tell time.

👀 What you should see

Shadow rotates clockwise through the day (in Northern Hemisphere). Longer shadow in morning and evening (low Sun); shorter at noon (high Sun). Shadow direction precisely tracks Earth's rotation.

Why it works: A vertical stick + a flat plane + sunlight = the oldest scientific instrument in human history. Each shadow position corresponds to a specific time because Earth's rotation is uniform. The shadow LITERALLY shows you Earth spinning. Jantar Mantar's Samrat Yantra (Delhi) is a giant version of exactly this design.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
