The Solar System — Sun, 8 planets, and more

The Solar System consists of the Sun, 8 planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets, and meteoroids — all held together by the Sun's gravity.

≈ 5 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

The Sun is the boss of a cosmic family. Eight planets orbit it like children running in circles: Mercury (smallest, closest, scorching hot), Venus (Earth's twin in size but a toxic furnace), Earth (home!), Mars (the red planet ISRO visited), Jupiter (so big 1,300 Earths could fit inside), Saturn (the one with gorgeous rings), Uranus (tilted on its side), and Neptune (icy and blue, the farthest). Remember the order with: My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Noodles. There are also dwarf planets (like Pluto), millions of asteroids, and comets with spectacular tails!

What this concept means

The Solar System comprises the Sun and all objects gravitationally bound to it: eight planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune), their natural satellites, five recognised dwarf planets (including Pluto and Ceres), the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, comets, and numerous smaller bodies. The four inner planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) are rocky/terrestrial. The four outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) are gas/ice giants. The Sun contains 99.86% of the Solar System's total mass.

Why it works this way

The Solar System is held together by gravity. The Sun's enormous mass (330,000 times Earth) creates a gravitational field that keeps all 8 planets and countless smaller bodies in orbit. The planets formed from the same rotating disc of gas and dust as the Sun, which is why they orbit in the same plane and direction.

Beyond the book

India's space achievements in the Solar System are remarkable: Chandrayaan-1 found water on the Moon (2008), Mangalyaan orbited Mars on the first attempt (2014), Chandrayaan-3 landed near the lunar south pole (2023), and Aditya-L1 reached the Sun-Earth L1 point (2024). ISRO is now planning Shukrayaan (Venus orbiter) and Mangalyaan-2. Fun fact: if you could drive a car at highway speed (100 km/h) non-stop to the Sun, it would take about 170 years. To Neptune, it would take over 5,000 years! The Solar System is incomprehensibly vast.

Real-world applications

  1. Mangalyaan's budget miracle — ISRO's Mars Orbiter Mission reached Mars in 2014 for $74 million — cheaper than an auto-rickshaw ride per kilometre compared to NASA's MAVEN.
  2. Planet mnemonic — My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Noodles — Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.
  3. Saturn's rings in a telescope — Even a basic 50x telescope shows Saturn's rings — one of the most stunning sights in amateur astronomy.
  4. Mars rovers searching for life — NASA's Perseverance rover is searching for signs of ancient microbial life on Mars — the biggest scientific question of our era.
  5. Pluto's demotion in 2006 — Pluto was reclassified as a 'dwarf planet' in 2006 — it hasn't cleared its orbital neighbourhood, a requirement for full planet status.
  6. Jupiter protects Earth — Jupiter's massive gravity deflects many asteroids and comets that might otherwise hit Earth — it's our cosmic shield.

Expert depth

The Solar System formed ~4.6 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of a molecular cloud. Conservation of angular momentum flattened the cloud into a protoplanetary disc, explaining why all planets orbit in roughly the same plane (the ecliptic) and direction (prograde). The frost line (~3 AU) separated the inner rocky planets (where volatiles evaporated) from the outer gas giants (where ices could accumulate). India's Mars Orbiter Mission (Mangalyaan, 2014) made ISRO the first Asian space agency to reach Mars orbit, at a cost of $74 million — less than the movie 'Gravity'. India's upcoming Venus mission (Shukrayaan) and Aditya-L1 solar observatory continue expanding our Solar System knowledge.
