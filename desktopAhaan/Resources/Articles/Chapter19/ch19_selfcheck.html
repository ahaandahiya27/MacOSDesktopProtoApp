Question 1 — from Chapter 19

What causes day and night on Earth?

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: The rotation of the Earth on its axis causes day and night. The side facing the Sun has day, and the opposite side has night.

Why: Earth spins (rotates) on its axis from west to east.

Question 2 — from Chapter 19

What is the solstice? Name the two solstices and their approximate dates.

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: A solstice is the day when one hemisphere receives the maximum or minimum sunlight due to Earth's axial tilt. The two solstices are: Summer Solstice (21 June — longest day in Northern Hemisphere) and Winter Solstice (22 December — shortest day in Northern Hemisphere).

Why: The word 'solstice' means 'Sun stands still' — the Sun reaches its highest/lowest noon position and appears to pause before reversing direction.

Question 3 — from Chapter 19

Spring tides occur when:

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: The Sun, Moon, and Earth are in a straight line (during new moon or full moon)

Why: Spring tides have nothing to do with the spring season — 'spring' here means 'to leap' (tides are extra high).

Question 4 — from Chapter 19

Stars appear to twinkle at night. Do they really twinkle? Explain the reason.

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: No, stars do not actually twinkle. They appear to twinkle because their light passes through Earth's atmosphere, which has layers of air at different temperatures and densities. These layers constantly refract (bend) the starlight in changing directions, causing the star's brightness to fluctuate rapidly — creating the twinkling effect.

Why: Stars are point sources of light (very far away, appearing as dots).

Question 5 — from Chapter 19

Write a detailed account of India's contributions to space exploration and astronomy, from ancient times to modern ISRO missions. Discuss how ancient Indian astronomers like Aryabhata contributed to our understanding of the cosmos, and how ISRO has continued this legacy with missions like Chandrayaan, Mangalyaan, and Aditya-L1.

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: India has a rich astronomical heritage spanning over 2,500 years. Ancient Indian astronomers made remarkable contributions: Aryabhata (476–550 CE) correctly stated that Earth rotates on its axis, calculated its circumference to within 0.27% accuracy, computed the year's length to within 3 minutes of the modern value, and explained eclipses as shadow phenomena — rejecting the Rahu-Ketu myth. Varahamihira (505–587 CE) compiled astronomical knowledge in the Panchasiddhantika. Brahmagupta (598–668 CE) advanced gravitational concepts and mathematical astronomy. The Surya Siddhanta, an ancient text, provided algorithms for planetary positions and eclipse predictions using trigonometry. Indian astronomy invented the concept of zero and the decimal system, which revolutionised mathematical calculations globally. In the modern era, ISRO (founded 1969, building on INCOSPAR from 1962) has carried forward this legacy spectacularly. India launched its first satellite Aryabhata (1975), named after the ancient astronomer. The PSLV and GSLV rockets became reliable workhorses. Chandrayaan-1 (2008) discovered water on the Moon. Mangalyaan (2014) reached Mars on the first attempt at a fraction of Western mission costs. Chandrayaan-3 (2023) achieved the first-ever south pole lunar landing. Aditya-L1 (2023) is studying the Sun from the L1 Lagrange point. Gaganyaan (planned) will be India's first crewed spaceflight. India's journey from Aryabhata's naked-eye observations to ISRO's interplanetary missions represents one of humanity's most inspiring scientific arcs.

Why: Step 1: Ancient foundations — Vedanga Jyotisha (~1400 BCE) was one of the earliest astronomical texts, used for timing rituals based on Sun and Moon positions.
