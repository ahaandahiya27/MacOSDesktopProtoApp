Beyond the Book — Three Mind-Stretchers About Fibre to Fabric

Three ideas that don't show up in the NCERT textbook but will change how you see this chapter.

≈ 9 min read · curious-kid level

The chapter teaches you where do the wool sweater your grandmother knitted and the silk saree your mother wears come from? both are animal fibres. This article goes a step further — three stretch topics that lead naturally into Class 8 and beyond. Read one a day; don't try to absorb them all at once.

1. Synthetic polymers — how nylon and polyester replaced wool and silk

In Class 7 you compared natural fibres (wool, silk) vs synthetic (nylon, polyester). Class 8 explains the chemistry. SYNTHETIC fibres are POLYMERS — long chains made by linking thousands of small molecules called monomers. Nylon was invented at DuPont in 1935 — its monomers are diamine + dicarboxylic acid. Polyester (Indian Reliance produces millions of tonnes) is made from terephthalic acid + ethylene glycol. The chains are spun while molten, drawn into threads, and cooled. Result: a fibre 5× stronger than wool, water-resistant, cheap to make. But — petroleum-derived (non-renewable), microplastic shedding (every wash pollutes water), 200+ year decomposition. Indian textile mills now mix natural + synthetic (poly-cotton) to balance the trade-offs.

2. Polymerisation reactions — how monomers become fibres

Class 8 shows that synthetic fibres are polymers. Class 9 reveals HOW the polymerisation happens. TWO main reaction types. ADDITION polymerisation: ethylene (C₂H₄) units add to each other in a chain, no byproducts. Polyethylene (plastic bags, water bottles) is made this way. CONDENSATION polymerisation: two different monomers join, releasing a small molecule (usually water) per link. Nylon-6,6 is condensation — adipic acid + hexamethylenediamine releases water. The Indian textile industry's polyester is also condensation polymerisation. WHY this matters: addition polymers can't be easily depolymerised (recycled); condensation polymers CAN be hydrolysed back to monomers. That's why nylon recycling is growing in Mumbai's textile circular economy.

3. Keratin and fibroin — the proteins behind wool and silk

In Class 7 you learned wool comes from sheep, silk from silkworms. Class 11 reveals these aren't just 'animal fibres' — they're SPECIFIC PROTEINS with very different structures. KERATIN (wool, hair, nails) — packed with sulphur bonds that give it strength + crimp. Wool's natural curl comes from disulphide bridges. Perms in salons CHEMICALLY break and re-form these bonds. FIBROIN (silk) — a much simpler protein, mostly glycine + alanine, with VERY LONG straight chains. The straightness is why silk reflects light beautifully (regular surface = mirror-like reflection). Why silk feels cool and wool feels warm: keratin's spiral structure traps air (insulator); fibroin's straight chains transfer heat away from skin. Two animals, two protein architectures, two completely different fabric properties.
