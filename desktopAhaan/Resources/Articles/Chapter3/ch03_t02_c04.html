Ethical concerns in silk production

Traditional silk production kills millions of silkworm pupae annually. Understanding the ethics — and exploring alternatives like peace silk — is part of being a conscious consumer.

≈ 10 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Here's a difficult question: How many silkworms die to make your silk saree? The answer is stark: 2,000 to 3,000 silkworm pupae are killed to produce a single saree. Scale that globally — billions of silkworms killed annually for fabric. For most people, this reality is invisible: we see the finished saree, not the cocoons steamed at 80°C to preserve the silk fibre. But ethical consumers, vegan activists, and animal welfare advocates have forced a conversation: Is silk production cruel? Is there a more ethical way to produce silk? Are there alternatives? This article explores these difficult questions with nuance, not judgment, examining the ethics of silk production from multiple perspectives.

In this article, we examine the scale of silkworm deaths in traditional silk production, explore alternative approaches (peace silk, eri, tasar), consider the environmental costs of alternatives, and develop critical thinking about what ethical consumption means.

What this concept means

In traditional silk production, cocoons are exposed to steam or heat at 80–100°C for 5–10 minutes to kill pupae inside and prevent moths from breaking the silk fibre. The numbers are staggering: a single 1-metre silk curtain requires approximately 1,000 pupae deaths. A silk saree (roughly 6 metres) requires 6,000 pupae. A single silk dress (2.5 metres) requires 2,500 pupae. At global scale, the silk industry stifles billions of pupae annually — insects with developed nervous systems that experience pain and suffering, even if not consciously. This reality has prompted several ethical responses. Peace silk (also called "ahimsa silk," from the Sanskrit word for "non-violence") uses an alternative method: instead of stifling cocoons, the moth is allowed to pupate and emerge, breaking the cocoon naturally. The cocoon is then harvested, meaning the fibre is broken into shorter pieces that must be spun (rather than reeled), producing a coarser, less lustrous silk. Peace silk advocates argue this respects the silkworm's life cycle. However, critics point out that peace silk production is less efficient (shorter fibres, more labour, higher price), raising the question of whether it's truly "peaceful" or simply a marketing concept. Additionally, some peace silk producers still use pesticides and industrial methods that harm insects and ecosystems. Eri silk and tasar silk offer middle grounds: eri silkworms are traditionally allowed to emerge before cocoon processing (non-violent by definition), while tasar silkworms are harvested from semi-wild forests where ecological impact is debated.

Why it works this way

The practice of stifling cocoons is a rational (if ethically troubling) economic decision. Preserving the continuous 1,200-metre fibre maximizes yield and quality — one cocoon produces one pure, lustrous yarn strand. If the moth emerges and breaks the cocoon, the fibre fragments into 100–200 short pieces that must be carded and spun, a labour-intensive, lower-quality process. This is why peace silk sarees cost 2–3 times more than traditional silk sarees. The silk industry's perspective is economic: stifling is the most efficient way to maximize profit and quality. The animal welfare perspective counters that this efficiency comes at the cost of billions of insect lives. Silkworms have 32 ganglia (nerve clusters) distributed throughout their body — a rudimentary nervous system, but a nervous system nonetheless. They respond to heat, light, and touch, suggesting some capacity for suffering. However, whether silkworms "experience" suffering as mammals do is philosophically debated. The ethical tension arises because we value silk's beauty and utility, yet we're uncomfortable with the means of production. This discomfort has spawned alternatives: peace silk (letting moths emerge), eri silk (inherently non-violent), lab-grown spider silk (no insects involved), and synthetic silk-like fibres (plastic-based, environmentally problematic). Each alternative has trade-offs — none is ethically perfect.

💡 Did you know?

In Jainism, one of India's ancient religions, non-violence (ahimsa) toward all living beings is a core principle. Some Jain communities have historically avoided wearing silk for this reason, viewing the production process as causing suffering. This religious perspective has influenced peace silk development — "ahimsa silk" is explicitly named after the Jain principle. Interestingly, Jain philosophy also acknowledges that all human activities cause some harm to living beings (even harvesting plants kills insects), making the ethics of consumption deeply complex and nuanced.

Real-world applications

  1. Peace silk saree market growth — An ethical consumer buys a peace silk saree at 2–3 times the price of traditional silk; it supports small-scale, moth-friendly sericulture and directly challenges conventional practice.
  2. Scale of pupa deaths — A silk mill steams 10,000 cocoons daily to kill pupae; mathematically, that's ~30 million silkworm deaths per factory annually. Multiply by hundreds of mills globally — billions of insects killed yearly.
  3. Vegan textile activism — Vegan activists argue all insect deaths are unethical; they wear organic cotton instead, unaware that conventional cotton farming kills millions of insects through pesticide application (estimated 1 billion+ insect deaths per cotton harvest).
  4. Industrial scale ethics — A 1-metre silk curtain requires ~1,000 pupae deaths; multiply by global fashion demand — millions of insects killed daily. This industrial scale makes the ethical question unavoidable for conscious consumers.
  5. Eri and tasar ethical alternatives — Eri and tasar silkworms are traditionally allowed to pupate and emerge before cocoon processing, making them inherently "peace" silks. However, they're coarser and less lustrous than mulberry, limiting their market appeal.
  6. Peace silk certification complexity — Peace silk certification aims to ensure ethical production, but standards vary. Some peace silk producers still use pesticides, monoculture mulberry plantations, or exploitative labour practices. True ethics requires examining the entire supply chain.
  7. Lab-grown spider silk biotechnology — Companies like Bolt Threads and AMSilk produce silk from fermented bacteria (using spider-silk protein genes), eliminating insect deaths entirely. Commercially available since 2020, but prices are still high (5–10x traditional silk).
  8. Environmental cost of alternatives — Synthetic silk alternatives (polyester, acrylic) use petroleum and generate microplastic pollution. Organic cotton requires massive water use and land (1.5 tonnes of water per kg of cotton). Is lab-grown silk more ethical? Likely yes, but energy and resource questions remain.
  9. Consumer awareness gaps — Most silk consumers are unaware of the pupa-killing practice. Once informed, many feel ethical discomfort but face practical trade-offs: peace silk is expensive, lab-grown silk is not yet mainstream, and other natural fibres have their own environmental costs.
  10. Cultural and economic complexity — In India and Asia, sericulture employs millions in rural areas, often women and marginalized communities. Shifting away from conventional silk production without providing alternative livelihoods raises its own ethical questions about economic justice.

🌍 In ethical consumer choices

Ethical consumption is rarely black-and-white. A traditional silk saree supports millions of farmers and workers in rural India, generating livelihoods that wouldn't exist otherwise. A peace silk saree respects insect life but costs 3x more, making it inaccessible to many. Lab-grown silk avoids insect deaths but depends on industrial biotechnology and currently carries high environmental costs. Organic cotton requires enormous water use in already water-stressed regions. The question isn't "Which is perfectly ethical?" (none are) but "Which trade-offs align with my values?" Understanding the full picture — the lives supported, the insects involved, the environmental impacts — transforms you from a passive consumer into someone making informed choices.

🧪 Try this at home

Research peace silk, traditional silk, and eri silk online. If you can, find samples or images of each. Compare the texture, lustre, and colour. Notice how peace silk is coarser and matte compared to traditional silk's fine lustre. Consider: What would you be willing to pay for ethical production? How do you balance aesthetic quality against ethical concerns? Discuss with family or friends — ethical consumption involves difficult trade-offs without perfect answers.

Beyond the textbook

The future of ethical silk production likely involves multiple approaches: peace silk for consumers who prioritize insect welfare; eri and tasar for sustainable alternatives with lower environmental impact; and lab-grown spider silk using biotech, which eliminates insects entirely while supporting tech workers and biotech industries. Meanwhile, research into plant-based silks (cellulose-based materials mimicking silk properties) aims to create a cruelty-free, eco-friendly alternative. The conversation about ethical silk production is deepening, challenging the assumption that luxury fabrics are worth their hidden costs.

🧠 Check your understanding: How many silkworm pupae are killed to produce a single silk saree?

Answer: Traditional silk production kills 2,000–3,000 silkworm pupae per saree by steaming or baking cocoons to prevent moths from breaking the silk fibre. This practice is economically efficient but raises significant ethical concerns about insect welfare at industrial scale.

🧠 What is peace silk and how does it differ from traditional silk?

Answer: Peace (ahimsa) silk allows silk moths to pupate and emerge from cocoons naturally, avoiding pupa deaths. However, the broken cocoon fibre must be spun rather than reeled, producing coarser, less lustrous silk that costs 2–3 times more than traditional silk.

🧠 What are the ethical trade-offs in choosing between different silk types and alternatives?

Answer: Traditional silk is affordable but kills billions of pupae annually. Peace silk respects insect life but is expensive and less refined. Eri silk is inherently non-violent but coarser. Lab-grown silk avoids insects but depends on biotechnology. Organic cotton requires massive water use. There's no perfectly ethical option — each involves trade-offs between ethics, price, quality, and environmental impact.
