Processing wool — the six steps

Wool processing has six steps: shearing, scouring, sorting, picking burrs, dyeing, and rolling/spinning into yarn.

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine a sheep. Now imagine the fluffy white sweater you'll buy in winter. Between them are six steps: (1) Shearing — cutting the fleece off the sheep, like a haircut; (2) Scouring — washing the fleece in warm water to remove dirt, grease, and dead skin; (3) Sorting — separating fibres of different qualities; (4) Picking burrs — removing tiny bits of plant matter stuck in the wool; (5) Dyeing — colouring the white wool any shade you want; (6) Rolling — straightening and spinning the fibres into long threads (yarn) ready for weaving. The whole journey takes weeks.

In this article, we explore the science, significance, and real-world applications of processing wool — the six steps.

What this concept means

The processing of wool from raw fleece to wearable yarn involves the following sequential steps. (1) Shearing — the fleece along with a thin layer of skin is removed from the sheep's body, usually once a year in spring or summer. (2) Scouring — the sheared wool is washed thoroughly in warm water to remove dust, grease, dirt, dead cells, and vegetable matter. (3) Sorting — the cleaned wool is sorted into different grades based on texture, length, and colour. (4) Picking out burrs — small bits of plant material that remain are removed by hand or machine. (5) Dyeing — the natural creamy-white wool is dyed in various colours. (6) Rolling — the dyed fibres are straightened, combed, and rolled into yarn that can be woven into fabric.

Why it works this way

Raw fleece is dirty, oily, full of plant fragments, and tangled. To turn it into a smooth thread, each problem has to be solved separately — washing, sorting, debris removal, colouring, alignment. Skipping any step leaves a defect in the final yarn.

💡 Did you know?

Modern industrial wool processing adds two more steps: carbonising (treatment with sulphuric acid to dissolve any plant matter that scouring missed) and carding (mechanical combing using fine-toothed rollers to align all fibres in the same direction). Each step has its own equipment and waste stream. Scouring effluent is heavily polluted with grease (lanolin) and detergent — modern mills recover the lanolin and sell it as a by-product (the same lanolin that ends up in your moisturising cream). Sustainability-conscious mills run closed-loop water systems.

Real-world applications

  1. Shearing day — Watching a shearer use clippers to remove fleece in one piece without harming the sheep
  2. Scouring at the mill — Hot water and detergent removes lanolin (grease) so wool changes from yellowish to white
  3. Sorting woolly fleece — Workers sort wool by quality; coarser fibres go to blankets, fine to clothing
  4. Carding machines — Steel rollers comb parallel wool fibres so they bind together properly during spinning
  5. Spinning thread — Threads are twisted together to create yarn strength; loose fibres would unravel
  6. Dyeing wool red — Wool absorbs plant dyes (madder, indigo) differently than cotton or synthetic fibres
  7. Felting jumper — Accidentally washing wool in hot water causes fibres to lock together, shrinking the jumper
  8. Spinning wheel — A grandmother spins raw fleece into thread on a traditional spinning wheel at home
  9. Weaving loom — A weaver interlaces wool warp and weft to create blankets and carpets
  10. Knitting sweater — A knitter converts spun wool yarn into a fitted sweater using needles and loops

🌍 In your world

Robotic shearing is being trialled in Australia (the world's largest wool producer) — sensors map the sheep's body in 3D, and a robotic arm shears with computer-controlled pressure. Faster than humans, but adoption is slow because skilled shearers still produce a smoother cut.
