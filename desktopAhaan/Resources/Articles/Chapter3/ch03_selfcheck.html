Question 3 — from Chapter 3

A textile lab measures fibre length under a microscope. Sample A: average 4 cm, uniform. Sample B: 2 cm with some 5 cm strays. Which sample makes BETTER yarn and why? Connect to spinning physics.

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: Sample A makes better yarn. Spinning relies on FRICTION between overlapping fibres twisted together. Uniform 4 cm fibres overlap consistently — every fibre in the yarn is held by many neighbours along its full length, giving smooth, strong yarn. Sample B's 2 cm fibres have shorter overlap per fibre = weaker holds; the stray 5 cm fibres create lumps where they protrude from the yarn surface. Uniformity matters as much as length — actually MORE, for industrial spinning.

Why: Spinning = friction-locked fibre overlap.

Question 5 — from Chapter 3

You're designing a uniform for a hill-station school where winter temperatures drop to 5°C and summer rises to 25°C. Pick the fibre composition (wool, cotton, polyester) for each season and JUSTIFY your choice using fibre properties.

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: Winter: 70% wool + 30% polyester blend. Wool traps air for insulation and absorbs moisture without feeling wet; polyester adds durability and faster drying. Pure wool is warmer but heavy + slow-drying. Summer: 60% cotton + 40% polyester. Cotton breathes (loose weave + water-absorbing) for cooling sweat away; polyester adds wrinkle-resistance and washability. Pure cotton wrinkles and shrinks; pure polyester is hot. Blends balance the trade-offs each fibre can't solve alone.

Why: Winter need: trap heat + tolerate damp → wool's air-trap + moisture-buffer dominates. Add polyester for durability.
