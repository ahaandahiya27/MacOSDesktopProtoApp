Chapter 3 · Topic 1

Wool — from sheep to sweater

An exploration of wool and related concepts in textile science

Topic overview · for curious 12-year-olds

Wool is the fibre that grows on certain animals. Getting wool from a sheep into a sweater takes six careful steps, each invented over thousands of years. But wool is more than just a useful material — it's a window into biology (how animals grow hair), chemistry (keratin proteins), history (trade routes that spanned continents), geography (pastoral regions that specialize in wool production), economics (a global industry worth billions), and ethics (animal welfare in farming). This topic introduces you to all of it.

This topic introduces you to the science, applications, and global significance of wool. Whether you're interested in fashion, chemistry, geography, or ethics, you'll find concepts here that matter.

What you'll learn in this topic

  • The fundamental concepts and terminology of wool and animal fibres
  • The science behind how wool fibres form and function
  • Real-world applications in industry, commerce, and daily life
  • Global geography and economics of wool production
  • Ethical, environmental, and sustainability considerations
  • How wool connects to other scientific disciplines

The 8 concepts in this topic

  1. Animal fibres vs plant fibres Animal fibres come from animals (wool, silk); plant fibres come from plants (cotton, jute, flax).
  2. Wool-yielding animals and the fleece Wool comes from the fleece — the thick layer of hair — of animals like sheep, goat, yak, camel, llama, alpaca, and vicuna.
  3. Processing wool — the six steps Wool processing has six steps: shearing, scouring, sorting, picking burrs, dyeing, and rolling/spinning into yarn.
  4. Hazards in the wool industry — sorter's disease Wool sorters are at risk of anthrax (sorter's disease) — a deadly bacterial infection from spores in raw fleece.
  5. Types and classifications of wool Wool is classified by fibre diameter, staple length, and colour, determining its use in textiles.
  6. Hair types in sheep — guard hair and undercoat Sheep grow two types of hair: coarse guard hairs and fine undercoat, which must be separated during processing.
  7. Fabric care and wool washing science Wool requires cool water and mild detergent because hot water and agitation cause fibres to lock together, shrinking the fabric permanently.
  8. Natural plant fibres vs animal fibres comparison Plant fibres (cotton, jute, linen) are cellulose-based and breathe well; animal fibres (wool, silk) are protein-based and provide insulation.

Why this topic matters

Understanding wool is essential to understanding the modern world. Textiles are part of the foundation of human civilization — they protect us, express our identity, and connect us globally through trade and culture. The choices we make about fabrics, based on our understanding of fibre science, affect environment, economy, and society.
