Silk — the cocoon-spun fabric

An exploration of silk and sericulture in textile science

Topic overview · for curious 12-year-olds

Silk is the only natural fibre that comes from a single thread spun by an insect. The process — from silkworm egg to glittering saree — is called sericulture, and it has been perfected over 5000 years in Asia. A silkworm caterpillar eats mulberry leaves for 25 days, growing and moulting, then spins a cocoon from a continuous thread 1200 meters long — all in about two weeks. That thread is unwound, twisted with others, dyed, and woven into the most luxurious natural fabric known to humans.

This topic introduces you to the science, applications, and global significance of silk. Whether you're interested in biology, chemistry, history, or the ethics of animal farming, you'll find concepts here that matter.

What you'll learn in this topic

  • The biology of silk production and the silkworm life cycle
  • The ancient technology of reeling and weaving silk
  • Different types of silk and sericulture practices worldwide
  • The ethics and environmental impact of silk production
  • Cultural significance of silk in Asia and globally
  • The economics of the global silk industry

The 4 concepts in this topic

  1. Sericulture and the silkworm life cycle Sericulture is the rearing of silkworms (the caterpillar of the silk moth) on mulberry leaves to produce silk.
  2. Reeling, weaving, and the journey to a saree Reeling is the unwinding of silk fibre from a cocoon; weaving combines silk threads into fabric on a loom.
  3. Types of silk and sericulture worldwide Different silk types (mulberry, tasar, eri, muga) are produced from different silkworm species adapted to regional climates and plants.
  4. Ethical concerns in silk production — animal welfare Traditional silk production kills silkworm pupae to harvest whole cocoons, raising ethical questions about insect welfare and sustainability.

Why this topic matters

Silk production connects biology, chemistry, technology, culture, economics, and ethics. It demonstrates how humans have learned to domesticate insects and extract their products for luxury goods. It also raises important questions about animal welfare, environmental sustainability, and fair trade. Understanding silk is understanding how ancient knowledge and modern commerce intersect.
