Chapter 3

Fibre to Fabric

Explore the science of fibres — from wool shearing to silk reeling to spider silk engineering

Chapter overview · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine this: a sheep grazes in a meadow. Its fleece grows thick and curly. A farmer shears it, washes it, combs it, spins it into yarn, and weaves it into a warm sweater that ends up in your closet. Or imagine this: a silkworm caterpillar munches mulberry leaves for weeks, then spins a cocoon from a single thread 1200 meters long. That thread is unwound, twisted, dyed, woven, and becomes a glittering saree worn at celebrations. Or this: in a chemistry lab, petroleum is transformed into polyester through a series of reactions, and that polyester is spun into yarn for your sports clothes.

This chapter is your guide to understanding textiles from the molecular level to the global supply chain. We'll explore animal fibres, plant fibres, synthetic fibres, and even engineered fibres from spider silk. We'll see how fibres are processed, classified, and used. And we'll grapple with the ethics and environmental impact of textile production.

By the end of this chapter, you'll never look at a piece of fabric the same way again.

The 3 topics in this chapter

1

Wool — from sheep to sweater

Wool is the fibre that grows on certain animals. Getting wool from a sheep into a sweater takes six careful steps, each invented over thousands of years.
2

Silk — the cocoon-spun fabric

Silk is the only natural fibre that comes from a single thread spun by an insect. The process — from silkworm egg to glittering saree — is called sericulture.
3

Synthetic vs Natural Fibres & Global Textile Industry

Compare synthetic and natural fibres, explore their environmental footprint, and discover how the global textile industry connects science to everyday life.

A brief history of textile innovation

  • 7000 BCE Cotton domestication in the Indus Valley (India)
  • 2700 BCE Discovery of silk in China; sericulture begins
  • 3000 BCE Sheep domestication in Mesopotamia; wool production begins
  • 1200 BCE Merino sheep bred in Spain for fine wool
  • 1733 John Kay invents the flying shuttle, revolutionizing weaving speed
  • 1769 James Hargreaves invents the Spinning Jenny; textile industrialisation begins
  • 1938 Nylon invented by DuPont; first fully synthetic fibre
  • 1941 Polyester invented; dominates modern textiles
  • 2013 Rana Plaza factory collapse in Bangladesh kills 1,134 workers, sparking fast-fashion ethics debate
  • 2017 Genetically modified spider silk developed at labs (Nature 2017); bioengineered fabrics emerge

Why this chapter matters

Textiles are woven into human history and daily life. From the cotton that clothed ancient India to the wool that warmed medieval Europe to the synthetic fibres that enable modern athletics — fibres tell the story of human ingenuity, trade, culture, and our relationship with nature. Understanding textiles is understanding civilization.
