Q1. You must be familiar with the following nursery rhyme: 'Baa Baa, black sheep, have you any wool?' (a) Which parts of the black sheep have wool? (b) What is meant by the white fleece of the lamb?

Model answer: (a) Wool comes from the fleece — the thick, curly hair covering the sheep's whole body, especially the back, sides, and belly. (b) White fleece means the lamb's hair is naturally white in colour. White wool is the most prized because it can be dyed any colour; black or grey wool can only be dyed to darker shades.

Q2. The silkworm is (a) a caterpillar (b) a larva. Choose the correct option: (i) only a (ii) only b (iii) both a and b (iv) neither a nor b.

Model answer: (iii) Both a and b. 'Caterpillar' is the everyday name for the larva of any moth or butterfly — so a silkworm IS a caterpillar AND it is a larva. The two words describe the same stage of the silk-moth life cycle.

Q3. Which of the following does not yield wool? (i) Yak (ii) Camel (iii) Goat (iv) Woolly dog. Why?

Model answer: (iv) Woolly dog. Dogs have fur, but their hair lacks the scales and crimp that make wool spinnable; their fibre doesn't lock into yarn. The other three — yak (Himalayan plateaus), camel (Rajasthan, Central Asia), goat (pashmina, mohair, cashmere) — all produce real wool.

Q4. What is meant by the following terms? (i) Rearing (ii) Shearing (iii) Sericulture

Model answer: (i) Rearing — taking care of animals to grow them up; sheep, silkworms, cattle are all reared on farms. (ii) Shearing — removing the fleece (wool-covered skin layer) from the sheep using clippers, usually once a year in spring; the sheep is not hurt because the wool is dead tissue, like cutting your hair. (iii) Sericulture — the practice of rearing silkworms specifically to produce silk fibre.

Q5. Given below is a sequence of steps in the processing of wool. Which are the missing steps? Shearing, ______, sorting, ______, ______, ______.

Model answer: Shearing → Scouring → Sorting → Picking burrs (removing burs and dirt) → Dyeing → Rolling and spinning into yarn. Six steps total.

Q6. Make sketches of the two stages in the life history of the silk moth which are directly related to the production of silk.

Model answer: The two stages directly involved are: (1) the larva / silkworm (the caterpillar that eats mulberry leaves for ~25 days then begins spinning a silk cocoon around itself), and (2) the pupa inside the cocoon (the silk thread surrounding the pupa is what's reeled to make silk). The cocoon is boiled (with the pupa inside) to soften the gum holding the silk threads together, then the single long thread is unwound — one cocoon yields about 600–900 metres of silk.

Q7. Out of the following, which are the two terms related to silk production? Sericulture, floriculture, moriculture, apiculture, silviculture. (Hint: tasar silk is obtained from a silkworm that feeds on arjun leaves.)

Model answer: Sericulture (rearing silkworms) and moriculture (growing mulberry trees, since mulberry silkworms feed only on mulberry leaves). Floriculture is flower growing, apiculture is beekeeping, and silviculture is forest growing — none of these are silk.

Q8. What is the difference between natural and synthetic fibres? Give an example of each.

Model answer: Natural fibres come from plants or animals and biodegrade easily — examples: cotton (from cotton plant), wool (from sheep), silk (from silkworms). Synthetic fibres are made from petroleum-based chemicals in factories — examples: polyester, nylon, acrylic. Natural fibres breathe better, regulate temperature, and decompose naturally; synthetic fibres are cheaper, stronger, wrinkle-resistant but don't biodegrade and shed microplastics in every wash.
