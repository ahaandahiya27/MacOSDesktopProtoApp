Types of silk and sericulture worldwide

Four types of silk — mulberry, tasar, eri, and muga — each from a different silkworm species, each suited to its region, each with unique properties.

≈ 10 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Not all silk comes from the same silkworm. The silkworm you learned about — Bombyx mori — produces mulberry silk, the finest and most lustrous. But in India, tribal communities and traditional weavers cultivate three other silkworm species: tasar in the dry forests of central India, eri in Assam and northeast India, and muga in the wetlands of Assam. Each species evolved to survive in its specific climate and feed on its native host plants. Each produces silk with different properties — tasar is rough and earthy, eri is soft but coarse, muga is naturally golden without dyeing. Understanding these four silks is key to understanding regional textile traditions across India and the world, and how geography, climate, and ecology shape what kind of fabric a region can produce.

In this article, we explore each silk type, the regions where they're produced, and the ecological and cultural significance of each.

What this concept means

Silk production is not limited to Bombyx mori (mulberry silkworm). Four main types of silk are produced commercially: mulberry, tasar, eri, and muga. Mulberry silk (from Bombyx mori) is produced wherever mulberry trees grow — especially in warm, humid lowlands. In India, the main producers are Karnataka (accounting for ~60% of national mulberry silk production), Tamil Nadu, and Andhra Pradesh. Tasar silk comes from silkworms (genus Antheraea) that feed on sal, oak, or ber forest leaves. Tasar is produced in dry forests of central India, particularly in Odisha, Jharkhand, and Chhattisgarh. It's a coarser, earthier silk with a rustic appeal. Eri silk is produced from silkworms that feed on castor leaves (Ricinus communis), found throughout northeast India and increasingly in Assam. Eri silk is soft but has a coarser texture than mulberry. Muga silk is the rarest and most precious; it comes from silkworms (Antheraea assamensis) native to Assam's wetlands, which feed on Som and Sualu leaves. Muga silk has a naturally golden colour that deepens with age — no dyeing is needed. Each silk type requires different sericulture practices, different climate conditions, and different market prices. Mulberry silk is the most affordable (and most abundant globally). Muga is the most expensive and is closely guarded as a cultural treasure in Assam.

Why it works this way

Each silk type evolved for its specific ecological niche and climate. Mulberry silkworms (Bombyx mori) originated in China and thrive in warm, humid lowlands with abundant mulberry cultivation — they produce the finest silk because they've been selectively bred for 4,000 years for that trait. Tasar silkworms (Antheraea species) evolved in India's dry deciduous forests where sal and oak trees provide their food. Tasar is coarser because these wild silkworms are less refined than domesticated mulberry silkworms; they're also hardier, requiring less intensive management. Eri silkworms evolved to feed on castor leaves and tolerate a wider range of temperatures than mulberry. Muga silkworms are native only to Assam's wetlands and feed exclusively on Som and Sualu leaves — they cannot survive outside this narrow ecological niche, making muga silk irreplaceable and deeply tied to Assamese cultural identity. The climate and geography of each region directly influence the silk type: Karnataka's warm, humid lowlands are perfect for mulberry's 4–5 crops per year. Odisha's dry forests support tasar's 1–2 crops. Assam's humid climate and specific vegetation support muga's highly specialized ecological requirements. This is why traditional silk production was (and remains) geographically bound — each region naturally produces the silk type best suited to its ecology.

💡 Did you know?

Muga silk is so prized in Assamese culture that a muga saree is considered a symbol of wealth and is often passed down through families as a heirloom. A pure muga saree can cost 10,000–50,000+ rupees and is a treasure that may be worn only once or twice in a lifetime — for weddings, important ceremonies, or festivals. The naturally golden colour of muga silk deepens over years of storage and wear, becoming even more lustrous. Many Assamese families have muga sarees that have been in the family for 100+ years, still beautiful and precious.

Real-world applications

  1. Mulberry sericulture in Karnataka — Karnataka's warm, humid climate allows 4–5 crops of mulberry silkworms per year; it's the world's leading mulberry silk producer, employing millions in farming, rearing, and processing.
  2. Tasar forest cocoons in Odisha — Tribal communities in Odisha's forests harvest tasar cocoons from sal and oak trees. Tasar production is semi-wild: the forests are managed carefully, but the silkworms feed on naturally available leaves, making it a low-chemical, sustainable practice.
  3. Golden muga saree tradition in Assam — An Assamese bride wears a muga silk saree with an inherent golden colour — no dyeing needed. The saree shimmers with a unique lustre that cannot be replicated in other silk types.
  4. Eri silk ethics — Eri silk's coarser texture suits shawls and furnishing fabrics. Uniquely, eri silkworms are not killed during cocoon harvesting; the moth is allowed to pupate and emerge, breaking the cocoon naturally. This makes eri "peace silk," appealing to ethically conscious consumers.
  5. Climate adaptation of each species — Mulberry needs 20–25°C year-round and high humidity; tasar tolerates 15–30°C and lower humidity; muga thrives in Assam's humid climate (15–35°C). Each species is physiologically adapted to its native climate — one cannot simply move muga production to Karnataka.
  6. Domestic use and ceremonial importance — Mulberry silk is everyday silk — affordable enough for daily wear and widely available. Tasar sarees are popular in central and eastern India, comfortable for warm, dry climates. Muga and eri sarees are cherished in Assam, with muga being ceremonial and eri being more practical.
  7. Market pricing and value — Mulberry silk: 500–1,500 rupees per saree (affordable). Tasar silk: 1,500–5,000 rupees (mid-range). Eri silk: 2,000–6,000 rupees. Muga silk: 10,000–50,000+ rupees (luxury). Prices reflect rarity, processing difficulty, and cultural significance.
  8. Global silk production distribution — China dominates mulberry silk (60–70% of global output). India ranks second in mulberry but is the world's largest producer of tasar and muga (90%+ of global supply). This gives India significant cultural and economic importance in luxury silk markets.
  9. Sustainability and traditional practices — Tasar and muga production involves minimal chemical inputs compared to intensively managed mulberry. Tribal communities in Odisha have practiced sustainable tasar harvesting for centuries, managing forests carefully to maintain cocoon yields without depleting the ecosystem.
  10. Regional textile identity — Each silk type is deeply woven into regional identity: Banaras silk sarees (mulberry) are synonymous with northern Indian bridal wear; Kanchipuram silk (mulberry) with southern Tamil weddings; Assamese muga with Assamese cultural pride; Odisha tasar with tribal heritage.

🌍 In your region's traditions

If you're in Karnataka or Tamil Nadu, mulberry silk sarees and dhotis are woven daily on handlooms in Kanchipuram and Banaras. In Odisha or Chhattisgarh, tribal communities wear tasar sarees that connect them to the dry forests their ancestors have inhabited for millennia. In Assam, a muga saree isn't just clothing — it's a statement of cultural identity, worn with pride in ceremonies and treasured as family wealth. Understanding these regional silks transforms you from someone who buys fabric into someone who understands geography, ecology, history, and cultural meaning woven into every thread.

🧪 Try this at home

If you can find examples of different silk types (mulberry, tasar, eri, muga), compare them: Feel the texture and lustre. Mulberry should feel the finest and smoothest. Tasar should feel coarser and more rustic. Eri should feel soft but coarse. Muga should have a warm, golden colour and a distinctive lustre. If you can't find samples, search online for images of muga sarees and notice the distinctive golden colour that no dye can replicate — this is the natural colour of muga fibroin protein.

Beyond the textbook

Climate change is affecting traditional sericulture. Rising temperatures in Karnataka are stressing mulberry silkworms; traditional pest-control methods are becoming less effective; and water scarcity threatens mulberry cultivation. In Assam, changing rainfall patterns and flooding are threatening muga habitat. Scientists are researching climate-resilient silkworm strains and exploring how to expand production to new regions. Simultaneously, conservation efforts aim to protect Assam's muga forests and Odisha's tasar forests as both ecological and cultural treasures.

🧠 Check your understanding: What are the four main types of silk?

Answer: Mulberry (from Bombyx mori, produced in warm lowlands), tasar (from Antheraea species, from dry forests), eri (from castor-eating worms, northeast India), and muga (from Antheraea assamensis, only in Assam). Each comes from a different silkworm species suited to its region's climate and ecology.

🧠 Why is muga silk so precious and why is it native only to Assam?

Answer: Muga silkworms feed exclusively on Som and Sualu leaves found only in Assam's specific wetland ecosystem. They cannot survive outside this narrow ecological niche. The naturally golden colour, exceptional lustre, and rarity make muga deeply significant to Assamese culture and identity.

🧠 How does climate affect which silk type is produced in each region?

Answer: Mulberry needs consistent warmth (20–25°C) and high humidity, allowing 4–5 crops/year in Karnataka. Tasar tolerates drier, cooler conditions (15–30°C) and feeds on forest trees, yielding 1–2 crops. Muga requires Assam's specific humid climate and native vegetation. Geography and ecology determine what silk each region naturally produces.
