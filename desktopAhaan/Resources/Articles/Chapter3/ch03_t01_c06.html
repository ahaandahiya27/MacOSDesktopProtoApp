Hair types in sheep — guard hair and undercoat

Sheep grow two types of hair: coarse guard hairs and fine undercoat, which must be separated during processing.

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Sheep have two layers of hair like you wearing an outer rain jacket over a soft jumper. The outer guard hairs are wiry and protect; the inner undercoat is soft and warm. Sorting these is crucial — mixing them ruins the final fabric quality.

In this article, we explore the science of guard hair and undercoat in sheep fleece.

What this concept means

Sheep fleece comprises two distinct hair types: medullated guard hairs (coarse, 20–50 µm, with air-filled hollow cores) and cortical fibres of the undercoat (fine, 15–25 µm, solid). Guard hairs account for 5–25% of fleece. Fine wool breeds like Merino have less than 5% medullated fibres; coarse breeds like Lincoln have 20% or more.

Why it works this way

Guard hairs evolved to protect sheep from rain and physical damage; undercoat provides insulation. The hollow structure of guard hair medulla makes them lighter and waterproof. During processing, mechanical separation (carding, combing) exploits these structural differences — coarser, stiff guard hairs behave differently from soft undercoat fibres, allowing machines to separate them.

💡 Did you know?

The medullary index — the proportion of the hollow core to total fibre diameter — determines wool quality. Guard hairs have high medullary indices (40%+); undercoat has almost none. Under a microscope, medullated fibres appear dark (the air scatters light), while solid cortical fibres appear bright. This difference is how wool sorters and quality-control systems classify and separate fibres mechanically.

Real-world applications

  1. Hand sorting by touch — A wool sorter can feel the difference: soft undercoat goes to fine jumpers, coarse guard hair to carpets
  2. Mechanical carding — Steel-toothed rollers trap coarse guard hairs; fine undercoat fibres pass through
  3. Microscopic inspection — Under magnification, hollow medulla in guard hairs is visible; solid cortex in undercoat is clear
  4. Softness and comfort — Just 5% coarse fibres mixed in makes wool feel itchy; 100% fine undercoat feels luxurious
  5. Dehairing process — Industrial mills use enzymatic or chemical scouring to remove kempy fibres (like guard hairs) — essential for premium yarn
  6. Colour and dyeing — Medullated guard hairs dye differently than solid undercoat, creating uneven colour if not separated first
  7. Tensile strength — Guard hairs provide structural strength to yarn; pure undercoat alone is weaker — blends balance softness and durability
  8. Breed selection in farming — Farmers choose Merino sheep (minimal medullated fibres) for fine wool; Lincoln sheep for coarser, stronger wool
  9. Industrial recycling — Removed guard hairs become low-grade felt, insulation, or non-woven textiles — maximizing value from every fleece
  10. Fleece colour patterns — Dark medullated hairs make wool appear grey; their removal reveals white undercoat beneath, affecting final product appearance

🌍 In your world

Your wool sweater exists because someone sorted guard hair from undercoat. If those two types were mixed, your sweater would itch. Understanding this two-layer system shows why every step of textile processing matters — even invisible ones like fibre sorting determine whether your clothing feels luxurious or scratchy.

🧪 Try this at home

Find a wool garment and examine its label. Look up the micron count if possible. Lower micron count means finer undercoat with fewer medullated fibres. Rub it on your neck — does it feel soft or scratchy? That's because of guard hair content.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: What are the two types of hair in sheep fleece?

Answer: Medullated guard hairs (coarse, 20–50 µm, with air-filled hollow cores) and cortical undercoat fibres (fine, 15–25 µm, solid).

🧠 Why does this matter?

Question: Why must guard hair be separated from undercoat during processing?

Answer: Mixing them creates uneven texture, colour, and dye uptake. Pure undercoat feels soft; guard hairs feel coarse and itchy — the final fabric quality depends on proper separation.

🧠 How can you tell the difference?

Question: How do machines distinguish guard hair from undercoat?

Answer: Mechanical carding uses different-sized rollers to trap coarse, stiff guard hairs while fine, flexible undercoat fibres pass through.
