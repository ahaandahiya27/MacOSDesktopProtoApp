Reeling cocoons into silk fibre

From a single cocoon spinning 1,200 metres of raw silk to twisting, dyeing, and weaving — the journey from cocoon to saree is a marvel of precision and craft.

≈ 10 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Pick up a Banaras silk saree or a Kanchipuram silk garment. Feel its weight, its sheen, its lustre. Now imagine the journey that fibre took: from a cocoon the size of a peanut, containing a continuous thread of silk 1,200 metres long, to the finished fabric wrapped around you. The process requires precision machinery, chemistry (dyeing), skill (weaving), and generations of accumulated knowledge. In India, this knowledge lives in the hands of reel operators in Karnataka's sericulture mills, dyers in Varanasi's silk workshops, and weavers in Kanchipuram's looms. Each step — cocoon sorting, stifling, reeling, throwing, dyeing, weaving — transforms raw cocoons into the world's most luxurious fabric.

In this article, we explore each stage of silk production, from cocoon to fabric, understanding the physics and chemistry that make silk so special.

What this concept means

Raw silk production involves five main stages: stifling, sorting, reeling, throwing, and dyeing. Stifling (also called "cooking") means killing the pupae inside cocoons by steaming or baking at 80–100°C for 5–10 minutes. If the pupa is left alive, the adult moth will emerge, breaking the continuous cocoon fibre into short pieces. Dead pupae, however, preserve the cocoon intact. Next, cocoons are sorted by colour, size, and defects — damaged cocoons are discarded or used for short-fibre silk (spun silk). Reeling is the process of unwinding the cocoon fibre. A cocoon is placed in hot water (80°C) to soften the sericin (a gum-like protein binding the fibre layers). The worker finds the fibre's end and begins unwinding, attaching it to a reel. Raw silk filaments are incredibly thin — a single filament from one cocoon is too fine for weaving, so 4–8 cocoons are unwound simultaneously and their filaments are twisted together to create a single strand of "raw silk yarn" approximately 1 mm thick. This strand still contains sericin, giving it a stiff feel and dull appearance. Throwing is the next stage: twisting and doubling these raw silk strands to create the correct thickness and strength for weaving looms. Finally, the raw silk is dyed using natural dyes (madder, indigo) or synthetic dyes, then woven into fabric on power looms or handlooms.

Why it works this way

Each stage of silk production is optimized for fibre quality and yield. Stifling must be done carefully: too cool or too brief, and the moth may emerge and damage the fibre; too hot or too long, and the fibre's protein structure (fibroin) may denature and weaken. The specific temperature (80–100°C) and duration (5–10 minutes) have been empirically optimized over centuries. Hot-water rinsing to soften sericin exploits the fact that sericin is a protein that dissolves in hot water — this prepares the fibre for unwinding without mechanical damage. Reeling requires skill: the operator must find the fibre's end (which is hidden in the cocoon's overlapping layers) and unwind it smoothly. A single filament is about 1 micron in diameter — far too thin for practical use. Twisting 4–8 filaments together creates a fibre about 1 millimetre thick, with enough tensile strength for weaving. The twisted fibre is then degummed (boiled in soap and water) to remove remaining sericin, which reveals the fibre's natural lustre and softness. Finally, dyeing works because silk's fibroin protein has many reactive amino groups that bond with dye molecules, allowing silk to absorb colour more readily than plant fibres (which require mordants). This is why Varanasi silk dyers can create rich, vibrant colours that seem to glow.

💡 Did you know?

The Silk Road — a network of trade routes spanning 6,400 km from China through India to Europe — was named after silk. For 1,500 years, only China knew how to cultivate silkworms and produce silk. The technology was so secret that anyone caught smuggling silkworm eggs out of China faced execution. Only around 550 CE did sericulture knowledge finally spread to India via Buddhist monks, transforming it into a global industry. Today, India is the world's second-largest silk producer, with an industry worth over $2 billion annually.

Real-world applications

  1. Cocoon boiling (stifling) — Cocoons are steamed or baked at 80–100°C for 5–10 minutes to kill pupae and prevent moths from breaking the cocoon; a single emerging moth destroys a 1,200-metre fibre into fragments.
  2. Filament unwinding and twisting — One cocoon yields approximately 1,200 metres of raw fibre; 4–8 cocoons are unwound simultaneously and their filaments twisted together to create a workable yarn thickness.
  3. Modern silk reel machinery — Automated reeling machines can unwind 50,000 cocoons daily, each feed mechanism precisely positioned to find the fibre's end and guide it to the reel without breaking.
  4. Throwing (twisting and doubling) — Raw silk filaments must be twisted and doubled to create the correct thickness and twist angle for power looms; traditional looms require lower twist, while modern looms tolerate higher twist.
  5. Degumming baths — After twisting, silk is boiled in soap and water (degumming) to remove sericin; this reveals the fibre's natural sheen and softens the feel. Degummed silk is 25–30% lighter than raw silk.
  6. Natural dye vats in Varanasi — Varanasi's silk dyers use madder root (for reds), indigo (for blues), and turmeric (for yellows) to create the characteristic colours of Banaras silk sarees. Each dye bath involves precise temperature and timing control.
  7. Synthetic dye alternatives — Modern sericulture mills increasingly use synthetic dyes (acid dyes, reactive dyes) because they're faster, cheaper, and produce consistent colours. However, natural dye devotees argue that naturally dyed silk lasts longer and feels better against skin.
  8. Handloom weaving traditions — In Kanchipuram, weavers on traditional wooden looms produce some of the world's most expensive sarees (2,000–50,000+ rupees), combining silk from Karnataka's sericulture mills with intricate zari (gold thread) weaving.
  9. Power loom efficiency — Power looms in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka can weave silk up to 10 times faster than handlooms, making silk fabric more affordable for everyday wear. A single power loom can produce 15–20 metres of silk per day.
  10. Waste silk utilization — Cocoons that are damaged or broken yield "short-fibre silk" that cannot be reeled. Instead, this waste is carded and spun into "spun silk," which is coarser but still luxurious and much cheaper than reeled silk.

🌍 In the world's luxury wardrobes

Every high-end silk garment — from Italian designer dresses to Japanese kimonos to Indian Banaras sarees — began as a cocoon in a sericulture farm. The journey from cocoon to finished fabric involves precision equipment, chemistry, and often decades of accumulated craft knowledge. When you feel the weight and lustre of a Kanchipuram saree or a Varanasi stole, you're experiencing the result of 1,500 years of sericulture technology refined to perfection.

🧪 Try this at home

If you can obtain cocoons (from sericulture suppliers or online), try this: Place a cocoon in hot water (70–80°C) for 3–5 minutes. With tweezers, gently find the fibre's end (usually at one pole of the cocoon) and carefully unwind it. Count how many metres you can unwind before the fibre breaks. Twist multiple unwound fibres together (from 4–6 cocoons) and feel the difference between single filaments and twisted yarn. This hands-on experience demonstrates why multiple cocoons are needed to create workable silk yarn.

Beyond the textbook

Modern silk production is exploring automation and biotech alternatives. Researchers are developing genetically modified silkworms that produce coloured silk directly (eliminating the need for dyeing) or silk with enhanced properties. Meanwhile, lab-grown spider silk (produced by fermentation of engineered bacteria) is being commercialized as a cruelty-free alternative to traditional sericulture. Companies like Bolt Threads and AMSilk are partnering with luxury fashion brands (including Adidas and Stella McCartney) to develop and commercialize bio-engineered silk fabrics that match or exceed the properties of traditional silk.

🧠 Check your understanding: How much raw silk can be obtained from one cocoon?

Answer: A single cocoon yields approximately 1,200 metres of raw silk fibre, but this single filament is too thin (~1 micron) for practical use. Therefore, 4–8 cocoons are reeled simultaneously, and their filaments are twisted together to create a single workable strand of yarn.

🧠 Why is stifling (killing pupae) necessary in silk production?

Answer: If the pupa inside a cocoon is allowed to develop and emerge as a moth, it will break through the cocoon, cutting the continuous 1,200-metre fibre into short, unusable pieces. By killing the pupa with heat (80–100°C), the cocoon remains intact, preserving the continuous fibre for reeling.

🧠 What is the difference between raw silk and degummed silk?

Answer: Raw silk contains sericin (a sticky protein coating) that makes it stiff and dull. Degumming (boiling in soap and water) removes sericin, revealing the fibre's natural softness and lustre. Degummed silk is 25–30% lighter and feels significantly softer than raw silk.
