Synthetic vs Natural Fibres & Global Textile Industry

An exploration of synthetic fibres, natural alternatives, and the global textile economy

Topic overview · for curious 12-year-olds

In 1938, a chemist named Wallace Carothers at DuPont invented nylon — the first fully synthetic fibre made entirely from petroleum and chemistry. Today, synthetic fibres dominate the global textile market. Polyester (invented 1941) is in your athletic wear, your school uniform, your bedsheets. Acrylic is in your winter coat. Spandex is in your swimsuit. Meanwhile, the global textile industry is a USD 700+ billion business employing 60+ million workers, mostly in Asia. Yet this industry also grapples with issues of labour rights, environmental pollution, and consumer ethics. This topic explores all of it: the science of synthetic fibres, the geography of textile production, and the big questions about sustainability and fairness.

This topic introduces you to synthetic fibres, compares them to natural fibres, and explores the global textile industry's economic and social impact.

What you'll learn in this topic

  • What synthetic fibres are and how they're made from petroleum
  • The properties of polyester, nylon, acrylic, and spandex
  • How synthetic fibres compare to natural fibres in durability, cost, and environmental impact
  • The geography of global textile manufacturing (China, India, Bangladesh, Vietnam)
  • Labour practices and worker rights in textile factories
  • Emerging innovations: spider silk engineering and biodegradable synthetic fibres

The 3 concepts in this topic

  1. Synthetic fibres vs natural fibres — properties and environmental impact Synthetic fibres (polyester, acrylic, nylon) are petroleum-based, durable, and wrinkle-resistant but non-biodegradable; natural fibres are breathable, biodegradable but require agriculture or animal farming.
  2. The global textile industry — economic and social impact The textile and fibre industry is worth USD 700+ billion annually, employing 60+ million workers, with significant economic impact on developing nations and complex labour practices.
  3. Spider Silk — Nature's Engineering Marvel Spider silk is stronger than steel by weight, tougher than Kevlar, and stretches like rubber — engineers are racing to copy it.

Why this topic matters

The global textile industry is woven into every aspect of modern life. The clothes on your back, the carpet beneath your feet, the curtains on your windows — all represent complex supply chains, economic decisions, and environmental consequences. Understanding synthetic vs natural fibres, the geography of production, and the ethics of the industry gives you the knowledge to make informed choices as a consumer and engaged global citizen.
