Three "What Ifs?" About Fibre to Fabric

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if humans had built clothing out of spider silk instead of silkworm silk?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Spider silk is several times stronger than steel by weight and stretches like rubber. A spider-silk bulletproof vest, ropes that don't break under tonnes, surgical sutures that dissolve and heal — all real possibilities. The problem: spiders are territorial cannibals. You can't farm them in dense colonies the way silkworms are farmed. Scientists have engineered goats, bacteria, and yeasts to produce spider silk proteins, but at industrial scale this remains expensive. India's silk industry could leap to a new century if the spider problem is solved.

What if 2 — What if synthetic fibres had never been invented?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Your wardrobe would be heavier, more wrinkly, more expensive, and slower-drying. Sports — especially marathons, climbing, surfing — would be much harder without quick-dry fabrics. Parachutes would still be silk (which is what they were until nylon was invented during WWII). On the positive side: no microplastics in oceans, no fossil-fuel input into clothing, no fashion-cycle waste because clothes would last longer and be valued more. Mid-20th-century textile science gave us comfort at the cost of pollution; rolling it back would reverse both.

What if 3 — What if you tried to make silk yourself from a silkworm in your garden?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: You'd need a mulberry tree (silkworms eat almost nothing else), the right silkworm eggs (Bombyx mori or one of the wild Indian species), a clean dry rearing tray, and 6 weeks of daily care. Each caterpillar makes ~600 metres of thread in its cocoon — enough to wrap your room a couple of times. To weave even a small handkerchief, you'd need 30+ cocoons. The math is why silk has been a luxury good for 4000 years.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
