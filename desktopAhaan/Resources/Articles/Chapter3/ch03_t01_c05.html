Types and classifications of wool

Wool is classified by fibre diameter, staple length, and colour, determining its use in textiles.

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Think of wool like pencil leads — thin leads (fine wool) are precious and soft, while thick leads (coarse wool) are sturdy but scratchy. Sheep naturally grow different grades of wool, and sorting these grades determines whether the fibre goes into a luxury cashmere-like sweater or a durable blanket.

In this article, we explore the science, significance, and real-world applications of wool classification and grades.

What this concept means

Wool is classified by fibre diameter (measured in microns; 1 micron = 0.001 mm), staple length (the length of a single fibre), and colour. Merino wool is fine (18–24 microns, soft, luxurious); Romney wool is medium (25–30 microns, durable); Coarse wool is thick (over 30 microns, strong, bristly). Fine wools are used in clothing close to skin (sweaters, undergarments); medium wools in everyday garments (coats, blankets); coarse wools in industrial applications (carpets, ropes, blankets). Additionally, wool is graded by colour (white, cream, brown, black, or mixed) and by the presence of vegetable matter (which lowers grade). Staple length determines how easily fibres can be spun into fine yarn — longer staples are easier to process and produce stronger yarn.

Why it works this way

Different sheep breeds naturally grow fibres of different diameters and lengths due to selective breeding over centuries. Merino sheep were bred for fine wool; Lincoln sheep for coarse, strong wool. The diameter of a fibre determines how it feels on skin (fine fibres feel soft and smooth; coarse fibres feel scratchy) and how it can be processed (fine fibres need delicate machinery; coarse fibres can withstand rougher handling). Staple length determines the ease of spinning — short fibres keep breaking and require high twist to hold together; long fibres can be spun into smooth, strong yarn easily.

💡 Did you know?

The Merino sheep is considered the gold standard of wool sheep because its wool is remarkably fine (18–24 microns) and uniform. A single Merino sheep produces about 5 kg of clean wool annually. In contrast, a coarse-woolled Leicester sheep produces 7 kg of thicker wool. Modern classification uses laser technology to measure fibre diameter to within 0.5 microns, and computerized sorters can process fleece grades with precision that human sorters can't match. The finest wool ever recorded was from a Merino sheep in Australia at just 11.6 microns — finer than cashmere, which is typically 14–16 microns.

Real-world applications

  1. Fine wool sweaters — Merino wool (18–24 microns) is processed into soft, non-itchy sweaters retailing for £50–150
  2. Durable work blankets — Coarse wool (over 30 microns) is woven into heavy blankets and picnic rugs that last decades
  3. Carpet manufacturing — Mixed grades and colours of wool are blended and dyed for carpet production
  4. Military uniforms — Medium-grade wool is used in uniforms because of durability and thermal insulation
  5. Luxury yarns for knitting — Fine Merino or cashmere wool is marketed as premium knitting yarn
  6. Felt production — Coarse wool felts more readily than fine wool, making it preferred for hats and felted crafts
  7. Outdoor sportswear — Merino wool is blended with synthetic fibres for lightweight, moisture-wicking activewear
  8. Heritage textile production — Indian weavers select specific wool grades for traditional textiles like Pashmina (ultra-fine) and Banaras brocades
  9. Specialty: medical compression garments — High-quality, fine wool is used in medical-grade stockings and garments
  10. Export grading standards — International standards (Micron Count) ensure consistency in wool trading and pricing globally

🌍 In your world

The wool in your closet is classified by micron count — a number you've probably never seen. A luxury Merino sweater might be "18 micron" (supremely soft); a budget wool sweater might be "28 micron" (still soft, but more durable). Check a wool label on a garment you own: it might specify "100% Merino wool" or "80% wool, 20% polyester", but it rarely specifies micron count because retailers assume you don't care. But now you know: micron count is the secret determinant of how that sweater will feel on your skin and how long it will last.

🧪 Try this at home

Find three wool garments in your home. Compare their labels and fibre composition. Try rubbing each on the back of your neck — which feels softest? Which feels scratchiest? Can you guess the micron count based on how it feels?

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: What are the main classification criteria for wool?

Answer: Fibre diameter (measured in microns), staple length, and colour. These determine the wool's use, price, and feel.

🧠 Why does this matter?

Question: Why is Merino wool more expensive than regular wool?

Answer: Merino wool is finer (18–24 microns) than regular wool, making it softer and more luxurious, and thus more desirable and costly.

🧠 How do you measure it?

Question: How is wool fibre diameter measured?

Answer: In microns (millionths of a meter). Modern classification uses laser technology to measure fibre diameter to within 0.5 microns.
