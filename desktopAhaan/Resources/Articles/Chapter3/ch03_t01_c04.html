Hazards in the wool industry — sorter's disease

Wool sorters are at risk of anthrax (sorter's disease) — a deadly bacterial infection from spores in raw fleece.

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

In the 19th century, wool sorters in Bradford, England started dying of a mysterious disease. They'd handle raw fleece, cut their fingers or breathe in dust, and within weeks they'd develop flu-like symptoms followed by severe respiratory illness or skin ulcers. The disease was anthrax — caused by the bacterium Bacillus anthracis. Anthrax spores, dormant in sheep wool, can remain viable for decades in soil and animal products. This is sorter's disease, and it's a powerful reminder that understanding the hazards of an industry is as important as understanding its products.

In this article, we explore the science, significance, and real-world applications of sorter's disease and occupational hazards in textile work.

What this concept means

Sorter's disease is anthrax infection (caused by Bacillus anthracis) contracted by workers handling raw or inadequately processed wool. The disease enters through cuts in the skin (cutaneous anthrax, most common), inhalation (inhalation anthrax, more serious), or ingestion (gastrointestinal anthrax, rare in wool workers). Anthrax is a zoonotic disease — it infects wild and domestic animals including sheep, cattle, and goats. When infected animals die and decompose, anthrax spores enter the soil and can persist for decades. Wool from infected animals may carry dormant spores that remain on the fibres through shearing and storage, posing an occupational hazard to anyone handling raw fleece without proper protection.

Why it works this way

Anthrax spores are extraordinarily hardy. They form a protective coat that allows them to survive harsh conditions — extreme heat, cold, drying, chemical disinfection — for years or decades. When disturbed (cut, inhaled, or ingested), the spore germinates into vegetative bacteria that multiply rapidly and produce toxins. The incubation period is 1–7 days, but the disease can progress from a minor skin lesion to systemic infection and death without treatment. Wool workers are uniquely at risk because they handle raw fleece that may contain spores, often in poorly ventilated spaces, with frequent skin cuts from broken fibres.

💡 Did you know?

Anthrax was the first bacterium definitively linked to disease by Robert Koch in 1876 — it was the foundation of the germ theory of disease. Louis Pasteur used anthrax to develop the first vaccine (1796). The disease remains a concern even today: in 2001, anthrax spores were weaponized and sent through the U.S. mail, killing 5 people and infecting 17 others. This tragedy highlighted how easily biological pathogens can be misused and the importance of occupational health measures in industries handling animal products.

Real-world applications

  1. 19th-century Bradford wool industry — Hundreds of wool sorters died of anthrax, leading to the first occupational disease laws in Britain
  2. Occupational Health and Safety regulations — Modern OSHA standards mandate PPE (masks, gloves, aprons) and ventilation in wool-handling facilities
  3. Raw wool treatment plants — Facilities that treat imported wool with steam, chemicals, or radiation to kill dormant spores before sorting
  4. Vaccination programs — Workers in high-risk industries can receive anthrax vaccine (AVA) for protection
  5. Zoonotic disease surveillance — Veterinary authorities monitor wild and domestic animal populations for anthrax to prevent contamination of wool supply
  6. Biosafety protocols in research — Laboratories working with Bacillus anthracis follow strict containment procedures (BSL-3 labs)
  7. Customs inspection of imported wool — Authorities screen raw wool imports for anthrax contamination using rapid diagnostic tests
  8. Hospital preparedness — Emergency rooms train staff to recognize anthrax symptoms and provide rapid treatment (antibiotics)
  9. Archaeological wool artifacts — Museum workers handling ancient woolen textiles follow decontamination protocols in case of anthrax spores
  10. Environmental remediation — Sites where infected animals died or wool was processed may require decontamination to remove dormant spores

🌍 In your world

If you wear wool, thank modern occupational safety regulations. The sorter who sheared, washed, and processed the fleece that went into your sweater worked under legal protections — proper ventilation, PPE, vaccinations, and regulated working conditions — that simply didn't exist a century ago. Anthrax in wool is now rare in developed countries, but it remains a risk in regions where animal diseases are less controlled and occupational protections are weaker.

🧠 Check your understanding

Question: What is sorter's disease?

Answer: Sorter's disease is anthrax infection contracted by workers handling raw or inadequately processed wool contaminated with Bacillus anthracis spores.

🧠 Why does this matter?

Question: Why did anthrax become such a problem in the wool industry?

Answer: Raw wool from infected animals carries dormant anthrax spores that remain viable for decades, and workers handling fleece had frequent skin cuts and inhaled dust without protection.

🧠 How has this changed?

Question: What protections exist for wool workers today?

Answer: Modern regulations mandate PPE, proper ventilation, raw wool treatment to kill spores, and vaccination programs for at-risk workers.
