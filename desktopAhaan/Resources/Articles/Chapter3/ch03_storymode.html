Story Mode — Fibre to Fabric in Five Scenes

5 short scenes from real life that bring this chapter to a kitchen table, a garden, a classroom — somewhere you can picture.

≈ 10 min read · narrative-tier

Textbook examples can feel abstract. These five scenes don't happen in a textbook — they happen in places you go. Read them in order; each scene tightens the chapter's big idea by another notch.

Scene 1 — Pashmina shawls from Ladakh's Changthangi goats

The pashmina shawl wrapped around your grandmother's shoulders started on a goat in Ladakh's Changthang plateau at -30°C. The Changthangi goat grows TWO coats: tough outer guard hairs and an incredibly fine inner fleece (less than 16 microns thick — a human hair is 70). Each goat produces only ~100 grams of pashmina per year. Pashmina is hand-combed (not shorn) during the spring moult, sorted, and woven in Kashmir. One shawl takes months and the wool of 3-5 goats.

Scene 2 — Banarasi silk saree — six months of weaving

A heavy Banarasi silk saree is woven on a hand loom by three weavers working together for 15 days to six months, depending on design complexity. The base is pure mulberry silk reeled from cocoons in West Bengal or Karnataka. The gold-and-silver zari is real metal thread wrapped around silk core. The motifs — bel buti, jhallar, kalga — date back to the Mughal era. One saree can use 5500 individual threads in the warp alone.

Scene 3 — Polyester t-shirt and microplastics in your washing machine

Every time you wash a polyester or nylon t-shirt, it sheds tens of thousands of microscopic plastic fibres into the rinse water. These microplastics flow through household drains, sewage plants (most can't filter them out), into rivers, and finally the ocean. They've been found in salt, fish, drinking water, and even human blood. A single 6-kg wash of synthetic clothes releases about 700,000 microfibres — that's part of why cotton and natural fibres are coming back in favour.

Scene 4 — Tasar silk weaving in Jharkhand's tribal villages

Mulberry silk is famous, but India also produces tasar, eri, and muga silk — none of which need mulberry trees. Tasar silkworms feed on sal and arjun trees that grow wild in Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh's forests. Tribal women have been rearing them for centuries. The cocoons are oval and golden-tan, the fabric has a coarser, more rustic texture than mulberry silk, and it's much cooler in summer. Muga silk from Assam is even rarer — its natural golden colour brightens with every wash.

Scene 5 — Why your wool sweater shrinks if you wash it hot

Wool fibres are covered in tiny overlapping scales — like roof tiles. When you wash wool in hot water and agitate it, the scales catch on each other and lock together permanently. The whole garment contracts: your sweater that fit yesterday is now child-sized. This irreversible felting is what wool felt is made from on purpose. To prevent it, wool needs cool water, mild detergent, and a flat-drying surface — no spinning, no wringing.

💬 What this is for

Concrete examples are how your brain anchors abstract ideas. Pick the scene you remember best after reading. The next time you see it in real life, you'll remember the chapter.
