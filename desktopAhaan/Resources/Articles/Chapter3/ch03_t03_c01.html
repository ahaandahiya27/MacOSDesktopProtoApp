Synthetic fibres vs natural fibres

Synthetic fibres are plastic-based; natural fibres are organic. Each has strengths and environmental costs — understanding both is key to sustainable choices.

≈ 10 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Your wardrobe is likely a blend of natural and synthetic fibres. Cotton t-shirt? Natural, from plants. Polyester jacket? Synthetic, from petroleum. Wool sweater? Natural, from animals. Acrylic blanket? Synthetic, a plastic polymer. Each material has emerged from a different source and carries different properties, different environmental costs, and different end-of-life fates. Natural fibres biodegrade — return to the earth. Synthetic fibres persist — a polyester garment discarded today may still exist in a landfill 200 years from now, fragmenting into microplastics that end up in fish stomachs and, eventually, in the human food chain. Yet synthetic fibres have advantages too: they're durable, wrinkle-resistant, and inexpensive. Understanding the trade-offs between natural and synthetic helps you make informed choices about what to wear and buy.

In this article, we compare natural fibres (plant and animal) with synthetic fibres (polyester, nylon, acrylic), examining their properties, environmental impacts, and the complex reality that no fibre is perfectly sustainable.

What this concept means

Synthetic fibres are polymers — long-chain molecules made from petroleum or coal tar. The main types are polyester (PET), nylon (PA), acrylic (PAN), and polyurethane (spandex). Polyester is produced by reacting ethylene glycol and terephthalic acid; the resulting polymer is spun into fibres, twisted into yarn, and woven into fabric. Nylon is similarly synthesized from petroleum derivatives and can be formed into extremely thin, strong filaments. Acrylic is polyacrylonitrile — a synthetic polymer that mimics wool's soft, insulating properties. These synthetic fibres share common characteristics: they're hydrophobic (water-repellent), they resist wrinkles (don't absorb water, so don't crease), they're strong and durable (lasting 10+ years in clothing), and they're inexpensive to produce at scale. Natural fibres (cotton, wool, silk, linen) are biological polymers: cellulose in plants, keratin or fibroin in animals. They're hydrophilic (water-absorbing), wrinkle easily, biodegrade relatively quickly (cotton in 5 months, wool in 1 year), and are more expensive to produce due to lower yield and labour intensity. The choice between natural and synthetic involves trade-offs: natural fibres are breathable and biodegradable but wrinkle easily and cost more; synthetic fibres are durable and cheap but petroleum-dependent and persistent in the environment.

Why it works this way

Natural fibres biodegrade because they're made of biological polymers that bacteria and fungi recognize as food. Cellulose (in cotton) and keratin (in wool) have been on Earth for billions of years — soil organisms have evolved to digest them. Synthetic polymers like polyester, by contrast, are only 70–80 years old (invented in the 1940s). Soil bacteria and fungi have not evolved enzymes to digest them — they're chemically foreign. In a landfill at room temperature, polyester will likely persist for 200+ years, slowly fragmenting into microplastics. The trade-off emerges because synthetic polymers offer advantages that evolved over decades: extreme durability means a polyester shirt lasts 10+ years without wearing out, amortizing manufacturing impacts over a longer lifetime. Nylon is exceptionally strong and water-resistant, valuable for rain gear and swimwear — properties cotton can't match. Acrylic mimics wool's warmth without the itchiness some people experience. These properties drive consumer choice. Simultaneously, the environmental cost is becoming visible: microplastics from synthetic clothing are found in ocean sediment, fish guts, and drinking water. Each wash of a synthetic garment releases 100–1,000 microfibre particles. A polyester fleece jacket sheds enough microfibres in one wash to harm fish larvae. The question becomes: Is the durability worth the persistent pollution? Or should we prioritize natural fibres despite their wrinkliness and higher price?

💡 Did you know?

Microplastics from synthetic clothing are entering the human food chain. Fish eat microplastics in ocean water, confusing them for food. Humans then eat the fish. Scientists have found microplastics in human blood, lungs, and placental tissue. The long-term health effects are unknown, but preliminary research suggests microplastics may cause inflammation and potentially harm organ function. This is an emerging environmental health crisis that nobody chose — it's an unintended consequence of choosing polyester pants.

Real-world applications

  1. Polyester shirt durability — A polyester shirt doesn't wrinkle, dries fast, and lasts 10+ years; but it sheds microfibres with every wash, releasing thousands of tiny plastic particles into waterways and fish guts.
  2. Cotton farming water cost — Growing cotton requires 10,000 litres of water per kg of fibre — 70 times more water than synthetic polyester production. But cotton decomposes; polyester persists.
  3. Acrylic blanket appeal — An acrylic blanket is soft, warm, and cheap (1/10 the price of wool); it's indistinguishable from wool by touch. Yet it's plastic — petroleum-based, and will persist in a landfill for 200+ years.
  4. Nylon stockings practicality — Nylon (PA66) stockings are sheer, strong, and wrinkle-proof; they survive wet conditions wool can't tolerate. Nylon's synthetic durability made sheer hosiery possible for the first time in history.
  5. Textile blends complexity — A 65% polyester / 35% cotton blend balances synthetic durability with natural breathability. But a blend is harder to recycle than a pure fibre — how do you separate polyester from cotton at end-of-life?
  6. Microfibre shedding from washing — Each wash of a synthetic garment releases 100–1,000 microfibre particles. A single fleece jacket can shed 2 grams of microplastics per wash — equivalent to 2,000 pieces of microplastic per garment per load.
  7. Polyester production scale — Polyester accounts for 50–60% of global fibre production — over 50 million tonnes annually. Each tonne will persist for centuries, accumulating in landfills and oceans.
  8. Natural fibre pesticide use — Conventional cotton is one of the world's most pesticide-intensive crops (uses 16% of global insecticides despite occupying only 2.5% of farmland). Organic cotton reduces this but still requires land and water.
  9. Recycled polyester growth — Recycled polyester (from plastic bottles or old clothing) reduces virgin petroleum extraction, but recycled fibres still shed microplastics and don't solve the persistence problem.
  10. Environmental trade-offs in choice — Natural fibres: biodegradable but high water use (cotton) or land use (wool). Synthetic fibres: low production cost but persistent pollution. No perfect option — each involves significant environmental trade-offs.

🌍 In your shopping decisions

The "best" fibre choice depends on context. For underwear and t-shirts you'll wear dozens of times before discarding, cotton or linen (natural, biodegradable) makes sense despite higher price. For rain gear or swimwear requiring durability and water-resistance, nylon or polyester are more practical. For everyday wear, blends offer compromise. The key is awareness: understanding that a polyester jacket carries a hidden environmental cost (microplastics), and that a cotton shirt carries a hidden water cost (10,000 litres per kg). Armed with this knowledge, you can make choices aligned with your values — whether that's minimizing water use, avoiding microplastics, or balancing all concerns.

🧪 Try this at home

Check the labels of 10 garments in your wardrobe. Categorize them: natural (cotton, wool, linen, silk), synthetic (polyester, nylon, acrylic), or blends. Which category dominates? For each synthetic garment, estimate how long you've worn it and how long you expect to wear it. Calculate: If the garment lasts 10 years and sheds 1,000 microfibres per wash, how many washes until it reaches end-of-life? (Washing once per week for 10 years = 500 washes = 500,000 microfibre particles from that one garment.) Does the durability justify the microplastic cost?

Beyond the textbook

Research into enzymatic PET degradation is exploring whether engineered bacteria (specifically, a mutated version of the enzyme PETase) can break down polyester plastic at industrial scale. Early results are promising — the enzyme can degrade PET 6 times faster than natural degradation. If successful, this technology could allow recycling of polyester into new fibres in months rather than waiting 200 years for natural decomposition. Simultaneously, lab-grown bio-silk and other biotech alternatives aim to create truly biodegradable synthetic-like performance. The future may involve hybrid materials: biodegradable synthetic-like fibres with performance properties matching petroleum-based synthetics.

🧠 Check your understanding: What is the main environmental difference between natural and synthetic fibres?

Answer: Natural fibres (cotton, wool) biodegrade relatively quickly (months to years) because soil organisms have evolved to digest biological polymers. Synthetic fibres (polyester, nylon) persist for 200+ years because they're chemically foreign — bacteria haven't evolved to digest them. Synthetic fibres fragment into microplastics that accumulate in ecosystems.

🧠 What are the trade-offs in choosing between cotton and polyester?

Answer: Cotton is biodegradable but requires massive water use (10,000 litres per kg) and pesticides. Polyester is durable and cheap but petroleum-dependent and releases microplastics throughout its lifetime. Neither is perfectly sustainable — the choice involves weighing water/pesticide impacts against plastic persistence.

🧠 Why do synthetic fibres shed microplastics when washed?

Answer: Synthetic fibres are spun from polymers (like polyester), which are physically fragile. Mechanical action during washing (friction with other clothes, drum tumbling) breaks the polymer chains, releasing tiny plastic fragments called microplastics. Each wash releases 100–1,000 particles, which end up in waterways and bioaccumulate in organisms.
