Sericulture and the silkworm life cycle

From a tiny egg to a cocoon-spinning larva in 25 days, the silkworm's life cycle is a marvel of biology — and the foundation of an ancient industry.

≈ 9 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine a creature that eats nothing but leaves, grows 50 times its size in three weeks, then spins a protective cocoon from a single continuous silk thread 1,200 metres long. This is the silkworm — not actually a worm at all, but a caterpillar (the larval stage of a moth). For nearly 4,000 years, humans have farmed silkworms in a practice called sericulture, building an entire industry around understanding and optimizing the silkworm's life cycle. Every step — from egg incubation to larval feeding to cocoon spinning — has been refined by generations of farmers, particularly in India, China, Japan, and across Southeast Asia.

In this article, we explore the four stages of the silkworm's life (egg, larva, pupa, moth), understand the precise timing and conditions required at each stage, and learn how sericulture farmers manage these insects to produce the world's most luxurious fibre.

What this concept means

The silkworm (Bombyx mori) is a moth whose larva feeds exclusively on mulberry leaves. The complete life cycle takes approximately 42–45 days from egg to egg, but the commercially valuable period is just 26–30 days: egg (10 days) → larva (25–30 days) → pupa (12–15 days inside the cocoon) → moth (5–7 days). Each stage has specific requirements. Eggs must be incubated at 24–26°C to hatch synchronously; a single female moth lays 300–500 eggs. Larvae hatch at about 2 millimetres and immediately begin eating mulberry leaves. They grow rapidly, increasing their body weight 10,000-fold in just 25 days (equivalent to a human newborn reaching 8 tonnes in 25 days!). During this time, they moult (shed their outer skin) four times as they outgrow their exoskeleton. After the fourth moult, the larva stops eating, purges its digestive system, and begins spinning. It secretes silk fibroin protein from glands in its head, extruding it through a single spinneret (a mouth opening). The larva moves its head in a figure-eight pattern, spinning the silk around itself to form a cocoon. After 2–3 days of cocoon building, the larva transforms into a pupa inside — a stage of metamorphosis lasting 12–15 days. Finally, the adult moth emerges, mates, and lays eggs, completing the cycle.

Why it works this way

The silkworm's life cycle is optimized for rapid growth and efficient silk production — the result of 4,000 years of natural selection and human-directed breeding. The brief egg stage (10 days at precise temperature) ensures all eggs hatch together, so all larvae are the same age and size. Synchronized larvae simplify farm management — they eat at the same time, they moult together, and they spin cocoons simultaneously. The larval stage lasts 25–30 days because this is the optimal period for maximum body size (and therefore maximum silk-gland development) with minimal mortality. The larva must eat continuously to fuel growth; mulberry leaves provide the exact nutrients needed. The four moults are driven by the hormone ecdysone, which triggers shedding when the larva outgrows its exoskeleton. After the final moult, a different hormone (juvenile hormone levels drop) signals the larva to stop eating and begin spinning — this is called "wandering stage" or "pre-spinning stage." The pupa spends 12–15 days metamorphosing inside the cocoon, transforming from a caterpillar body plan to a winged moth body plan — this requires complete cellular reorganization. Finally, the adult moth emerges, seeks a mate (using pheromones), and the female lays eggs, restarting the cycle. This entire schedule is driven by photoperiod (day length) and temperature — farmers exploit this by controlling incubation temperature to time cocoon production for optimal drying and processing.

💡 Did you know?

Sericulture is often practiced as a women-centered craft in India and Southeast Asia. In many villages in Karnataka, Assam, and Odisha, women and girls feed silkworms, manage cocoon drying, and perform rearing tasks. This creates significant economic opportunities — a small family can earn 50,000–100,000 rupees per crop, with 4–5 crops per year in warm regions. The practice has preserved traditional knowledge and skills for centuries, making sericulture not just an industry but a cultural heritage.

Real-world applications

  1. Mulberry farm ecosystem — A sericulture farmer grows mulberry trees solely to feed silkworm caterpillars; 20–25 kg of fresh mulberry leaves are needed to produce 1 kg of cocoons.
  2. Egg incubation technology — Silk moths lay 300–500 eggs; sericulture labs keep them at precisely 25°C (within ±1°C) for exactly 10 days to ensure synchronized hatching and uniform growth.
  3. Larval feeding schedule — A 2 mm silkworm larva eats mulberry leaves for 25–30 days, growing approximately 50 times its size. Farmers feed fresh leaves 4–5 times daily to support this explosive growth.
  4. Cocoon spinning behaviour — At day 26–28, the larva stops eating and spins a protective cocoon of continuous silk fibre. The spinning process takes 2–3 days and produces approximately 1,200 metres of raw silk.
  5. Pupa transformation inside cocoon — Inside the sealed cocoon, the larva transforms into a pupa; 12–15 days later, it becomes an adult moth with wings and reproductive organs.
  6. Stifling (killing pupae) — Commercial sericulture kills pupae before they emerge (by steaming or baking the cocoons at 80–100°C) to prevent the moth from breaking the cocoon and damaging the silk fibre. One cocoon yields ~1,200 m of fibre; a broken cocoon is worthless.
  7. Temperature management for crop timing — Farmers control incubation and rearing temperature to time cocoon production; cool storage delays development, allowing farmers to stagger crops and supply markets year-round.
  8. Disease prevention in sericulture — Silkworms are susceptible to bacterial and fungal diseases (pebrine, muscardine). Farmers maintain strict hygiene, sterilize rearing beds, and use disease-resistant strains to prevent epidemics.
  9. Silk-producing countries and regions — China produces 60–70% of global mulberry silk; India ranks second. Within India, Karnataka dominates (accounting for 60% of Indian silk production), while Assam specializes in muga silk, and Odisha in tasar silk.
  10. Economic impact of sericulture — India's silk industry employs over 2 million people, mostly in rural areas. A single silkworm rearing cycle takes 45 days; with 4–5 cycles per year in warm regions, small farmers can sustain livelihoods entirely from sericulture.

🌍 In traditional practice

Sericulture is deeply embedded in the cultures of Karnataka, Assam, and Odisha. Families have practiced it for generations, passing down knowledge about optimal mulberry varieties, feeding schedules, disease prevention, and cocoon drying. In many villages, the rhythm of life follows the silkworm's cycle — planting mulberry in spring, rearing silkworms in summer and autumn, harvesting cocoons, and processing silk over winter. This connection to the silkworm's biology is not abstract science but lived experience, encoded in local language, tradition, and agricultural practice.

🧪 Try this at home

If you can obtain silkworm eggs (available from sericulture suppliers or online), try rearing them in a controlled environment. Feed fresh mulberry leaves (or lettuce as a substitute) 2–3 times daily. Observe the moults, the larva's growth, and the cocoon-spinning behaviour. Keep detailed notes on timing: when do moults occur? How long is the spinning stage? Can you match your observations to the 42–45-day life cycle described here?

Beyond the textbook

Recent research has explored the silk fibroin protein itself, finding that it has tensile strength comparable to mild steel — making it valuable not just for textiles but for biomedical applications. Scientists have spliced the silk-protein gene from silkworms into bacteria and yeast, allowing them to produce silk proteins in fermentation tanks (without raising silkworms). Companies like Bolt Threads and AMSilk are commercializing lab-grown silk using this biotechnology. Simultaneously, researchers are studying the silkworm's genome to identify genes responsible for silk production, colour, and robustness — aiming to breed even more productive and disease-resistant silkworms.

🧠 Check your understanding: What are the four stages of the silkworm life cycle?

Answer: Egg (10 days) → Larva (25–30 days eating mulberry leaves) → Pupa (12–15 days inside cocoon) → Adult moth (5–7 days, mating and laying eggs). The entire cycle takes 42–45 days.

🧠 Why do sericulture farmers control incubation temperature?

Answer: Temperature controls the rate of development. At precisely 25°C, eggs hatch together after 10 days (synchronized). Farmers can delay hatching by storing eggs at cool temperatures, allowing them to time cocoon production for market demands and stagger multiple crops per year.

🧠 Why is the larval stage only 25–30 days?

Answer: This period is optimal for maximum body growth (needed to develop large silk glands) while minimizing disease risk and mortality. A 25-day larva grows 50 times its initial size. Longer rearing increases disease risk; shorter rearing yields smaller silk glands and less cocoon.
