Two Mini Projects for Chapter 3

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🔥 Burn test — identify the fibre in your t-shirt

~20 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • Small samples (1 cm each) of cotton t-shirt, polyester t-shirt, wool sweater, and silk fabric if possible
  • A safe surface (ceramic plate or aluminium foil)
  • A matchstick or lighter (with adult supervision)
  • Pinching tweezers

Steps

  1. Lay the samples on the ceramic plate, well apart.
  2. Hold one sample with tweezers above the plate. Touch the corner with the flame for one second.
  3. Pull back. Observe how it burns and what it leaves behind.
  4. Notice the SMELL — cotton smells like burning paper. Wool and silk smell like burning hair. Polyester smells chemical and beads into hard plastic.
  5. Repeat for each sample, comparing flame colour, smoke, ash, and smell.

👀 What you should see

Cotton: yellow flame, burns to soft ash, paper smell. Wool/silk: small flame, beads into crumbly black, hair smell. Polyester: melts before burning, drips, hard plastic bead remains.

Why it works: Each fibre family has a different chemistry. Cotton is cellulose (plant carb — burns clean). Wool and silk are protein (sulphur-containing amino acids — smell like burning hair). Polyester is plastic (a long carbon-hydrogen chain — melts first, then burns). The burn test is so reliable that costume designers and textile inspectors still use it to spot-check fibre content.

🐛 Mini sericulture — raise a silkworm at home

~60 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A box of silkworm eggs (sericulture suppliers in Mysore / Bangalore sell starter packs)
  • Fresh mulberry leaves (one tree near your house, or order)
  • A clean cardboard tray with a lid (paper-towel-lined)
  • A cool dry spot, ~25°C
  • Three weeks of patience

Steps

  1. Place the eggs on the tray. They hatch in 7–10 days into tiny black caterpillars.
  2. From day 1, feed mulberry leaves daily — chop fresh leaves for the first week.
  3. Clean the tray daily — remove old leaves and droppings.
  4. The caterpillars grow for ~25 days, shedding skin 4 times and getting much bigger.
  5. When they stop eating and turn translucent, provide a brush of small twigs.
  6. They spin a cocoon around themselves in 2–3 days.
  7. If you want to harvest silk: dip the cocoon in hot water briefly and unwind. If you want to see the moth: wait 14 days; the moth emerges (and your silk thread becomes broken pieces).

👀 What you should see

Egg → caterpillar (silkworm) → cocoon → moth, the full life cycle in your bedroom over ~6 weeks.

Why it works: This is the only project where you can watch the whole insect metamorphosis up close — and it's the same species (Bombyx mori) on which the entire silk industry runs. The ethical question (kill the pupa to get whole silk, or let the moth emerge?) is also live: 'ahimsa silk' lets the moth emerge first and gathers the broken cocoons. The fibre is coarser, but no insect dies.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
