The global textile industry

USD 700 billion annually, 60 million workers, often in exploitative conditions — the textile industry is massive, urgent, and deeply human.

≈ 10 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

The clothes you're wearing right now are part of a global industry worth USD 700 billion annually, employing 60 million workers across the world. This industry is a story of contradiction: It creates livelihoods for millions of people in poor countries; it also exploits those same workers with low wages, unsafe factories, and inhumane hours. It drives economic growth in developing nations; it also contributes to environmental degradation, water pollution, and climate change. It delivers affordable fashion to billions; it also generates mountains of textile waste. Understanding the textile industry requires grappling with these complexities — not dismissing it, but recognizing its massive social and environmental impacts, and supporting efforts toward more ethical and sustainable practices.

In this article, we explore the global textile industry's scale, supply chains, labour practices, and the growing movement toward sustainability and justice.

What this concept means

The textile industry spans fibre production, yarn spinning, fabric weaving, garment manufacturing, dyeing, finishing, and distribution. Major players include China (world's largest producer, 60–70% of global output), India (second-largest, and home to significant traditional sericulture and handloom sectors), Bangladesh, Vietnam, Indonesia, and Pakistan. The industry employs 60 million people globally — most in developing countries where labour is cheap. Wages are typically USD 3–5 per day in Bangladesh, India, and Pakistan — well below living wage standards. Working conditions are often hazardous: poor ventilation in dyeing facilities (exposing workers to toxic chemicals), long hours (12–14-hour days, 6–7 days a week), unsafe machinery without proper guards, and minimal safety regulations. Factory disasters have exposed these conditions: the Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh in 2013 killed 1,134 garment workers, catalyzing international labour-rights movements. Beyond labour, the textile industry causes severe environmental damage. Cotton production uses 16% of global insecticides. Dyeing and finishing are the most polluting stages, releasing heavy metals and toxic chemicals into rivers. In India, the Kanpur region (home to leather tanning) has been called the "most polluted city in the world" due to textile industry pollution. Water scarcity is another crisis: a single cotton t-shirt requires 2,700 litres of water to produce. Globally, textile production uses 79 trillion litres of water annually. The industry also generates 92 million tonnes of textile waste yearly — much of which is discarded in developing countries, creating mountains of unwearable, un-recyclable clothing.

Why it works this way

The textile industry's current structure emerged from global economics and labour arbitrage. In the mid-20th century, high-wage countries (USA, Europe) outsourced garment manufacturing to low-wage countries (China, India, Bangladesh) to minimize production costs. This drove economic development in poor countries — millions of people moved from farming into factory work, generating household incomes and tax revenue. But global corporations competed fiercely on price, driving garment prices down (a t-shirt for USD 5), which forced manufacturers to minimize labour costs and cut corners on safety. The result: a system where Western consumers enjoy cheap fashion, while garment workers endure poverty wages and dangerous conditions. Environmental damage was externalized — factories dumped chemicals into rivers because regulations were weak or unenforced, and cleanup costs weren't borne by manufacturers or brands. Textile waste accumulated because fast fashion (cheap clothing designed to be worn a few times then discarded) became the norm, encouraged by marketing and planned obsolescence. The system benefits from these inequities: cheap labour and lax regulations maximize corporate profit. Changing it requires systemic pressure: labour laws with teeth, environmental regulations, fair-trade certification, consumer awareness, and brand accountability.

💡 Did you know?

The Rana Plaza collapse on April 24, 2013, was a turning point. A garment factory building in Dhaka, Bangladesh, collapsed due to poor structural integrity (earlier cracks had been noted but ignored). The collapse killed 1,134 workers, making it the deadliest industrial accident in history. The tragedy was a wake-up call: major fashion brands (H&M, Zara, Gap) that had outsourced manufacturing to the factory suddenly faced public outrage. The incident catalyzed the Bangladesh Accord on Fire and Building Safety, international labour monitoring, and increased pressure on brands for supply-chain transparency.

Real-world applications

  1. Bangladeshi garment factory wages — A worker sewing shirts for a Western brand earns USD 100–150 per month, working 12–14-hour days, 6–7 days per week. A USD 20 t-shirt sells for USD 5 wholesale — the worker's share is about 30 cents.
  2. Chinese power-loom dominance — China's power looms can weave 50 times faster than Indian handlooms, dominating global textile production. A single Chinese mill can produce what takes 50 Indian handloom weavers to produce.
  3. Fair-trade label economics — A fair-trade cotton t-shirt costs USD 20 vs. USD 5 for non-certified; the difference goes to farmer cooperatives, school funding, and healthcare in producing communities. Fair-trade garments empower workers and communities.
  4. Child labour in cotton harvesting — In India, approximately 10 million children work in cotton fields (harvesting, hand-pollination). Fair-trade certification requires ending child labour and ensuring school access — a powerful incentive for reform.
  5. Leather tanning pollution in Kanpur — Leather tanning (preparing hides for leather goods) releases hexavalent chromium and other toxins into the Ganges River in Kanpur, making the water unsuitable for drinking or agriculture. Thousands of workers are exposed to toxic chemicals.
  6. Fast fashion waste cycle — Fast-fashion brands release new collections weekly, encouraging consumers to buy cheaply and discard frequently. A garment worn 3–5 times is discarded. Global textile waste: 92 million tonnes/year; much ends up in landfills in developing countries.
  7. Dyeing and chemical pollution — Dyeing is the second-most polluting stage (after agriculture) in textile production. Reactive dyes release heavy metals (chromium, lead, cadmium) into waterways. A single garment factory can pollute as much as a city of 50,000 people.
  8. Water scarcity in cotton regions — A single cotton t-shirt requires 2,700 litres of water to produce. In India, Pakistan, and Central Asia, cotton farming depletes groundwater, causing wells to run dry and forcing farmers into debt and migration.
  9. Textile-to-textile recycling challenges — Most used clothing ends up in landfills because recycling is complex (fibre blends are hard to separate, dyes are difficult to remove). Only 15% of global textile waste is recycled; the rest accumulates.
  10. Supply chain transparency movements — Consumers increasingly demand to know where their clothes are made, by whom, and under what conditions. Apps and websites (e.g., GoodOnYou) rate fashion brands on labour and environmental practices, empowering informed choices.

🌍 In global economic justice

The textile industry is a microcosm of global inequality: cheap clothes for rich consumers depend on low wages for poor workers. A USD 5 t-shirt is only affordable because a garment worker earns less than USD 150 per month. A fair-trade alternative (USD 20) attempts to address this inequity by ensuring workers earn living wages. But fair-trade represents only ~1% of global garment sales — most people buy cheap, fast fashion without awareness of its human and environmental costs. Understanding this system is the first step toward change: choosing fair-trade or secondhand clothes, supporting labour-rights organizations, and demanding transparency from brands.

🧪 Try this at home

Check the labels of your clothes and note where they're made. (Likely: China, Bangladesh, India, Vietnam, Indonesia.) Research wages in those countries for garment workers. If your t-shirt cost USD 5, estimate what the factory worker earned (typically 5–10% of retail price). Calculate: If a worker earns USD 100/month and works 26 days/month, they earn USD 3.85/day. How many t-shirts would they need to produce daily to earn that? (Each t-shirt takes roughly 2 hours: so 4 shirts/day to earn USD 100/month.) Does this feel fair? Research fair-trade and organic clothing alternatives — how much does the same t-shirt cost? Where does the price difference go?

Beyond the textbook

The textile industry is moving toward greater sustainability and fairness, driven by consumer demand, labour movements, and technological innovation. Recycled polyester from plastic bottles reduces virgin petroleum extraction. Regenerated cellulose (viscose, lyocell) from wood pulp offers a more sustainable plant-based alternative to synthetic polymers. Lab-grown spider silk eliminates resource-intensive sericulture. Blockchain technology enables supply-chain transparency, allowing consumers to trace a garment from farmer to factory to retail. Fair-trade certification and labour audits are expanding. Simultaneously, circular fashion models (rental, secondhand, repair) aim to reduce waste. The future of textiles will likely involve blending these approaches: sustainable materials, ethical labour practices, reduced waste, and informed, conscious consumption.
