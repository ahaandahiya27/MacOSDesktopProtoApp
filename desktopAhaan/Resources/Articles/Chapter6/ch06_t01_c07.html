Galvanisation and Preventing Rust

How a thin coat of zinc saves bridges, buildings, and billions of dollars.

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

We have learned that iron rusts when it meets oxygen and water. Rusting destroys iron structures and costs the world enormous sums of money. So how do engineers fight back? One of the best weapons is galvanisation -- coating iron with a layer of zinc. Let us explore how it works and why it is so effective.

What Is Galvanisation?

Galvanisation is the process of applying a protective zinc coating to iron or steel to prevent rusting. The most common method is hot-dip galvanisation: the iron object is dipped into a bath of molten zinc at about 450 degrees Celsius. When it is pulled out, a thin, even layer of zinc sticks to the surface and hardens as it cools.

The zinc layer acts as a barrier between the iron and the environment. Water and oxygen cannot reach the iron, so rusting cannot begin.

Why Zinc?

You might wonder: why zinc and not some other metal? There are two big reasons:

1. Zinc is more reactive than iron

In chemistry, metals are ranked by their reactivity. Zinc is more reactive than iron. This means that even if the zinc coating gets scratched and the iron underneath is exposed, the zinc will react with oxygen and water before the iron does. The zinc "sacrifices" itself to protect the iron. Scientists call this sacrificial protection.

2. Zinc oxide forms a tough shield

When zinc reacts with oxygen and moisture, it forms a thin layer of zinc oxide and zinc carbonate on the surface. Unlike iron oxide (rust), which is flaky and porous, zinc oxide is tough and sticks firmly to the surface. It actually seals the zinc underneath, slowing down further corrosion. So the zinc protects itself while also protecting the iron!

Where does the name come from?

Galvanisation is named after Luigi Galvani, an Italian scientist who studied electricity in the 18th century. He discovered that muscles twitch when touched by different metals -- an early step towards understanding electrochemistry.

Other Methods of Preventing Rust

Galvanisation is not the only way to fight rust. Here is a roundup of all the major methods:

Painting

A coat of paint keeps air and moisture away. This is the most common method for large structures. The Eiffel Tower in Paris is repainted every seven years using about 60 tonnes of paint!

Oiling and Greasing

Machine parts, bicycle chains, and tools are kept oiled. The oil forms a thin waterproof layer. It needs to be reapplied regularly because oil can dry out or wash off.

Electroplating

Iron can be coated with metals like chromium (chrome plating) or nickel. This gives a shiny, attractive, rust-proof finish. Bicycle handlebars, car bumpers, and taps are often chrome-plated.

Alloying -- Making Stainless Steel

Mix iron with chromium (about 10-12%) and nickel, and you get stainless steel. The chromium forms an invisible oxide layer that stops further corrosion. Stainless steel is used for kitchen sinks, surgical instruments, cutlery, and food-processing equipment.

Using Sacrificial Anodes

Large ships and underground pipelines are protected by attaching blocks of zinc or magnesium to the iron structure. These blocks corrode instead of the iron. They are replaced periodically -- much cheaper than replacing an entire ship hull!

The Eiffel Tower's battle with rust

Built from 7,300 tonnes of iron in 1889, the Eiffel Tower would have crumbled long ago without protection. It has been repainted 19 times. Each painting takes about 18 months and uses 60 tonnes of paint in three colours (darker at the bottom, lighter at the top) to create an even appearance from the ground.

Try this at home

Find two iron nails. Coat one with a thick layer of clear nail polish (simulating a protective coating) and leave the other bare. Place both in a glass of salty water. Check every day for a week. Which nail rusts first? This demonstrates how a protective barrier prevents rusting.

Why Rust Prevention Matters

According to some estimates, about 25% of the world's steel production each year goes to replacing structures lost to corrosion. That is a staggering amount of material, energy, and money. Effective rust prevention methods like galvanisation save resources, keep structures safe, and reduce waste.

Imagine if bridges, railway tracks, and water pipes had no protection against rust. They would need replacing every few years instead of lasting decades. Galvanisation and other methods literally hold our infrastructure together.
