Q1. Classify the following as physical or chemical changes: tearing paper, ripening of fruit, cooking food, melting wax, burning candle, glowing electric bulb.

Model answer: Physical: tearing paper (paper still paper), melting wax (wax still wax — can be re-frozen), glowing electric bulb (filament heats but doesn't change identity once cooled). Chemical: ripening fruit (new sugars and aroma compounds form), cooking food (Maillard + protein denaturation), burning candle (wax + O₂ → CO₂ + water — new substances, irreversible).

Q2. State whether the following statements are True or False. In case a statement is false, write the corrected statement. (a) Cutting a log of wood into pieces is a chemical change. (b) Formation of manure from leaves is a physical change. (c) Iron pipes coated with zinc do not get rusted easily. (d) Iron and rust are the same substances.

Model answer: (a) False — cutting wood is a physical change (smaller pieces, same wood). (b) False — composting leaves into manure involves bacterial breakdown into different substances, so it's a chemical change. (c) True — galvanising (zinc coating) protects iron from rusting (sacrificial protection). (d) False — iron is the metal element Fe; rust is iron oxide Fe₂O₃·H₂O, a different compound with different properties.

Q3. Anaerobic bacteria digest animal waste and produce biogas (Change-A). The biogas is then burnt as fuel (Change-B). Which of these are chemical changes?

Model answer: Both Change-A and Change-B are chemical changes. (A) Bacterial digestion converts complex organic waste (proteins, carbs) into methane + CO₂ — new substances, irreversible. (B) Burning biogas: methane + oxygen → CO₂ + water + heat — new substances, irreversible. Both involve new compounds, energy release, and cannot be reversed by simple physical means.

Q4. Explain why burning of wood and cutting it into small pieces are considered as two different types of changes.

Model answer: Cutting wood is a physical change — the smaller pieces are still wood, with the same colour, density, chemical composition. You could (in principle) glue them back. Burning wood is a chemical change — wood combines with oxygen to form CO₂, water vapour, and ash. The new substances have different colour, density, and composition. You cannot un-burn the wood to get the log back. New substances + irreversibility = chemical change.

Q5. Describe how crystals of copper sulphate are prepared.

Model answer: (1) Take warm distilled water in a beaker. (2) Dissolve copper sulphate powder into it, stirring, until no more dissolves (saturated solution). (3) Filter to remove any undissolved bits. (4) Cover the filtrate with paper (so dust doesn't fall in) and leave undisturbed for 1-2 days in a cool place. (5) As water slowly evaporates and the solution cools, blue copper sulphate crystals form on the bottom and sides. (6) The crystals are characteristic — five-sided blue crystals showing the regular shape of the molecule itself.

Q6. Explain how painting an iron gate prevents it from rusting.

Model answer: Rusting needs three things: iron, water, and oxygen. Paint forms a continuous physical barrier between the iron surface and the air + moisture around it. Without water and oxygen reaching the iron, the rusting reaction cannot start. As long as the paint stays intact, the gate stays rust-free. Where paint chips off, rusting begins immediately. This is why old iron gates need repainting every 2–3 years (or after monsoon damage).

Q7. Explain why rusting of iron objects is faster in coastal areas than in deserts.

Model answer: Three reasons all stronger near the coast. (1) Humidity is high — air carries lots of water vapour, the key reactant in rusting. (2) Salt particles in sea-air act as an electrolyte that speeds the rusting reaction many times. (3) Temperature is moderate-to-warm, the optimum range for the chemistry. In dry deserts, water is scarce — the reaction barely happens; metal artefacts found in Rajasthan deserts are often centuries old and barely rusted, while same-age objects in coastal Kerala are flakes.

Q8. Give an example of a chemical reaction for each of the following situations: (a) A pleasant smell is produced. (b) A pleasant smell vanishes.

Model answer: (a) Baking a cake — the Maillard reaction between sugars and amino acids in the batter, plus carbonisation of crust starch, releases the warm-buttery-sweet smell of fresh cake. (b) Curdling of milk — the fresh-milk aroma is replaced by the sharp lactic-acid smell of dahi. Both are chemical changes — neither aroma can be reversed.
