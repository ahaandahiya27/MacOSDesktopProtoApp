Three "What Ifs?" About Physical and Chemical Changes

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if iron didn't rust?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Roman aqueducts and Ashokan iron pillars would still be in pristine condition (the Iron Pillar of Delhi actually IS — its unique alloy resists rust). Bridges and ships would last centuries instead of decades. India's railways wouldn't spend ~₹10,000 crore yearly on rust repair. Architecture would change — taller, lighter structures because we wouldn't need to over-engineer for corrosion. Cars and motorbikes would last a generation. The downside: discarded iron would never return to soil (rust eventually breaks down) — the planet would have iron pollution where today rust returns iron to the earth.

What if 2 — What if cooking didn't change food chemically?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Raw food only. No bread (yeast fermentation = chemical change), no roti (Maillard browning = chemical), no boiled rice (starch gelatinisation = chemical), no dahi (lactic fermentation = chemical), no chai (oxidation of tea leaves), no cooked dal (protein denaturation). We'd eat fruits, raw vegetables, some seeds, raw fish. Carnivores can do this; humans have shorter intestines specifically because cooking pre-digests food for us. Without chemical cooking, our entire diet and digestion biology would change.

What if 3 — What if you could SEE chemical reactions happening at atomic scale?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: You'd see something like a dance: molecules approaching each other, bonds stretching, breaking, new bonds forming in different patterns. A burning match would look like billions of CO₂ and water molecules being assembled from carbon and oxygen, exhaling heat. Rusting iron would look like oxygen molecules attaching to surface iron atoms, slowly building up brownish iron oxide layer. You'd see WHY some reactions are fast (matches) and some slow (rusting) — it's just how often the right molecules meet at the right energy. This is exactly what Class 11 'kinetic theory' tries to give you with equations.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
