Phase Transitions — Solid, Liquid, Gas, Plasma

The four states of matter and the energy that transforms one into another

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

You already know that water can be a solid (ice), a liquid (water), or a gas (steam). But did you know there is a fourth state — plasma — that makes up more than 99 percent of the visible universe? Stars, lightning bolts, and neon signs are all plasma. The transitions between these four states are called phase transitions, and every single one of them is a physical change. The substance stays the same; only the behaviour of its particles shifts dramatically.

What this concept means

A phase transition (also called a change of state) is the transformation of matter from one state to another — solid to liquid, liquid to gas, and so on. Each state is characterised by a different arrangement and energy level of particles:

  • Solid — Particles are packed tightly in fixed positions. They vibrate but do not move from place to place. Solids have a definite shape and volume.
  • Liquid — Particles are close together but free to slide past each other. Liquids have a definite volume but take the shape of their container.
  • Gas — Particles are far apart and move rapidly in all directions. Gases have neither a definite shape nor a definite volume; they expand to fill any container.
  • Plasma — At extremely high temperatures, electrons are stripped from atoms, creating a soup of ions and free electrons. Plasma conducts electricity and responds to magnetic fields.

Why it works this way

Phase transitions are all about energy. Every substance has particles that are attracted to each other by intermolecular forces (in covalent substances) or ionic/metallic bonds (in ionic compounds and metals). In a solid, these forces hold particles in fixed positions. To move from solid to liquid (melting), you must add enough energy to partly overcome these forces, allowing particles to slide past each other. To move from liquid to gas (boiling), you must add even more energy to completely overcome the forces and let particles fly apart.

The temperature at which a substance melts is called its melting point, and the temperature at which it boils is its boiling point. These are characteristic physical properties of each substance. Water melts at 0 degrees Celsius and boils at 100 degrees Celsius (at standard atmospheric pressure). Iron melts at 1,538 degrees Celsius. Helium boils at minus 269 degrees Celsius.

An important detail: during a phase transition, the temperature of the substance remains constant even though you are adding heat. This is because the energy goes into overcoming intermolecular forces rather than increasing the kinetic energy (speed) of the particles. This absorbed energy is called latent heat. For water, the latent heat of fusion (melting) is 334 joules per gram, and the latent heat of vaporisation (boiling) is a whopping 2,260 joules per gram — which is why steam burns are so much more severe than hot water burns.

Sublimation is the direct transition from solid to gas, skipping the liquid phase. Dry ice (solid CO2) does this at room temperature and pressure. Mothballs (naphthalene) also sublimate slowly, which is why they shrink over time and fill your wardrobe with a distinctive smell.

Deposition is the reverse — gas directly to solid. Frost on a window pane forms by deposition: water vapour in the air contacts the cold glass and forms ice crystals without first becoming liquid water.

Did you know?

Plasma makes up more than 99 percent of the visible matter in the universe. Every star — including our Sun — is a gigantic ball of plasma. On Earth, natural plasma is rarer: you see it in lightning bolts, the aurora borealis, and the ionosphere (the upper atmosphere). Humans create plasma in fluorescent lights, neon signs, plasma TVs, and welding arcs. Despite being the most common state of matter in the cosmos, plasma was the last to be identified, named by Irving Langmuir in 1928.

Real-world applications

  1. Steam engines — Water is boiled to produce steam, which expands and pushes pistons. The liquid-to-gas transition powers the machine.
  2. Freeze-drying food — Food is frozen and then placed in a vacuum. The ice sublimates directly to vapour, preserving the food without cooking it.
  3. Air conditioning — Refrigerant fluid cycles between liquid and gas states, absorbing heat from indoors (evaporating) and releasing it outdoors (condensing).
  4. Welding — Metals are heated past their melting points and fused together. On cooling, the molten metal solidifies into a strong joint.
  5. Cloud formation and rain — Water evaporates from oceans, rises, cools, and condenses into cloud droplets. When droplets grow heavy enough, they fall as rain.
  6. Casting metals — Molten metal is poured into moulds and allowed to solidify. Every metal tool, engine block, and sculpture starts as a liquid.
  7. Dry ice special effects — Dry ice sublimates at minus 78.5 degrees Celsius, producing dense, cold CO2 gas that creates dramatic fog effects on stage.
  8. Plasma cutting — A jet of plasma at 20,000 degrees Celsius cuts through metal like butter, used in manufacturing and construction.
  9. Distillation — Boiling a mixture and condensing the vapour separates components by their boiling points. Used to produce spirits, purify water, and refine petroleum.
  10. Snow-making machines — Water is sprayed into cold air, where it freezes into tiny ice crystals. Ski resorts use this to guarantee snow on the slopes.

In your kitchen right now

Boil a kettle and watch the spout. The clear gap between the spout and the white "steam" cloud is where the water exists as invisible, superheated gas. The white cloud you see is actually not steam — it is tiny liquid water droplets formed by condensation as the hot gas meets cooler room air. So you are watching two phase transitions in quick succession: liquid to gas (inside the kettle) and gas to liquid (in the visible cloud outside).

Try this at home

Observe sublimation without dry ice. Place a few mothballs (naphthalene) on a saucer in a well-ventilated area (not enclosed, and with adult supervision). Check on them every few days. You will notice they slowly shrink and eventually disappear entirely. They have sublimated — turned directly from solid to gas — without ever becoming liquid. The naphthalene vapour is what you smell. This is a slow-motion phase transition happening right before your eyes.

Check your understanding

Question: Why does your wet laundry dry on a clothesline even on a day when the temperature is well below 100 degrees Celsius?

Answer: Boiling is not the only way water can become a gas. Evaporation occurs at any temperature when individual water molecules at the surface gain enough kinetic energy (from the sun, wind, or warmth of the air) to break free from the liquid and enter the gas phase. On a windy, sunny day, evaporation happens faster because the wind carries away water vapour molecules, preventing them from re-condensing onto the clothes. Over time, all the liquid water evaporates even though the temperature never reached 100 degrees Celsius.

Try another

Question: Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) is placed on a table at room temperature. It produces a thick, white fog but leaves no puddle. Explain what is happening in terms of phase transitions.

Answer: Dry ice sublimates — it transitions directly from solid to gas without passing through the liquid phase. At standard atmospheric pressure, carbon dioxide does not exist as a liquid; it goes straight from solid (at minus 78.5 degrees Celsius) to gas. The white fog you see is not CO2 gas (which is invisible). It is water vapour from the air condensing into tiny liquid droplets because the surrounding air is cooled dramatically by the dry ice. So you actually see two phase transitions: sublimation of CO2 (solid to gas) and condensation of atmospheric water (gas to liquid).
