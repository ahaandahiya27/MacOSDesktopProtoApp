Chemical Change -- New Substance, New Properties

When matter transforms into something completely different.

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Strike a match. In a flash, the matchstick head bursts into flame, producing heat, light, smoke, and ash. What you are left with is utterly different from what you started with. That is a chemical change -- and once it happens, there is no going back.

What Is a Chemical Change?

A chemical change (also called a chemical reaction) is a change in which one or more new substances are formed. The atoms in the original substances rearrange themselves and bond together in new ways, producing products with completely different properties.

Think of it like rearranging LEGO bricks. You start with a LEGO car. You pull the bricks apart and use the same bricks to build a LEGO house. The bricks (atoms) are the same, but the structure (substance) is entirely new.

Everyday Examples

  • Burning wood: Wood + oxygen turns into ash, carbon dioxide, and water vapour.
  • Rusting of iron: Iron + oxygen + moisture turns into iron oxide (rust).
  • Cooking an egg: Heat causes the proteins to change permanently.
  • Curdling of milk: Adding lemon juice or vinegar creates solid curds and liquid whey.
  • Digesting food: Enzymes break down food into simple substances your body can use.
  • Ripening of fruit: Complex chemical reactions change the colour, taste, and texture.

What Happens to Atoms During a Chemical Change?

Atoms are never created or destroyed during a chemical change. This is the Law of Conservation of Mass, proposed by Antoine Lavoisier in the 18th century. Instead, atoms simply rearrange. The bonds holding atoms together in the original substances (called reactants) break apart, and new bonds form to create different substances (called products).

For example, when iron rusts:

Iron + Oxygen + Water → Iron oxide (rust)

The iron atoms and oxygen atoms are still there, but they are now joined together in a new way, forming a new compound with new properties -- reddish-brown, flaky, and brittle instead of shiny and strong.

Chemical changes in your body

Right now, your body is running thousands of chemical reactions. Your cells are breaking down glucose to release energy (respiration), building proteins from amino acids, and even copying DNA. Life itself is powered by chemical changes!

Energy in Chemical Changes

Chemical changes always involve energy. Reactions that release energy (usually as heat or light) are called exothermic reactions. Burning is exothermic -- that is why fire is hot. Reactions that absorb energy are called endothermic reactions. When you dissolve ammonium nitrate in water, the solution gets cold because the reaction absorbs heat from the surroundings.

Exothermic examples

  • Burning fuels (wood, petrol, natural gas)
  • Rusting of iron (slow, so you do not feel the heat)
  • Setting of cement

Endothermic examples

  • Photosynthesis (plants absorb light energy)
  • Cooking (food absorbs heat energy)
  • Cold packs used for sports injuries

Did you know?

The explosion of a firecracker is a chemical change that happens in less than one-thousandth of a second. In that tiny blink, solid chemicals are converted into hot gases that expand so fast they create a shock wave -- the bang you hear.

Why Chemical Changes Are Usually Irreversible

Once the atoms have rearranged and new bonds have formed, it takes a lot of energy or a completely different reaction to put them back the way they were. You cannot un-burn a matchstick. You cannot un-rust a nail (well, not easily). The products are so different from the reactants that "undoing" the change would require its own separate chemical process.

However, some chemical changes can be reversed. For example, some reactions can be reversed by heating, adding another chemical, or passing electricity through the products. These are less common but important in industry.

Try this at home

Place a small piece of bread in a toaster (with an adult's help). The bread turns brown and crispy -- that is a chemical change called the Maillard reaction. New flavour molecules are formed. Try to "un-toast" the bread. You cannot! That tells you it was a chemical change.

Quick check: Why is burning wood a chemical change?

Because new substances are formed -- ash, carbon dioxide gas, and water vapour. The wood cannot be recovered from these products. The composition of the matter has changed.

Quick check: What is the difference between an exothermic and an endothermic reaction?

An exothermic reaction releases energy (heat/light) to the surroundings. An endothermic reaction absorbs energy from the surroundings.

Quick check: Rearranging atoms without creating or destroying any is described by which law?

The Law of Conservation of Mass. The total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products.
