Physical Change -- Same Stuff, Different Shape

When matter changes its look but keeps its identity.

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Imagine taking a ball of clay and squishing it into a flat pancake. It looks completely different now, but it is still clay. That is the heart of a physical change: the substance stays the same even though its shape, size, or state may look totally different.

What Is a Physical Change?

A physical change is any change in which no new substance is formed. The chemical composition -- the tiny molecules and atoms that make up the material -- remains exactly the same. Only the physical properties change. These properties include things like shape, size, colour, state of matter (solid, liquid, gas), and temperature.

Here is a simple way to think about it: if you could somehow zoom in with a super-powerful microscope and look at the molecules before and after the change, you would see the same molecules in both cases. They might be moving faster or slower, they might be arranged differently, but they are the same molecules.

Everyday Examples

  • Melting ice: Solid water (ice) turns into liquid water. The molecules are still H₂O.
  • Boiling water: Liquid water turns into steam (water vapour). Still H₂O!
  • Tearing paper: The paper is now in smaller pieces, but each piece is still paper.
  • Dissolving sugar in water: The sugar seems to disappear, but it is still there -- you can taste it. (More on this tricky case later!)
  • Breaking a glass: Sad, but still glass.
  • Cutting wood: Smaller pieces, same wood.
  • Freezing juice into an ice lolly: It changes state, but the juice is still juice.

Changes of State -- The Classic Physical Changes

The most common physical changes involve changes of state. Matter can exist in three common states: solid, liquid, and gas. Moving between these states is always a physical change because the molecules themselves do not change -- only how fast they move and how tightly they are packed.

Melting and Freezing

When you heat a solid, its particles gain energy and vibrate faster. At a certain temperature (the melting point), the particles have enough energy to break free from their fixed positions and slide over each other. The solid becomes a liquid. Cooling a liquid reverses this -- the particles slow down and lock into place again. That is freezing.

Evaporation and Condensation

Heat a liquid further and the fastest-moving particles escape from the surface as gas. This is evaporation. If the liquid reaches its boiling point, bubbles of gas form throughout the liquid -- that is boiling. The reverse, when gas cools and turns back into liquid, is called condensation. Think of water droplets forming on a cold glass of lemonade on a hot day.

Sublimation

Some solids can skip the liquid stage entirely and turn straight into gas. This is called sublimation. Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) does this -- it turns into CO₂ gas without ever becoming a liquid at normal air pressure. Naphthalene balls (mothballs) also sublimate slowly, which is why they shrink over time.

Try this at home

Take an ice cube out of the freezer and place it on a plate. Watch it melt into water. Now pour that water into a pan and, with an adult's help, heat it until it boils. You have just watched two physical changes in a row: solid to liquid, then liquid to gas. And the substance was always H₂O!

Why Are Physical Changes Usually Reversible?

Because no new substance is created, you can often undo a physical change. Melt ice? Freeze it back. Dissolve salt in water? Evaporate the water and get the salt back. Break a stick? Okay, that one is hard to reverse perfectly, but the wood itself has not changed into something else.

Reversibility is a handy clue, but be careful -- it is not a perfect test. Some physical changes are very difficult to reverse in practice (like unscrambling an egg's shape), and some chemical changes can be reversed under special conditions. We will explore this more in the article on reversible vs irreversible changes.

Fun fact

Water is one of the few substances that expands when it freezes. That is why ice floats on water -- and why pipes can burst in very cold winters!

Physical Changes and Energy

Physical changes involve energy, even if no new substance is formed. Melting ice needs heat energy (the ice absorbs it from its surroundings). Boiling water needs even more heat. Condensation and freezing release heat back into the surroundings. Scientists call the energy needed to melt a substance its latent heat of fusion, and the energy needed to boil it is the latent heat of vaporisation.

Even though energy is absorbed or released, the chemical identity of the substance does not change. The molecules are still the same; they just have more or less energy.

How to Tell If a Change Is Physical

Ask yourself these questions:

  1. Is the substance the same before and after? (If yes, it is physical.)
  2. Can I get the original substance back easily? (If yes, probably physical.)
  3. Were any new gases, smells, colours, or precipitates produced? (If no, likely physical.)

If you can answer "yes" to the first two and "no" to the third, you are almost certainly looking at a physical change.

Quick check: Is breaking a pencil a physical or chemical change?

Physical change. The pencil is now in two pieces, but each piece is still made of the same wood and graphite. No new substance has been formed.

Quick check: When water evaporates from a puddle, what kind of change is it?

Physical change. Liquid water has become water vapour (gas), but the molecules are still H₂O. If the vapour cools, it condenses right back into liquid water.

Quick check: Name three physical changes you might see before lunch today.

Possible answers: melting butter on toast, boiling water for tea, cutting vegetables, dissolving sugar in milk, ice melting in a glass of water.
