Rusting of Iron -- The Famous Chemical Change

How oxygen and water team up to slowly eat away at iron.

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Have you ever seen an old bicycle left out in the rain? Over time, it gets covered in a reddish-brown, flaky coating. That coating is rust -- and it is one of the most famous examples of a chemical change you will ever encounter.

What Is Rust?

Rust is the common name for iron oxide, a compound with the chemical formula Fe₂O₃. It forms when iron reacts with oxygen in the presence of water (moisture). The reaction can be written simply as:

Iron + Oxygen + Water → Iron oxide (rust)

Rust is very different from iron. Iron is shiny, strong, and grey. Rust is dull, brittle, reddish-brown, and crumbly. Because a completely new substance with new properties is formed, rusting is a chemical change.

Conditions Needed for Rusting

For iron to rust, it needs both oxygen and water. Remove either one, and rusting stops. Scientists have confirmed this with a classic experiment using three test tubes:

Test TubeConditionsResult
AIron nail in ordinary water (contains dissolved oxygen)Rusts
BIron nail in boiled water (sealed, no oxygen) with a layer of oil on topDoes not rust
CIron nail in a dry tube with calcium chloride (absorbs moisture)Does not rust

This experiment proves that both water and oxygen are essential. Neither alone is enough.

Try this at home

Take three iron nails. Place one in a glass of water, one in a sealed plastic bag with some dry rice (to absorb moisture), and leave one on a dry windowsill. Check them after a week. Which nail has rusted the most? This simple experiment shows you the conditions needed for rusting.

Why Is Rusting a Problem?

Rust is not just ugly -- it is destructive. Rusting weakens iron and steel structures. Bridges, ships, cars, railway tracks, and buildings all suffer from rust damage. Around the world, rusting costs billions of dollars every year in repairs and replacements.

Unlike iron, rust is porous (full of tiny holes). This means that once rusting starts on the surface, water and oxygen can seep through to the iron beneath, and the rusting continues deeper and deeper. If left unchecked, rust can eat through an entire iron sheet.

Rusting is slow but steady

Rusting is a slow chemical reaction. Unlike burning, which happens in seconds, rusting can take days, weeks, or months. But it never stops as long as iron, water, and oxygen are in contact. Salty water speeds up rusting considerably -- that is why cars near the sea rust faster.

The Statue of Liberty

The Statue of Liberty in New York has an iron framework hidden inside its copper skin. Over the decades, the iron rusted badly. In the 1980s, the entire internal iron structure had to be replaced with stainless steel during a massive restoration project. Rust had been silently damaging the statue for nearly 100 years!

How to Prevent Rusting

Since rusting needs water and oxygen, prevention focuses on keeping one or both away from the iron. Here are the main methods:

  • Painting: A coat of paint creates a barrier between the iron and the air. This is why bridges and gates are painted regularly.
  • Oiling and greasing: A thin layer of oil prevents water and oxygen from touching the iron surface. Used on machine parts and tools.
  • Galvanisation: Coating iron with a layer of zinc. Even if the zinc coating gets scratched, zinc reacts with oxygen first and protects the iron underneath. (We have a whole article on this!)
  • Electroplating: Coating iron with another metal (like chromium or nickel) using electricity. This gives a shiny, rust-proof finish.
  • Alloying: Mixing iron with other elements like chromium and nickel to make stainless steel, which resists rusting.
  • Storing in dry conditions: Keeping iron tools dry and adding silica gel packets to toolboxes to absorb moisture.

Did you know?

The surface of Mars is red because it is covered in iron oxide -- rust! Mars is sometimes called the "Red Planet" because its soil and rocks contain huge amounts of rust formed billions of years ago.

Rusting vs Corrosion

Rusting is actually a specific type of a broader process called corrosion. Corrosion is the gradual destruction of any metal by chemical reactions with its environment. Copper corrodes to form a green coating (verdigris), silver tarnishes to become black, and aluminium forms a thin oxide layer. But we only use the word "rusting" for iron and steel. Other metals corrode, but they do not rust.

Interestingly, aluminium's oxide layer is actually helpful. Unlike rust, aluminium oxide forms a tight, invisible shield that stops further corrosion. That is why aluminium pots and window frames last a long time without being painted.

Quick check: What two substances must be present for iron to rust?

Oxygen (from the air) and water (moisture). Both are needed; remove either one and rusting will not occur.

Quick check: Why is rusting considered a chemical change and not a physical change?

Because a new substance -- iron oxide (Fe₂O₃) -- is formed. Iron oxide has completely different properties from iron. The change is irreversible under normal conditions.

Quick check: Give two methods of preventing rusting.

Any two of: painting, oiling/greasing, galvanisation (zinc coating), electroplating, alloying (stainless steel), or storing in dry conditions.
