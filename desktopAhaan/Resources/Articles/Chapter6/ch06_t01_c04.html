Reversibility — When Can You Undo a Change?

Some changes rewind easily; others are a one-way street

≈ 8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Freeze water into ice, then melt it back into water. Easy. Now try un-burning a matchstick. Impossible. The difference between these two situations lies in a single, powerful idea: reversibility. Some changes can be undone; others cannot. Understanding why is the key to predicting what will happen to materials when you heat, cool, mix, or transform them.

What this concept means

A reversible change is one that can be undone to recover the original substance in its original form. The classic example is melting and freezing: ice melts into water (add heat), and water freezes back into ice (remove heat). The original substance — H2O — is recovered completely.

An irreversible change is one that cannot be undone to recover the original substance. Burning wood is the most dramatic example: the wood is converted into ash, carbon dioxide, and water vapour. You cannot reassemble these products back into wood.

Here is the important nuance: reversibility is not the same as being physical or chemical. Most physical changes are reversible, and most chemical changes are irreversible — but there are exceptions on both sides.

Physical changes that are easily reversible

  • Melting and freezing (ice to water and back)
  • Evaporation and condensation (water to steam and back)
  • Dissolving and crystallising (salt in water, then evaporate to recover salt)
  • Stretching and releasing a rubber band
  • Magnetising and demagnetising iron

Physical changes that are hard to reverse

  • Tearing paper — the fibres are separated and cannot easily be re-joined
  • Breaking a glass — you cannot un-shatter it without melting and remoulding
  • Mixing paint colours — once blue and yellow are stirred together, separating them back is virtually impossible
  • Cutting hair — the strands cannot be reattached (though they will grow back biologically)

Chemical changes that are reversible

Yes, some chemical changes can be reversed, though it usually requires significant energy or a separate chemical reaction:

  • Dissolving zinc in hydrochloric acid produces zinc chloride and hydrogen gas — but electrolysis can reverse this, depositing zinc from the solution
  • Heating limestone (CaCO3) produces quicklime (CaO) and CO2. Adding water to quicklime and then CO2 can regenerate calcium carbonate
  • Charging a rechargeable battery reverses the chemical reaction that occurred during discharging

Why it works this way

Reversibility depends on two factors: energy and entropy (disorder).

For a physical change like melting, the molecules are rearranged but not broken apart. To reverse the change, you simply remove the energy you added (cool the liquid), and the molecules settle back into their original crystalline arrangement. The energy cost of reversal is modest, and the disorder introduced is small.

For a chemical change like burning, however, the original molecules are completely destroyed. Carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen bonds in wood are broken, and new bonds form to make CO2 and H2O. These products disperse into the atmosphere — the carbon dioxide mixes with the air, the water vapour drifts away. Even if you could somehow reverse the chemical reaction, you would need to collect every molecule of CO2 and H2O that escaped, which is practically impossible. The entropy (disorder) of the system has increased enormously, and the second law of thermodynamics tells us that natural processes tend to increase disorder, not decrease it.

Physical changes that are hard to reverse (like tearing paper) fall in the middle. The molecules are unchanged, so in principle the change is reversible. But the practical difficulty of aligning millions of broken fibres makes it effectively irreversible without specialised processes (like paper recycling, which pulps the paper and re-forms it — a reversal, but not to the original sheet).

Did you know?

Rechargeable batteries are one of the most elegant examples of reversible chemical changes in everyday life. In a lithium-ion battery (the kind in your phone), lithium ions move from one electrode to the other when the battery discharges, and the chemical reaction reverses when you plug in the charger. This back-and-forth can happen hundreds of times before the battery degrades. Each cycle is a complete chemical change, reversed by electrical energy.

Real-world applications

  1. Refrigeration — Fridges exploit the reversible physical change of a refrigerant gas condensing and evaporating in a continuous cycle to keep food cold.
  2. Rechargeable batteries — Reversible chemical changes allow phones, laptops, and electric cars to recharge thousands of times.
  3. Water purification — Distillation (evaporation followed by condensation) exploits reversible physical changes to separate pure water from dissolved impurities.
  4. Recycling metals — Aluminium and steel are melted and re-formed — reversible physical changes that reduce the need for mining new ore.
  5. Dry cleaning — Solvents dissolve stains (reversible physical change), which are then separated from the clothes by evaporation.
  6. Sugar refining — Raw sugar is dissolved, purified, and recrystallised. Each step is a reversible physical change.
  7. Erasable ink — Special inks become transparent when heated (by the friction of an eraser) and reappear when cooled. A reversible physical change in the dye molecules' arrangement.
  8. Shape-memory alloys — Certain metal alloys (like nitinol) can be bent and then restored to their original shape by heating. Used in stents, braces, and spacecraft components.
  9. Cloud seeding — Introducing particles into clouds to promote condensation (a reversible physical change) and trigger rainfall.
  10. Thermal energy storage — Materials like molten salt store solar energy by absorbing heat (melting) and release it by solidifying — a reversible physical change used in solar power plants.

In your kitchen right now

Open the freezer and look at the ice tray. Freeze, melt, freeze, melt — you can cycle water between solid and liquid as many times as you like, and you will always get the same substance. Now look at the toaster. Once bread is toasted, you cannot un-toast it. The Maillard reaction has created new flavour compounds and the starches have been chemically altered. The freezer demonstrates perfect reversibility; the toaster demonstrates irreversibility. Both are in the same kitchen, side by side.

Try this at home

Test reversibility with three experiments. (1) Dissolve a spoonful of salt in warm water, then pour some onto a plate and let it evaporate in the sun. Did you get the salt back? (Yes — reversible.) (2) Fold a piece of paper into an aeroplane, then unfold it. Can you perfectly restore the flat sheet? (Nearly, but creases remain — partly reversible.) (3) With an adult's help, toast a piece of bread. Can you un-toast it? (No — irreversible.) Rank the three from most to least reversible and explain why.

Check your understanding

Question: A potter shapes clay into a pot and lets it air-dry. Is this reversible? What if the pot is then fired in a kiln at 1,000 degrees Celsius?

Answer: Air-drying is reversible: adding water to the dry clay softens it, and you can reshape it. This is a physical change. However, firing the pot in a kiln causes a chemical change — the clay minerals undergo permanent chemical transformation (sintering and vitrification), forming new crystalline structures. The fired pot cannot be turned back into soft clay, no matter how much water you add. The kiln converts a reversible physical change into an irreversible chemical one.

Try another

Question: Is mixing milk and water reversible? How about curdling milk with lemon juice?

Answer: Mixing milk and water is a physical change that is theoretically reversible (you could separate them by careful distillation or other techniques, since the milk components and water are simply intermingled). In practice, it is difficult. Curdling milk with lemon juice, however, is a chemical change: the citric acid causes the casein protein in milk to denature and coagulate, forming curds and whey. This cannot be reversed — you cannot turn paneer back into milk.
