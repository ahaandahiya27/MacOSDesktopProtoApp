Chemical Changes

When atoms rearrange and new substances are born

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Light a match and watch it burn. In a matter of seconds, a wooden stick topped with potassium chlorate and sulphur transforms into ash, smoke, and a burst of light. The original substances are gone, replaced by entirely new ones. This is a chemical change — the most powerful kind of transformation matter can undergo. Chemical changes build your bones, power your car, rust your bicycle, and cook your dinner. Understanding them is understanding the engine room of the universe.

What this concept means

A chemical change (also called a chemical reaction) is a process in which one or more substances are converted into one or more entirely new substances with different chemical properties. Unlike physical changes, which merely rearrange molecules in space, chemical changes break the bonds within molecules and form new bonds, creating new molecules altogether.

The starting substances are called reactants; the new substances produced are called products. A chemical change can be represented by a chemical equation. For example, when iron reacts with oxygen and water to form rust:

4 Fe + 3 O2 + 6 H2O → 4 Fe(OH)3

Iron, oxygen, and water are the reactants. Iron hydroxide (which eventually becomes rust, Fe2O3) is the product. The product has completely different properties from the reactants: it is reddish-brown, flaky, and brittle, while iron is grey, shiny, and strong.

Chemical changes are generally irreversible under ordinary conditions. You cannot un-burn a match, un-rust a nail, or un-cook an egg. The new bonds formed in the products are stable, and reversing them requires significant energy or a completely different chemical process.

Why it works this way

At the atomic level, a chemical change involves the breaking and forming of chemical bonds. Atoms are held together in molecules by shared or transferred electrons. In a chemical reaction, enough energy is supplied (by heat, light, electricity, or another catalyst) to break some of these bonds. The freed atoms then rearrange and form new bonds with different partners, creating new molecules.

Consider the burning of methane (natural gas):

CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O

The C-H bonds in methane and the O=O bonds in oxygen are broken. New C=O bonds (in carbon dioxide) and O-H bonds (in water) are formed. The products — carbon dioxide and water — have utterly different properties from the reactants. Carbon dioxide is a colourless gas that extinguishes fire; water is a liquid that sustains life. Neither resembles methane (a flammable gas) or oxygen (a gas that supports combustion).

Chemical changes are often irreversible because the new bonds are typically more stable (lower energy) than the original ones. The reaction releases energy as it moves toward a more stable state. To reverse the reaction, you would need to pump that energy back in and force the system uphill, which rarely happens spontaneously.

Did you know?

The human body runs on chemical changes. Digestion breaks down large food molecules into smaller ones through hydrolysis reactions. Cellular respiration oxidises glucose to produce carbon dioxide, water, and the energy molecule ATP. Even the signals traveling along your nerves involve chemical changes — ions moving through channels, neurotransmitters binding to receptors, and enzymes switching molecules on and off. You are, quite literally, a walking, talking chemical reaction.

Real-world applications

  1. Combustion engines — Cars burn petrol (a chemical change) to produce the energy that moves them.
  2. Photosynthesis — Plants convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen using sunlight.
  3. Medicine — Drugs work by triggering or blocking specific chemical changes in the body.
  4. Electroplating — Chemical changes at electrodes deposit a thin layer of metal (like chromium or gold) onto objects.
  5. Soap making — Fats react with sodium hydroxide (saponification) to produce soap and glycerol.
  6. Cement setting — Cement powder reacts with water in a chemical change called hydration, forming a hard, stone-like material.
  7. Fireworks — Carefully chosen chemicals undergo rapid combustion and decomposition to produce colours, sounds, and shapes in the sky.
  8. Digestion — Enzymes in your stomach and intestines catalyse chemical changes that break food into absorbable nutrients.
  9. Bleaching clothes — Bleach (sodium hypochlorite) chemically breaks down the coloured molecules in stains, rendering them colourless.
  10. Vulcanising rubber — Adding sulphur to natural rubber and heating it creates cross-links between polymer chains, making the rubber harder, more durable, and resistant to heat.

In your kitchen right now

Look at the gas stove. When you turn the knob and press the igniter, methane gas from the pipeline meets a spark and bursts into a blue flame. That flame is a chemical change in action: methane molecules are breaking apart and recombining with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water vapour. The blue colour comes from the excited CH radicals formed during combustion. Every second that flame burns, billions of billions of methane molecules undergo this irreversible transformation.

Try this at home

Place a small piece of steel wool (available at hardware stores) on a ceramic plate. With an adult's help, touch a 9-volt battery's terminals to the steel wool. The fine iron fibres will glow orange and begin to burn, producing iron oxide — rust. The steel wool shrinks and becomes brittle black ash. This is a chemical change: iron (Fe) reacts with oxygen (O2) in the air to form iron oxide (Fe2O3). The original shiny, flexible iron is gone, replaced by a crumbly, dark oxide. You cannot reverse this with bare hands.

Check your understanding

Question: How can you tell the difference between a physical change and a chemical change?

Answer: Look for these key differences: (1) In a physical change, no new substance is formed; in a chemical change, new substances with new properties appear. (2) Physical changes are usually reversible; chemical changes are usually irreversible. (3) Chemical changes are often accompanied by signs such as colour change, gas evolution, temperature change, precipitate formation, or new odours. (4) At the molecular level, physical changes rearrange molecules without breaking them; chemical changes break and reform chemical bonds.

Try another

Question: A student heats sugar gently and it melts into a clear liquid. She continues heating and it turns brown, then black, and releases a smell of caramel. At what point did a chemical change begin?

Answer: The melting of sugar into a clear liquid is a physical change — the sucrose molecules are intact, just in a liquid state. The chemical change begins when the sugar starts to turn brown. This browning is caramelisation: the sucrose molecules break down into smaller molecules (including various aldehydes and ketones that produce the caramel smell). The eventual blackening is further decomposition into carbon. The colour change, the new smell, and the formation of new substances all signal that a chemical change has occurred.
