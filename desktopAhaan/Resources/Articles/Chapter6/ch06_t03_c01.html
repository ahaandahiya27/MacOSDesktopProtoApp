Kitchen chemistry — baking soda and vinegar

Mixing baking soda (a base) and vinegar (an acid) produces salt, water, and CO₂ gas — the same neutralisation reaction you learned about, fizzing in front of your eyes.

≈ 2 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Pour a spoon of BAKING SODA (sodium bicarbonate) into a glass and add a splash of VINEGAR (dilute acetic acid). It fizzes and bubbles for a minute, then stops. What just happened? A neutralisation reaction. The acid in vinegar reacted with the base (baking soda) producing three things: WATER, a SALT (sodium acetate, dissolved invisibly), and CARBON DIOXIDE gas (the fizz). It's the same equation that runs in volcano-shaped science projects, in baking powder making cakes rise, and in some fire extinguishers. The kitchen is a chemistry lab in disguise.

The textbook version

Baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO₃) is a mild base. Vinegar contains acetic acid (CH₃COOH, ~5%). The reaction: NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa (sodium acetate) + H₂O + CO₂↑. This is a classic acid-base neutralisation producing salt + water, plus CO₂ (because the base contains a carbonate group). The CO₂ gas is what makes the fizz. Same reaction is used in: (a) baking powder (a dry mix of NaHCO₃ + a solid acid like cream of tartar) — when wet batter is heated, CO₂ is released, making cakes and bread rise; (b) some fire extinguishers — a solution of NaHCO₃ + acid generates CO₂ which smothers small fires; (c) school 'volcano' science projects.

Why does this happen?

Acids and bases neutralise each other. Baking soda is a base; vinegar is a (mild) acid. When they mix, the acid donates H⁺ and the base accepts it, breaking down to release CO₂ gas — the chemistry you see fizzing.

Where you see this in the real world

  • School 'volcano' project — Baking soda + vinegar + red food colouring + dish soap in a plastic-bottle volcano produces a foamy 'lava' eruption. [Kitchen]
  • Cake rising in the oven — Baking powder in the batter slowly releases CO₂ when heated — the trapped bubbles make the cake rise. [Kitchen]
  • Bicarbonate fire extinguisher — Older extinguishers contained a NaHCO₃ + dilute acid mixture; activation produced CO₂ that smothered the flames. [Building]

Going deeper

The reaction is rate-controlled by the dissociation of acetic acid (weak acid pKa ≈ 4.76) and the surface area of available NaHCO₃. CO₂ release rate determines the visible fizz. Industrially, the Solvay process makes ~50 Mt/year of sodium carbonate (washing soda) from CO₂ + NH₃ + NaCl — humanity's largest deliberate use of this acid-base salt chemistry. Baking-powder formulation is a topic in cereal-science engineering: the timing of CO₂ release (immediate vs delayed via temperature-activated acid components) controls dough rise profile.

Beyond the book

In Class 9 / 10 Chemistry, this is the canonical neutralisation reaction. JEE Chemistry covers the full acid-base equilibrium and pKa calculations. Class 12 / NEET Biology includes bicarbonate as the major blood buffer (CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻) — same chemistry, life-critical context.
