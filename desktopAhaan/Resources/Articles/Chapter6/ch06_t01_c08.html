Physical vs Chemical -- Tricky Cases

Dissolving sugar, curdling milk, and other changes that make students scratch their heads.

≈ 6 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

By now you know the basics: physical changes keep the same substance, chemical changes create new ones. But some everyday changes sit right on the boundary and can trick even teachers! Let us tackle the most confusing cases one by one.

Case 1: Dissolving Sugar in Water

When you stir sugar into water, it seems to disappear. Is this a chemical change?

Answer: Physical change. The sugar molecules (sucrose, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) are still intact. They have simply spread out among the water molecules. The proof? Evaporate the water and you get your sugar crystals back, exactly as they were. No new substance has been formed.

The taste test

If you taste the water, it is sweet. That tells you the sugar is still there, just invisible. If the sugar had been destroyed (chemical change), it would not taste sweet anymore.

Case 2: Dissolving Salt in Water

Similar to sugar, dissolving salt (NaCl) in water is a physical change. The Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions separate and spread out in the water, but they are still the same ions. Evaporate the water and salt crystals reappear.

However, there is a subtlety: when salt dissolves, the NaCl crystal lattice breaks apart into individual ions. Some scientists consider this a borderline case because the crystalline structure is destroyed. But since no new chemical substance is created, most textbooks classify it as a physical change.

Case 3: Curdling of Milk

Add a few drops of lemon juice or vinegar to warm milk. Within minutes, thick white clumps (curds) form and separate from a watery liquid (whey). Is this physical or chemical?

Answer: Chemical change. The acid in the lemon juice causes the milk protein (casein) to denature and clump together. The curds have different properties from the original milk -- they are solid, have a different texture, and cannot be turned back into liquid milk. A new substance has been formed.

Case 4: Melting of Wax

When a candle burns, the wax at the top melts and runs down the side. There are actually two changes happening at the same time:

  • Melting of wax (near the flame) -- physical change. Solid wax becomes liquid wax. Same substance, different state.
  • Burning of wax vapour (in the flame) -- chemical change. The wax vapour reacts with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water.

So a burning candle is both a physical and a chemical change happening together!

Candle science

The famous scientist Michael Faraday gave a series of Christmas lectures in 1848 called "The Chemical History of a Candle." He showed that something as simple as a candle involves melting, capillary action, evaporation, combustion, and the production of new gases -- all in one little flame.

Case 5: Dissolving Baking Soda in Water

Drop baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) into water. It dissolves and you might see a few tiny bubbles. Is this physical or chemical?

Answer: Mostly physical. The baking soda simply dissolves -- the ions spread out in the water. A tiny amount of CO₂ might be released (especially in warm water), which would be a chemical reaction, but the main process is dissolution (physical). Compare this with adding baking soda to vinegar, which is clearly chemical -- vigorous fizzing and lots of CO₂!

Case 6: Cutting a Magnesium Ribbon vs Burning It

Cutting a magnesium ribbon with scissors is a physical change -- you have smaller pieces of the same metal. But if you hold that ribbon in a flame, it burns with a dazzling white light and leaves behind a white powder called magnesium oxide. That is a chemical change because a new substance (MgO) has been formed.

Same material, two completely different changes depending on what you do to it!

Case 7: Mixing Iron Filings and Sulphur Powder

This is a classic exam question. Simply mixing iron filings and sulphur powder together is a physical change -- you can separate them again using a magnet (iron is magnetic, sulphur is not).

But if you heat the mixture strongly, the iron and sulphur react to form iron sulphide (FeS), a completely new compound. Iron sulphide is not magnetic, does not look like iron or sulphur, and cannot be separated by a magnet. That is a chemical change.

Why tricky cases matter

In real science, the boundary between physical and chemical changes is not always black and white. Chemists have to think carefully about what is really happening at the molecular level. This kind of critical thinking is exactly what makes science exciting -- and what makes good scientists.

A Decision Flowchart

When you are unsure, run through these questions in order:

  1. Has a new substance been formed? (Check if properties like colour, taste, smell, or chemical formula have changed.)
  2. Can you get the original substance back easily? (If yes, likely physical.)
  3. Did you see signs of a chemical reaction? (Gas, colour change, temperature change, precipitate.)

If a new substance has been formed, it is a chemical change -- regardless of whether it looks dramatic or not. Rusting is quiet and slow, but it is still chemical!

Challenge yourself

Classify each of these as physical or chemical: (a) drying wet clothes, (b) souring of milk, (c) making a dough ball from flour and water, (d) baking the dough into bread. Answers: (a) physical, (b) chemical, (c) physical, (d) chemical.

Quick check: Is dissolving sugar in water a physical or chemical change?

Physical change. The sugar molecules remain intact. You can recover the sugar by evaporating the water.

Quick check: A burning candle involves both physical and chemical changes. Explain.

The melting of solid wax into liquid wax is a physical change (same substance, different state). The burning of wax vapour in the flame is a chemical change (wax + oxygen produces CO₂ and H₂O -- new substances).

Quick check: How can you tell the difference between a mixture of iron and sulphur vs the compound iron sulphide?

The mixture can be separated using a magnet (iron is magnetic). Iron sulphide cannot be separated by a magnet. Also, adding dilute acid to iron sulphide produces foul-smelling hydrogen sulphide gas, while the mixture reacts differently.
