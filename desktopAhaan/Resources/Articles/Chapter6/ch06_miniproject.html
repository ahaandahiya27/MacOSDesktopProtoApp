Two Mini Projects for Chapter 6

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🔬 Grow your own copper sulphate crystal

~60 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • Copper sulphate powder (chemist shop)
  • A glass jar with warm water
  • A pencil and a piece of thread
  • A small paperweight
  • Adult supervision (CuSO₄ is poisonous if swallowed — wash hands after)

Steps

  1. Dissolve as much copper sulphate as the warm water will hold (saturated, bright blue).
  2. Tie a thread to the middle of a pencil. Tie a small paperclip or weight to the loose end.
  3. Lay the pencil across the jar mouth so the weighted thread hangs in the solution.
  4. Cover loosely with paper. Leave undisturbed for 2-3 days in a cool place.
  5. Crystals grow along the thread — first tiny seeds, then bigger five-sided blue crystals.
  6. If you wait longer, a single large crystal can form (1-2 cm).

👀 What you should see

Blue, five-sided crystals strung along the thread like a necklace.

Why it works: As water evaporates and the solution cools, it becomes oversaturated. The dissolved copper sulphate molecules find each other, lock into their natural geometric shape (a five-sided crystal lattice), and stick together at the thread. Each molecule going from solution to crystal is a physical change — same compound, just orderly arrangement. The same process makes salt pans, sugar candy, and natural mineral crystals.

🔥 Iron rusting in real time — a 3-day demo

~4320 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • 3 clean iron nails (same kind)
  • 3 small jars
  • Water, oil, vinegar
  • A magnifying glass (optional)

Steps

  1. Put one nail in a dry jar.
  2. Put the second nail in a jar of fresh water (don't seal it).
  3. Put the third in a sealed jar of cooking oil.
  4. Leave all three on a windowsill for 3 days.
  5. Each day, observe with magnifying glass. Photograph if you can.

👀 What you should see

Day 1: tiny brown specks appear on the wet nail. Day 3: the wet nail is partly orange. The dry nail and the oil-submerged nail are unchanged (no water and oxygen access).

Why it works: The wet nail has all three conditions for rusting: iron, oxygen (dissolved in water), and water itself. The dry nail has only iron + oxygen (the air is dry indoors), too little water. The oil-submerged nail has iron + water (in the oil's trace moisture) but oxygen can't reach through the oil layer. Three jars, three conditions, one chemistry lesson.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
