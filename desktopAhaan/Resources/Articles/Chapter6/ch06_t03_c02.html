Fireworks and sparklers — chemistry of colour and bang

Fireworks burn metal salts to produce colours (strontium = red, copper = blue, sodium = yellow), oxidisers (KNO₃) to keep the burning going, and powder shells that explode upward — every Diwali display is a chemistry exhibition.

≈ 3 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

On Diwali night the sky lights up with red, green, blue, gold sparkles — and that's not magic, it's chemistry. Each colour comes from a different METAL SALT burnt inside the firework: STRONTIUM gives red, COPPER gives blue-green, SODIUM gives yellow, BARIUM gives green, ALUMINIUM gives silver-white sparks. The metals are mixed with an OXIDISER (potassium nitrate KNO₃, or potassium chlorate) so the fire can keep burning even high in the air. A black-powder charge launches the shell upward, then a timed fuse ignites the colour mix at peak height. The BANG of a firecracker comes from explosive nitrates and aluminium powder reacting almost instantly to produce hot expanding gas.

The textbook version

Fireworks chemistry is based on emission spectroscopy of metal ions. Heated metal salts in a flame emit characteristic colours due to electron transitions: Sr²⁺ — bright red (~605 nm); Li⁺ — crimson red; Cu²⁺ + Cl⁻ → CuCl emitter — blue-green; Ba²⁺ — apple green; Na⁺ — yellow (~589 nm); K⁺ — pale violet; Mg/Al — white due to incandescent metal oxide particles. Components of a typical firework shell: (1) Fuel — charcoal, sulphur, or organic binders. (2) Oxidiser — KNO₃ (potassium nitrate, 'saltpetre'), KClO₃ or KClO₄. (3) Colour-producing metal salt. (4) Binder — dextrin or starch. The black powder propellant in the base launches the shell; a time-fuse ignites the burst charge that ignites the 'stars' (compressed pellets containing colour + fuel + oxidiser).

Why does this happen?

Different elements emit different colours when their electrons drop back to ground state after being excited by heat. Fireworks use that fact: pick the metal salt for the colour you want, mix with an oxidiser for sustained burning, package in a shell that launches up and explodes at altitude.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Diwali sky shows — Indian Diwali fireworks blanket every city with the colours of Sr, Cu, Na, Ba in carefully-timed displays. [Weather]
  • Olympic ceremony fireworks — Modern Olympic shows use computer-controlled launches; each colour is a chemistry choice — silver (Mg/Al), gold (Fe filings), red (Sr). [Technology]
  • Flame-test in chemistry lab — Class 10 / 11 students learn to identify metal ions by dipping a wire dipped in a salt into a flame — same physics as fireworks. [Technology]

Going deeper

Firework chemistry is at the intersection of pyrotechnics and atomic emission spectroscopy — the same electron-transition physics used to identify elements in stars. Green-firework chemistry has improved sharply due to environmental concerns: barium chlorate (toxic, hazardous) is being replaced by less harmful boron compounds; perchlorates that pollute drinking water are being eliminated. India's Sivakasi (Tamil Nadu) makes ~90% of Indian fireworks; the industry employs ~200,000 workers and faces ongoing safety regulation. Aerial-show choreography uses computer-controlled firing sequences synchronised with music — Disney and Sony use proprietary timing software.

Beyond the book
