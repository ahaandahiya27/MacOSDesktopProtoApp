Common Mistakes in Large Numbers Around Us

The places students slip on this chapter — and the one-line fix for each.

&#8776; 5 min read &middot; revision-tier &middot; 7 fixes

These wrong answers come back year after year. None are silly — they are almost right, which is what makes them worth knowing. Read each, then cover the fix and try to spot what is off.

1. “One million and one lakh are the same.”

🚫 The mistake

A belief that feels right — but misses a key detail.

The fix: One million = 10,00,000 = 10 lakhs. So one million is TEN times one lakh, not equal. Easy to mix up because both are ‘big numbers’.

2. “Indian and American comma conventions describe different-sized numbers.”

🚫 The mistake

A belief that feels right — but misses a key detail.

The fix: Both systems describe the same number. The differences are only in WHERE commas go and WHAT we call each unit. The underlying digit string is identical.

3. “One billion = 100 lakh.”

🚫 The mistake

A belief that feels right — but misses a key detail.

The fix: One billion = 100 CRORE = 10,000 lakh. The factor is 10,000, not 100.

4. “If a calculator can press +1000 only, it can never reach exactly 1,000 if you start from 1.”

🚫 The mistake

A belief that feels right — but misses a key detail.

The fix: Starting from 1, you’d reach 1,001 after one press, 2,001 after two, etc. — never landing on 1,000 with that single button. But starting from 0, one press reaches exactly 1,000. The starting point matters.

5. “‘Rounded’ numbers are wrong numbers.”

🚫 The mistake

A belief that feels right — but misses a key detail.

The fix: Rounded numbers are deliberate approximations chosen because the exact value isn’t needed. They are correct AT THE PRECISION CLAIMED. A population ‘around 75,000’ is right even if the exact figure is 76,068 — provided we mean ‘to the nearest ten thousand’.

6. “Multiplying always makes things bigger.”

🚫 The mistake

A belief that feels right — but misses a key detail.

The fix: Multiplying by a number between 0 and 1 (like 0.5 or 0.1) makes things SMALLER. ‘Multiplying by 5 is the same as multiplying by 10 and dividing by 2’ — that division step shrinks the intermediate result.

7. “The product of two 3-digit numbers has exactly 6 digits.”

🚫 The mistake

A belief that feels right — but misses a key detail.

The fix: It has either 5 or 6 digits. Smallest 3-digit × smallest 3-digit = 100 × 100 = 10,000 (5 digits). Largest 3-digit × largest 3-digit = 999 × 999 = 998,001 (6 digits). So 3-digit × 3-digit gives a 5- or 6-digit answer, never a 4-digit or 7-digit answer.
