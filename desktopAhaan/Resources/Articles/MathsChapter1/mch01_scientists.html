Minds Behind Large Numbers Around Us

Real mathematicians whose work lies underneath this chapter.

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Mathematics was built by real people, often centuries apart, each adding one idea. Here are the minds behind the ideas in this chapter.

Āryabhaṭa (c. 476 – 550 CE)

Indian. Indian mathematician who systematised place-value notation and zero in his Sanskrit treatise Āryabhaṭīya. His use of place-value with zero is foundational to all later positional arithmetic — including the Indian numbering system that gives us lakh and crore.

Āryabhaṭa was one of the great early masters of place-value notation. Long before lakh and crore became everyday words across South Asia, his work with the decimal system (using digit-based positional notation and explicit zero) provided the mathematical bedrock for representing arbitrarily large numbers. His Āryabhaṭīya (499 CE) handles astronomical computations involving numbers in the tens of millions — and the positional notation is what made those tractable. The Indian system of named milestones (lakh, crore, arab) sits on top of his (and his colleagues’) positional-arithmetic foundation.

Brahmagupta (c. 598 – 668 CE)

Indian. Rajasthani mathematician who codified arithmetic with zero as a number — laying the rules for negative numbers, zero in division, and place-value subtraction.

Brahmagupta’s Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE) was the first systematic treatment of arithmetic where zero behaves as a number in its own right. This was the missing piece that allowed positional notation to ‘scale’ without ambiguity — you could now distinguish 1,00,000 from 1,000 from 10 because the zeros in each position carry meaning. Without Brahmagupta’s formalisation, the entire Indian numbering system (and its derivatives in the Arab and European worlds) would have lacked rigour.
