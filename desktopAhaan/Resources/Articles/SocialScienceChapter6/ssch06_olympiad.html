Challenge 1 [Level 5 — hardest]

After the Mauryas fell, historians call the next period the 'Age of REORGANISATION' rather than an 'age of chaos' or 'age of collapse'. Explain what the word 'reorganisation' captures about this time that 'collapse' would miss.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

'Collapse' would suggest only ruin — an empire breaking apart and nothing taking its place. But that is not what happened. When the Maurya empire broke up around 185 BCE, many new kingdoms rose in its place and actively ORGANISED themselves into states: they won and held territory (through warfare but also peaceful matrimonial alliances), built administrations, minted coins, patronised art (like the Shunga railings at Bharhut and the Gandhara and Mathura schools), and grew rich on agriculture and long-distance trade reaching as far as Rome. So the political map was re-drawn and re-built, not simply destroyed. 'Reorganisation' captures this constructive side — the energetic forming of new powers and a flowering of culture and trade — which the word 'collapse' would completely miss.

How to crack it

  1. Note that 'collapse' implies only destruction with nothing replacing the empire.
  2. Show what actually happened: many new kingdoms arose and organised themselves into states.
  3. Give evidence of building, not just breaking: territory won, art patronised, trade and culture flourishing.
  4. Conclude 'reorganisation' captures the constructive re-drawing of the political map.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Treating the period as pure decline — it was politically fragmented but culturally rich and active.
  • Forgetting that kingdoms expanded peacefully through matrimonial alliances, not only by war.

🚀 Now try this twist

Name the two main ways the new kingdoms of this age gained control over territory.

Open the answer

Through warfare, and through matrimonial alliances — marriages arranged between royal families.

  1. Recall the two routes to territory in this age.
  2. Name warfare and matrimonial alliances.

Challenge 2 [Level 4 — stretch]

Under the Satavahanas, princes were often named after their MOTHERS (for example 'Gautamiputra' = son of Gautami), and stone inscriptions record powerful queens such as Gautami Balashri making grand donations and performing Vedic rituals. (a) What does this evidence suggest about the position of women in Satavahana society? (b) Why are such inscriptions especially reliable evidence for historians?

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

(a) It suggests that women — at least royal women — held an unusually respected and influential position. Naming a king after his mother shows the mother's line and identity mattered publicly, and a queen like Gautami Balashri making large donations and performing important rituals shows women could control wealth and play leading religious and public roles. (b) Inscriptions are especially reliable because they were carved in stone AT THE TIME, often by or for the very people involved, so they are first-hand, datable evidence that has survived unchanged — unlike later stories that could be exaggerated or altered in re-telling. When an inscription itself names a queen and her deeds, historians can trust that she really existed and did those things.

How to crack it

  1. Read the clues: mother-names for kings + queens making donations and performing rituals.
  2. Infer (a): royal women had real status, wealth and public/religious influence.
  3. Explain (b): inscriptions are contemporary, first-hand, datable and survive unchanged.
  4. Conclude such evidence is more trustworthy than later retellings.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Concluding women were equal in EVERY way — the evidence is mainly about royal women.
  • Forgetting to explain WHY inscriptions are good evidence (contemporary and first-hand).

🚀 Now try this twist

Why is an inscription carved during a ruler's own reign better evidence than a story written centuries later?

Open the answer

Because it is a first-hand record made at the time and preserved unchanged in stone, while a much later story may have been exaggerated, forgotten in parts, or altered as it was passed down.

  1. Inscription = contemporary, first-hand, unchanged.
  2. Later story = second-hand, may be altered.
  3. So the inscription is more reliable.

Challenge 3 [Level 5 — hardest]

The southern kingdoms grew very prosperous. Explain their TWO different sources of wealth — one earned by ENGINEERING the land, the other earned from the SEA — using the Cholas' Grand Anicut and the Cheras' and Pandyas' Roman trade as your examples.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

Source 1 — engineering the land: the Cholas, under King Karikala, built the Kallanai or Grand Anicut, a dam across the Kaveri that diverted river water to irrigate farmland. Reliable irrigation meant bigger, steadier harvests, so wealth came from a richer, better-watered agriculture (and the dam was so well made it is still in use today). Source 2 — the sea: the Cheras of the western coast and the Pandyas of Madurai sat near busy ports and grew rich through MARITIME TRADE, exporting prized goods like spices, ivory, timber and pearls as far as the Roman Empire and West Asia, which paid in gold. So the south prospered in two ways at once — by mastering its rivers to grow more food, and by selling its special products across the seas.

How to crack it

  1. Identify source 1: land engineering — the Chola Grand Anicut irrigates the Kaveri farmland → farming wealth.
  2. Identify source 2: the sea — Chera/Pandya maritime trade exporting spices, ivory, pearls to Rome → trade wealth.
  3. Give the concrete examples for each (Grand Anicut; Roman trade).
  4. Conclude the south prospered through BOTH agriculture and overseas trade.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Naming only one source of wealth instead of both land and sea.
  • Forgetting which dynasty did which — the Cholas built the Grand Anicut; the Cheras/Pandyas led the sea trade.

🚀 Now try this twist

What did the southern kingdoms export to the Roman Empire?

Open the answer

Goods such as spices, ivory, timber and pearls.

  1. Recall the prized southern exports.
  2. List spices, ivory, timber, pearls.

Challenge 4 [Level 5 — hardest]

Under the Kushanas, the Gandhara and Mathura schools of art arose at the SAME time, yet their statues looked different. Compare the two schools, and explain what the Gandhara style in particular reveals about India's contact with the wider world.

Try it yourself first — then open the worked solution

The Mathura school had a distinctly INDIAN style, drawing on local traditions and tastes. The Gandhara school, by contrast, BLENDED Greco-Roman features (such as wavy hair, flowing toga-like robes and realistic faces in the classical Western manner) with Indian subjects like the Buddha. The fact that Gandhara art mixes Greek/Roman and Indian styles is itself evidence of contact: the northwest had been ruled by Indo-Greeks and connected to the West through the Silk Route that the Kushanas controlled, so artists there had seen and absorbed Western artistic ideas. In other words, the Gandhara style is a 'fingerprint' of India's wide trade and cultural links — you can read history's connections in the very shape of the sculptures.

How to crack it

  1. Describe the Mathura school: a distinctly Indian style.
  2. Describe the Gandhara school: a Greco-Roman + Indian blend.
  3. Explain the contact behind Gandhara: Indo-Greek rule + the Silk Route under the Kushanas.
  4. Conclude that the blended style is evidence of India's links with the wider world.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Saying both schools looked the same — Mathura was Indian in style, Gandhara was a blend.
  • Missing the inference: the Greco-Roman blend in Gandhara is EVIDENCE of contact, not just decoration.

🚀 Now try this twist

Which features in a Buddha statue would hint that it was made by the Gandhara school?

Open the answer

Greco-Roman touches such as wavy hair, realistic classical-style faces and flowing, toga-like robes blended onto the Indian subject of the Buddha.

  1. Recall Gandhara blends Greco-Roman and Indian features.
  2. Name a couple of the Western-style features.
