History, geography and society are full of 'what if' turning points. Think about each question yourself first — then open the panel to compare your reasoning.

What if the last Maurya emperor had not been assassinated in 185 BCE?

Think first, then open to compare

If the Mauryan line had stayed strong, the empire might have held its provinces together for longer, and the political 'reorganisation' into many competing kingdoms could have been delayed. A strong central power would also have kept the northwest frontier well defended, making it much harder for the Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Kushanas to enter. We might then have seen far less of the rich cultural blending — the Gandhara art, the syncretic coins of Kanishka — that grew precisely because many kingdoms and incoming peoples met and mixed. The fragmentation, though it brought conflict, was also what made the great cultural confluence of this age possible.

What if we had no coins or inscriptions from this period?

Think first, then open to compare

We would know far less about the Age of Reorganisation. So much of our knowledge comes from physical evidence: Satavahana coins showing ships prove their sea trade; Indo-Greek coins, found in excavations, give us most of what we know about those rulers; Kanishka's coins reveal his titles and the gods he honoured; and inscriptions like the Hathigumpha and the Nashik and Naneghat records tell us about Kharavela and powerful Satavahana queens. Without these durable sources, the rulers, religions, trade and even the role of women in this age would be almost a blank — showing how vital coins and inscriptions are to historians.
