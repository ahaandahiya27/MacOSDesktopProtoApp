When the Maurya Empire Broke Apart

How the assassination of the last Maurya emperor around 185 BCE shattered one empire into many kingdoms, why the period is called the 'Age of Reorganisation', and how rulers won and held territory through warfare and matrimonial alliances.

  1. The Fall of the Mauryas and the Age of ReorganisationAround 185 BCE the last Maurya emperor was assassinated, the empire broke up, and many competing kingdoms arose — an age historians call the 'Age of Reorganisation'.
  2. How Kingdoms Won Territory: Warfare and Matrimonial AlliancesThe new kingdoms gained control over land through warfare or through peaceful matrimonial alliances — marriages arranged between royal families.

The Shungas and the Satavahanas

The Shunga dynasty of north and central India, its revival of Vedic ritual and the carved railings of the Bharhut Stupa; and the Satavahanas of the Deccan, their sea trade with Rome, coins showing ships, and the custom of naming kings after their mothers.

  1. The Surge of the ShungasPushyamitra Shunga founded the Shunga dynasty over parts of north and central India and performed the ashvamedha yajna to declare himself a powerful ruler.
  2. The Bharhut Stupa and Shunga ArtThe Shungas patronised art and architecture, most famously adding beautifully carved railings and reliefs to the Bharhut Stupa in present-day Madhya Pradesh.
  3. The Satavahanas of the Deccan and Their Sea TradeThe Satavahanas ruled large parts of the Deccan from the 2nd century BCE, growing rich on agriculture and maritime trade that reached as far as the Roman Empire.
  4. Life under the Satavahanas: Powerful Queens and Mother-NamesIn Satavahana tradition princes were often named after their mothers, and inscriptions reveal powerful queens like Gautami Balashri who made grand donations and performed Vedic rituals.

The Chedis and the Kingdoms of the South

The Chedi dynasty of Kalinga under the Jain monk-king Kharavela and his Hathigumpha inscription; and the three crowned kingdoms of the south — the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas — along with the Sangam Age of Tamil poetry.

  1. The Chedis of Kalinga and King KharavelaAfter the Mauryas declined, Kalinga rose as a power under the Chedi dynasty, whose ruler Kharavela — a devoted follower of Jain teachings — left the famous Hathigumpha inscription.
  2. The Three Crowned Kingdoms and the Sangam AgeIn the south, three powerful kingdoms — the Cheras, Cholas and Pandyas — rose to prominence, in an era named the 'Sangam Age' after its great body of Tamil poetry.
  3. The Cholas and the Grand AnicutThe Cholas were a powerful southern dynasty whose king Karikala built the Kallanai or Grand Anicut, an ancient water-diversion dam on the Kaveri that is still in use.
  4. The Cheras, the Pandyas and Sea-Trade with RomeThe Cheras of the western coast and the Pandyas of Madurai grew prosperous through trade — exporting spices, ivory, timber and pearls to the Roman Empire and West Asia.

Invaders from the Northwest: Indo-Greeks and Shakas

The arrival of foreign rulers through the weakened northwest frontier — the Indo-Greeks, descended from Alexander's satraps, who blended Greek and Indian culture as seen in the Heliodorus pillar and their coins; and the Shakas (Indo-Scythians), in whose period the Shaka Samvat calendar was developed.

  1. The Indo-Greeks and Cultural BlendingThe Indo-Greeks, descended from satraps left behind by Alexander, ruled the northwest and blended Greek and Indian culture in governance, art, language and daily life.
  2. The Shakas and the Shaka Samvat CalendarThe Shakas, also called Indo-Scythians, invaded the northwest after the Indo-Greeks; during their period the Shaka Samvat calendar was developed, later adopted as India's National Calendar.

The Kushanas, Art and the Confluence of Cultures

The Kushanas under Kanishka, their control of the Silk Route, the fusion Gandhara and Mathura schools of art, and the chapter's big idea — that this competitive age was also one of remarkable cultural confluence and flourishing Sanskrit literature.

  1. The Kushanas and KanishkaThe Kushanas, originally from Central Asia, built an empire from Central Asia into northern India; their greatest ruler, Kanishka, controlled the Silk Route and encouraged art and culture.
  2. The Gandhara and Mathura Schools of ArtUnder the Kushanas two great schools of art arose — the Gandhara school, blending Greco-Roman and Indian features, and the Mathura school, with a distinctly Indian style.
  3. Cultural Confluence: The Tapestry of the PastDespite constant political conflict, the Age of Reorganisation was a time of remarkable cultural exchange and assimilation, with Indian themes dominant and Sanskrit literature flourishing.
