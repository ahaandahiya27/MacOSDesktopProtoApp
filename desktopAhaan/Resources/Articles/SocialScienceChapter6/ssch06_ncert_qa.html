Q1. Why was the post-Maurya era also known as the era of reorganisation? (NCERT page 119)

After the last Maurya emperor was assassinated around 185 BCE, the empire broke up and many new kingdoms emerged across the subcontinent — often these had earlier been tributary kingdoms under Mauryan overlordship. These 'new' kingdoms constantly competed to become powerful, through warfare and matrimonial alliances, so the political map of India changed significantly. Because the existing regions were being rearranged into new competing kingdoms, scholars call this the 'era of reorganisation'. The weakened northwest also opened the door to foreign invaders, adding to the reshaping of political powers.

Q2. Write a note on the Sangam literature in 150 words. (NCERT page 130)

Sangam literature is the oldest body of literature from south India, produced during the rise of the three crowned kingdoms — the Cheras, Cholas and Pandyas — roughly between the 2nd-3rd century BCE and the 3rd century CE. The word 'sangam' comes from the Sanskrit 'sangha', meaning 'association' or 'coming together', and here refers to an assembly of poets. It consists of several collections or anthologies of poems. Sangam poetry expresses, with great skill and delicacy, personal emotions such as love, as well as societal values like heroism and generosity. Because this poetry defined the entire era, the period itself came to be called the 'Sangam Age'. Historians consult Sangam literature closely because it offers a rich window into the society, trade, kingship and culture of ancient south India. The epic Silappadikaram, composed soon after, continues these themes of justice and a ruler's dharma.

Q3. Which rulers mentioned in this chapter included their mother's name in their title, and why did they do so? (NCERT page 126)

The Satavahana rulers included their mother's name in their titles. For example, the king Gautamiputra Satakarni was named after his mother, Gautami Balashri — 'Gautamiputra' means 'son of Gautami'. They did so because, in the Satavahana tradition, princes were often named after their mothers, which signals the high status and influence royal women held in that society. Gautami Balashri herself was a powerful queen who donated land to Buddhist monks and had an important inscription carved at Nashik. Another Satavahana queen, a widow, even performed Vedic rituals including the ashvamedha yajna. Using the mother's name thus reflected the respect and real power of women in the Satavahana kingdom.

Q4. How did foreign invaders assimilate into Indian society and contribute to cultural confluence? (NCERT page 140)

Foreign invaders who entered through the northwest — the Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Kushanas — arrived as conquerors but were much influenced by India's rich local culture. The Indo-Greeks blended Greek and Indian elements in governance, art, language and daily life; the Heliodorus pillar even shows an Indo-Greek ambassador praising the Indian god Vasudeva. Their coins carried Greek and Indian deities alike. The Shakas left behind the Shaka Samvat calendar, still used in India today. Under the Kushana king Kanishka, coins featured the Buddha and Shiva, and the Gandhara and Mathura schools fused Greco-Roman and Indian styles. So the invaders contributed new artistic and cultural elements, but Indian themes remained dominant, producing a genuine cultural confluence — a 'tapestry' woven from many threads.
