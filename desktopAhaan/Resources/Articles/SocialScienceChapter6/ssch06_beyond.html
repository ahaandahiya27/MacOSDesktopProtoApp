Indo-Roman trade and the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea [Class 11]

The Satavahana, Chera and Pandya trade with Rome is documented not only by Indian coins but by a remarkable Greek sailing manual, the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, written in the 1st century CE. It lists Indian ports, the goods bought and sold there, and even the best sailing seasons using the monsoon winds. Indian exports of pepper, pearls, ivory and textiles drew so much Roman gold that the Roman writer Pliny complained India was draining Rome's treasury. Large hoards of Roman coins found in south India confirm this. Studying these sources together shows how historians cross-check different kinds of evidence — foreign texts, local coins and archaeology — to reconstruct ancient trade.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the Cheras, Pandyas and Satavahanas traded with the Roman Empire.

Gandhara, Mathura and the first human image of the Buddha [Class 11]

Before the Kushana period, the Buddha was usually shown only by symbols — a footprint, an empty seat, a wheel — never as a person (the 'aniconic' style seen at Bharhut). It was in the Gandhara and Mathura schools, under the Kushanas, that the Buddha first began to be carved in human form. Gandhara, drawing on Greco-Roman art, gave him a realistic, draped, almost classical look in grey schist; Mathura, drawing on the older Indian yaksha tradition, gave him a fuller, energetic form in red sandstone. The move to human-form deities here is one of the great turning points in Indian art, and it 'laid the groundwork' for the deity sculptures of later Hindu and Buddhist temples.

Reading society from a queen's name [Class 10]

Some Satavahana kings carried their mother's name in their own — the most famous, Gautamīputra Sātakarni, literally means 'Sātakarni, son of Gautamī'. To historians this small detail is a window into society. Why would a king advertise his mother? It suggests mothers and queens held real standing in Satavahana families, more than the simple 'father-to-son' picture we often assume for ancient kingdoms. We do not have to guess: a long inscription at Nashik was issued by the king's mother, Gautamī Balaśrī, praising her son's deeds after his death — a queen speaking in stone, in her own voice. This is how social history is done. Where chronicles give us only battles and dates, a naming custom and one royal inscription let historians ask harder questions: who held power inside the family, and how do we know? The lesson is that even a name can be evidence.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the Satavahanas used 'mother-names' (matronymics).

The calendar on India's government documents [Class 9]

The Shaka Samvat (Shaka era) that began in this age of invaders did not stay in the past — it is still an official calendar of India today. When the Republic of India needed a single national calendar in 1957, a committee chose the Shaka era, with year 1 fixed at 78 CE. That is why the date on the masthead of the Gazette of India, on All India Radio broadcasts, and on many government notices is given in Shaka years alongside the Gregorian date — to find the Shaka year, you subtract 78 (or 79 early in the year) from the Common Era year. So a calendar started by the Shaka rulers nearly two thousand years ago quietly survives in the paperwork of a modern democracy. It is a neat example of how the deep past keeps living inside the present.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the Shakas gave India the Shaka Samvat calendar.

The Grand Anicut: a dam still working after 1,800 years [Class 9]

Most things built two thousand years ago survive only as ruins — but the Chola king Karikala's Kallanai, or Grand Anicut, on the Kaveri river is still doing its job today. It is not a tall wall that stops the river; it is a long, low barrier laid across the stream to RAISE and DIVERT the water, spreading it through channels across the delta so that a wide area of farmland can be irrigated. That is why engineers call it a 'water-diversion' structure rather than a storage dam. Built in stone without modern cement, it was so well designed that the British later strengthened it rather than replacing it, and it still helps water one of South India's richest rice regions. The Grand Anicut shows that ancient Indians were not only artists and traders but also skilled hydraulic engineers, and that good public works can serve people for almost two millennia.

You'll want to know first: Knowing Karikala Chola built the Kallanai/Grand Anicut on the Kaveri.

The Kushanas and the Silk Route: India plugged into the world [Class 10]

The Kushanas, who came from Central Asia, ended up controlling a huge stretch of the Silk Route — the network of caravan roads carrying silk, spices, gold and ideas between China, Central Asia, India and the Roman world. Sitting astride those roads made the Kushana realm rich and turned India into a busy crossroads of trade and culture rather than an isolated land. Their coins are wonderful evidence: Kanishka's coins show the king on one side and a mix of deities — the Buddha, Shiva, and Greek and Persian gods — on the other, telling us that many faiths met and were respected under one ruler. Along these same routes Buddhism travelled north into Central Asia and China, carried by merchants and monks. The Kushana age shows how trade routes move far more than goods: they move beliefs, art styles and people, knitting distant civilisations together.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the Kushanas controlled large parts of the Silk Route.
