🪙 Design a coin like an ancient king (about 60 min)

What you need

  • A circle of cardboard or air-dry clay
  • A pencil, toothpick or blunt nail to carve
  • Paints or markers
  • Reference pictures of Satavahana, Indo-Greek and Kushana coins

Steps

  1. Choose which kingdom you are a ruler of — Satavahana, Indo-Greek, Chera, or Kushana.
  2. On one face, design a 'royal' side: your portrait or a symbol, plus a title (like 'King of Kings').
  3. On the other face, choose an emblem or deity that shows your kingdom's values — a ship for sea trade, a deity for your faith, an animal emblem.
  4. Carve or draw both sides onto your cardboard or clay disc.
  5. Add a short inscription in block letters around the edge, the way Brahmi or Greek script ran around real coins.
  6. Paint it to look like gold, silver or copper.
  7. Write three sentences explaining what your coin would tell a future historian about your kingdom.

👀 What you should see

You will discover that a tiny coin can carry a king's name, faith, trade and values all at once — which is exactly why historians treat ancient coins as treasure-troves of evidence.

🧠 Why it works

Real Satavahana, Indo-Greek and Kushana coins are among the main sources historians use, because rulers deliberately chose what to stamp on them. By designing your own, you experience first-hand how images and inscriptions encode a whole society.

📜 Build the Age of Reorganisation timeline (about 75 min)

What you need

  • A long sheet of paper or a roll of chart paper
  • A ruler and coloured pens
  • Your textbook for dates

Steps

  1. Draw a long horizontal line. Mark '200 BCE' at the left and '300 CE' at the right, with '0' (the BCE-to-CE turn) in the middle.
  2. Mark 185 BCE — the assassination of the last Maurya emperor by Pushyamitra Shunga.
  3. Add the dynasties as coloured bars where they ruled: Shungas, Chedis, Satavahanas, Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Kushanas, and the southern Cholas-Cheras-Pandyas.
  4. Place the key rulers above their dynasty's bar: Pushyamitra Shunga, Kharavela, Gautamiputra Satakarni, Karikala, Kanishka.
  5. Mark the transition point from BCE to CE and write how the counting changes there.
  6. Count and write down how many years the whole period covers, from the first year of the 2nd century BCE to the last year of the 3rd century CE.
  7. Decorate each dynasty with a tiny symbol — a ship for the Satavahanas, a horse for the ashvamedha, a coin for the Kushanas.

👀 What you should see

You will see at a glance that several dynasties ruled at the SAME time in different regions, and how the period bridges the BCE-to-CE divide — making the 'reorganisation' of the map visible.

🧠 Why it works

Laying overlapping dynasties on one line shows that history is not a single line of kings but many parallel stories. Marking the BCE-CE turn reinforces how historians measure time, just as the textbook's 'Let's Remember' box asks.
