Atomic Clocks and Modern Time

Why your phone's clock is more accurate than your watch · Class 7 Science

How accurate is 'accurate'?

For thousands of years humans measured time crudely — by the sun, by burning candles, by dripping water. Then came mechanical clocks (1300s), pendulum clocks (1656), spring-driven watches (1700s), quartz watches (1927), and finally atomic clocks (1955). Each generation was about 1,000× more accurate than the last.

Where the second comes from now

Since 1967, one second is officially defined as:

9,192,631,770 cycles of the microwave radiation absorbed by a caesium-133 atom transitioning between two specific energy levels.

That's an absurd-sounding number, but it's a number that NEVER changes. Every caesium atom in the universe vibrates at exactly the same frequency. So an atomic clock based on caesium is, in effect, asking the atom 'what time is it?' nine billion times every second.

How atomic clocks work

  1. A small chamber of caesium gas is heated until atoms fly out.
  2. A microwave generator shoots radiation at a precisely tuned frequency.
  3. When the frequency matches the caesium atom's resonance, the atom 'sings back' — a detector picks up the signal.
  4. Computer software locks the generator to that exact frequency.
  5. Counting 9,192,631,770 of those cycles = one second.

How accurate?

  • Caesium clocks: error of about 1 second every 100 MILLION years.
  • Newer optical-lattice clocks (using strontium or ytterbium atoms): about 1 second every 15 BILLION years — longer than the age of the universe.

What we use atomic clocks for

  • GPS satellites each carry 4 atomic clocks. They time radio signals so precisely that your phone can locate you within 1 metre on Earth. A 1-microsecond clock error would put your GPS off by 300 metres.
  • Mobile phone networks sync their cell towers to atomic clocks so calls hand off cleanly.
  • Financial trading — stock exchanges timestamp transactions to atomic-clock precision.
  • Scientific research — testing Einstein's relativity by comparing clocks at different altitudes (yes, time really does run faster on mountain tops than at sea level — predicted by general relativity, confirmed using atomic clocks).
Your smartphone's clock is automatically synchronised many times per day to an atomic clock somewhere — usually via NTP (Network Time Protocol) servers. That's why your phone's time is always exact, even when your wall clock drifts.

Try this at home

Compare three timekeepers right now: your phone (atomic-clock synced), your wall clock (probably quartz, drift ~15 sec/month), your wristwatch (similar). Set them all to your phone's exact second. Come back in two weeks and compare again. The wall clock and wristwatch have drifted slightly; your phone hasn't.

Looking ahead
