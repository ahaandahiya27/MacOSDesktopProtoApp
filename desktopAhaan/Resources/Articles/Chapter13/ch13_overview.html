Motion and Time

Chapter 13 · Class 7 Science

What is motion?

An object is in motion if its position changes with time, compared to some reference (a tree, a road sign, a building). If nothing around it seems to change, we say it is at rest. Even when you sit still, the Earth is carrying you through space at about 30 km/s — so motion is always relative.

Speed = distance ÷ time

If a car covers 80 km in 1 hour, its speed is 80 km/h. Speed tells you how fast position is changing.

Some typical speeds:

  • Walking — about 5 km/h
  • Cycling — 15 km/h
  • Car on highway — 80 km/h
  • Train — 100–160 km/h
  • Airliner — about 900 km/h
  • Light — 300,000 km/s — the universe's speed limit

Uniform vs non-uniform motion

  • Uniform motion — equal distance in equal time. A train cruising at 80 km/h.
  • Non-uniform motion — speed changes (accelerates or decelerates). A falling apple, a car at a traffic light, a sprinter.

The distance–time graph

On a graph with time on the x-axis and distance on the y-axis:

  • Flat line → at rest.
  • Straight slanted line → uniform speed (slope = speed).
  • Curve getting steeper → speeding up.

Measuring time

For thousands of years, humans have invented better and better clocks:

  • Sundials (~2000 BCE) — accurate to about 15 minutes.
  • Water and sand clocks — for short, indoor intervals.
  • Pendulum clocks (1656) — accurate to seconds per day.
  • Quartz watches (1927) — seconds per year.
  • Atomic clocks (1955) — drift by 1 second in 100 million years.
Galileo's discovery: the time for one swing of a pendulum depends only on its length, not on the mass or the angle. Longer string → slower swing. T = 2π √(L/g).

Speedometer vs odometer

A car's speedometer tells you how fast it's going right now. The odometer counts the total kilometres travelled. Same gearbox, different jobs.
