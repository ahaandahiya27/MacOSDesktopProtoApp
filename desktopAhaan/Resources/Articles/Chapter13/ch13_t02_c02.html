Why a Pendulum Always Takes the Same Time

Galileo's discovery that changed timekeeping · Class 7 Science

Galileo's church-lamp moment

In 1582 a teenage Galileo watched a swinging lamp in a Pisa cathedral. He timed it against his own pulse and noticed something strange: whether the lamp swung wide or narrow, each swing took the same time.

Why this is true

For small swings (up to ~10°), the time taken for one swing depends only on the length of the pendulum, not on how heavy the bob is or how wide it swings. A long pendulum swings slowly; a short one swings fast.

The formula (a peek)

Class 9 Physics formalises this: T = 2π√(L/g), where T is the period, L is length, and g is gravity. A 1-metre pendulum on Earth has T ≈ 2 seconds. A 25-cm pendulum has T ≈ 1 second.

From discovery to clocks

It took ~80 years from Galileo's observation for Christiaan Huygens (1656) to actually build the first pendulum clock. It was 10× more accurate than any previous timepiece — soon every European city had one.

If you take a 1-metre pendulum to the Moon (where g is 1/6 of Earth's), its period becomes ~5 seconds instead of 2 seconds. A clock built for Earth would run slowly on the Moon — until you shortened the pendulum.

Try this at home

Tie a small heavy nut to a 30 cm thread. Hold the end and let it swing. Time 20 complete swings, divide by 20. Repeat with the thread at 60 cm. The longer one should take ~1.4× as long per swing — that's the √2 from √L.

Looking ahead

Class 9 Physics covers simple harmonic motion and the pendulum formula. Class 11 Physics goes deeper with angular oscillation and damping. JEE and NEET both ask pendulum problems regularly — they test fundamentals + numerical skill.
