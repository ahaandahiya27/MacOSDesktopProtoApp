From Sundials to Atomic Clocks — 4000 Years of Timekeeping

Why time gets more precise each century · Class 7 Science

Sundials and water clocks (3000 BCE)

The Egyptians used vertical sticks (gnomons) and their shadows to mark hours. Water clocks (clepsydras) trickled water from one pot to another — also used in ancient India and China. Accuracy: ±15 minutes per day on a sunny day; worse on cloudy ones.

Mechanical clocks (~1300 CE)

Early European mechanical clocks used falling weights and a notched wheel. They drifted ~15 minutes per day. Tower clocks in churches were the era's most reliable.

Pendulum clocks (1656)

Huygens' pendulum design cut error to ~10 seconds per day. By 1900 the best pendulum clocks were accurate to within 1 second per month — good enough for railways and observatories.

Quartz clocks (1927)

A small slice of quartz crystal vibrates a precise number of times per second when an electric current passes through it. Quartz watches are accurate to ±2 seconds per month — and dirt-cheap to make. By 1980, almost every wristwatch in the world was quartz.

Atomic clocks (1955)

The current world standard counts the vibrations of caesium-133 atoms — exactly 9,192,631,770 vibrations per second define one second. Modern atomic clocks lose less than 1 second in 30 million years. They run GPS, the internet, the stock market, and global telecom.

GPS depends on atomic clocks so precisely that engineers have to correct for Einstein's relativity — clocks on satellites tick slightly faster than ones on the ground (less gravity), by about 38 microseconds per day. Without the correction, GPS would be off by 10 km in 24 hours.

Try this at home

Compare time on your phone (atomic), your wall clock (quartz), and a wind-up wristwatch if you have one (mechanical). Wait a week. The mechanical one will have drifted by minutes; the others stay synced.

Looking ahead

Class 11 Physics covers wave-period and frequency. Class 12 Physics introduces resonance (the principle behind quartz crystals). Pursuing physics in college, you encounter optical lattice clocks — the next generation, expected to lose 1 second in the age of the universe.
