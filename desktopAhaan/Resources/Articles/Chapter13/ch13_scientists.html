Every chapter has a person behind it — someone who first asked the right question, ran the right experiment, or wrote down the answer. This is a one-page portrait of the scientist most tied to this chapter's content.

Aryabhata

476–550 CE · Indian (Ancient)

🎧 In one line

Indian astronomer-mathematician who calculated Earth's rotation, the length of the year, and the value of π — fifteen centuries before modern instruments.

Aryabhata was born in 476 CE, probably in Patna (then Pataliputra). At age 23 he wrote the Aryabhatiya — a treatise that proposed: Earth rotates on its axis (heretical at the time — most thought the sky rotated and Earth was still); the length of a sidereal year is 365.25858 days (modern value: 365.25636 — off by less than 1 minute!); the value of π is approximately 3.1416 (correct to 4 decimals); and the diameter of Earth is ~12500 km (modern value: 12742 — under 2% off). All this without telescopes, computers, or anything beyond geometry and patient observation of the sky. He understood eclipses as shadows, not as demons swallowing the Sun. He invented the concept of place-value notation that later traveled to Arab mathematicians and then to Europe — the foundation of the decimal system. Class 7 measures time with stopwatches; Aryabhata measured it with the spinning Earth. India's first satellite (1975) is named after him. His treatise predicts the day length to better than 1 minute — a precision achieved by patience, not technology.
