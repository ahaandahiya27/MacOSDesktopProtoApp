Two Mini Projects for Chapter 13

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

⏱️ Build a simple pendulum and find what affects time period

~20 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A heavy bob (a small stone, ball, or nut)
  • A 1-metre piece of string
  • A stand or hook (a doorknob works)
  • A stopwatch (phone is fine)

Steps

  1. Tie the bob to the string; hang the string from a fixed point. The bob should hang freely.
  2. Measure the string length carefully (top of bob to the hook).
  3. Pull the bob to one side by ~10 cm. Let go.
  4. Count 20 complete oscillations (one back-and-forth = 1 oscillation). Time it.
  5. Time period = total time ÷ 20.
  6. Now CHANGE one thing at a time: (a) Make the string half as long. Re-measure. (b) Use a heavier bob. Re-measure. (c) Pull the bob 20 cm instead of 10. Re-measure.
  7. Compare all four time periods.

👀 What you should see

Shorter string: shorter period (about √2 less for half length). Heavier bob: SAME period (surprising!). Bigger swing: ~same period (for small swings). Conclusion: only LENGTH matters significantly.

Why it works: The math: T = 2π√(L/g). Length is the only variable; gravity (g) is constant on Earth. Bob mass cancels out. Galileo found this in 1602; this experiment reproduces his discovery. It's the basis of every pendulum clock — make the pendulum 0.994 m long and one half-swing = 1 second.

🚲 Measure your own walking + cycling + running speeds

~30 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A measuring tape or known distance (e.g., a 100m school track lane)
  • A stopwatch (phone)
  • Yourself, a friend to time you, or both

Steps

  1. Measure or use a known 100m straight stretch.
  2. Walk at normal pace for 100m. Have friend time it. Calculate speed = 100 / time.
  3. Repeat at brisk walking pace, then jogging, then sprinting.
  4. Optionally: do it on a cycle too.
  5. Plot a bar chart of your speeds in m/s and km/h.
  6. Compare with: Usain Bolt's 100m record (~10.4 m/s), an Olympic cyclist (~16 m/s), a Mumbai local train (~10 m/s avg).

👀 What you should see

Walk: ~1.4 m/s = 5 km/h. Jog: ~2.5 m/s = 9 km/h. Sprint: ~6 m/s = 21.6 km/h. Cycle: ~5 m/s = 18 km/h. (Your numbers will vary.)

Why it works: You're literally computing speed = distance/time with real measurements. The exercise makes the formula physical — you experience what a different m/s value FEELS like. Then comparing to known speeds (Bolt, trains) gives intuition for what 'fast' means.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
