Uniform vs Non-Uniform Motion

Same speed all the time vs speed that changes · Class 7 Science

Two kinds of motion you've already noticed

A train cruising on a straight track at 80 km/h — its speed doesn't change. That's uniform motion. An apple falling from a tree — it speeds up as it falls. That's non-uniform motion.

Both are real. Both are useful. They behave very differently.

Uniform motion

An object in uniform motion travels EQUAL distances in EQUAL time intervals. Distance ÷ time always gives the same answer no matter when you measure. Distance vs time graphed comes out as a STRAIGHT LINE.

Pure uniform motion is rare in real life. Frictionless gliders on a smooth horizontal track, planets in stable circular orbits, light in a vacuum — these are the closest examples. Even a 'cruise control' car is only approximately uniform — small road bumps and air-resistance changes mean small speed variations.

Non-uniform motion

Equal distances in UNEQUAL time intervals — or equivalently, unequal distances in equal time intervals. The car accelerates from a red light, the apple falls from the tree, a runner starts a sprint. Distance vs time graphed comes out as a CURVE (steepening if speeding up, flattening if slowing down).

The slope tells the speed

On any distance-time graph:

  • Slope = speed at that instant.
  • Flat line (zero slope) = at rest.
  • Straight slanted line (constant slope) = uniform motion.
  • Curve with increasing slope = accelerating (speed increasing).
  • Curve with decreasing slope = decelerating (speed decreasing).

This single insight — that the slope of a distance-time graph IS the speed — is your first encounter with calculus.

Average speed vs instantaneous speed

For a 100 km trip done in 2 hours: average speed = 50 km/h. But your speedometer was probably showing anything from 0 km/h (at red lights) to 90 km/h (on the highway). The number on the speedometer is the instantaneous speed — the speed RIGHT NOW. Average speed tells you the journey overall; instantaneous speed tells you what's happening this second.

Real-life examples

  • Uniform: Earth in orbit around the Sun (almost — it's slightly elliptical), a steady-state river current.
  • Non-uniform — speeding up: a car accelerating, an apple falling, a sprinter from the starting block.
  • Non-uniform — slowing down: a car braking, a ball rolling on grass, a moving cycle once you stop pedalling.
  • Non-uniform — varying: a city bus on its route, accelerating between stops and braking at each.

Try this at home

Roll a marble on a smooth flat floor — uniform motion (it'll only slow slightly from friction). Roll the SAME marble down a slope — non-uniform, clearly speeding up. You can FEEL the difference because the falling-with-gravity case is what gives a real 'whoosh' under your hand.

Looking ahead

Class 11 Physics 'Motion in a Straight Line' formalises this with acceleration (a = Δv / Δt) — the rate at which velocity changes. The three equations of motion for constant acceleration are:

  • v = u + at
  • s = ut + ½at²
  • v² = u² + 2as

Every JEE Physics paper has multiple kinematics questions using these formulas — especially problems with non-uniform deceleration (like cars braking) or motion under gravity (the apple-falls problem solved with a = 9.8 m/s²). Class 12 connects motion to forces (F = ma — Newton's second law).
