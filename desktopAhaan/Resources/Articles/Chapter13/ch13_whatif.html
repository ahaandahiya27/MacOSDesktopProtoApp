Three "What Ifs?" About Motion and Time

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if time ran backward — could you predict it?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Newton's laws of motion are time-reversible — if you film a billiard ball collision and play it in reverse, it looks like a valid collision. But on a larger scale, time has a DIRECTION (entropy increases — shattered glass doesn't reassemble). A movie of a glass shattering, played backward, looks impossible. Time-reversal symmetry holds at microscopic scales but breaks at large scales. This is one of the great open questions in physics: why does time have a direction? Class 11's thermodynamics chapter has the start of an answer.

What if 2 — What if a pendulum's length was 1 km?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Time period T = 2π√(L/g) = 2π√(1000/9.8) ≈ 63 seconds. A complete swing would take a minute. The largest practical pendulum on Earth was Foucault's pendulum (1851, Paris), 67m long, period ~16 seconds — used to demonstrate Earth's rotation visibly. A pendulum 1 km long is impractical (couldn't build a stand tall enough), but the math says it would tick once per minute. A weather-balloon-mounted pendulum could probably work in principle.

What if 3 — What if all clocks on Earth stopped at the same instant?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: GPS would fail (it depends on atomic clock signals). Stock markets would freeze (they rely on millisecond-accurate timestamps). Phones, internet, and aircraft navigation would error. The financial system would collapse within hours. But Earth would keep rotating and the Sun would keep rising — natural time wouldn't stop. We'd revert to sundials, water clocks, and lunar observation, like Aryabhata. Within months, civilization would re-bootstrap with mechanical clocks. The fragility is on the precision side, not the existence side.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
