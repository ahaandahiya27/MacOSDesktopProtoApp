Number Play: Worked NCERT Questions

Textbook questions from this chapter, each with a clear worked answer.

&#8776; practice &middot; 8 questions

These are the kind of questions the NCERT chapter asks. Try each one yourself first, then check the worked answer.

Q1. In the 'taller children in front' game, is the statement 'If a person says 0, then they are the tallest in the group' always, sometimes, or never true?

Answer: Sometimes true. Saying 0 only means no TALLER child stands IN FRONT; a taller child could stand behind them. So they may or may not be the tallest.

Q2. Can five number-cards, each odd, sum to 30? Why or why not?

Answer: No. The sum of five odd numbers is odd (an odd count of odds gives an odd total), but 30 is even. So it is impossible.

Q3. What kind of number do you get when you add several even numbers? Several odd numbers?

Answer: Adding even numbers always gives an even number. Adding odd numbers gives an even number if there is an even count of them, and an odd number if there is an odd count.

Q4. Show that the sum of two odd numbers is always even.

Answer: Write the odds as 2m+1 and 2n+1. Their sum is 2m+2n+2 = 2(m+n+1), a multiple of 2, hence even. This holds for all m, n, so it is a proof.

Q5. Write the next three terms of 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21.

Answer: Each term is the sum of the previous two: 13+21=34, 21+34=55, 34+55=89. So the next three terms are 34, 55, 89.

Q6. In how many ways can 4 beats be filled with short (1-beat) and long (2-beat) syllables?

Answer: 5 ways. The count satisfies F(n)=F(n−1)+F(n−2): F(3)=3, F(2)=2, so F(4)=3+2=5. (The rhythms are SSSS, SSL, SLS, LSS, LL.)

Q7. Solve T + T + T = UT, where the answer's units digit equals T.

Answer: 3T must end in T, so 2T is a multiple of 10, giving T = 0 or 5. T = 0 gives a one-digit sum, so T = 5: 5+5+5 = 15, with U = 1.

Q8. Who first discovered the sequence 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, … and in what context?

Answer: Indian scholars such as Pingala and later Virahāṅka described it centuries before Fibonacci, while studying the rhythms of poetry — counting how many arrangements of short and long syllables fill a given number of beats. Hence it is called the Virahāṅka–Fibonacci sequence.
