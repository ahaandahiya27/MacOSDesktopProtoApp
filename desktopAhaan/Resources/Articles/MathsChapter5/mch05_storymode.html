Maths can feel like it lives only inside the textbook. It doesn't. Here are real situations where the ideas of "Parallel and Intersecting Lines" quietly do the work.

Checking walls are parallel without a giant ruler

A mason can't extend two walls to infinity to check if they're parallel. Instead they lay a straight plank across both (a transversal) and measure the corresponding angles. If the two angles are equal, the walls are parallel. The corresponding-angles fact turns an impossible test into a quick local measurement.

Zebra crossings and Z-angles

The white stripes of a zebra crossing are parallel. The kerb line cuts across them as a transversal, and the equal angles it makes at each stripe are corresponding and alternate angles in action — the same geometry that keeps the stripes looking evenly slanted.

The right angle in construction

Builders lay out perpendicular walls using a 3-4-5 triangle: a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 units always has a right angle. It's a protractor-free way to guarantee 90°, used on building sites for thousands of years — perpendicularity made practical.
