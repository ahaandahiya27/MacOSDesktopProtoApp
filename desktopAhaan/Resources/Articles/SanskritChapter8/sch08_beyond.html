Beyond the Book — The Hardest Speech-Rule in the World

The chapter's title sets a standard for speech that almost nobody meets. Here is why it is so hard — and why so many traditions repeat it.

≈ 6 min read · curious-kid level

Every culture has rules about how to speak. Almost none of them are easy. The chapter's two-condition rule is one of the hardest.

1. The two conditions

Be useful (हितम्). Be pleasant (मनोहारि). Both, not either. The verse calls the combination दुर्लभम् (hard to find) — and almost every adult who has tried to speak this way will recognise the truth of that.

It is easy to be honest and harsh ('your essay is terrible'). It is easy to be sweet and useless ('your essay is wonderful, dear'). Being honest AND kind — pointing out a real problem without making the listener defensive — takes care.

2. The same teaching in three traditions

The Tamil classic तिरुक्कुरल् (Thirukkural, ~1st-5th century CE) devotes an entire chapter to इनियवै कूरल् ('sweet speech') — say true things sweetly.

The Buddha listed सम्यक्-वाक् (right speech) as one of the eightfold path's eight steps — speak truth, speak with kindness, speak only what helps.

Even the modern psychologist Marshall Rosenberg's 'Nonviolent Communication' rediscovers the same rule. The wisdom keeps coming back, in different languages, century after century.

3. A useful Sanskrit refinement

A famous Sanskrit verse adds the missing third clause:
सत्यं ब्रूयात्, प्रियं ब्रूयात्, न ब्रूयात् सत्यम् अप्रियम् ।
'Speak truth, speak pleasantly; do not speak truth that is unpleasant.'

The verse has been argued about for two thousand years. Does it mean 'never deliver bad news'? Or does it mean 'work HARDER to find a kind way to say true things'? The interpretation matters.

4. The chapter's quiet ambition

When a Sanskrit textbook teaches a 7-year-old this verse, it is not asking them to be perfect. It is planting a sentence in their head that will reappear — at age 15, 25, 50 — every time they have to give bad news, every time someone hurts them with a careless word, every time they are about to be unkind themselves.

Subhashitas work like time-release medicine: dropped in early, they keep dissolving for decades.

💡 Try this

For one day, try the two-condition test on every sentence before you say it: is it true AND kind? You will find yourself silent often. That is the point.

📝 Stretch your thinking

Is there ever a duty to speak unpleasant truth? The chapter says ‘rare is speech both useful and pleasant’ — but what if it is impossible to make a particular truth pleasant? What do you do then?
