Beyond the Book — Why Sanskrit Has Eight Cases (and English Has Almost None)

Most modern European languages have lost their case endings. Sanskrit kept all of them. What did everyone else lose?

≈ 6 min read · curious-kid level

The chapter taught you seven cases plus the vocative — eight forms per noun. English has almost no case endings. Why the gap?

1. They all started together

Linguists are almost certain that around 6,000 years ago there was a single language spoken in what is now southern Russia or eastern Europe — they call it Proto-Indo-European (PIE). From it came Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Persian, Russian, German, English, Hindi, and many more.

Reconstructed PIE had about EIGHT cases. Sanskrit preserved all eight (the chapter's seven plus vocative). Most descendants have lost some — or most — of them.

2. A scale of preservation

Here is roughly how Indo-European languages compare on case-preservation today:

Sanskrit: 8 cases (full system) — preserved.
Russian: 6 cases — well preserved.
Latin (Classical): 6 cases.
Ancient Greek: 5 cases.
German: 4 cases.
Modern Hindi: roughly 2–3 cases.
English: almost none — just the difference between he (subject) and him (object).

Sanskrit is the most conservative survivor. English is among the most simplified.

3. Why simplification happens

When a language relies on word ENDINGS to mark roles, it can put words in almost any order — Sanskrit can say 'Rama the lion kills' or 'the lion Rama kills' or 'kills the lion Rama', and the endings tell you who is doing the killing.

When endings start to wear away (a slow historical process), word order has to do the work instead. English compensates for losing its endings by FIXING word order: 'the lion kills Rama' and 'Rama kills the lion' now mean different things only because of the order.

4. What you gain — and lose

A free-word-order language like Sanskrit lets a poet rearrange words for emphasis and metre without losing meaning. Sanskrit poetry IS this freedom.

A fixed-order language like English is easier to learn (just memorise word order; no endings to drill). But it is poetically more limited.

Both choices are real trade-offs. There is no winning language. Sanskrit kept the rich machinery; English shed it. Each pays a price.

💡 Try this

Translate one short Sanskrit sentence (e.g. 'देवः बालकं पश्यति' = 'the god sees the boy') by changing word order: बालकं देवः पश्यति, पश्यति देवः बालकम्. All correct. Notice how the meaning stays.
