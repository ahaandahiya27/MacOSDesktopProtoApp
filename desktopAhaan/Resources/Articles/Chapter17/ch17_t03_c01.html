Why Deforestation Hurts Everyone

The cascade of consequences · Class 7 Science

Cutting one tree feels small

But deforestation at industrial scale — a million hectares a year globally — triggers a chain reaction that affects climate, water, soil, biodiversity, and human health, often thousands of kilometres downstream.

The cascade of consequences

1. Soil erosion

Without root systems holding it together, topsoil washes away within months. India loses an estimated 16 billion tonnes of topsoil every year — most of it from deforested slopes. Topsoil takes 100–1000 years to form; it's gone in one rainy season.

2. Reduced rainfall

Trees pump moisture into the air via transpiration. A mature tropical tree puts 100+ litres of water vapour per day into the atmosphere. When forests are cut, regional rainfall drops. The Amazon basin and the Western Ghats both show measurable rainfall decline downwind of deforested zones.

3. Flooding

Forest floors absorb rain like a giant sponge — slowly releasing it into streams over weeks. Bare land sheds rain immediately. So deforested watersheds alternate between deadly floods (rainy season) and droughts (dry season). Uttarakhand's 2013 floods were intensified by deforestation upstream.

4. Climate change acceleration

Forests are huge carbon stores. A mature tropical forest holds 200–300 tonnes of CO₂ per hectare. Cutting and burning that forest releases the CO₂ into the atmosphere. Globally, deforestation accounts for about 10% of human carbon emissions — second only to fossil fuel burning.

5. Biodiversity loss

Tropical forests hold ~70% of the world's terrestrial species but cover only 6% of the land surface. Cutting a hectare can wipe out endemic species that exist nowhere else. Once lost, gone forever. We're currently in the planet's 6th mass extinction event, driven primarily by habitat loss.

6. Indigenous people displaced

About 5% of the world's population — Adivasi/tribal communities in India, Amazon tribes, Pygmies in Africa — live IN forests. Deforestation strips them of their homes, livelihoods, and cultural heritage.

7. Disease emergence

Wildlife displaced by deforestation moves into human-occupied areas, increasing zoonotic disease spillover — Ebola, Nipah, SARS, COVID-19 all probably originated this way. Deforestation literally makes pandemics more likely.

The single chainsaw seems small. But globally, the world lost 4.1 million hectares of primary tropical forest in 2022 alone — an area roughly the size of Switzerland. That's a slow-motion disaster compounding every year.

India's response

  • Project Tiger (1973) — protected tiger reserves now cover 75,000 sq.km, indirectly saving the forests they live in.
  • Forest Conservation Act 1980 — central permission required to divert forest land.
  • Joint Forest Management — village committees co-manage forest patches.
  • Chipko Movement (1970s, Uttarakhand) — village women hugging trees to stop loggers. Inspired forest-protection movements worldwide.
  • National Mission for a Green India — aims to plant 6.3 million hectares.
