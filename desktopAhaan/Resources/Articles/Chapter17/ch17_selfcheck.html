Question 1 — from Chapter 17

Describe the layers of a forest. What kinds of organisms live in each?

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: A forest has four main layers: (1) Canopy — the tops of tall trees, home to birds and monkeys. (2) Understorey — smaller trees in filtered light, home to creepers and tree-dwelling animals. (3) Shrub layer — bushes and herbs. (4) Forest floor — fungi, ferns, insects, decomposers, fallen leaves rotting back into humus.

Why: Layer 1 — Canopy: tallest trees, full sunlight, birds and monkeys.

Question 3 — from Chapter 17

A 100-hectare forest is cut down. Trace what happens to the LOCAL CLIMATE over 5 years (rainfall, temperature, humidity), and explain WHY each change occurs.

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: Year 1: Trees gone; ground exposed. Rain falls but no canopy to slow it — runoff increases dramatically. Year 1-2: Temperature rises 2-4°C locally because trees cooled by transpiration + shade are gone. Humidity drops because transpiration is the main local moisture source; rain becomes more erratic. Year 3-5: Reduced cloud formation downwind (transpiration was a major water-vapour source) → less rain. Soil erodes; albedo (reflectivity) shifts as bare earth vs. green canopy reflects differently. By year 5: hotter, drier, more erratic rainfall — a measurable LOCAL DROUGHT cycle, even though global climate hasn't changed.

Why: Trees: shade + transpiration + canopy break of rain.

Question 4 — from Chapter 17

Why is the forest floor often dark even at noon?

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: The dense canopy of tall trees blocks most sunlight from reaching the ground, keeping the floor shaded and cool.

Why: Tall trees form a leafy canopy ~30 m above the ground.

Question 5 — from Chapter 17

A government policy proposes paying tribal communities a yearly stipend to PROTECT their forest. Evaluate this as a conservation strategy — what could go right, what could go wrong, and what design would maximise success?

🧠 Answer in your head first

Don't peek. Speak the answer out loud, then check.

Answer: Could go right: (1) Aligns economic incentive with conservation (don't have to cut trees for income). (2) Recognises traditional forest management expertise. (3) Reduces conflict between conservation goals + local livelihoods. Could go wrong: (1) Outside corruption — stipends siphoned by middlemen. (2) Insufficient amount — pressure to log returns. (3) Dependency — community loses autonomy. (4) Verification problem — who confirms the forest is protected? Maximise success: (a) Direct bank transfers (cut middlemen). (b) Long-term contracts (10+ years) to give planning horizon. (c) Community-led monitoring + outside audit. (d) Stipend tied to forest health metrics, not just non-logging. (e) Cap stipend so it doesn't replace ALL income — leaves residual incentive for forest-friendly enterprise.

Why: Stipend design: aligns incentive with conservation.
