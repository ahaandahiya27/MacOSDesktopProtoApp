Forest Strata

Why a forest is built like a four-storey building · Class 7 Science

A forest has floors

If you've only ever walked through a forest looking around at eye level, you've missed three-quarters of it. A mature forest is layered vertically — different plants and animals occupy different heights, like flats in a four-storey building. Ecologists call these layers strata.

Four storeys of life

  • Canopy (top floor): The umbrella of the tallest trees. Bathed in sunlight. In tropical forests can be 30-50 metres up. Most leaves are here, so most photosynthesis happens here. Home: birds, monkeys, leopards, fruit bats.
  • Understory: Smaller trees and woody vines, growing in the filtered light below the canopy. Young trees waiting for an old canopy giant to fall so they can grow up into its spot. Home: smaller birds, snakes, larger insects.
  • Shrub layer: Bushes, ferns, and saplings about waist-to-shoulder high. Where most browsing animals like deer feed. Home: rodents, larger ground birds, lizards.
  • Forest floor: Dark, damp ground covered in leaf litter, mosses, fungi, and slowly-decaying logs. Most of the year-round biodiversity actually lives HERE — insects, worms, salamanders, ground-nesting birds, and an enormous community of soil microbes.
A square metre of tropical forest floor contains an estimated 1,000+ different invertebrate species. Far more biodiversity per square metre than the canopy above.

Why strata?

Light is the answer. Sunlight is the energy source for all forest life, and it's a finite resource: only as much as the canopy receives. As light filters down through the leaves, less and less reaches each layer below. Plants and animals have evolved over millions of years to specialise in whichever light level they can use most efficiently.

Strata are also defensive zones

Different layers offer different shelters. Canopy birds nest where ground predators can't reach. Shrub-layer animals hide where canopy predators can't see them. Forest-floor species use leaf litter as camouflage. Every species has a 'niche' that fits its anatomy, diet, and predator-avoidance strategy.

Try this at home

Visit your nearest park or wooded area. Look up — at three different heights: the very tops of the tallest trees, the mid-height of smaller trees, and your shoulder level. Note which birds and insects you see at each level. Most species will stick to one layer. You've just observed forest stratification in action — even in an urban park.

Looking ahead

Class 12 Biology 'Ecosystem' formalises this idea as ecological pyramids:

  • Pyramid of biomass (the dry weight of organisms at each trophic level).
  • Pyramid of energy (always upright, due to the 10% rule — only ~10% of energy at each level reaches the next).
  • Pyramid of numbers (can be inverted in some food chains).

Class 12 'Biodiversity and Conservation' covers India's major forest types — tropical evergreen (Western Ghats), tropical deciduous (Central India), mangrove (Sundarbans), montane (Himalayas) — each with its own stratification pattern. NEET asks ecological-pyramid and biome-classification questions every year.
