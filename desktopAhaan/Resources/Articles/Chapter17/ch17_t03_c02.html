Why India Cuts Forests — And What It Costs

Mining, dams, agriculture and the slow loss · Class 7 Science

How much forest is left

India's recorded forest area is ~21% of its land — below the 33% target set in the National Forest Policy. The Forest Survey of India reports forest cover every two years. The North-East and Andamans still have dense tropical forests; the Western Ghats and Central India have shrinking ones.

The four big drivers

  • Agriculture — clearing forest for crops, especially shifting cultivation in the North-East.
  • Dams and reservoirs — projects like Sardar Sarovar submerged ~37,000 ha of forest in the Narmada valley.
  • Mining — coal in Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh, iron ore in Karnataka, bauxite in Odisha — all under thick forest.
  • Roads and urban sprawl — highway construction through Western Ghats fragments forests into isolated patches.

What it costs

  • Air quality drops — fewer trees absorb less CO₂ and dust.
  • Rivers run lower — forests act as sponges that release water year-round.
  • Wildlife extinctions — Indian leopard, Asiatic lion, Hangul deer all critically endangered.
  • Tribal displacement — millions of forest-dwelling Adivasi communities lose homes and livelihoods.

The law

The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 requires Central Government approval before any forest land is diverted. The Forest Rights Act, 2006 recognises tribal communities' rights over forest land they have traditionally used.

When Project Tiger started in 1973, India had only 1,800 tigers. Today there are ~3,000 — proof that protecting tiger habitat (which means protecting the entire forest ecosystem) actually works. India hosts 75% of the world's wild tigers.
