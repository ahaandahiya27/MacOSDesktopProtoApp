The Forest Floor — Where Death Becomes Soil

Fungi, bacteria and the recycling crew · Class 7 Science

The leaf-fall arithmetic

A mature tropical forest drops about 5–10 tonnes of leaves per hectare per year. If nothing broke them down, in 100 years a forest floor would have a metre-thick blanket of leaves. Instead, decomposers turn it all back into soil within months.

The decomposer team

  • Fungi (like mushrooms) secrete enzymes that break down tough cellulose and lignin.
  • Bacteria finish off the smaller molecules.
  • Earthworms, millipedes, termites chew leaf litter into smaller bits — speeding the work of microbes 10×.

How fast is decomposition?

A fallen leaf in a tropical rainforest decomposes in 6 months. The same leaf in a temperate forest takes 1–3 years. In the freezing Arctic, decomposition is so slow that dead vegetation forms peat — and over millennia, coal.

Why the forest is self-sustaining

Tree absorbs nutrients from soil → grows leaves → sheds leaves → decomposers break leaves down → nutrients go back into the soil → tree absorbs them again. A forest needs no fertiliser. The whole cycle is closed.

Cut down a forest and the cycle breaks. Within a few years, exposed soil washes away in monsoon rains. What's left becomes too poor to support trees — this is the start of desertification.

Try this at home

Lift a flat stone in any forest or garden. Underneath you'll see white fungal threads (mycelium), woodlice, millipedes, ants. That's the decomposer crew at work — you're looking at recycling in real time.
