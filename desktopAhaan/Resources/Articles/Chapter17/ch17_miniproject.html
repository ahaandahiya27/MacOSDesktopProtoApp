Two Mini Projects for Chapter 17

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🍂 Build a mini decomposer column — leaves to soil in 30 days

~30240 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A 2-litre plastic bottle (clean, dry)
  • Fallen leaves and twigs (from a tree near your home)
  • Some kitchen scraps (vegetable peels, no oil/cooked food)
  • A handful of garden soil
  • Water

Steps

  1. Cut the plastic bottle open near the top to make a deep cup.
  2. Add layers: bottom = 2 cm soil, then 2 cm dry leaves, then 1 cm wet kitchen scraps, repeat layers until ¾ full.
  3. Sprinkle water — soil should be damp, not soggy.
  4. Cover loosely (allow air in).
  5. Observe weekly for 4 weeks. Note: smell, colour, what's happening to the leaves.

👀 What you should see

Week 1: leaves still recognisable. Week 2: surface looking darker, smell of damp earth. Week 3: hard to tell where leaves end and soil begins. Week 4: dark crumbly compost — much less volume than you started with. Tiny worms or insects may appear. The mass that 'disappeared' became CO₂, water vapour, and decomposer bodies.

Why it works: You're replicating a forest floor in miniature. Decomposers (already present in soil and on the leaves) break down the organic matter. The dark crumbly final product is HUMUS — the same humus that makes forest soil so fertile. Conservation lesson: leaves aren't 'litter to be cleaned up' — they're nutrient feed for next year's growth.

🌳 Adopt a tree — observe one tree across a month

~1800 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A notebook + pencil
  • A measuring tape
  • A camera or phone
  • A nearby tree (any type)

Steps

  1. Choose any tree near your home or school — neem, peepal, banyan, jamun, gulmohar.
  2. Day 1: measure its CIRCUMFERENCE at chest height. Note its HEIGHT (estimate). Count leaves on one branch. Note flowers/fruit.
  3. Daily check for 1 month: any new leaves? Fallen leaves? Birds visiting? Insects? Squirrels? Spider webs? Any change in light coming through?
  4. Weekly: take a photo from the same spot.
  5. End of month: review your notes. What changed?

👀 What you should see

More than you'd expect. Most trees go through a daily/weekly cycle of leaf fall, new growth, bird visits. You'll likely notice: (1) Different birds visit at different times. (2) Insect populations change with weather. (3) Leaf colour shifts as season changes. (4) The tree breathes — you can almost feel a busy ecosystem.

Why it works: Observation is the first step in ecology. Most of us see trees as background — this project makes one tree a LIVING ORGANISM that you can name and know. After this project, you'll never see a tree the same way again. Many great ecologists started with a single tree they watched for a year.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
