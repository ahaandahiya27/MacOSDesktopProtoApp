Food Webs and Energy Flow

The 10% rule that shapes every ecosystem · Class 7 Science

Energy enters the forest from the Sun

About 1 kilowatt of solar energy strikes every square metre of Earth on a sunny day. Plants capture about 1% of that through photosynthesis (Ch 1). All other life on land — from ants to elephants — depends on what plants captured. Energy enters at the bottom, flows upward through eaters of eaters, and is mostly lost as heat at each step.

The food chain ladder

Every species in an ecosystem fits into a trophic level:

  • Trophic Level 1 — Producers (autotrophs): green plants, algae, some bacteria. Make their own food.
  • Trophic Level 2 — Primary consumers (herbivores): deer, rabbits, grasshoppers, cows. Eat plants.
  • Trophic Level 3 — Secondary consumers (small carnivores): snakes, foxes, frogs. Eat herbivores.
  • Trophic Level 4 — Tertiary consumers (apex predators): tigers, eagles, lions. Eat secondary consumers.
  • Decomposers: fungi, bacteria, worms. Recycle dead organisms from any level back to nutrients.

Food chains vs food webs

A food chain is a single sequence: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle. A food web is the actual reality — dozens of interconnected food chains, because most species eat (and are eaten by) several different others.

The 10% rule

At each trophic level, only about 10% of the energy makes it to the next level. The other 90% is lost as:
  • Heat (respiration in the body).
  • Movement (chasing, escaping, mating).
  • Indigestible parts (cellulose, bones, hair).

That single rule shapes everything:

  • It takes 1,000 kg of grass to support 100 kg of grasshopper, which supports 10 kg of frog, which supports 1 kg of snake, which supports 0.1 kg of eagle.
  • That's why top predators are RARE — there's just not enough energy for many of them.
  • That's why food chains rarely have more than 4-5 levels — beyond that, there's almost no energy left.

Ecological pyramids

Visualise trophic levels as a pyramid:

  • Pyramid of energy — always wider at the bottom, narrower at the top (because of the 10% rule).
  • Pyramid of biomass — usually wider at bottom (more plant mass than animal mass), but inverted in aquatic ecosystems where phytoplankton turn over fast.
  • Pyramid of numbers — can be inverted in parasite chains (one tree → thousands of insects → millions of bacteria).

Why this matters for conservation

  • Removing a top predator can cause prey populations to explode and destroy the plant layer (the 'trophic cascade' — yellowstone wolves are a famous example).
  • Pollutants like DDT become MORE concentrated as they move up the food chain — small fish absorb DDT, bigger fish concentrate it, eagles at the top get the lethal dose. This is biomagnification.
  • Removing decomposers stops nutrient recycling. An ecosystem without fungi and bacteria would smother in its own dead matter within years.

Try this at home

Sit in a park or garden for 10 minutes with paper and pencil. List everything alive you spot. Draw arrows showing 'eats' relationships: grass → ant → spider → sparrow. You've drawn a food chain. Combine three of them and you get a food web. You're an ecologist.
