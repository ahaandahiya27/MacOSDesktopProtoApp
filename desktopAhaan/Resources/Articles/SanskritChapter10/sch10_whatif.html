"What If?" — दशमः कः?

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook teaches what IS. Real understanding comes from asking what IF? Pick a question below, pause and think for a minute, then read one possible discussion.

What if 1 — the boys had lined up and pointed at each other to count?

The question: An obvious counting method that would have caught the mistake.

🧠 Think first

Why didn't the parable's boys try this?

One discussion path: Because each one trusted his own count more than the obvious procedure. Pride in one's own seeing is the very thing the parable critiques. The story works because the boys' OWN method is what blinds them — not stupidity but self-trust without checking. The Vedāntic point: we trust our own perception of the world (which leaves us out) and miss the obvious.

What if 2 — NO traveler had come along — the boys stayed convinced the tenth had drowned?

The question: Suppose nobody pointed it out. They go home, mourn the lost tenth.

🧠 Think first

Would the truth ever surface?

One discussion path: Eventually someone would notice — a parent counting heads at home, perhaps. The parable does not say truth is hidden forever; it says SEEING it requires either an outside perspective or careful self-questioning. The 'traveler' role can be played by a teacher, a book, a difficult moment, a friend. Without them, blindness is much stickier.

What if 3 — the boys had actually been NINE all along — the tenth a misremembering?

The question: A different shape of the same confusion.

🧠 Think first

What would the parable mean then?

One discussion path: Then there would be NO Vedāntic truth to discover — the boys were correct that there was no tenth. The parable's force depends on there being a tenth they keep missing. Vedānta is making a specific claim: there IS a self each of us is missing. Not an absence, but a presence we don't see.

📝 Stretch your thinking

None of these has one 'correct' answer — they are for arguing about. Try one on a friend or a parent and see if their discussion path differs from yours.
