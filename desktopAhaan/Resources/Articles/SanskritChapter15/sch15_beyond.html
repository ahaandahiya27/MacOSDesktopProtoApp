Beyond the Book — Sanskrit's Verb Forest

Sanskrit has about 2,000 verb roots. From each one it can build a vast family of forms. English has about that many verbs total — and a tiny family per verb.

≈ 6 min read · curious-kid level

The chapter taught you how one root (पठ्, पठति, पठतः, पठन्ति) makes nine present-tense forms. That is only the beginning of what each Sanskrit root can do.

1. Pāṇini's 2,000 roots

The classical Sanskrit grammarian Pāṇini (Chapter 13's hero) lists about 2,000 verb roots in his धातुपाठ ('roots-recitation'). He groups them into ten classes (गणाः) — verbs in each class share patterns of stem-formation.

The chapter's पठ् is in the 1st class (भ्वादिगण), the largest. The chapter's अस् ('to be') is in the 2nd class (अदादिगण). कृ ('to do') is in the 8th class. The classification predicts how each root behaves.

2. The derivational families

Each Sanskrit verb root can launch a whole FAMILY of related forms, far beyond simple tense changes:

Present: करोति (he does).
Past: अकरोत् (he did).
Future: करिष्यति (he will do).
Imperative: करोतु (let him do!).
Causative: कारयति (he MAKES someone do).
Desiderative: चिकीर्षति (he WANTS to do).
Intensive: चेक्रीयते (he does INTENSELY/repeatedly).
Past participle: कृतः (done).
Agent-noun: कर्ता (the doer).
Action-noun: कार्यम् (the action / what is to be done).

Each of those is a distinct word, distinct meaning, distinct form. From one root.

3. The implication

English makes verbs do a lot with a little. Sanskrit makes its verbs carry MORE meaning per word. Look at कारयति ('he makes someone else do') — Sanskrit packs the causative idea INSIDE the verb. English needs four words: 'he makes him do'.

The Sanskrit way is dense and elegant. The English way is more analytic — you build meaning by stringing words. Different design philosophies.

4. The art of the derivational chain

Try this: from पठ् ('read') Sanskrit can build पाठयति (he TEACHES — i.e. causes to read), पिपठिषति (he WANTS to read), पाठ्यते (it IS read), पाठकः (the teacher), पठितव्यम् (what should be read), पठित्वा (having read), पठितुम् (to read), पाठनम् (the act of teaching)…

ALL of these are forms of one root. Sanskrit's verb forest is genuinely a forest — every root grows a tree.

Memorise one root well, and a hundred words open up.

💡 Try this

Take any Sanskrit root from the chapter (पठ्, कृ, अस्). Try to list five different words built from it. You will be surprised how easy it is.

📝 Stretch your thinking

English has perhaps 200,000 distinct words. Sanskrit, with 2,000 roots and a productive derivation system, can in principle generate millions. Why might a language choose richness via derivation rather than via storing more separate words?
