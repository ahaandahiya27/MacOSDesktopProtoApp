"What If?" — धातुरूपाणि

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook teaches what IS. Real understanding comes from asking what IF? Pick a question below, pause and think for a minute, then read one possible discussion.

What if 1 — Sanskrit had only ONE tense?

The question: Imagine a language with no past, no future — only present.

🧠 Think first

Could it still discuss history, prophecy, dreams?

One discussion path: Yes, but laboriously — via TIME PHRASES ('yesterday, he goes'). Mandarin Chinese does something close to this, marking time with words rather than verb endings. The strategy WORKS but offloads work from grammar onto vocabulary. Sanskrit's many tenses encode time precisely and quickly.

What if 2 — every verb had to be irregular?

The question: Sanskrit's अस् 'to be' is irregular. Imagine ALL ~2,000 roots were too.

🧠 Think first

Would the language be learnable?

One discussion path: Barely. The whole point of regular conjugation patterns is that learning ONE verb in a class teaches you HUNDREDS. Irregularity is the price of frequency: the most-used verbs (be, do, go, eat) tend to be irregular in many languages because high-frequency use polishes them into unique shapes. Sanskrit's mostly-regular system is what makes 2,000 roots manageable.

What if 3 — the entire grammar had to be memorised orally for a thousand years?

The question: Sanskrit was largely transmitted by memory long before being written down.

🧠 Think first

What kept it stable?

One discussion path: Three things: (1) precise rules like Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī, which is small enough to memorise; (2) intense oral training in gurukulas; (3) ritual contexts where mistakes were socially expensive. Without writing, those three substitutes had to do all the preservation. The fact that Sanskrit survived intact across millennia of oral-only transmission is one of human civilisation's quiet miracles.

📝 Stretch your thinking

None of these has one 'correct' answer — they are for arguing about. Try one on a friend or a parent and see if their discussion path differs from yours.
