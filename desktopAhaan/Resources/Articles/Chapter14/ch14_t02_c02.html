Joule's Law — Why Wires Get Hot

The single equation behind heaters, bulbs and fuses · Class 7 Science

What happens inside a wire

Electric current is moving electrons. When electrons drift through a wire, they bump into the wire's atoms — like marbles rolling through a pegboard. Each collision transfers a bit of energy to the atom, making it vibrate faster. Vibrating atoms = hotter wire.

Joule's law

The heat produced in a wire is given by:

H = I² × R × t

where I is current (amperes), R is resistance (ohms), and t is time (seconds). The heat goes up as the square of current — double the current, four times the heat.

Why this matters

  • Heater coils use high-R nichrome wire so they get red-hot.
  • Bulb filaments use tungsten (very high melting point) wound into a tight coil for high R.
  • Fuses use a thin low-melt wire so it melts at a known current value, breaking the circuit before damage spreads.
  • Power-transmission wires are thick (low R) and use high voltage (low I) — to keep I²R losses small over long distances.

LED vs filament

An old 60-W filament bulb wastes ~95% of its energy as heat. An LED bulb of the same brightness uses only 7-10 W and stays cool. Joule heating is the enemy of efficient lighting — and LEDs win by avoiding it.

When you charge a phone, the back of the phone gets warm. That's I²R heating in the wires and battery. Excessive heating is why some phones with bad batteries swell or catch fire — temperature rises beyond what the materials can handle.

Try this at home

Touch a working laptop's charger brick after 30 min. It is warm. That's I²R heat from the small wires inside, converting the higher input voltage to the lower laptop voltage.
