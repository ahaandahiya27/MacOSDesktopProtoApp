Two Mini Projects for Chapter 14

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🧲 Build your own electromagnet with a battery and a nail

~20 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • An iron nail (~10 cm)
  • 1.5 m of thin insulated copper wire (24 gauge magnet wire)
  • A 1.5 V cell with holder (or two cells for a stronger magnet)
  • Some small iron objects: paper clips, pins, staples

Steps

  1. Strip 1 cm of insulation off both ends of the copper wire.
  2. Wrap the wire tightly around the nail — 50+ turns. Leave 10 cm free at each end.
  3. Connect the two free ends to the cell terminals (one to + and one to −).
  4. Bring the nail tip near the paper clips.
  5. Lift them! Now disconnect one wire — what happens?

👀 What you should see

When connected, the nail picks up paper clips. When disconnected, they fall off. More turns of wire = stronger magnet. Two cells (3 V) = stronger magnet than one.

Why it works: Current flowing through the coil creates a magnetic field. The iron nail (core) concentrates and amplifies the field. The result: an on-demand magnet. This is exactly how industrial cranes, MRI machines, doorbells, and electric motors are built — the principle scales from this nail to multi-tonne cranes.

🍋 Lemon battery — make electricity from fruit

~30 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • 3 lemons (juicy, ripe)
  • 3 copper coins or copper strips
  • 3 zinc-coated (galvanised) nails or zinc strips
  • Insulated wires with clips on the ends
  • A small LED or a multimeter

Steps

  1. Roll each lemon to break the membranes inside.
  2. Insert one copper coin and one zinc nail into each lemon, NOT touching each other.
  3. Connect: zinc of lemon 1 → copper of lemon 2 (wire). Zinc of lemon 2 → copper of lemon 3 (wire). Now you have a series of 3 lemons.
  4. Connect free zinc to one LED leg, free copper to the other.
  5. Observe.

👀 What you should see

The LED glows dimly. One lemon alone gives ~0.9 V; three in series give ~2.7 V — enough to light a small LED. The lemons act as wet-cell batteries.

Why it works: Lemon juice (citric acid) is an electrolyte — ions flow between the zinc and copper electrodes. The zinc loses electrons to the acid; the copper gains them. Electrons travel through the external wire (your LED) from zinc to copper. Same principle as a 9 V battery — different electrolyte and electrodes, but identical chemistry. Alessandro Volta's first battery (1800) used the same idea.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
