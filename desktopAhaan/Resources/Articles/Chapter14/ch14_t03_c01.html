Electromagnets — Ørsted to Modern Cranes

Chapter 14 · Topic 3 · Concept 1 · Class 7 Science

From a twitching compass to industrial cranes

Ørsted's 1820 demonstration was simple — a wire connected to a battery, placed over a compass. When current flowed, the compass needle deflected. A current makes magnetism. Two centuries later, the same principle picks up cars in a junkyard.

Making an electromagnet

  1. Get an iron bar (a nail or rod works for a school demonstration).
  2. Wrap insulated copper wire tightly around it — many turns, all in the same direction.
  3. Connect the two ends of the wire to a battery (1.5 V cell for small experiments; industrial cranes use enormous power supplies).
  4. Current flows → magnetic field forms around each loop of wire → fields add up inside the iron core → core becomes strongly magnetised.
  5. Disconnect the battery → magnetism almost completely vanishes within a fraction of a second.

Why iron?

Iron, nickel, cobalt, and a few alloys are ferromagnetic — they get hugely magnetised when placed inside a magnetic field. Inside an iron-core electromagnet, the field strength can be 1000× stronger than an air-core coil of the same dimensions. The iron also drops its magnetism fast when current stops — perfect for switchable magnets.

What makes an electromagnet stronger:
  • More turns of wire — each loop adds to the total field.
  • More current — proportional to field strength.
  • Iron (or soft steel) core — concentrates the field.
  • Compact coil — closer turns = stronger field.

The right-hand rule

To find the direction of the magnetic field inside a coil:

  • Wrap your right hand around the coil with fingers curling in the direction of conventional current flow.
  • Your thumb points to the NORTH pole of the resulting electromagnet.

Reverse the current direction, and north and south swap — that's why electric motors can rotate.

Industrial applications

  • Scrap-metal cranes: a huge electromagnet at the end of a crane boom picks up cars or steel beams. Operator switches off the current → the car drops where the operator wants it.
  • Maglev trains (Shanghai, Japan): electromagnets levitate the train and propel it without touching the rails. Top speed: ~600 km/h.
  • MRI machines: a powerful superconducting electromagnet creates a uniform magnetic field that aligns hydrogen nuclei in your body. Radio pulses image them. The whole technology rests on Ørsted's tiny 1820 deflection.
  • Magnetic separators in mines: extract iron-bearing ore from crushed rock.
  • Electric door locks, vending machines, hard-drive read heads, microphone diaphragms — all electromagnets.

Try this at home

Wrap ~30 turns of insulated copper wire (~1 metre total) around an iron nail. Strip the two ends and connect to a 1.5 V cell. Pick up paper clips with the nail — they should stick easily. Now disconnect the cell — the clips fall off. Switchable magnetism in a children's experiment.

Looking ahead

Class 10 'Magnetic Effects' formalises this with the solenoid (a long current-carrying coil) and shows how it functions identically to a bar magnet. Class 12 'Moving Charges and Magnetism' adds:

  • Biot-Savart law: B = μ₀I / (2πr) for a straight wire; B = μ₀NI / L for a solenoid.
  • Force on a current in a magnetic field: F = BIL — the principle behind every electric motor.
  • Electromagnetic induction (Faraday's law): a CHANGING magnetic field creates a current — the reverse of Ørsted's effect. This is the principle behind every electric generator + transformer + wireless charger.

JEE Physics asks 3-4 problems on magnetic forces and induction every year. The whole modern electrical grid runs on the consequences of Ørsted's accidental 1820 observation.
