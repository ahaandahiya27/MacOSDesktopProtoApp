Circuits & Current

Topic 1 of Chapter 14 · Class 7 Science

The big idea

Electricity is one of the most useful tricks humans ever learned — it lights your room, runs your fridge, charges your phone, and powers the device you're reading this on. But every electric appliance, from a torch to a laptop, works on the same simple plan: a closed loop of conductors that lets charge flow continuously. That loop is called a circuit, and the moving charge is called current.

One sentence: a current flows only when there is a closed path from one terminal of a battery, through wires and components, back to the other terminal.

The simplest circuit ever

A cell (or a battery — a stack of cells), two wires, and a bulb. Connect the bulb to the cell using the wires, and the bulb glows. Cut any wire and the bulb goes off. That's it. Everything else in electricity is a clever variation on this idea.

What flows in a wire?

Tiny charged particles called electrons. Every metal wire is packed with them. When you close a circuit, all the free electrons in the wire start drifting together — slowly, but in a continuous stream. This stream is what we call an electric current, measured in amperes (A).

Series and parallel — two ways to wire devices

  • Series — devices are wired one after another, on a single loop. Cut any one, all go out. Old Christmas-tree lights were wired this way.
  • Parallel — each device has its own loop, all sharing the same cell. Cut one, the others stay on. House wiring is parallel.

Not everything conducts

Some materials let current flow easily — these are conductors (copper, silver, aluminium, most metals, human skin when wet). Others block the flow — these are insulators (rubber, plastic, dry wood, glass, air). That's why electric wires have a copper core inside a rubber/plastic sleeve.

Looking ahead

In Class 10 (Electricity) you'll meet the equation that runs all of this: Ohm's Law, V = IR. You'll learn how to combine resistors in series (R = R₁ + R₂) and in parallel (1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂), and how to calculate the current flowing through any branch.

In Class 12 / JEE Physics (Current Electricity) this expands into Kirchhoff's Voltage and Current Laws, Wheatstone bridges, metre bridges, potentiometers, and the resistivity-temperature relationship. Multiple JEE questions every year come from this chain. NEET Physics tests the same fundamentals in lighter form.
