Q1. Draw in your notebook the symbols to represent: electric cell, switch in OFF position, electric bulb, battery, switch in ON position, and wire.

Model answer: Electric cell: longer line | shorter thick line. Battery: multiple cells joined ||||. Switch OFF: gap in line with arc above. Switch ON: line completed. Bulb: circle with X inside or ⊗. Wire: simple straight line ───. These are the standard symbols used in circuit diagrams worldwide.

Q2. Mark 'T' if the statement is true and 'F' if it is false: (a) To make a battery of two cells, the negative terminal of one cell is connected to the negative terminal of the other cell. (b) When current is switched on through a wire, a compass needle placed near it gets deflected. (c) An electromagnet does not attract a piece of iron. (d) An electric bell has an electromagnet.

Model answer: (a) F — to make a battery, you connect POSITIVE of one cell to NEGATIVE of the next (in series). Negative-to-negative cancels and gives zero voltage. (b) T — Ørsted's discovery. The compass deflects because current creates a magnetic field. (c) F — an electromagnet (current-carrying coil with iron core) definitely attracts iron when current flows. (d) T — every electric bell has a small electromagnet that pulls the striker against the bell, then releases it, then pulls again — making the ringing.

Q3. Do you think an electric circuit can have more than one switch?

Model answer: Yes — and most household circuits do. Your home's main switch can cut all power; individual room switches can cut their lights/fans only. Stair lighting often uses 'two-way switches' — one at top, one at bottom — so either can turn the same light on or off. Industrial circuits often have many switches, with safety logic ensuring no equipment runs without permission. The Class 7 single-switch circuit is the simplest case; real circuits scale up.

Q4. The bulb in the circuit shown in Figure does not glow. Can you identify the problem? Make necessary changes in the circuit to make the bulb glow.

Model answer: Common reasons a bulb in a circuit doesn't glow: (1) Switch is OFF. (2) Cells (battery) are connected wrong way — same poles together cancel out. (3) Loose wire connection. (4) Bulb filament is broken (looks intact but no light). (5) Bulb's terminals aren't touching the circuit. To fix: check switch is ON; check cells connected positive-to-negative-to-positive; tighten all wire ends; test bulb in a known-working circuit.

Q5. Name any two effects of electric current.

Model answer: (1) HEATING effect — current passing through a wire makes it hot. This is used in electric heaters, geysers, electric kettles, irons. The bulb filament glows because it's so hot. (2) MAGNETIC effect — current passing through a coil creates a magnetic field. Used in electric bells, doorbells, motors, electromagnets, MRI scanners, hard drives. Both effects together = vast modern technology. (A third effect: chemical, in batteries and electroplating.)

Q6. When the current is switched on through a wire, a compass needle kept nearby gets deflected from its north-south position. Explain.

Model answer: This is Ørsted's 1820 discovery. A current-carrying wire creates a magnetic field around itself, perpendicular to the wire (forming circles around the wire). A compass needle aligns with any magnetic field. Normally, only Earth's magnetic field reaches it, so it points north-south. When current flows in the nearby wire, the wire's magnetic field adds to Earth's — strong enough nearby that the compass needle now points along the COMBINED field, deflected from true north. Turn off current → field disappears → needle returns to north-south.

Q7. Will the compass needle show deflection when the switch in the circuit shown by Figure is closed?

Model answer: Yes. Closing the switch completes the circuit; current flows in the wire. By Ørsted's discovery, current creates a magnetic field; the compass needle responds to that field. The exact direction of deflection depends on the direction of current flow (rule: right-hand thumb in current direction, fingers curl in field direction). When switch opens → no current → no field → needle returns to north-south.

Q8. Name two safety measures commonly used in electric circuits and appliances.

Model answer: (1) FUSE — a deliberately weak wire that melts when current exceeds safe limit, breaking the circuit before wires overheat or cause fire. (2) Earthing/Grounding — connecting metal cases of appliances to ground via a third wire. If a live wire accidentally touches the metal case, current flows safely to ground (not through a person touching the case). Modern homes also use MCBs (auto-resetting fuses) and ELCBs (Earth Leakage Circuit Breakers) that detect tiny leakage and trip instantly — saving lives.
