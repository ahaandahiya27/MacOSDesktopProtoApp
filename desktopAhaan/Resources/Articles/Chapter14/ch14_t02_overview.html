Heating Effect of Current — Topic Overview

Topic 2 of Chapter 14 · Class 7 Science

Resistance turns electricity into heat

Every wire pushes back a little against the electrons trying to flow through it. That push-back is resistance. When current flows through resistance, some of the electrical energy turns into heat. This is the heating effect of electric current — and it's behind every electric appliance that warms or cooks something in your home.

The amount of heat produced is given by Joule's law: H = I²Rt. Heat (H) is proportional to the SQUARE of current (I), to the resistance (R), and to the time (t). Double the current → four times the heat.

What we use the heating effect for

  • Electric iron — a nichrome coil heats up; the heat presses out wrinkles in cloth.
  • Electric kettle, toaster, heater — same principle, just shaped differently.
  • Electric stove, induction stove — direct heating for cooking.
  • Incandescent bulb — a tungsten filament heats white-hot at ~2500 °C, producing light as a side effect (95% of the input energy is wasted as heat).
  • Hair dryer, geyser, room heater — heating is the entire point.
  • Fuse and MCB — heating intentional, designed to MELT or trip when current exceeds a safe limit.

Why nichrome?

Most heating elements use nichrome (nickel + chromium alloy) because it has:

  • High resistance — produces lots of heat per ampere.
  • Very high melting point (~1400 °C) — doesn't melt at red-hot working temperatures.
  • Doesn't oxidise even when red-hot — a chromium-oxide layer protects it.
  • Cheap compared to platinum or tungsten.
