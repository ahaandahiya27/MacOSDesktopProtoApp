Magnetic Effect of Current — Topic Overview

Topic 3 of Chapter 14 · Class 7 Science

A current creates a magnetic field

In 1820, the Danish physicist Hans Christian Ørsted noticed that a compass needle near a current-carrying wire deflected. This was the first proof that electricity and magnetism are connected. From that single observation, the whole field of electromagnetism was born — and with it, the electric motor, the generator, the transformer, the loudspeaker, the MRI scanner, and the entire modern electricity-driven world.

The discovery was accidental — Ørsted was demonstrating a current-carrying wire in a lecture when he noticed a nearby compass needle twitch. Within months he had published, and within a year Ampère had worked out the maths. Electromagnetism: discovered, named, and formalised in 18 months.

The basic facts

  • A straight wire carrying current produces a circular magnetic field around it.
  • The field direction follows the right-hand rule — point your right thumb in the direction of conventional current, and your curled fingers show the field direction.
  • Stronger current → stronger field. Closer to the wire → stronger field.
  • If you coil the wire into a loop, the fields stack — making a strong magnet inside the loop.

The electromagnet

Wrap a coil of insulated wire around an iron nail, connect to a battery, and the nail becomes a magnet — but only while the current flows. Disconnect the battery and the magnetism vanishes. That switchable magnetism is the trick that makes:

  • Scrap-metal cranes — pick up cars; drop them on command.
  • Electric bells, buzzers — electromagnet pulls a hammer; spring brings it back; repeat.
  • Loudspeakers — current through a voice coil makes it move a paper cone, creating sound waves.
  • Electric motors — rotating electromagnets push against permanent magnets.
  • MRI machines — giant superconducting electromagnets image inside the human body.
  • Magnetic locks on bank doors and hotel rooms.
