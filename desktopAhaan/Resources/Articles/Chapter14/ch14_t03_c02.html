The Magnetic Effect — From Doorbells to MagLev Trains

Oersted's accident and what came of it · Class 7 Science

Oersted's lucky lecture (1820)

Danish physicist Hans Christian Oersted placed a compass near a wire connected to a battery. Closing the switch made the compass needle swing. He had stumbled onto the link between electricity and magnetism — kicking off the entire electrical age.

A current makes a magnetic field

Any wire carrying current is surrounded by a circular magnetic field. Coil the wire into a tight spiral (a solenoid) and the fields add up — you have an electromagnet. Strength depends on:

  • Number of turns in the coil.
  • Amount of current.
  • Iron core in the middle.

Useful electromagnets

  • Doorbells — electromagnet pulls a hammer to strike a bell.
  • Electric motors — alternating fields rotate a coil between magnets (everything from fans to washing machines uses this).
  • Scrap-yard cranes — huge electromagnets lift cars, then switch off to drop them.
  • MagLev trains — levitate above tracks using powerful electromagnets, reaching 600 km/h.
  • MRI machines — superconducting electromagnets generate fields 30,000× Earth's, imaging soft tissue without radiation.

Indian context

Mumbai's local trains and Delhi Metro use electric motors driven by overhead-wire current. India's first MagLev project is being studied for the Mumbai-Pune corridor. Hospital MRI scanners are now found in every district headquarters.

An electromagnet is exactly as strong as the current flowing through it. Turn the current off and the magnetism vanishes — that's why a junkyard crane drops cars on demand. Permanent magnets can't be switched off.

Try this at home

Wind 50 turns of insulated copper wire around an iron nail. Connect briefly to a 1.5 V battery. The nail will pick up pins, paper clips, and steel nuts. Disconnect — everything drops. You have built an electromagnet.

Looking ahead

Class 10 Physics has a Magnetic Effects of Current chapter. Class 12 Physics goes deep — Ampere's law, Biot-Savart law, electromagnetic induction. JEE Advanced and NEET both ask numerical problems. Electrical engineering builds on these for motor and generator design.
