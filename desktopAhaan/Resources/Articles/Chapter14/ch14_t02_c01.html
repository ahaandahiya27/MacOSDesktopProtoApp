The Filament Bulb and Joule's Law

Chapter 14 · Topic 2 · Concept 1 · Class 7 Science

The light bulb that lit the modern world

Thomas Edison's 1879 incandescent light bulb works on a single principle: pass enough current through a thin wire, and the wire heats up so much it glows white-hot, emitting visible light. The trick is finding a wire that doesn't melt or burn.

Inside a modern incandescent bulb

  • Filament — a coiled tungsten wire ~6 micrometres thick. Tungsten because of its ultra-high melting point (3422 °C) — higher than any other metal.
  • Glass envelope — protects the filament from air, which would oxidise it instantly at high temperature.
  • Inert gas filling — argon or nitrogen (with no oxygen). Slows tungsten evaporation. Without it, the filament would last only minutes.
  • Two contact terminals at the base — connect to the circuit.
  • The filament reaches about 2,500 °C in normal use — hot enough to emit a yellow-white glow.
Only ~5% of the input electrical energy becomes visible light. The other ~95% is wasted as heat. That's why incandescent bulbs are being phased out worldwide in favour of LEDs, which convert ~25-40% of electricity to light.

Joule's Law — the equation

James Joule worked out in the 1840s that the heat produced in a resistor depends on three things:

H = I² × R × t
where H = heat (joules), I = current (amperes), R = resistance (ohms), t = time (seconds).

Notice that the dependence on I is SQUARED. Double the current, and you get FOUR TIMES the heat. Triple the current, NINE times the heat. This is why pushing too much current through a wire can be catastrophic — the heat scales quadratically.

Worked example

A bulb with resistance 240 Ω carries 0.25 A for 1 hour (3,600 s).
H = (0.25)² × 240 × 3600 = 0.0625 × 240 × 3600 = 54,000 joules = 54 kJ = ~13 kilocalories.

That's roughly the energy in a small spoonful of sugar — released continuously over the hour, lighting your room.

Why filaments fail

Over time, tungsten slowly evaporates off the white-hot filament and condenses on the cooler glass (that's the black inside-bulb stain in old bulbs). When the filament gets thin enough, it overheats locally and snaps — and the bulb 'blows'.

Try this at home

An incandescent bulb gets noticeably hot within seconds — feel the air above one safely. A modern LED bulb of equivalent brightness stays cool to the touch. Same light output; vastly different waste heat.
