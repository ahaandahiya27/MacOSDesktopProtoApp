Conductors and Insulators

Chapter 14 · Topic 1 · Concept 4

Why some things carry current and others don't

Materials are split into two big families when it comes to electricity:

  • Conductors — let electric current pass through easily. Their atoms have loose, "free" electrons that can drift when a cell pushes.
  • Insulators — block electric current. Their atoms hold their electrons tightly; no drift can happen.
A wire is conductor (copper) inside insulator (rubber/plastic). The copper carries the current; the rubber keeps it from leaking into your hand or shorting against the next wire.

Common conductors

  • Silver — the best of the common metals, used in high-end electronics contacts.
  • Copper — slightly worse than silver but vastly cheaper. The standard for household wiring.
  • Aluminium — used for high-voltage overhead transmission lines (lighter than copper).
  • Iron and steel — conductors but with more resistance.
  • Graphite — a non-metal conductor; used inside pencils and old battery cores.
  • Human body — a moderate conductor, especially when wet. This is why electric shocks are dangerous.
  • Salt water — a good conductor; pure distilled water isn't.

Common insulators

  • Rubber, PVC plastic — wrapped around wires.
  • Glass, porcelain — used in high-voltage power-pole insulators.
  • Dry wood, dry paper — insulators only when dry.
  • Air — yes, the air around us is a near-perfect insulator. That's why uninsulated overhead transmission lines work — they're suspended in air. A lightning strike is what it looks like when air's insulation suddenly breaks down.
  • Mica — used inside electric iron heating elements; it survives high temperatures and doesn't conduct.

Why this matters for safety

Three real-life consequences:

  • Wire insulation is what keeps electricity inside your appliance and away from your skin. Never touch a wire with cracked or melted insulation.
  • Your skin is a much better conductor when it's wet. Touching a plug with wet hands can lead to a serious shock — even from low-voltage household electricity.
  • The third "earth" pin in an Indian 3-pin plug is connected to a wire that runs to a metal rod buried in the ground. If a fault inside an appliance lets the live wire touch its metal body, the current rushes to ground instead of through you.

Semiconductors — the in-between

There's a third category you'll meet in Class 12: semiconductors. These are materials (silicon, germanium) that are insulators when cold and conductors when slightly impure or warm. Every transistor, every microchip, every solar cell uses semiconductors — the entire computing industry is built on this third category.

Try this at home

A homemade conductivity tester. Build a simple circuit: a 1.5 V cell, a small bulb, and two bare wire ends. Bridge the bare ends through different materials one by one — a metal spoon, a wooden pencil's body, the graphite tip of the pencil, a paper clip, a strip of rubber, a glass of tap water, a glass of salt water, a fresh lemon. Note when the bulb glows brightly, faintly, or not at all. You've made a conductivity tester for less than ₹100.

Looking ahead

In Class 10 (Electricity) the "ease of flow" idea becomes resistance (R), measured in ohms (Ω). Conductors have low R, insulators have huge R. Ohm's law V = IR ties this to the current.

In Class 12 / JEE Physics:

  • Resistivity ρ — a per-material property. R = ρL/A — resistance scales with length and inversely with cross-section.
  • Temperature dependence — metal resistivity rises with temperature; semiconductor resistivity falls.
  • Superconductors — exotic materials whose resistance drops to zero below a critical temperature. JEE asks the basic idea; advanced courses use them in MRI machines.

In Class 12 (Semiconductors) the whole "in-between" category gets its own chapter — leading to diodes, transistors, and the entire field of modern electronics. NEET is light on this but still tests basic conductor / insulator definitions and Ohm's law.
