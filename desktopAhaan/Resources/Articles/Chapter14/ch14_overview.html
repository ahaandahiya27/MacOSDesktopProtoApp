Electric Current and its Effects

Chapter 14 · Class 7 Science

What is an electric current?

An electric current is the flow of tiny charged particles called electrons through a conductor (usually a wire). It can flow only when there is an unbroken loop — a closed circuit — from one terminal of a battery, through the components, back to the other terminal.

A switch is just a controlled break in the loop. Open it → current stops. Close it → current flows again.

Series vs parallel

You can wire devices two ways:

  • Series — one path through everything. One bulb fuses → all bulbs go off. Old Christmas lights worked like this.
  • Parallel — each device has its own loop. One bulb fuses → others stay lit. House wiring is parallel.

The heating effect

When electrons squeeze through a wire that has resistance, they bump into atoms and the wire heats up. We use this in:

  • Electric heaters and toasters
  • Hair dryers
  • Electric irons
  • Incandescent bulbs (filament glows white-hot)
  • Fuses (a thin wire that melts to break a dangerous circuit)

The magnetic effect — Ørsted's discovery

In 1820, Danish scientist Hans Christian Ørsted noticed that an electric current makes a compass needle swing. Every electric motor, doorbell, electromagnet, MRI machine, and loudspeaker on Earth depends on this single observation.

Build an electromagnet

Wrap insulated wire around an iron nail; connect it to a battery. The nail becomes a magnet — but only while current flows. More turns of wire = stronger magnet. Industrial cranes use giant electromagnets to lift cars and scrap metal.

Safety first

  • Never touch a plug with wet hands.
  • Never replace a blown fuse with a thicker wire — that defeats the safety.
  • Earthing (the third pin in your plug) sends stray current safely to the ground.
  • MCBs (Miniature Circuit Breakers) trip and can be reset; fuses melt and must be replaced.
