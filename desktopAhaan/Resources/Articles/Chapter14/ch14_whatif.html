Three "What Ifs?" About Electric Current and its Effect

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if all electrons stopped flowing for 1 minute on Earth?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Everything that uses electricity would simultaneously fail. Lights, computers, phones, fridges, hospitals (life-support, MRI), traffic signals, ATMs, internet — all dark. Cars with combustion engines would still run (mechanical spark from battery — but if battery electrons stop, spark plugs wouldn't fire either, so cars die too). Even your brain uses tiny electric currents in neurons — so YOU would be unconscious. After 60 seconds when electrons resume, hospitals would race to restart patients on life-support. Civilization is electron-flow dependent in ways we barely notice.

What if 2 — What if you could create a permanent electromagnet (no current needed)?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: You CAN — kind of. By passing current through a coil around a piece of iron, the iron gets MAGNETISED. If the iron has enough 'magnetic memory' (high coercivity), it stays magnetised even after current stops — that's a permanent magnet. So electromagnets and permanent magnets aren't opposites; permanent magnets are 'fossilised electromagnets'. You can demagnetise them by heating (random thermal motion erases the alignment), hammering (mechanical shock disturbs alignment), or applying an opposing field.

What if 3 — What if your home's voltage was 24 V instead of 230 V?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Safer — you wouldn't die from touching wires. But: current needed would be 230/24 ≈ 10× larger for the same appliance. Wires would have to be 10× THICKER. Power loss in wires goes up by 100× (proportional to current squared). Indian homes would need bus-bar-sized wires. The reason for high voltage: thinner wires can carry the same power. Tradeoff: higher voltage = more dangerous but more efficient. Some countries DO use 110V (USA); India and Europe chose 230V for efficiency.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
