The Cell and the Closed Loop

Chapter 14 · Topic 1 · Concept 2

What is a cell?

A cell is a small chemical pump for electrons. Inside it, a chemical reaction produces an excess of negative charge at one end (the negative terminal, marked −) and a shortage at the other (the positive terminal, +). When you connect a wire between the two terminals, electrons flow from − to + through the wire, doing useful work along the way.

A battery is just a stack of cells connected together. A typical 9 V battery is six 1.5 V cells in series.

The four pieces of any circuit

Almost every circuit you'll ever meet has four kinds of components:

  • A source — usually a cell or a battery — that supplies the energy.
  • Conductors — usually metal wires — that carry the current from place to place.
  • A switch — a controlled gap that opens or closes the loop.
  • One or more loads — bulbs, motors, heaters, LEDs — that turn the electrical energy into something useful (light, motion, heat).

The "open" vs "closed" trick

Every switch is just a tiny break in the wire that you can open and close. When the switch is closed, the loop is complete, electrons flow, the bulb glows. When the switch is open, the loop is broken, electrons stop, the bulb goes dark. That's why we say "the switch is on" or "the switch is off" — same idea.

What drains a cell?

The chemicals inside a cell are finite. Every time electrons flow, the chemicals on the inside react and slowly get used up. When the reaction finishes, the cell can no longer push electrons — we say the cell is "dead". Rechargeable batteries reverse this chemistry when you plug them in.

Try this at home

Lemon battery. Stick a piece of clean copper wire and a galvanised (zinc-coated) iron nail into a lemon, about 2 cm apart. Connect a low-voltage LED across the two metals. The acid juice inside the lemon completes a tiny chemical cell — about 0.7 V, enough to light a small LED very faintly. (One lemon often isn't enough — try three or four in series.)

Looking ahead

In Class 10 (Electricity) the cell's "push" gets a name: potential difference or voltage (V), measured in volts. Ohm's law V = IR ties voltage, current and resistance together.

In Class 12 / JEE Physics the cell gains two new ideas:

  • EMF (ε) — the maximum voltage a cell would deliver if no current were drawn.
  • Internal resistance (r) — the cell's own resistance, which means terminal voltage V = ε − Ir is always slightly less than the EMF when current flows.

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) says the sum of voltage rises and drops around any closed loop equals zero — directly generalising the "closed loop" idea from this Class 7 article. NEET Physics tests cells, series/parallel combinations and the EMF idea regularly.
