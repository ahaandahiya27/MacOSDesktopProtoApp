What is an Electric Current?

Chapter 14 · Topic 1 · Concept 1

Current is moving charge

Every metal wire is packed with tiny negatively-charged particles called electrons. In a normal piece of copper sitting on the table, the electrons jiggle around in random directions — there's no net movement. But when you connect that wire to the two terminals of a battery, all those electrons start drifting in the same direction. That continuous stream is what we call an electric current.

One sentence: an electric current is a flow of electric charge, just like a water current is a flow of water.

How fast do electrons actually move?

Surprisingly slowly. Inside a typical wire, the average "drift speed" of an electron is only a fraction of a millimetre per second — slower than an ant crawls. But the effect travels through the wire almost at the speed of light, because all the electrons start moving at the same time, like a row of marbles in a tube. Push one in at the back, one pops out the front almost instantly.

The unit — ampere

Current is measured in amperes (A), named after André-Marie Ampère, a 19th-century French physicist. The instrument that measures it is called an ammeter, and it's always wired in series with the device (the current has to flow through the meter for it to read).

For scale:

  • A torch bulb draws about 0.3 A.
  • A phone charger pulls about 1 A from the wall.
  • An electric kettle uses about 8 A.
  • A lightning bolt is roughly 30,000 A — for a millisecond.

Why a closed loop?

Current cannot start unless there's a complete path from the battery's positive terminal, through the wires and the device, and back to the battery's negative terminal. If the path is broken anywhere — by an open switch, a snapped wire, or a missing bulb — the entire loop stops carrying current. Everything between the break and the break is dark.

Conventional current vs electron flow

Here's a quirk of history: conventional current is shown as flowing from + to − in textbooks. But electrons (the actual charge carriers in a wire) flow from − to +, because they are negatively charged. This is because Benjamin Franklin guessed the direction wrong in the 1700s, and the convention stuck. Both descriptions are correct — they just describe opposite things.

Try this at home

Build the smallest possible circuit. Take a fresh AA cell, a small bulb (the kind in a school physics kit, with two wires already attached), and a piece of insulated copper wire. Touch one bulb-wire to the + end of the cell, and the other bulb-wire to the − end via the copper wire. The bulb glows. Now move the copper wire away — it goes off. You've just opened a circuit.

Looking ahead

In Class 10 (Electricity) the relationship between current, voltage and resistance becomes Ohm's Law: V = IR. You'll calculate the current in a circuit given the cell's voltage and the resistor's resistance.

In Class 12 / JEE Physics current is defined precisely as I = dQ/dt (charge flowing per second). You'll meet:

  • Drift velocity vd = (I)/(neA), connecting macroscopic current to the microscopic electron flow.
  • Resistivity ρ and how it changes with temperature.
  • Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) — sum of currents at a node = 0 (charge is conserved).

These tools let you analyse any circuit, no matter how tangled. NEET Physics tests Ohm's law and series/parallel combinations every year.
