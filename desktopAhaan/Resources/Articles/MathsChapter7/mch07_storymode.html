Maths can feel like it lives only inside the textbook. It doesn't. Here are real situations where the ideas of "A Tale of Three Intersecting Lines" quietly do the work.

Why bridges are full of triangles

A triangle is the only polygon that can't flex: fix its three sides and the shape is locked. A four-sided frame can collapse into a slanted parallelogram, but a triangle holds firm. That's why bridges, cranes, electricity pylons and roof trusses are packed with triangles — the triangle inequality and rigidity together make them the strongest simple shape.

The two set-squares

Every geometry box has two set-squares: a 45-45-90 (isosceles right triangle) and a 30-60-90 (scalene right triangle). They're shaped to give exactly those angles for drawing — a direct everyday use of triangle classification by both sides and angles.

Shortest path is a straight line

When you cut across a park instead of walking two sides of it, you're using the triangle inequality: the direct side is shorter than the detour through a corner. This same rule defines 'distance' in everything from maps to the abstract spaces used in artificial intelligence.
