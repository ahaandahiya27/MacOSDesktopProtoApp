"What If?" — A Tale of Three Intersecting Lines

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best way to really understand an idea is to ask what if? Pick a question below, pause and think for a minute, then read one possible discussion.

What if 1 — a triangle's angles didn't add to 180°?

The question: What if you drew a triangle on a beach ball?

🧠 Think first

Measure the corners of a triangle drawn on a globe — what do they add to?

One discussion path: On a curved surface they add to more than 180°. The familiar 180° rule is true on a flat page; geometry on a sphere bends it. The 'rule' depends on the surface.

What if 2 — buildings braced with squares, not triangles?

The question: What if engineers used four-sided frames everywhere?

🧠 Think first

Push gently on a square frame of sticks — what happens?

One discussion path: A square frame collapses sideways into a slanted shape; a triangle cannot change shape at all. That rigidity is why triangles, not squares, hold up bridges and towers.

What if 3 — any three lengths made a triangle?

The question: What if 2 cm, 3 cm and 10 cm could form a triangle?

🧠 Think first

Try to draw it — can the short sides reach?

One discussion path: They can't meet — the two short sides are too short to span the long one. The triangle inequality (any two sides beat the third) is really 'the straight path is shortest' in disguise.

📝 Stretch your thinking

None of these has one 'correct' answer — they are for arguing about. Try one on a friend or a parent and see whether their discussion path differs from yours.
