Ocean acidification — CO₂ from the air dissolving in the sea

About 30% of the CO₂ humans put into the atmosphere dissolves in the oceans, forming weak carbonic acid; this is making seawater more acidic and dissolving coral reefs.

≈ 3 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Every year humans release ~40 billion tonnes of CO₂ into the air by burning coal, oil, gas, and forests. About a third of that CO₂ dissolves in the ocean — and dissolved CO₂ reacts with water to make CARBONIC ACID (H₂CO₃). The oceans have become measurably more acidic — pH dropped from ~8.2 (pre-industrial) to ~8.1 today, a 30% increase in H⁺ ions. Corals, shellfish, sea-urchins, and tiny ocean creatures called pteropods build their shells from calcium carbonate — and CARBONATE DISSOLVES IN ACID. So as oceans acidify, shell-building gets harder and harder. The Great Barrier Reef is already losing coral cover; in some regions, oyster farms have collapsed. This is climate change's chemistry twin.

The textbook version

Ocean acidification is a direct consequence of rising atmospheric CO₂. About 30% of all anthropogenic CO₂ emitted since 1750 has been absorbed by the oceans. Once in seawater, CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ — releasing protons. Surface-ocean pH has dropped from ~8.2 (pre-industrial) to ~8.1 (today) — a 30% increase in H⁺ ion concentration (pH is logarithmic). Projections to 2100 (RCP 8.5): another 0.3 unit drop. Effects: (1) Calcifying organisms (corals, molluscs, foraminifera, coccolithophores, pteropods) cannot build shells/skeletons as efficiently; some begin to DISSOLVE. (2) Coral bleaching is exacerbated. (3) Fish behaviour (sensory, predator-avoidance) is impaired at high CO₂. (4) Marine food webs disrupted. India's western coast is particularly vulnerable; CMFRI has documented declining shellfish landings in Karnataka and Kerala.

Why does this happen?

More CO₂ in the air = more CO₂ dissolves in the sea (Henry's Law). Dissolved CO₂ + water = carbonic acid + H⁺. Higher H⁺ = lower pH = more acidic. Carbonate shells (made of CaCO₃) dissolve in acid. So increasing atmospheric CO₂ directly attacks every shell-building creature in the oceans.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Coral reef bleaching + dissolving — The Great Barrier Reef has lost over 50% of its coral since 1995 due to warming AND acidification. [Weather]
  • Pacific oyster industry collapse (2008) — Washington-state oyster farms suddenly lost their larvae; the cause was acidified upwelling water dissolving the larval shells. [Kitchen]
  • Pteropod shell dissolution — Sea butterflies (pteropods) caught in Southern Ocean show visible shell damage under SEM; a base of the food chain at risk. [Animals]

Going deeper

Ocean carbonate chemistry is governed by the bicarbonate buffer system. The aragonite saturation horizon (Ω_arag = 1 below which aragonite dissolves) has risen ~200 m in the Pacific since pre-industrial times. Climate-change-driven acidification has 10× faster pH change rates than the Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM, 56 million years ago) — likely the fastest acidification in 300 million years. Mitigation strategies include reduced fossil-fuel emissions (primary), seaweed and shellfish aquaculture (sequesters CO₂), and at-scale ocean alkalinity enhancement (controversial). India's Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR-CMFRI) runs ocean acidification monitoring stations off both coasts.
