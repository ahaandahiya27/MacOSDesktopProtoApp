Three "What Ifs?" About Acids, Bases and Salts

Hypothetical questions with no single right answer. Think hard before you read the discussion.

≈ 7 min read · brain-stretcher level

The textbook tells you what is. The best science teachers also ask what if? Pick any one of the questions below. Pause. Think for at least one minute before scrolling. Then read the discussion.

What if 1 — What if your saliva were as acidic as lemon juice?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: Your teeth would dissolve within months. Tooth enamel is calcium phosphate — acid attacks it, dissolving the surface and exposing the softer dentin underneath. Real example: people who chronically vomit (eating disorders, severe pregnancy nausea) DO get enamel damage from repeated stomach acid contact. Normal saliva is slightly basic (pH 6.7–7.4) precisely because saliva needs to NEUTRALISE the food acids you eat — it's your tooth's first defender.

What if 2 — What if the ocean became as acidic as soda (pH 4)?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: The shells of every shellfish, coral, and plankton would dissolve. Coral reefs would vanish in a year. Fish that depend on those food chains would collapse. Mumbai's Arabian Sea fishing industry — which feeds millions — would face mass die-off. We're already partway: human CO₂ emissions have lowered ocean pH from 8.2 (pre-industrial) to 8.1 today — small but measurable, and shellfish farms in Goa are already noticing thinner shells. Acidification is a slow planetary crisis.

What if 3 — What if you could make a battery out of a lemon?

🧠 Think first

Pause. Write down your one-sentence guess before reading.

One discussion path: You can! Push a copper coin and a zinc nail into a lemon. The citric acid acts as electrolyte. Copper attracts electrons, zinc donates them — current flows through the lemon. A single lemon gives ~0.7 volts; connect 3 in series and you can light a tiny LED. This is exactly how every commercial battery works — two metals + an electrolyte (acid or base). Alessandro Volta invented the first battery in 1800 with the same principle but using brine-soaked paper.

💬 How to use this

These are not exam questions. The answer marked ‘one discussion path’ is one valid line of reasoning — your own may be different and still good. Practising this kind of thinking is how scientists become scientists.
