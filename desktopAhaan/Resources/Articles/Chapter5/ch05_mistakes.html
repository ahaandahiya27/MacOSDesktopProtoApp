Five Wrong Answers Class 7 Students Give

The five places almost every Class 7 student trips up on Chapter 5 — and the one-line fix for each.

≈ 5 min read · revision-tier

Examiners say the same wrong answers come back year after year. None of these mistakes are silly — they are almost right. That is exactly what makes them dangerous. Read each one, cover the answer, and try to spot what is off before peeking.

1. “All acids are dangerous and burn.”

🚫 The mistake

A common belief that's almost right — but misses the key detail.

The fix: Strong acids like sulphuric and hydrochloric burn skin, but most household acids are weak and safe to consume: citric (lemons), acetic (vinegar), lactic (curd), tartaric (tamarind), malic (apples), ascorbic (vitamin C in oranges). Your stomach makes HCl — a strong acid — but a thick mucus layer protects your stomach lining. Strength and concentration both matter; pH 6 lemonade and pH 1 pool acid are both 'acidic' but vastly different.

2. “Lemons are basic because they're alkaline forming.”

🚫 The mistake

A common belief that's almost right — but misses the key detail.

The fix: Some health blogs claim lemons are 'alkaline' because they leave an alkaline ash after digestion. That's misleading — in your mouth and stomach, lemon juice is acidic (pH ~2-3). What the body does with the remnants AFTER metabolism is a different story; calling lemons 'alkaline foods' confuses chemistry with biology. The chemistry test (litmus) is unambiguous: lemon juice turns blue litmus red.

3. “Soap is a base because it's alkaline.”

🚫 The mistake

A common belief that's almost right — but misses the key detail.

The fix: Soap is a salt — sodium stearate, made by neutralising fatty acid (from oil) with sodium hydroxide (a strong base). The reaction is called saponification. The final soap is technically neutral-to-mildly basic, but it cleans by emulsifying oil, not by being a base. The 'soapy feel' that bases share with soap is because both contain similar molecules.

4. “Indicators only work for acids and bases.”

🚫 The mistake

A common belief that's almost right — but misses the key detail.

The fix: Indicators detect any pH change. Universal indicator (Class 10) shows a full rainbow from red (very acidic) through yellow (slightly acidic) through green (neutral) to blue (basic) to purple (very basic). Even your eyes have a sort of indicator: anthocyanin pigments in red cabbage and turmeric extract are natural indicators that some Indian dyers used for centuries.

5. “Antacid medicines completely neutralise stomach acid.”

🚫 The mistake

A common belief that's almost right — but misses the key detail.

The fix: Antacids only neutralise EXCESS acid that's causing burning. Your stomach immediately replenishes the missing acid (within an hour). Too much antacid use can leave your stomach unable to kill ingested bacteria or digest protein. Doctors recommend lifestyle changes (smaller meals, fewer fried foods) over chronic antacid use — the body's acid is there for a reason.

📝 Stretch your thinking

Pick any two of these and explain them to a younger sibling using a one-line analogy each. If you can compress a mistake into one sentence with one image, you have understood it.
