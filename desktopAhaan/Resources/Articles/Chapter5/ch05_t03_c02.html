The chemistry of tooth decay

Bacteria in your mouth eat sugar and produce acids that dissolve tooth enamel (calcium phosphate) — that's how a cavity starts; brushing with fluoride toothpaste and rinsing after sweets prevent it.

≈ 3 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

After you eat anything sugary, bacteria living on your teeth feast on the sugar and produce LACTIC ACID as a by-product. This acid dissolves the calcium-phosphate enamel — the hard mineral shell of your tooth. Over weeks and months of repeated acid attacks, a microscopic hole forms; the hole grows into a CAVITY. Saliva fights back — it contains bicarbonate that neutralises acid; calcium and phosphate that re-mineralise enamel. Fluoride toothpaste swaps softer hydroxyapatite for harder fluorapatite — making teeth more resistant. Rinsing your mouth or chewing sugar-free gum after eating sweets gives saliva a fighting chance.

The textbook version

Tooth enamel is composed primarily of hydroxyapatite — Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂ — a calcium-phosphate mineral; the hardest substance in the human body. Dental caries (cavities) form through a four-step process: (1) bacteria (mainly Streptococcus mutans) colonise tooth surfaces as plaque; (2) when sugar is consumed, the bacteria ferment it to produce lactic acid; (3) the acid lowers oral pH from ~7 to ~5 within minutes, dissolving hydroxyapatite (demineralisation); (4) if acid attacks recur faster than saliva can buffer/remineralise, a cavity forms. Prevention: (a) reduce sugar frequency (not just amount — frequency matters more because each sugar pulse triggers an acid attack); (b) brush teeth with fluoride toothpaste (fluoride substitutes into apatite making fluorapatite, harder and acid-resistant); (c) rinse mouth or chew sugar-free gum after eating to boost saliva; (d) regular dental check-ups.

Why does this happen?

Teeth are made of a calcium-phosphate mineral. Sugar feeds bacteria that produce acid. Acid + mineral → dissolved mineral = cavity. Saliva fights back chemically (bicarbonate buffer) and structurally (re-deposit). Time and frequency tip the balance one way or the other.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Diwali sweets and dentist visits — Indian dentists report a measurable spike in cavity cases in the weeks after Diwali — frequent sugar exposure overwhelms enamel. [Kitchen]
  • Fluoride toothpaste — Toothpaste contains 1000-1500 ppm fluoride; daily brushing replaces softer enamel hydroxyl groups with harder fluoride. [Human body]
  • Sugar-free chewing gum — Stimulates saliva flow → buffers acid → speeds remineralisation. Studies show gum-chewing after meals reduces caries by 28%. [Kitchen]

Going deeper

Caries microbiology has shifted from a 'specific-bacteria' model to a complex 'ecological plaque hypothesis' — biofilm dysbiosis driven by frequent sugar pulses. New protective strategies include xylitol (a sugar alcohol that bacteria can't metabolise but find), arginine + calcium agents that boost alkalising bacteria, and recombinant lactoperoxidase. The WHO Global Oral Health Status Report 2022 estimates ~2.5 billion people live with untreated caries — the most prevalent disease worldwide. Indian fluoridation programmes through community salt and water reach hundreds of millions; school dental-checkup programmes target rural children.
