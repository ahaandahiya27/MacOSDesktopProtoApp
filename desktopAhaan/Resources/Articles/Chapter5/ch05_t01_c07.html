Acids and Bases in the Kitchen

How baking soda, vinegar, curd, and lemon juice work their magic in recipes

≈ 6 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Your kitchen is a chemistry lab in disguise. Every time your grandmother adds a pinch of baking soda to make fluffy dhokla, or squeezes lemon juice over dal, or uses curd to marinate paneer — she is putting acids and bases to work. Let’s explore the delicious science hiding in everyday cooking.

Why does lemon prevent browning?

When you cut an apple or banana, enzymes in the fruit react with oxygen in the air and turn the flesh brown. Citric acid from lemon juice lowers the pH on the fruit’s surface, slowing down these enzymes. That is why a squeeze of lemon keeps your fruit salad looking fresh!

The magic of baking soda

Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) is a mild base. When it reacts with an acid — like the citric acid in lemon juice or the lactic acid in curd — it produces carbon dioxide gas (CO₂). These tiny gas bubbles get trapped in the batter and make it rise, giving you soft, fluffy cakes, idlis, and dhoklas.

NaHCO₃ + Acid → Salt + Water + CO₂ (gas)

Try this at home

Put a spoonful of baking soda in a glass. Pour in some vinegar. Watch the eruption of fizzing bubbles! Those bubbles are carbon dioxide gas — the very same gas that makes your cakes rise in the oven.

Baking soda vs baking powder

Students often confuse these two. Here is the difference:

  • Baking soda is pure sodium bicarbonate (a base). It needs an acid in the recipe (like curd, lemon juice, or buttermilk) to react and produce gas.
  • Baking powder is baking soda already mixed with a dry acid (usually cream of tartar, which contains tartaric acid) and a filler like cornstarch. It produces gas on its own when water is added — no extra acid needed.

That is why recipes that use curd or lemon juice usually call for baking soda, while recipes without any acid ingredient use baking powder instead.

Remember it this way

Baking soda = needs a partner (an acid). Baking powder = already has a partner built in.

Fermentation — acids made by microbes

When you leave dosa or idli batter overnight, tiny microorganisms (bacteria and yeast) feed on the sugars in the rice and urad dal. They produce lactic acid and carbon dioxide as by-products. The acid gives the batter a slightly sour taste, and the CO₂ bubbles make it fluffy.

The same process turns milk into curd. Lactobacillus bacteria convert the lactose (milk sugar) into lactic acid, which thickens the milk and gives curd its tangy flavour.

Pickles stay fresh for months

When vegetables are soaked in vinegar or allowed to ferment, the acidic environment (low pH) prevents harmful bacteria from growing. That is why pickles, kimchi, and sauerkraut can last for months without refrigeration — the acid acts as a natural preservative.

Cleaning with acids and bases

Acids and bases are also the secret behind many kitchen cleaning tricks:

  • Vinegar (acid) dissolves hard-water stains and lime scale on taps and kettles.
  • Lemon juice (acid) removes tarnish from copper-bottom pans — rub half a lemon with a pinch of salt.
  • Baking soda (base) absorbs odours in the fridge and can scrub stubborn stains off pots.
  • Dish soap (base) cuts through grease because bases react with oils and fats.

Neutralisation in cooking

Sometimes a dish turns out too sour (acidic). What do you do? Add a pinch of baking soda (a base) to neutralise the excess acid. The sourness reduces, and tiny bubbles may appear from the CO₂ produced.

Similarly, if a dish is too bland and you want tanginess, add an acid like lemon juice or tamarind paste.

Cooking is really just applied chemistry!

Tea and lemon

Have you noticed that tea becomes lighter in colour when you add lemon juice? Tea contains tannins, which act as a natural indicator. The citric acid from the lemon changes the colour of the tannins, making the tea appear paler. Add a base like baking soda, and the tea turns darker!

Quick check — Why does dosa batter become sour after being left overnight?

Answer: Microorganisms (bacteria like Lactobacillus and yeast) in the batter feed on sugars and produce lactic acid as a by-product. This acid gives the batter its slightly sour taste.

Quick check — What is the difference between baking soda and baking powder?

Answer: Baking soda is pure sodium bicarbonate (a base) and needs an acid in the recipe to react and produce CO₂ gas. Baking powder already contains baking soda mixed with a dry acid, so it produces gas when water is added without needing an extra acid ingredient.

Quick check — How would you reduce the sourness of a dish that has too much tamarind?

Answer: Add a small pinch of baking soda (a base). It will neutralise some of the excess tartaric acid from the tamarind, reducing the sour taste.
