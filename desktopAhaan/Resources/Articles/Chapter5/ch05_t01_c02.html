Bases — The Bitter, Soapy Family

Slippery, bitter, and the perfect partner to neutralise an acid

≈ 6 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

If acids are the sour family, then bases are the bitter, soapy family. Think about the slippery feel of soap on your hands, or the bitter taste of a karela (bitter gourd). Bases are the chemical opposite of acids, and when they meet, something magical happens — they neutralise each other. Let’s dive in and discover what bases are all about.

What is a base?

A base is a substance that tastes bitter (in safe, dilute forms), feels slippery or soapy to the touch, and turns red litmus paper blue. When dissolved in water, a base releases hydroxide ions (OH−).

A base that dissolves in water is called an alkali. So all alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis. For example, copper hydroxide is a base, but it does not dissolve in water, so it is not an alkali.

Remember it this way

Alkali = a base that dissolves in water. Think of it like this: every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square. Similarly, every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali.

Bases are just as common as acids. You use them every day without realising it:

Why does soap feel slippery?

Soap is made using a strong base (sodium hydroxide). The slippery feeling you get when you rub soap between your fingers is actually a property of bases. The base reacts with the oils on your skin, creating a slippery layer.

Properties of bases

All bases share these common properties:

  • Bitter taste — safe bases like baking soda taste slightly bitter. (Never taste unknown substances!)
  • Soapy or slippery feel — bases feel slick between your fingers.
  • Turn red litmus blue — this is the classic litmus test for a base, the opposite of what acids do.
  • Conduct electricity — alkalis produce ions in water that carry electric current.
  • React with acids — bases neutralise acids to form a salt and water.
  • Corrosive — strong bases like sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) can burn skin and dissolve grease.

Strong bases are dangerous

Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), also called caustic soda, is used in drain cleaners. It is extremely corrosive and can cause severe chemical burns. Always handle strong bases with gloves and goggles in a lab setting.

Bases vs alkalis — clearing up the confusion

This is a point that confuses many students, so let’s make it crystal clear:

  • A base is any substance that can neutralise an acid.
  • An alkali is a base that dissolves in water to form a solution.

Examples of alkalis (bases that dissolve): sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide (slightly soluble).

Examples of bases that are not alkalis (they don’t dissolve): copper oxide, iron hydroxide, zinc oxide.

When your textbook says “alkaline solution,” it means a solution of a base in water. Pure water itself is neutral — neither acidic nor basic.

How bases react

With acids (neutralisation)

This is the most important reaction of bases. When a base meets an acid, they react to form a salt and water:

Base + Acid → Salt + Water

For example:

NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O

(Sodium hydroxide + Hydrochloric acid → Common salt + Water)

With metals

Some strong bases react with certain metals (like aluminium and zinc) to produce hydrogen gas:

2NaOH + Zn → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂

Whitewashing walls

When walls are whitewashed, the painter uses calcium hydroxide (slaked lime) mixed with water. Over a few days, it reacts with carbon dioxide in the air to form calcium carbonate (limestone), which gives the wall a shiny white coating. The reaction is:

Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O

Bases in your body

Your body uses bases too! Here are some examples:

  • Saliva is slightly basic, which helps neutralise acids in your mouth and protects your teeth.
  • Blood is slightly basic (pH about 7.4). Your body works hard to keep it in this narrow range.
  • Bile, produced by your liver, is basic. It neutralises the acidic food coming from your stomach into the small intestine so that intestinal enzymes can work properly.

Try this at home

Dissolve a pinch of baking soda in a small cup of water. Dip a strip of red litmus paper (or a turmeric-stained paper strip) into the solution. Notice the colour change — red litmus turns blue, and turmeric paper turns reddish-brown. That confirms baking soda is a base!

Quick check — What is the difference between a base and an alkali?

Answer: A base is any substance that can neutralise an acid. An alkali is a base that dissolves in water. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.

Quick check — What colour does red litmus paper turn in a basic solution?

Answer: Red litmus paper turns blue in a basic (alkaline) solution.

Quick check — Name two bases found in your body

Answer: Saliva (slightly basic) and bile (produced by the liver, basic enough to neutralise stomach acid in the small intestine). Blood is also slightly basic at pH 7.4.
