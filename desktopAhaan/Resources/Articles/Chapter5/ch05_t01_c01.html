Acids — The Sour Family

What makes lemons sour, vinegar tangy, and your stomach powerful

≈ 6 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Bite into a raw lemon and your face scrunches up. Taste a drop of vinegar and your tongue tingles. That sharp, sour sensation is the signature of an acid. Acids are one of the most important families of chemicals in science, and they are hiding in plain sight all around you — in fruits, in fizzy drinks, in your own stomach, and even inside the batteries that power your toys.

What is an acid?

An acid is a substance that tastes sour (in safe, dilute forms), turns blue litmus paper red, and releases hydrogen ions (H+) when dissolved in water. The word “acid” itself comes from the Latin acidus, meaning sour.

Not all acids are dangerous. Many are found in the food you eat every day. However, some acids — like the sulphuric acid inside a car battery — are extremely corrosive and must never be touched or tasted.

Safety first!

Never taste, touch, or smell an unknown chemical. The “sour taste” test only applies to foods you already know are safe, like lemon juice or curd. In a lab, always use indicators instead of your senses.

Acid in your stomach

Your stomach produces hydrochloric acid (HCl) to help digest food and kill harmful bacteria. The stomach lining has a special mucous coating that protects it from being dissolved by its own acid. Pretty clever engineering by your body!

Properties of acids

All acids share a set of common properties:

  • Sour taste — safe, edible acids taste sour (think lemon, vinegar, raw mango).
  • Turn blue litmus red — this is the classic test for an acid.
  • Conduct electricity — when dissolved in water, acids produce ions that allow electric current to flow.
  • React with metals — many acids react with metals like zinc or iron to produce hydrogen gas and a salt.
  • React with bases — acids and bases neutralise each other to form a salt and water.
  • Corrosive — strong acids can eat through metals, cloth, and even skin.

Natural acids vs manufactured acids

Natural (organic) acids

These acids come from living things — plants and animals. Citric acid from fruits, lactic acid from milk, and formic acid from ants are all natural acids. They tend to be weak acids, meaning they are not very corrosive and are generally safe in the small amounts found in food.

Mineral (inorganic) acids

These are manufactured in factories and are usually much stronger. The three most important mineral acids are:

  • Hydrochloric acid (HCl) — used in cleaning and pickling metals.
  • Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) — called the “king of chemicals”; used in batteries and fertiliser manufacturing.
  • Nitric acid (HNO₃) — used to make fertilisers and explosives.

Fizzy drinks and your teeth

Carbonated drinks contain carbonic acid, and many also have phosphoric acid or citric acid added for flavour. These acids slowly dissolve tooth enamel over time, which is one reason dentists recommend limiting fizzy drinks.

How do we know something is an acid?

Scientists never rely on taste to identify an acid. Instead, they use special substances called indicators. The simplest indicator is litmus paper:

  • Dip blue litmus paper into the liquid.
  • If the paper turns red, the substance is an acid.
  • If it stays blue, the substance is not an acid (it might be a base or neutral).

You will learn much more about indicators in the next article!

Acids and metals — a fizzy reaction

When a dilute acid is poured over a reactive metal like zinc, a chemical reaction happens. You can see tiny bubbles forming — that is hydrogen gas escaping. The general equation looks like this:

Metal + Acid → Salt + Hydrogen gas

For example:

Zinc + Hydrochloric acid → Zinc chloride + Hydrogen gas

The hydrogen gas produced can be tested by bringing a burning matchstick near the mouth of the test tube — it burns with a pop sound.

Try this at home (with an adult)

Put a small piece of an antacid tablet into a glass of vinegar. Watch the fizzing! The tablet contains a base, and the vinegar is an acid. The bubbles you see are carbon dioxide gas produced during the neutralisation reaction.

A brief history of acids

  • ~3000 BCE — Ancient Egyptians used vinegar (acetic acid) to preserve food.
  • 8th century — Jabir ibn Hayyan, an Arab alchemist, discovered hydrochloric acid and nitric acid.
  • 1661 — Robert Boyle introduced the use of indicators to test for acids and bases.
  • 1884 — Svante Arrhenius defined an acid as a substance that produces H+ ions in water.
Quick check — Name three natural acids and where they are found

Answer: (1) Citric acid — in lemons and oranges. (2) Lactic acid — in curd and yoghurt. (3) Acetic acid — in vinegar. (Other valid answers include tartaric acid in tamarind, formic acid in ant stings, and ascorbic acid in amla.)

Quick check — What happens when blue litmus paper is dipped in an acid?

Answer: The blue litmus paper turns red. This colour change is the classic test for an acidic substance.

Quick check — What gas is produced when zinc reacts with hydrochloric acid?

Answer: Hydrogen gas (H₂) is produced. It can be identified by the “pop” sound it makes when a burning matchstick is brought near it.
