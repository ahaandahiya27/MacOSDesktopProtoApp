Bases — An Overview

Slippery, bitter and the perfect antidote to acids

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

If acids are the sour side of chemistry, bases are their smooth, slippery counterpart. Run a bar of soap between your fingers — that slick feeling is a base at work. Dissolve a spoonful of baking soda in water and taste it — the faintly bitter flavour is another hallmark of a base. Bases are everywhere: in the soap that cleans your hands, the toothpaste that protects your teeth, the antacid that soothes a sour stomach, the lime that farmers spread on fields, and even the ammonia in household cleaners. In this overview we will map out Topic 2 and prepare you for a deep dive into the world of bases.

What makes something a base?

According to Arrhenius, a base is a substance that produces hydroxide ions (OH−) when dissolved in water. The hydroxide ion is a combination of one oxygen atom and one hydrogen atom carrying a negative charge. It is the “signature particle” of bases, just as H+ is the signature of acids.

For example, when sodium hydroxide (NaOH) dissolves in water:

NaOH(aq) → Na+(aq) + OH−(aq)

The OH− ions are responsible for the characteristic properties of bases: bitter taste, soapy feel, turning red litmus blue, and the ability to neutralise acids.

An important distinction: not all bases dissolve in water. Those that do are called alkalis. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and potassium hydroxide (KOH) dissolve easily, so they are alkalis. Copper hydroxide (Cu(OH)2) does not dissolve, so it is a base but not an alkali. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.

Why it works this way

The OH− ion is the mirror image of the H+ ion. Where H+ donates a proton, OH− accepts one. When an acid (H+ supplier) meets a base (OH− supplier), the two ions combine instantly:

H+ + OH− → H2O

This is the essence of neutralisation — the acid and base cancel each other out, producing water and a salt. It is one of the most fundamental reactions in chemistry and explains why antacids work, why adding lime to acidic soil helps crops grow, and why your blood has buffers to keep its pH steady.

The soapy or slippery feel of bases comes from their interaction with the oils and fats on your skin. The OH− ions begin to break down (saponify) the thin layer of natural oils on your fingertips, creating a tiny amount of soap right there on your skin. This is also why concentrated bases are dangerous — they can dissolve organic tissue just as effectively as strong acids, though by a different chemical mechanism.

Did you know?

The word “alkali” comes from the Arabic al-qali, meaning “ashes of saltwort.” Saltwort is a desert plant whose ashes are rich in sodium carbonate and potassium carbonate. Arab chemists in the Middle Ages dissolved these ashes in water and used the alkaline solution to make soap. They were among the first to manufacture solid bar soap, a technology that eventually spread to Europe and the rest of the world. So every bar of soap you use has roots in medieval Arabic chemistry!

Real-world applications

  1. Cleaning — Soaps, detergents, and household cleaners are basic; they dissolve grease and kill bacteria.
  2. Cooking — Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) is a mild base used to leaven bread and cakes.
  3. Medicine — Antacids like Milk of Magnesia (Mg(OH)2) neutralise excess stomach acid.
  4. Agriculture — Lime (calcium hydroxide) is added to acidic soil to raise pH and improve crop growth.
  5. Water treatment — Calcium hydroxide (slaked lime) is added to water to raise pH and remove impurities.
  6. Paper manufacturing — Sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) breaks down wood into pulp for making paper.
  7. Soap manufacturing — Sodium or potassium hydroxide reacts with fats in a process called saponification to produce soap.
  8. Textile industry — NaOH is used to process cotton fibres (mercerisation) to make them stronger and shinier.
  9. Drain cleaners — Concentrated NaOH or KOH dissolves hair, grease and food blockages in pipes.
  10. Dental care — Toothpaste contains mild bases to neutralise acids produced by mouth bacteria, protecting enamel.

In your kitchen right now

Pick up a box of baking soda (sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO3). Dissolve half a teaspoon in a glass of water and taste it — you will notice a faintly bitter, slightly soapy flavour. That is the taste of a base. Now rub a tiny bit of the solution between your fingers — it feels slippery. These two sensations, bitterness and slipperiness, are the sensory signatures of bases, just as sourness is the signature of acids.

Try this at home

Take a strip of turmeric-stained paper (dip white paper in turmeric water and let it dry). Now rub it against a wet bar of soap. The yellow turmeric will turn reddish-brown where it touches the soap, confirming that soap is basic. Rinse the paper under tap water with a few drops of vinegar, and the colour will shift back toward yellow as the acid neutralises the base. This is a quick, visual demonstration of the acid-base tug-of-war.

Check your understanding

Question: What is the difference between a base and an alkali?

Answer: A base is any substance that can neutralise an acid. An alkali is a base that dissolves in water, producing hydroxide ions (OH−). All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis. For example, NaOH (soluble) is an alkali, while Cu(OH)2 (insoluble) is a base but not an alkali.

Try another

Question: Why does a base feel slippery to the touch?

Answer: The OH− ions in a base react with the natural oils and fats on your skin, breaking them down in a process similar to saponification (soap-making). This produces a thin layer of soap-like substance on your skin, which feels slippery.
