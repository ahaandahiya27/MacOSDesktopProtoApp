Q1. State differences between acids and bases.

Model answer: Acids: sour to taste, turn blue litmus red, often react with metals to release hydrogen, examples are citric acid (lemon), acetic acid (vinegar), HCl (stomach). Bases: bitter to taste, soapy feel, turn red litmus blue, examples are baking soda, soap, milk of magnesia, lime. Acids and bases neutralise each other to form salt + water.

Q2. Ammonia is found in many household products such as window cleaners. It turns red litmus blue. What is its nature?

Model answer: Ammonia turns red litmus blue, which is the characteristic behaviour of a base. So ammonia is basic in nature (technically a weak base in water — it dissolves to form ammonium hydroxide).

Q3. Name the source from which litmus solution is obtained. What is the use of this solution?

Model answer: Litmus is extracted from a type of lichen — a partnership between fungi and algae that grows on rocks in cool, damp environments. The blue and red varieties are used as indicators: blue litmus turns red in acid, red litmus turns blue in base. It's the simplest acid-base test in the lab.

Q4. Is the distilled water acidic, basic, or neutral? How would you verify it?

Model answer: Distilled water is neutral — neither acidic nor basic. To verify: dip blue litmus into it (no colour change) and red litmus (no colour change). With universal indicator, distilled water shows green (the neutral colour). The chemistry of neutrality: equal numbers of H⁺ and OH⁻ ions (which you'll learn at Class 10).

Q5. Describe the process of neutralisation with the help of an example.

Model answer: Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid and a base to form a salt and water. Example: HCl (acid) + NaOH (base) → NaCl (salt) + H₂O (water). The acid's sour properties and the base's bitter properties cancel each other. Indicators show this directly — adding base to acid bit by bit changes the colour to neutral green, then to base-blue. Everyday example: ENO neutralising stomach acidity.

Q6. Mark T or F. (i) Nitric acid turns red litmus blue. (ii) Sodium hydroxide turns blue litmus red. (iii) Sodium hydroxide and HCl neutralise each other to form salt + water.

Model answer: (i) False — nitric acid is an acid; it turns BLUE litmus RED. (ii) False — sodium hydroxide is a base; it turns RED litmus BLUE. (iii) True — HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O, classic neutralisation.

Q7. Dorji has a few bottles of soft drinks. He should not drink them because they (i) taste sweet. (ii) are acidic. (iii) have CO₂ in them. (iv) all of the above.

Model answer: (iv) all of the above — but the main concern is (ii): they are acidic (carbonic acid + phosphoric acid + citric acid combined, pH 2.5-3.5, comparable to vinegar). Frequent acid exposure dissolves tooth enamel. Sugar (sweet) feeds the cavity-causing bacteria. Dissolved CO₂ makes the acid stronger. All three reasons together damage teeth.

Q8. Explain why chalk powder is sprinkled in some places like museums where antique items are kept.

Model answer: Air pollution sometimes contains sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides — both acid-forming. These gases can settle on old artefacts (especially marble and limestone), slowly dissolving them. Chalk (calcium carbonate) is a mild base; sprinkled in the museum it reacts with traces of these gaseous acids before they reach the artefact, sacrificing itself to protect the antiques. This is acid-base chemistry as a preservation technique.
