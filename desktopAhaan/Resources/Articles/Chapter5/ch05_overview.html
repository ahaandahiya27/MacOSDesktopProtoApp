Chapter 5 · Overview

Acids, Bases and Salts

The chemistry hiding in your kitchen, your stomach, and the rain

≈ 4 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Squeeze a lemon onto your tongue and you wince — that sharp sourness is an acid at work. Rub a bar of soap between your fingers and feel how slippery it is — that is a base. Mix the two together in just the right amounts, and you get a salt plus water. This simple reaction, called neutralisation, explains everything from how antacid tablets calm your tummy to why farmers spread lime on their fields.

Did you know?

The word acid comes from the Latin word acidus, meaning “sour”. The word alkali (another name for a soluble base) comes from Arabic al-qaly, meaning “ashes of plants” — because early chemists extracted bases from wood ash!

Why does this chapter matter?

Acids and bases are not just laboratory chemicals. They are in the food you eat (citric acid in oranges, acetic acid in vinegar), in your body (hydrochloric acid in your stomach), and in nature (acid rain, ocean water). Understanding them helps you make sense of cooking, medicine, farming, and even environmental science.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to test any household substance and tell whether it is acidic, basic, or neutral — and explain why it behaves that way.
