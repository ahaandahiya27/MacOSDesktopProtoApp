Two Mini Projects for Chapter 5

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🥬 Red cabbage rainbow — a natural pH indicator

~30 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • Half a red cabbage
  • A pot of water
  • A small grater or knife (adult help)
  • Six clear glasses
  • Test liquids: lemon juice, vinegar, soap water, baking soda solution, water, milk

Steps

  1. Chop the red cabbage finely.
  2. Boil it in water for 10 minutes — the water turns deep purple.
  3. Strain and cool the purple liquid; this is your indicator.
  4. Pour a little indicator into each of six glasses.
  5. Add one test substance to each glass and watch the colour change.
  6. Lemon and vinegar → pink/red. Baking soda and soap → green/blue. Milk and water → stay purple (~neutral).

👀 What you should see

A rainbow of colours across the six glasses, with strong acids (lemon) at red-pink and strong bases (soap) at green-blue.

Why it works: Red cabbage contains anthocyanin pigments that change colour depending on H⁺ concentration in solution. In acid, the anthocyanin's structure flips to absorb green light (you see pink/red). In base, it flips again to absorb red light (you see green/blue). This is exactly how universal indicators work in chemistry labs, just from your vegetable shelf.

🌋 Vinegar volcano — acid-base reaction in motion

~15 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A plastic bottle (small)
  • A few drops of dishwashing liquid
  • A few drops of red food colour
  • 2-3 tablespoons baking soda
  • 1 cup vinegar
  • A tray to contain the mess

Steps

  1. Put the bottle on the tray. Add baking soda, food colour, and dishwashing liquid into the bottle.
  2. Stand back.
  3. Pour the vinegar in quickly.
  4. Watch the foamy red 'lava' erupt out of the bottle.

👀 What you should see

A frothy red fountain erupting from the bottle as the reaction proceeds.

Why it works: Vinegar (acetic acid) + baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) → sodium acetate + water + CO₂. The CO₂ gas escapes from the liquid as bubbles. The dishwashing liquid traps the bubbles into foam (otherwise CO₂ would just escape as gas). The food colour makes the foam visible. It's a controlled, harmless display of neutralisation — same chemistry as ENO in water.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
