Why a Lemon Can Clean a Brass Lamp

A kitchen chemistry case study in acid-metal reactions

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

In many Indian households, there is a simple trick that has been passed down for generations: rub a tarnished brass lamp or vessel with half a lemon dipped in salt, and it comes out gleaming like new. No fancy chemicals, no expensive polish — just a fruit and some salt. This everyday magic is actually a beautiful example of acid chemistry at work. Let us break down exactly what is happening, molecule by molecule, and discover why a humble lemon is one of the best brass cleaners on the planet.

What is brass and why does it tarnish?

Brass is an alloy — a mixture of two metals: copper (Cu) and zinc (Zn), typically in a ratio of about 60:40. When brass is new, it has a bright golden-yellow colour. But over time, the surface reacts with oxygen, moisture, carbon dioxide and sulphur compounds in the air. This creates a dull, dark layer of tarnish.

The tarnish is mainly composed of:

  • Copper oxide (CuO) — a black compound formed when copper reacts with oxygen.
  • Copper carbonate (CuCO3) — a greenish compound formed when copper reacts with CO2 and moisture. The green patina you see on old copper roofs and statues (like the Statue of Liberty) is largely copper carbonate.
  • Zinc oxide (ZnO) — a white compound from the zinc component.

This tarnish layer is only a few micrometres thick, but it is enough to make the brass look dull, dark or greenish instead of bright and golden.

What this concept means

When you rub a lemon on tarnished brass, the citric acid (C6H8O7) in the lemon juice reacts with the metal oxides and carbonates in the tarnish layer, dissolving them. The key reactions are:

CuO + 2H+ → Cu2+ + H2O

CuCO3 + 2H+ → Cu2+ + H2O + CO2↑

The H+ ions from citric acid attack the metal compounds, breaking them apart. The copper ions (Cu2+) dissolve into the lemon juice (giving it a faint blue-green tint if you look closely), the water evaporates or is wiped away, and any CO2 escapes as gas. What remains is the fresh, untarnished brass surface underneath — bright and golden once more.

Why add salt?

The traditional method calls for dipping the lemon in common salt (sodium chloride, NaCl) before rubbing. The salt serves two purposes:

  1. Mild abrasive: The salt crystals act as a gentle scouring agent, physically scrubbing the tarnish layer and exposing more surface area for the acid to attack.
  2. Chemical boost: The chloride ions (Cl−) from the salt help dissolve copper compounds more effectively. Copper chloride (CuCl2) is highly soluble in water, so the dissolved tarnish is easily wiped away rather than redepositing on the surface.

The combination of chemical dissolution (by the acid) and physical abrasion (by the salt) makes the lemon-salt method far more effective than either one alone.

Why it works this way

This is a classic acid-base reaction in disguise. The metal oxides (CuO, ZnO) are basic oxides — they can react with acids. The carbonates (CuCO3) also react readily with acids, producing salt, water and carbon dioxide. Citric acid, though weak, provides enough H+ ions at the surface of the metal to dissolve the thin tarnish layer over a few minutes of rubbing.

If you used a strong acid like hydrochloric acid instead, the cleaning would be faster, but the acid would also attack the brass metal itself, pitting and damaging the surface. Citric acid is just right: strong enough to dissolve the tarnish but too weak (at the concentration found in lemon juice) to attack the underlying metal significantly. Nature has provided the perfect cleaning agent.

Did you know?

The famous Statue of Liberty in New York was originally the colour of a shiny copper penny. Over decades, the copper surface reacted with air, rain (slightly acidic) and sea spray to form a thick layer of copper carbonate and other compounds. This green coating is called a patina, and it actually protects the copper underneath from further corrosion. If you cleaned the Statue of Liberty with lemons, it would go back to being copper-coloured — but conservators deliberately leave the patina because it acts as a shield.

Other acids that clean metals

Lemon is not the only acid used for cleaning metals. Here are some other common examples:

  • Vinegar (acetic acid) — cleans copper pots, removes water stains from stainless steel, and dissolves limescale from taps.
  • Tamarind (tartaric acid) — traditionally used in South India to clean brass and copper vessels. A paste of tamarind pulp works much like lemon juice.
  • Tomato ketchup (citric + acetic acid) — surprisingly effective at polishing copper. The acids dissolve tarnish while the thick paste keeps the acid in contact with the surface.
  • Dilute hydrochloric acid — used industrially to “pickle” steel, removing rust and scale from large metal sheets before galvanising.
  • Phosphoric acid — the active ingredient in many commercial rust-removal products. It converts iron oxide (rust) into iron phosphate, a dark, stable coating.

Real-world applications

  1. Household brass cleaning — Lemon-salt paste is a cheap, effective and non-toxic way to polish brass lamps, door handles and decorative items.
  2. Copper vessel maintenance — In Indian kitchens, copper water pots are cleaned with tamarind or lemon to keep them bright and hygienic.
  3. Jewellery care — Mild acid solutions clean tarnished silver and copper jewellery without damaging the metal.
  4. Industrial pickling — Steel mills use acid baths to remove oxide scale from steel before coating or painting.
  5. Restoration of antiques — Conservators use carefully controlled acid treatments to clean ancient bronze and copper artefacts.
  6. Coin collecting — Collectors sometimes use gentle acid cleaning to remove corrosion from old copper coins (though purists prefer to leave the patina).
  7. Kitchen descaling — Citric acid powder is used to descale coffee machines, kettles and dishwashers.
  8. Rust removal — Phosphoric acid converts rust to a stable compound, preparing iron for painting.
  9. Dental cleaning — Mild acid etchants are used in dentistry to prepare tooth surfaces before applying fillings or braces.
  10. Archaeological cleaning — Archaeologists use dilute acids to carefully clean bronze artefacts found in excavations.

In your kitchen right now

Find a tarnished brass or copper item — a small vessel, a lamp, or even an old coin. Cut a lemon in half, dip the cut face in table salt, and rub it firmly on the tarnished surface. Within a minute you will see the dark tarnish dissolving away and the bright metal appearing underneath. Rinse with water and dry. You have just used acid chemistry to restore metal to its original shine, using nothing but a fruit and a mineral.

Try this at home

Compare three cleaning methods. Take three similar tarnished copper coins (or three equally tarnished spots on a copper vessel). Clean spot 1 with plain water and a cloth. Clean spot 2 with lemon juice alone (no salt). Clean spot 3 with lemon juice plus salt. After two minutes of rubbing each, compare the results. Spot 1 will show almost no change. Spot 2 will be somewhat brighter. Spot 3 will be the shiniest. This proves that the acid does the chemical work, the salt adds abrasion and chloride chemistry, and water alone is nearly useless against tarnish.

Check your understanding

Question: What chemical compounds make up the tarnish on a brass surface?

Answer: The tarnish is mainly composed of copper oxide (CuO), copper carbonate (CuCO3) and zinc oxide (ZnO). These form when the copper and zinc in brass react with oxygen, carbon dioxide and moisture in the air over time.

Try another

Question: Why is citric acid from a lemon better for cleaning brass than a strong acid like HCl?

Answer: Citric acid is a weak acid, so it dissolves the thin tarnish layer (metal oxides and carbonates) without attacking the underlying brass metal. A strong acid like HCl would remove the tarnish very quickly but would also corrode the brass itself, pitting and damaging the surface. Citric acid offers a gentle, controlled cleaning.
