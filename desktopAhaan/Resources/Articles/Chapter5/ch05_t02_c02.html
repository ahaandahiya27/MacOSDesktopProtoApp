Salts in everyday life

A salt is the ionic compound formed when an acid and base neutralise each other — table salt (NaCl) is just one of thousands.

≈ 2 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

When you say 'salt', you probably mean the white crystals on your roti — sodium chloride, NaCl. But chemically, every neutralisation reaction creates a different salt. Mix nitric acid with potassium hydroxide and you get potassium nitrate (used in fertilisers). Mix sulphuric acid with calcium hydroxide and you get gypsum (used in plaster). Salts are everywhere: in fertilisers, fireworks, photography chemicals, medicines, baking, and even in your sweat.

The textbook version

A salt is the ionic compound formed when the hydrogen ion of an acid is replaced by a metal ion (or another positive ion) from a base. The most familiar salt is sodium chloride (NaCl) — common table salt — formed from hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide. Other common salts include: potassium nitrate (KNO₃) used in fertilisers and fireworks, calcium sulphate (gypsum) used in plaster, ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃) used in fertilisers, copper sulphate (CuSO₄) used in pesticides. Many salts dissolve in water; some don't. Salts can be neutral, acidic, or basic depending on the strengths of the parent acid and base.

Why does this happen?

Acids release H⁺ ions; bases release OH⁻ ions and a positive metal ion. When they neutralise, H⁺ + OH⁻ become water. The leftover positive metal and negative non-metal ion pair up to form a new ionic compound — a salt.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Sea salt from evaporation pans — Coastal salt-makers in Tamil Nadu and Gujarat let seawater evaporate in shallow ponds, leaving NaCl crystals behind. The same NaCl as you sprinkle on your dal. [Weather]
  • Plaster of Paris (gypsum) — Calcium sulphate (a salt) when heated and mixed with water forms a quick-setting paste used in surgical casts, sculptures, and building decorations. [Building]
  • Copper sulphate as a fungicide — Blue copper sulphate crystals dissolved in water are sprayed on grape vines to prevent fungal disease — used worldwide as the 'Bordeaux mixture'. [Plants]

Going deeper

The chemistry of salts underpins half of modern industry. Sodium chloride is the raw material for chlor-alkali industry — electrolysis of brine produces NaOH (caustic soda), Cl₂ (chlorine gas), and H₂ (hydrogen) at industrial scale. Calcium phosphate is the salt that makes your bones rigid; hydroxyapatite, a related salt, is the main component of tooth enamel. Sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) is amphoteric — it can act as both a mild acid and a mild base, which is why it's used in everything from cooking to medicine to fire extinguishers.

Beyond the book

Mahatma Gandhi's 1930 Salt March (Dandi) protested British monopoly on salt — at the time, NaCl made by Indians from the sea without paying tax was illegal. Gandhi walked 240 miles to the coast, scooped up natural salt, and broke the law symbolically. A neutralisation product (NaCl from HCl + NaOH in seawater) became the symbol of Indian independence.
