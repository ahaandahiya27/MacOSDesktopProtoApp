The pH Scale

A numbered ruler from 0 to 14 that tells you exactly how acidic or basic something is

≈ 6 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Litmus paper can tell you whether something is an acid or a base, but it cannot tell you how strong that acid or base is. Is lemon juice more acidic than vinegar? Is baking soda more basic than soap? To answer questions like these, scientists use a special measuring tool called the pH scale.

What is pH?

The letters “pH” stand for “potential of Hydrogen.” It is a number that tells you the concentration of hydrogen ions (H+) in a solution. The pH scale runs from 0 to 14:

  • pH 0–6: Acidic (the lower the number, the stronger the acid)
  • pH 7: Neutral (neither acidic nor basic — pure water)
  • pH 8–14: Basic / alkaline (the higher the number, the stronger the base)

Think of it as a number line

Imagine a ruler going from 0 to 14. The middle (7) is neutral. Go left towards 0 and things get more and more acidic. Go right towards 14 and things get more and more basic. Simple!

How do we measure pH?

There are several ways to measure the pH of a substance:

1. Universal indicator paper (pH paper)

This is a special strip of paper soaked in a mixture of several indicators. When you dip it into a solution, it changes to a specific colour. You then compare that colour to a colour chart printed on the packet to read the pH number. This is more precise than simple litmus paper because it gives you an approximate number, not just “acid or base.”

2. Universal indicator solution

A liquid version of the above. Add a few drops to your test solution, and the colour change tells you the pH.

3. Digital pH meter

Scientists and industries use electronic devices called pH meters. You dip the probe into the liquid, and the screen displays the exact pH value (like 4.6 or 8.2). This is the most accurate method.

pH meters in daily life

Swimming pool managers use pH meters every day to check that the pool water stays between pH 7.2 and 7.8. If it goes too low (acidic), it can irritate swimmers' eyes and skin. If it goes too high (basic), the chlorine disinfectant stops working properly.

Why does pH matter?

pH is not just a number on a chart. It has real consequences:

  • Your body: Blood must stay at pH 7.35–7.45. Even a small change can be life-threatening.
  • Farming: Most crops grow best in soil with a pH between 6 and 7.5. Farmers test and adjust soil pH regularly.
  • Aquatic life: Fish need water between pH 6.5 and 8.5 to survive. Acid rain can lower lake pH and kill fish.
  • Digestion: Different parts of your digestive system have different pH levels — the stomach is very acidic (pH 1–2), while the small intestine is mildly basic (pH 7.5–8.5) so that different enzymes can work at their best.
  • Tooth decay: When pH in the mouth drops below 5.5, tooth enamel begins to dissolve.

Each step is ten times stronger!

The pH scale is logarithmic. This means that a substance with pH 3 is not just “a little more acidic” than one with pH 4 — it is ten times more acidic! And pH 2 is a hundred times more acidic than pH 4. That is why battery acid (pH 0) is so incredibly dangerous compared to lemon juice (pH 2).

Neutral substances

A substance with pH exactly 7 is called neutral. It is neither acidic nor basic. Pure water is the best example of a neutral substance. However, most tap water is not perfectly neutral — it may be slightly acidic or slightly basic depending on the minerals dissolved in it.

Other roughly neutral substances include sugar solution and cooking oil (though oil does not dissolve in water, so pH testing does not really apply to it).

Try this at home

If you can get universal indicator paper (pH strips) from a pharmacy or online, test the pH of common household substances: lemon juice, tap water, baking soda solution, vinegar, milk, soapy water. Record the colours and pH numbers in a table. You will be amazed at the range!

Quick check — What pH value is neutral?

Answer: pH 7 is neutral. Pure water has a pH of 7.

Quick check — A solution has a pH of 3. Is it acidic or basic? Is it strong or weak?

Answer: It is acidic (any pH below 7 is acidic). Since it is close to 0, it is a fairly strong acid. Examples include vinegar and orange juice.

Quick check — Why is pH important for farmers?

Answer: Most crops grow best in soil with a pH between 6 and 7.5. If the soil is too acidic or too basic, plants cannot absorb nutrients properly and may fail to grow. Farmers test and adjust soil pH by adding lime (to reduce acidity) or gypsum (to reduce alkalinity).
