Acids in industry — sulphuric, hydrochloric, nitric

Three strong acids do most of the world's industrial work: sulphuric makes fertilisers and refines petroleum, hydrochloric cleans metals, nitric makes explosives and dyes.

≈ 3 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Industrial chemistry runs on three workhorse acids. SULPHURIC ACID (H₂SO₄) is the most-produced chemical on Earth — over 250 million tonnes a year. It's used to make fertilisers (super-phosphate for crops), refine petroleum into petrol and diesel, clean rust off metal, and inside every car battery. HYDROCHLORIC ACID (HCl) cleans metals before they're painted or coated, processes leather, and pickles food in the kitchen. NITRIC ACID (HNO₃) makes explosives, dyes, and fertilisers. India's biggest fertiliser plants in Kanpur and Trombay run rivers of sulphuric and nitric acid 24 hours a day — invisible chemistry behind every kilogram of food on your plate.

The textbook version

Three strong mineral acids dominate industrial chemistry: (1) Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) — over 250 million tonnes produced globally per year (largest of any chemical). Made by the Contact Process from sulphur → SO₂ → SO₃ → H₂SO₄. Uses: phosphate fertiliser manufacture (60% of consumption), petroleum refining, metal pickling, lead-acid car batteries. (2) Hydrochloric acid (HCl) — produced by direct chlorination of hydrogen or as a by-product of organic chlorination. Uses: metal cleaning (descaling, pickling), food processing (gelatine, corn syrup), leather processing. (3) Nitric acid (HNO₃) — made from ammonia via the Ostwald process. Uses: fertilisers (ammonium nitrate), explosives (TNT, dynamite), dyes, plastics. India is the world's 3rd-largest sulphuric-acid producer; key plants in Trombay, Sindri, Jamshedpur.

Why does this happen?

Strong acids cheaply make positively-charged H⁺ ions available in huge quantities. H⁺ ions react with almost everything — metals, oxides, organic compounds, biological tissue. Industries exploit this for cleaning, reacting, refining. Cheap, plentiful, versatile — the workhorse trio runs the chemical economy.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Lead-acid car battery — Every internal-combustion-engine car contains ~5 L of dilute sulphuric acid inside its battery; the chemistry inside is reversible (charging vs discharging). [Travel]
  • Kanpur leather pickling — Massive HCl vats clean and prepare hides at India's leather-belt factories — the same process Roman tanners used 2000 years ago. [Kitchen]
  • Diwali fireworks — Nitric-acid-derived nitrates are the oxidisers in fireworks shells that send sparks high into the sky. [Weather]

Going deeper

Industrial acid chemistry hinges on equilibrium and economics. The Contact Process for H₂SO₄ operates the SO₂ → SO₃ step at 400-450 °C with V₂O₅ catalyst — a temperature compromise between thermodynamic favourability (Le Chatelier: exothermic so lower-T favoured) and kinetic rate (higher T = faster). Green-chemistry initiatives are reducing acid intensity: the Solvay process for soda ash uses less acid than the older Leblanc process; bio-based succinic acid is replacing petrochemical maleic anhydride. Acid-rain mitigation requires capturing SO₂ from coal-fired plants before it oxidises to H₂SO₄ in the atmosphere.
