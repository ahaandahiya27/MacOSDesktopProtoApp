Why It Rains in Cherrapunji and Not in Jaisalmer

Two Indian towns, two extreme climates — and the science that explains the difference

~8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

India is a land of astonishing contrasts. In the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, the village of Mawsynram (near Cherrapunji) receives over 11,000 millimetres of rain per year — making it the wettest place on Earth. Meanwhile, 2,500 kilometres to the west, Jaisalmer in the Thar Desert of Rajasthan receives barely 170 millimetres — less rain in an entire year than Cherrapunji can get in a single day. Both places are in the same country, at roughly the same latitude, under the same Sun. So why is one drenched while the other is parched? The answer lies in geography, wind patterns and the mechanics of the Indian monsoon.

What this concept means

The dramatic difference in rainfall between Cherrapunji and Jaisalmer is a textbook example of how geography shapes weather and climate. Three factors are at play: the source of moisture, the path of monsoon winds and the role of mountains.

Moisture source — the Bay of Bengal: During the southwest monsoon season (June to September), warm, moist winds blow from the Bay of Bengal towards the Indian subcontinent. These winds carry enormous quantities of water vapour picked up from the warm tropical ocean. Cherrapunji sits in the direct path of these moisture-laden winds. Jaisalmer, deep in the interior of northwestern India, is far from any major water body and receives the monsoon winds only after they have already lost most of their moisture over the Gangetic plain.

Orographic lift — the Khasi Hills: The Khasi Hills of Meghalaya rise steeply from the flat plains of Bangladesh to the south. When the moist Bay of Bengal winds hit these hills, they are forced upward rapidly. As the air rises, it expands and cools. Cool air cannot hold as much moisture as warm air, so the water vapour condenses into clouds and falls as torrential rain. This process — rain caused by air being forced over mountains — is called orographic rainfall. Cherrapunji sits in a funnel-shaped valley on the southern slopes of the Khasi Hills, perfectly positioned to trap and intensify this effect.

Rain shadow — the Thar Desert: After crossing the Western Ghats and the Aravalli range, the monsoon winds have lost most of their moisture. Jaisalmer lies in the rain shadow — the dry side of the mountains. Additionally, the Aravalli range in Rajasthan runs parallel to the direction of the monsoon winds rather than perpendicular, so it fails to block or lift the winds effectively. The result: Jaisalmer receives very little monsoon rain.

Why it works this way

The physics behind this contrast boils down to one principle: warm air holds more moisture than cold air. When the moisture-laden monsoon winds from the Bay of Bengal are forced upward by the Khasi Hills, the air cools at a rate of about 6.5 degrees Celsius per kilometre (the environmental lapse rate). As it cools, it reaches its dew point and dumps its moisture as rain — sometimes over 1,000 millimetres in a single day at Cherrapunji.

Once the air has crossed the mountains, it has lost most of its water. As it descends on the other side, it actually warms up (compressed by higher pressure at lower altitudes), becoming even drier. This is the rain shadow effect, and it explains why areas on the leeward (downwind) side of mountains are often arid.

Jaisalmer suffers a double disadvantage. First, it is far from the moisture source (the ocean). Second, the Aravalli hills do not effectively intercept the monsoon winds because of their orientation. The winds slide past rather than being forced upward. Combine these factors with intense heating of the desert surface (which can push surface temperatures above 50 degrees Celsius in summer), and you get an extremely hot, dry climate with scarce, unreliable rainfall.

The contrast is so extreme that Cherrapunji, despite being among the wettest places on Earth, actually faces water shortages in the dry season (November to February) because its steep slopes cause rainwater to run off quickly rather than being stored. Jaisalmer, meanwhile, has developed ingenious water-harvesting systems — stepwells, tankas (underground cisterns) and johads (earthen dams) — to capture and store every precious drop. Geography creates the problem; human ingenuity provides the solution.

Did you know?

In July 1861, Cherrapunji recorded 9,300 millimetres of rain in a single month — roughly 54 times the total annual rainfall of Jaisalmer, all in 31 days. The nearby village of Mawsynram now holds the official record for highest average annual rainfall (about 11,872 mm), edging out Cherrapunji. Both villages are within 15 kilometres of each other in the Khasi Hills.

Real-world applications

  1. Dam placement — Engineers build dams on the windward (rainy) side of mountains where rivers carry abundant water from orographic rain.
  2. Crop selection — Meghalaya grows tea, rice and oranges (water-loving crops); Jaisalmer grows millet and cluster beans (drought-resistant crops).
  3. Water harvesting — Rajasthan's traditional rainwater harvesting systems (tankas, johads, baoris) are models of sustainability studied worldwide.
  4. Reforestation — Planting trees on hillsides can enhance orographic rainfall and reduce soil erosion in high-rainfall areas.
  5. Tourism — Cherrapunji attracts visitors with its living root bridges and waterfalls; Jaisalmer draws tourists with its golden fort and desert safaris.
  6. Urban planning — Cities in rain shadow zones need different infrastructure (water pipelines, desalination) than those in high-rainfall zones (drainage, flood barriers).
  7. Military geography — Understanding rain patterns helps armies plan operations in different terrains across India.
  8. Renewable energy — High-rainfall areas have hydroelectric potential; dry, sunny areas like Jaisalmer are ideal for solar farms.
  9. Climate education — The Cherrapunji-Jaisalmer contrast is used globally as a classic teaching example of orographic rainfall and rain shadows.
  10. Disaster management — Flash floods in Meghalaya and drought in Rajasthan both require distinct disaster-response strategies shaped by understanding local rainfall patterns.

In your kitchen right now

Imagine blowing gently across the top of a hot cup of tea. The steam (moist air) moves horizontally. Now hold a cold spoon in the path of the steam. Droplets immediately form on the spoon — the cold surface forces the moist air to cool and condense, just as the Khasi Hills force monsoon winds to rise, cool and drop their rain. The spoon is your personal mountain range, creating a tiny orographic rainfall event right above your tea cup.

Try this at home

Model orographic rainfall. Place a mound of clay or a small hill-shaped object on a tray. On one side (the "ocean" side), place a shallow dish of hot water. Cover the whole setup loosely with cling film. Watch as the warm, moist air from the hot water rises, hits the "mountain," cools, and forms droplets on the cling film on the windward side. The leeward side stays dry. You have modelled exactly what happens at Cherrapunji and Jaisalmer.

Check your understanding

Question: What is orographic rainfall?

Answer: Orographic rainfall occurs when moist air is forced to rise over a mountain or hill. As the air rises, it expands and cools. When it cools below its dew point, water vapour condenses into clouds and falls as rain on the windward (uphill) side of the mountain. The leeward (downhill) side receives much less rain, creating a rain shadow. This mechanism explains the heavy rainfall at Cherrapunji and the dryness of areas beyond the Khasi Hills.

Try another

Question: Why does Cherrapunji sometimes face water shortages despite being one of the wettest places on Earth?

Answer: Cherrapunji's rainfall is highly seasonal — most of it falls during the monsoon months (June to September). The village sits on steep slopes, so rainwater runs off quickly rather than soaking into the ground. During the dry season (November to February), there is very little rain and the steep terrain offers few natural storage sites. Deforestation has worsened runoff. As a result, despite receiving enormous annual rainfall, the village can struggle with water availability for several months each year.
