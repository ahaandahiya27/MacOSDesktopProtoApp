Story Mode — Weather, Climate and Adaptations of Animals to Climate in Five Scenes

5 short scenes from real life that bring this chapter to a kitchen table, a garden, a classroom — somewhere you can picture.

≈ 10 min read · narrative-tier

Textbook examples can feel abstract. These five scenes don't happen in a textbook — they happen in places you go. Read them in order; each scene tightens the chapter's big idea by another notch.

Scene 1 — Mawsynram, Meghalaya — the wettest place on Earth

Mawsynram in Meghalaya gets about 11,800 mm of rain a year (12 metres — taller than a 4-storey building). The reason: monsoon clouds from the Bay of Bengal hit the Khasi Hills and dump everything in one funnel-shaped basin. The same air, going 500 km north, leaves the Brahmaputra valley relatively dry. Same monsoon, different geography, vastly different climate. The kids in Mawsynram carry knups (cane umbrellas) all year.

Scene 2 — Snow leopard in the Hemis range, Ladakh

At 4,500 m altitude in Ladakh's Hemis National Park, temperatures drop to -30°C in winter. The snow leopard's adaptations: thick fur (10 cm long, double-layered), wide paw pads that act like snowshoes, a long tail it wraps around its body for warmth, large nasal cavities that warm cold air before it reaches the lungs. Each adaptation traces to a specific climate problem. Class 7 evolution by climate, in one cat.

Scene 3 — Siberian cranes flying 5000 km to Bharatpur

Every November, Siberian cranes leave their breeding grounds near the Arctic Circle and fly to Keoladeo National Park (Bharatpur) in Rajasthan. They cover 5,000 km in a few weeks, flying at 30-50 km/h, often in V-formation to save energy. They winter in India's marshy wetlands (when Siberia freezes solid), then fly back in March. The Sarus crane, India's tallest flying bird, doesn't migrate but stays year-round in the same Bharatpur wetlands. Two strategies, same wetland.

Scene 4 — Coral bleaching in Lakshadweep's reefs

Lakshadweep's coral reefs once held the most colourful coral in the Indian Ocean. In 2016 and again in 2020, sea temperatures around the islands rose just 2°C above normal for several months. The tiny algae living inside coral got stressed and were expelled. The coral lost its colour (the algae provided it) AND its main food source (the algae provided 90% of coral nutrition). About 70% of Lakshadweep coral died in those events. Climate change isn't a future problem — it's reshaping Indian waters right now.

Scene 5 — Why Mumbai and Delhi have such different climates

Mumbai (19°N, on the Arabian Sea) and Delhi (28°N, deep inland) are different in two ways. (1) Mumbai is coastal — the sea moderates everything. Mumbai's January-July range: 18-30°C. Delhi's: 7-45°C. (2) Mumbai gets the monsoon directly off the sea (2200 mm/year). Delhi gets the spent monsoon (800 mm/year, mostly in 3 months). Latitude alone doesn't explain climate; proximity to water and altitude do most of the work.

💬 What this is for

Concrete examples are how your brain anchors abstract ideas. Pick the scene you remember best after reading. The next time you see it in real life, you'll remember the chapter.
