Chapter 7 · Overview

Weather, Climate and Adaptations of Animals to Climate

How the atmosphere shapes life on Earth — and how life shapes itself in return

~8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Step outside your door and you are instantly wrapped in the atmosphere — a swirling, restless ocean of air that can be blazing hot at noon and surprisingly chilly by evening. This chapter is about that ever-changing blanket of air we call weather, the long-term patterns it settles into that we call climate, and the extraordinary ways living creatures have learned to survive whatever the sky throws at them. From the dripping canopy of a tropical rainforest to the frozen silence of the Arctic, every organism carries the story of its climate in its body. Let us begin that story.

A timeline of discovery

Humans have watched the sky for millennia, but our scientific understanding grew in leaps. The timeline below traces the key milestones that turned sky-watching into modern climate science.

Aristotle (c. 350 BC) wrote Meteorologica, the first systematic book about weather. He tried to explain rain, wind, thunder and lightning using logic rather than myth. Many of his ideas were wrong — he thought comets were atmospheric phenomena — but the attempt to reason about weather was revolutionary.

Galileo Galilei (1592) invented the thermoscope, an early thermometer that showed changes in temperature by the rise and fall of water in a glass tube. For the first time, humans could measure warmth rather than just feel it. Within decades, weather stations began recording temperature data that we still use today.

Sir Francis Beaufort (1805) created his famous wind scale while serving in the British Royal Navy. The Beaufort Scale ranks wind from 0 (calm) to 12 (hurricane-force). Sailors could now describe wind strength consistently across the globe, making weather reports far more useful.

John Tyndall (1859) demonstrated in his London laboratory that certain gases — carbon dioxide, water vapour, methane — absorb heat radiation. He was the first to explain why the Earth is warmer than bare sunlight alone would predict. This was the birth of greenhouse-gas science.

Charles David Keeling (1958) began precise measurements of atmospheric carbon dioxide at the Mauna Loa observatory in Hawaii. His graph, now called the Keeling Curve, showed an unmistakable upward trend. It became one of the most important datasets in all of science.

IPCC (1988) — The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change was established by the United Nations to assess the science of climate change. Thousands of scientists contribute to its reports, which summarise what we know about how and why the climate is changing.

Paris Agreement (2015) — Representatives from 196 nations agreed to limit global warming to well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels. For the first time, nearly every country on Earth committed to reducing greenhouse-gas emissions.

What this chapter covers

This chapter is divided into three major topics. Topic 1: Weather explores day-to-day atmospheric conditions — temperature, humidity, wind, clouds, and precipitation. You will learn how weather instruments work, why storms form, and what separates weather from climate. Topic 2: Adaptations dives into the remarkable features organisms develop to survive their climate — thick fur for polar bears, humps full of fat for camels, drip-tip leaves in rainforests, and the epic migrations that carry birds across continents. Topic 3: Big Climate Stories zooms out to examine large-scale phenomena: the Indian monsoon, global climate change, and the surprisingly varied microclimates that exist right in your own neighbourhood.

Why it works this way

Everything in this chapter connects to one central idea: energy from the Sun drives weather, weather over time creates climate, and climate sculpts life. The Sun heats the Earth unevenly — the equator receives more energy than the poles, land heats faster than water, mountains force air upwards. These differences create wind, rain, and seasons. Over millions of years, organisms that cope better with their local climate survive and reproduce, passing on helpful traits. That is natural selection at work, and it explains the stunning variety of body plans, behaviours and survival tricks you will meet in these articles.

Did you know?

The atmosphere weighs about 5.15 quadrillion tonnes — roughly 5,150,000,000,000,000 tonnes — yet it is thinner, relative to the Earth, than the skin on an apple. Almost all weather happens in the lowest layer, the troposphere, which extends only about 12 kilometres above sea level. Commercial aircraft cruise near the top of this layer, sometimes flying above the clouds.

Real-world applications

  1. Agriculture planning — Farmers decide when to sow, irrigate and harvest based on weather forecasts and climate records for their region.
  2. Disaster preparedness — Cyclone warnings save thousands of lives each year along India's coasts by giving people time to evacuate.
  3. Urban design — City planners study microclimates to decide where to plant trees, build shade structures and position ventilation corridors.
  4. Clothing and textile industry — Woollen mills thrive in cold-climate regions; cotton production follows hot, sunny belts.
  5. Renewable energy — Wind farms are placed where climate data shows consistent, strong winds; solar panels go where sunshine hours are greatest.
  6. Aviation — Pilots use weather data every single flight to choose altitude, route and fuel load, avoiding turbulence and storms.
  7. Wildlife conservation — Understanding adaptations helps biologists protect endangered species by preserving the specific habitats they need.
  8. Medicine and public health — Heatwave alerts and cold-wave warnings help hospitals prepare for surges in weather-related illness.
  9. Tourism — Holiday destinations market themselves by climate: Goa's sunny winters, Shimla's cool summers, Leh's dry mountain air.
  10. International policy — Climate science informs treaties like the Paris Agreement, shaping laws that affect every industry on the planet.

In your kitchen right now

Open your refrigerator. Inside, the temperature is about 4 degrees Celsius — roughly the average temperature of a chilly hill station. Food stays fresh because cold slows down bacterial growth. Your fridge is, in a sense, a tiny climate zone. The Arctic works the same way: low temperatures preserve things. Explorers have found centuries-old food caches in the permafrost, still remarkably intact. Climate controls decay, in your kitchen and at the top of the world.

Try this at home

Make a weather diary. For one week, step outside at the same time every day and record: (1) temperature (use a kitchen thermometer hung in the shade), (2) whether the sky is clear, partly cloudy or overcast, (3) wind — calm, light breeze or strong, (4) any precipitation. At the end of the week, look for patterns. Was the morning always cooler? Did cloudy days feel different from sunny ones? You have just done what meteorologists do — only they use fancier instruments and keep records for decades.

Check your understanding

Question: What is the key difference between weather and climate?

Answer: Weather describes the atmospheric conditions at a specific place over a short period (hours to days), while climate is the average pattern of weather measured over a long period, typically 30 years or more. Weather can change in minutes; climate changes over decades or centuries.

Try another

Question: Why is the Keeling Curve considered one of the most important graphs in science?

Answer: The Keeling Curve shows a continuous, precise record of atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration since 1958. It clearly demonstrates that CO2 levels are rising year after year, providing direct evidence that human activities (mainly burning fossil fuels) are increasing greenhouse gases. This data underpins much of modern climate science and policy.
