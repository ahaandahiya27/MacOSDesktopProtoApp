Coral bleaching — when oceans get too warm

Corals normally host colourful algae that feed them in exchange for shelter; warm seawater stresses corals into expelling the algae, leaving them ghost-white and starving — that's coral bleaching.

≈ 3 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Coral reefs look like underwater rainforests — colourful, alive with fish. But the colour isn't from the corals themselves. Tiny algae called ZOOXANTHELLAE live INSIDE the coral animals. The algae photosynthesise and feed the coral; the coral provides shelter and minerals. A perfect partnership. But when seawater gets too warm, the corals STRESS and EXPEL their algae. Now there's no colour and no food. The white skeleton beneath is exposed — the coral has 'bleached'. If conditions return to normal in a few weeks, corals can take up new algae and recover. If the heat lasts longer, they die. The Great Barrier Reef has lost over 50% of its coral cover since 1995 due to repeated bleaching events.

The textbook version

Reef-building (scleractinian) corals are animals (cnidarians) in symbiosis with single-celled photosynthetic algae called zooxanthellae (Symbiodinium spp.). The algae live inside coral tissue, photosynthesise sunlight, and provide >90% of the coral's energy needs; the coral supplies inorganic nutrients (CO₂, nitrogen waste). When seawater temperature exceeds the corals' tolerance threshold (typically 1-2 °C above local summer maximum) for several weeks, oxidative stress damages the photosynthetic machinery of zooxanthellae. The coral expels them as a defence. Without algae, the coral animal is largely transparent — revealing the white CaCO₃ skeleton beneath = 'bleached'. Recovery is possible if the algae return within ~4-6 weeks. Prolonged bleaching causes coral starvation and death. Major mass-bleaching events: 1998, 2010, 2014-2017, 2020-2024 — driven by El Niño and underlying ocean warming.

Why does this happen?

Corals depend on symbiotic algae for most of their food. The algae depend on cool, clear water. Warm water kills the algae's photosynthesis, the coral expels them, and the partnership collapses. Without intervention, both partners die.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Great Barrier Reef bleaching events — Five mass-bleaching events since 1998 have left over half the GBR coral cover dead. [Weather]
  • Lakshadweep reef monitoring — Indian researchers track coral cover quarterly; the 1998 El Niño killed nearly 80% of Lakshadweep corals; partial recovery since. [Weather]
  • Aquarium coral keepers — Hobbyists know to keep saltwater aquaria below ~28 °C — any higher and their corals start to lose colour. [Kitchen]

Going deeper

Coral-thermal physiology is being studied at molecular detail: heat-shock proteins in zooxanthellae break down above thermal thresholds. Some Symbiodinium clades (Durusdinium) are more heat-tolerant than others (Cladocopium); assisted-evolution research is breeding 'super-corals' with heat-tolerant symbionts. India's Lakshadweep and Andaman & Nicobar reefs have experienced repeated bleaching; CMFRI tracks coral health via satellite SST + diver surveys. The IPCC AR6 projects that 70-90% of coral reefs will be lost at 1.5 °C of warming; 99% at 2 °C — making coral the canary in the climate-change coal mine.

Beyond the book

In Class 12 Biology 'Biodiversity and Conservation' coral reefs are listed as the most diverse marine ecosystems. NEET Biology asks about coral symbiosis. UPSC Environment covers Indian reef status and global climate hotspots. JEE Earth Sciences uses bleaching threshold modelling as a coupled ocean-climate problem.
