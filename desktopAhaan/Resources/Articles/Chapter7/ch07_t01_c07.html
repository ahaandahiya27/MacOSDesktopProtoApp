Extreme Weather Events — Cyclones, Droughts, Floods

When weather turns dangerous and how we can be prepared

≈ 7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Most days, weather is ordinary — a bit of sun, maybe some rain. But sometimes, weather becomes extreme and dangerous. Cyclones tear through coastal cities, droughts parch farmland for months, and floods submerge entire villages. Understanding these events is the first step to staying safe and building a more resilient world.

Cyclones — Storms from the Sea

A cyclone (called a hurricane or typhoon in other parts of the world) is a massive spinning storm that forms over warm ocean water. Here is how it develops:

  1. Warm ocean water (above 27 °C) evaporates rapidly, sending huge amounts of water vapour into the air.
  2. The rising moist air cools and condenses, releasing heat energy that makes the air rise even faster.
  3. Earth's rotation causes the rising air to spin (the Coriolis effect).
  4. A low-pressure centre, called the eye, forms at the middle. The eye is calm, but the surrounding eyewall has the most violent winds.
  5. The cyclone moves across the ocean and can strike coastal areas with devastating force.

Cyclone Effects

  • Strong winds — up to 250 km/h or more, destroying buildings and uprooting trees.
  • Storm surge — the sea rises several metres above normal, flooding coastal areas.
  • Heavy rain — causing inland flooding even far from the coast.

Cyclones in India

India's eastern coast (Bay of Bengal) is especially vulnerable to cyclones. The 1999 Odisha super cyclone killed over 10,000 people. Since then, India has dramatically improved its early warning systems. When Cyclone Fani hit Odisha in 2019, over 1.2 million people were evacuated in advance, and the death toll was kept below 90 — a remarkable achievement.

Droughts — When Rain Fails

A drought occurs when a region receives significantly less rainfall than normal for an extended period — weeks, months, or even years. Unlike cyclones and floods, droughts develop slowly, but they can be just as destructive.

Causes of Droughts

  • Weak or delayed monsoon: If the southwest monsoon does not arrive on time or is weaker than usual, rainfall drops sharply.
  • El Nino effect: Changes in Pacific Ocean temperatures can weaken the Indian monsoon.
  • Deforestation: Fewer trees mean less transpiration and less moisture recycled into the atmosphere.
  • Over-extraction of groundwater: When people pump out more water than nature can replenish.

Effects of Droughts

  • Crops fail and food prices rise.
  • Water sources (wells, reservoirs, rivers) dry up.
  • Animals and livestock suffer from lack of water and food.
  • People may have to migrate to other areas in search of water.

Did You Know?

India experienced severe droughts in 2002, 2009, and 2015 when the monsoon was weak. The 2015 drought affected about 330 million people across 10 states. Drought is one of the biggest threats to India's food security.

Floods — When There Is Too Much Water

A flood occurs when water overflows from rivers, lakes, or the sea onto normally dry land. While droughts mean too little water, floods mean too much, too fast.

Causes of Floods

  • Heavy monsoon rain: When intense rainfall exceeds a river's capacity.
  • Cyclone-related rain and storm surge: Coastal flooding during cyclones.
  • Dam failure: If a dam breaks, the stored water rushes downstream.
  • Urbanisation: Concrete and tarmac prevent water from soaking into the ground, increasing surface runoff.
  • Deforestation: Tree roots hold soil and absorb water; without them, water runs off faster.

Effects of Floods

  • Homes, schools, and hospitals are submerged or destroyed.
  • Crops are ruined, causing food shortages.
  • Contaminated water spreads diseases like cholera and dysentery.
  • Roads and bridges are washed away, cutting off communities.

Mumbai Floods

On 26 July 2005, Mumbai received 944 mm of rain in a single day — more than many cities get in an entire year. The entire city was paralysed. Over 1,000 people died, and thousands were stranded. This event highlighted the dangers of poor drainage and unplanned urbanisation in a city prone to heavy monsoon rain.

Onset Sudden (hours to days) Gradual (weeks to months) Sudden to gradual

Being Prepared

We cannot prevent extreme weather, but we can prepare for it:

  • Early warning systems: The IMD and NDMA (National Disaster Management Authority) issue alerts for cyclones, floods, and heatwaves.
  • Evacuation plans: Coastal communities should know where to go when a cyclone warning is issued.
  • Rainwater harvesting: Storing monsoon water helps during droughts.
  • Flood-resistant construction: Elevated buildings, better drainage, and wetland preservation reduce flood damage.
  • Afforestation: Planting trees helps absorb rainwater and reduces both flood runoff and drought risk.

What You Can Do

Learn about the weather risks in your area. Does your city flood during the monsoon? Is your region drought-prone? Talk to your family about an emergency plan. Know where the nearest shelter is and keep an emergency kit with water, food, a torch, and important documents.

Test Yourself

Q1: What is the calm centre of a cyclone called?

Answer: The eye of the cyclone.

Q2: Give two human activities that can make floods worse.

Answer: Urbanisation (concrete surfaces prevent water absorption) and deforestation (removes trees that absorb rainwater).

Q3: Why do droughts often follow a weak monsoon?

Answer: Most of India depends on monsoon rain for its water supply. If the monsoon brings less rain than usual, reservoirs, rivers, and groundwater are not adequately recharged, leading to water scarcity.
