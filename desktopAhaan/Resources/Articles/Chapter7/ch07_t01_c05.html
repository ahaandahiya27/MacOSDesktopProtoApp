The Water Cycle — Evaporation, Condensation, Precipitation

The endless journey of every water molecule on Earth

~8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

The water in your glass might once have been part of a glacier that covered Scandinavia 10,000 years ago. Before that, it might have been sweat on the brow of a dinosaur. Water is never created or destroyed on Earth — it simply moves, endlessly, between the oceans, the atmosphere, the land, rivers, glaciers and living organisms. This grand circulation is called the water cycle (also known as the hydrological cycle), and it is the engine that connects every weather event you will ever experience. Without the water cycle, there would be no rain, no rivers, no fresh water — and no life as we know it.

What this concept means

The water cycle has several key stages, each driven by energy from the Sun and the force of gravity.

Evaporation is the process by which liquid water turns into water vapour (a gas). It happens whenever water is exposed to air — from ocean surfaces, lakes, rivers, puddles and wet soil. The Sun provides the energy that breaks hydrogen bonds between water molecules, allowing them to escape into the air. About 86 per cent of global evaporation comes from the oceans. The rate of evaporation increases with higher temperatures, lower humidity, stronger winds and greater surface area.

Transpiration is evaporation from plants. Plants absorb water through their roots, transport it up through their stems, and release it as vapour through tiny pores in their leaves called stomata. A single large tree can transpire hundreds of litres of water per day. Together, evaporation and transpiration are sometimes called evapotranspiration.

Condensation occurs when water vapour cools and changes back into liquid droplets. As moist air rises and expands (because air pressure decreases with altitude), it cools. When it reaches the dew point — the temperature at which the air is saturated — vapour condenses onto tiny particles (dust, pollen, sea salt) called condensation nuclei, forming clouds. Condensation also happens close to the ground as dew (on cool surfaces at night) or fog (when a layer of air near the ground cools below its dew point).

Precipitation is water falling from clouds to the ground. It occurs when cloud droplets or ice crystals grow large enough for gravity to pull them down. Forms of precipitation include rain, drizzle, snow, sleet (rain that freezes on the way down) and hail (balls of ice formed by repeated cycling within thunderstorm updrafts). Precipitation is the atmosphere's way of returning water to the Earth's surface.

Collection and runoff: Once precipitation reaches the ground, water follows several paths. Some flows over the surface as runoff, joining streams and rivers that carry it back to the ocean. Some soaks into the ground (infiltration), replenishing underground aquifers. Some is absorbed by plant roots. Some falls directly into the ocean. Glaciers and ice caps store water as ice for thousands of years. Eventually, all water returns to the ocean, and the cycle begins again.

Why it works this way

The water cycle is powered by two forces: solar energy and gravity. The Sun provides the heat that drives evaporation and keeps the atmosphere warm enough for water vapour to exist. Gravity pulls precipitation to the ground and drives runoff downhill toward the ocean. The cycle is continuous because water can exist in three phases — solid (ice), liquid (water) and gas (water vapour) — within the temperature range found on Earth's surface. This is actually quite rare in the solar system. Mars is too cold (water is mostly ice); Venus is too hot (water vapour was lost to space long ago). Earth sits in the "Goldilocks zone" where all three phases coexist, enabling the water cycle that sustains life.

Energy is exchanged at every stage. Evaporation absorbs energy (cooling the surface — this is why sweating cools you). Condensation releases energy (warming the atmosphere — this is what powers thunderstorms and cyclones). These energy transfers link the water cycle directly to weather and climate. In fact, the water cycle moves more energy around the planet than wind does.

The total amount of water on Earth is essentially fixed — about 1.4 billion cubic kilometres. Of this, about 97.2 per cent is in the oceans (salty), about 2.1 per cent is locked in ice caps and glaciers, and less than 1 per cent is available as fresh water in rivers, lakes, groundwater and the atmosphere. This tiny fraction is constantly recycled through the water cycle, making it available again and again. A single water molecule spends, on average, about 3,200 years in the ocean, 300 years in a glacier, 2 weeks in a river, and about 9 days in the atmosphere before being recycled.

Did you know?

At any given moment, only about 0.001 per cent of Earth's total water is in the atmosphere as vapour or cloud droplets. Yet this tiny fraction is responsible for all the rain, snow and storms on the planet. If all the water vapour in the atmosphere condensed at once, it would cover the entire Earth with about 25 millimetres of water — roughly a one-inch layer. The atmosphere replenishes this supply through evaporation about every 9 days.

Real-world applications

  1. Drinking water supply — Cities depend on the water cycle to refill reservoirs, rivers and aquifers that provide tap water.
  2. Agriculture — Rain-fed farming across India relies entirely on precipitation from the water cycle; irrigation supplements it using stored water.
  3. Hydroelectric power — The water cycle lifts water (via evaporation) to high altitudes, from where it flows downhill through turbines to generate electricity.
  4. Flood prediction — Understanding how much rainfall will become runoff versus infiltration helps engineers predict flood levels.
  5. Groundwater management — Infiltration replenishes underground aquifers; over-pumping without adequate recharge leads to falling water tables.
  6. Forest conservation — Forests enhance transpiration and rainfall locally; deforestation can reduce regional precipitation.
  7. Desalination — In water-scarce regions, humans mimic evaporation and condensation to remove salt from seawater.
  8. Climate modelling — Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas; modelling its behaviour is crucial for predicting climate change.
  9. Snowmelt forecasting — In Himalayan river systems, spring snowmelt provides water for hundreds of millions; predicting it depends on understanding the water cycle.
  10. Urban drainage — City planners design storm drains based on expected runoff from paved surfaces during heavy rain.

In your kitchen right now

Fill a glass with cold water on a warm day. Within minutes, droplets form on the outside of the glass. This is condensation — the warm, moist air around the glass cools when it touches the cold surface, and water vapour in the air turns back into liquid droplets. You are watching the same process that forms clouds in the sky, just on a smaller scale. The glass acts like a cold mountaintop, and the droplets on it are like dew or fog.

Try this at home

Build a mini water cycle. Place a small cup of water inside a large transparent bowl. Cover the bowl tightly with cling film (plastic wrap). Place a small stone or coin on top of the cling film, directly above the cup, to create a dip. Set the whole setup in direct sunlight. After an hour or two, you will see droplets forming on the underside of the cling film (condensation). They will trickle down towards the lowest point (where the stone is) and drip into the cup or onto the bowl floor (precipitation). You have built a self-contained water cycle powered by the Sun.

Check your understanding

Question: Why is transpiration considered part of the water cycle?

Answer: Transpiration is the process by which plants absorb water from the soil through their roots and release it as water vapour through stomata in their leaves. This vapour enters the atmosphere, just like water that evaporates from oceans and lakes. It then condenses into clouds and eventually falls as precipitation. Plants therefore act as intermediaries in the water cycle, moving water from the ground to the atmosphere. In tropical forests, transpiration can account for a significant portion of regional rainfall.

Try another

Question: If the Sun suddenly stopped shining, what would happen to the water cycle?

Answer: Without solar energy, evaporation would slow dramatically and eventually stop as temperatures plummeted. With no evaporation, no new water vapour would enter the atmosphere, and clouds would dissipate. Precipitation would cease. Rivers would stop being fed by rain and would eventually run dry. The water cycle would grind to a halt, and virtually all water would end up frozen on the surface or in the oceans (which would also freeze). Life as we know it could not survive.
