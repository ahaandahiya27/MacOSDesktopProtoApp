Climate change and shifting habitats

As Earth warms, the temperature zones where each animal evolved are moving — towards the poles and up mountains; species that can't move fast enough face extinction.

≈ 3 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Tigers thrive in warm forests; polar bears in icy seas; mountain marmots in cold high meadows. Each species evolved over thousands of years for the exact climate where it lives. But Earth is now WARMING faster than ever in recorded history — by ~1.1 °C since 1900. That doesn't sound like much, but it means: tropical forests creeping north, ice caps shrinking, alpine zones moving up the mountain. Animals must MOVE WITH their climate — or die where they are. Polar bears are losing sea ice. Western Ghats frogs are forced upslope. Wild yaks in Ladakh have only so much higher to climb. If a species can't keep up, it goes extinct.

The textbook version

Climate change refers to long-term shifts in average global temperature, precipitation patterns, and weather extremes caused mainly by greenhouse-gas emissions from human activity since the Industrial Revolution. Effects on biodiversity: (1) Range shifts — species are documented to be moving poleward (~17 km per decade on average) and upslope (~11 m per decade) to track their temperature niche. (2) Phenological shifts — flowering, migration, and breeding are happening earlier in spring; mismatches between predator and prey, or pollinator and flower, are emerging. (3) Direct mortality — heat waves, fires, droughts kill species directly. (4) Habitat loss for ice-dependent species (polar bears, walruses, emperor penguins). (5) Species at high altitude and high latitude have nowhere left to go. IPCC AR6 estimates 14% of species at very high risk of extinction by 2050 if warming exceeds 2 °C.

Why does this happen?

Each animal species has a 'thermal niche' — a range of temperatures where it can survive and breed. As climate warms, that niche moves. The animal must follow it. Species that can move (birds, mammals on continuous habitat) often manage; species that can't (corals, summit-dwelling mountain plants) get squeezed to extinction.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Western Ghats frogs upslope — Indian climate-biology surveys document amphibians moving 30-100 m higher up Western Ghats peaks in 30 years. [Animals]
  • Arctic polar bears — Sea-ice loss has cut polar-bear hunting time, leading to thinner bodies, fewer cubs, and a 30% population decline in some regions. [Animals]
  • Indian rhino habitat shift — Kaziranga's grassland is shifting as Brahmaputra floods become more intense — rhinos retreat to higher patches. [Animals]

Going deeper

Climate-niche modelling combines current species distributions with future climate projections (CMIP6 RCP scenarios) to predict habitat shifts. The IUCN Red List incorporates climate vulnerability assessments alongside traditional threat criteria. Phenological-mismatch studies in temperate forests show flowering advancing ~3 days per decade; pollinators advancing only ~1.5 days — a growing mismatch. Climate-induced range shifts also bring new species into contact, creating opportunities for hybridisation, novel competition, and pathogen exposure (e.g., West Nile virus in temperate birds). India's Western Ghats and Eastern Himalaya are both biodiversity hotspots particularly vulnerable to climate range-shifts.
