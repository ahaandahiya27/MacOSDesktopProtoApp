What Is Weather?

The day-to-day state of the atmosphere at a particular place and time

~7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

You wake up, glance out the window and decide whether you need an umbrella or sunglasses. Without realising it, you have just done what meteorologists spend their entire careers doing — observing the weather. Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a specific place and time. It includes temperature, humidity, air pressure, wind, cloud cover and precipitation. Unlike climate, which describes long-term patterns, weather is a fleeting snapshot that can change within minutes. A cloudless morning can turn into a thunderstorm by afternoon. That unpredictability is exactly what makes weather so fascinating — and so important to understand.

What this concept means

When scientists talk about weather, they are describing the state of the atmosphere using a handful of measurable quantities. Let us look at each one in turn.

Temperature tells us how hot or cold the air is. It is measured in degrees Celsius (in most of the world) or degrees Fahrenheit (in the United States). Temperature changes throughout the day: it is usually coolest just before sunrise and warmest in the early afternoon.

Humidity is the amount of water vapour present in the air. When humidity is high, the air feels sticky and sweat does not evaporate easily, making you feel hotter than the thermometer suggests. Relative humidity is expressed as a percentage — 100 per cent means the air is holding as much moisture as it can at that temperature.

Air pressure (also called atmospheric pressure or barometric pressure) is the weight of the air column above a point. High pressure usually brings clear skies; low pressure often brings clouds and rain. Air pressure is measured in millibars (mb) or hectopascals (hPa). Standard sea-level pressure is about 1013 hPa.

Wind is the movement of air from high-pressure areas to low-pressure areas. It is described by both speed (in kilometres per hour or knots) and direction (the compass direction from which the wind blows — a "westerly" wind comes from the west).

Cloud cover describes how much of the sky is covered by clouds. Meteorologists measure it in oktas — eighths of the sky. A reading of 0 oktas means a perfectly clear sky; 8 oktas means completely overcast.

Precipitation is any water — liquid or solid — that falls from the atmosphere to the ground. Rain, drizzle, sleet, snow and hail are all forms of precipitation. It is measured in millimetres of depth using a rain gauge.

Why it works this way

Weather exists because the Sun heats the Earth unevenly. The equator receives more direct sunlight than the poles. Land heats and cools faster than water. Mountains force air to rise and cool. These imbalances create differences in temperature and pressure across the globe, and the atmosphere constantly tries to equalise them by moving air (wind) and moisture (clouds and rain) from one place to another. Because the Earth rotates, these air movements are deflected by the Coriolis effect, creating the large spiralling weather systems you see on satellite images.

Think of it like a pot of water on a stove. The burner heats the bottom unevenly. Hot water rises, cooler water sinks, and convection currents swirl around. The atmosphere behaves the same way, only on a planetary scale, with sunlight as the burner and the Earth's surface as the bottom of the pot.

Did you know?

The highest temperature ever reliably recorded on Earth was 56.7 degrees Celsius (134 degrees Fahrenheit) at Furnace Creek in Death Valley, California, on 10 July 1913. The coldest was minus 89.2 degrees Celsius (minus 128.6 degrees Fahrenheit) at the Soviet Vostok Station in Antarctica on 21 July 1983. That is a range of nearly 146 degrees Celsius — and life exists at both extremes.

Real-world applications

  1. Choosing what to wear — Temperature and rain forecasts determine whether you grab a jacket or a t-shirt before leaving home.
  2. School schedules — In many Indian states, schools adjust timings during extreme heat or cold spells based on weather advisories.
  3. Farming decisions — Farmers plant, irrigate and harvest based on expected weather; an unexpected frost can destroy an entire citrus crop.
  4. Flight operations — Fog, thunderstorms and wind shear all affect take-off and landing schedules at airports.
  5. Outdoor events — Wedding planners, sports organisers and concert promoters consult weather forecasts weeks in advance.
  6. Fishing — Fishermen in coastal India watch wind and wave forecasts before venturing out to sea.
  7. Health alerts — Governments issue heatwave, cold-wave and air-quality warnings based on weather data.
  8. Road safety — Fog, ice and heavy rain cause accidents; weather reports help traffic authorities issue advisories.
  9. Energy demand — Electricity boards predict demand spikes during heatwaves and cold spells to avoid blackouts.
  10. Insurance — Crop insurance, property insurance and travel insurance all use weather data to assess risk and set premiums.

In your kitchen right now

Open the lid of a pot of boiling water and watch the steam rise. Within moments, tiny droplets form on any cool surface nearby — the underside of a cabinet, a window, even your glasses. That condensation is the exact same process that creates clouds in the atmosphere. Warm, moist air meets a cooler surface, loses energy, and water vapour turns back into liquid droplets. You are witnessing weather science every time you cook.

Try this at home

Create a temperature log. Place a thermometer (a simple kitchen thermometer works) in a shaded spot outside your window. Record the temperature at 7 AM, 12 PM, 4 PM and 8 PM for five consecutive days. Plot the readings on a graph with time on the x-axis and temperature on the y-axis. You will notice a pattern: temperatures dip at night, rise after sunrise, peak in the early afternoon, and fall again by evening. This daily cycle is called the diurnal temperature variation, and it is one of the most fundamental rhythms in weather science.

Check your understanding

Question: A student says, "Delhi has hot weather." Is this statement about weather or climate? Explain.

Answer: It depends on context. If the student means "Delhi is hot today," that is a statement about weather — the current atmospheric condition. But if the student means "Delhi is generally hot during summer," that describes climate — the long-term average. The key difference is the timescale: weather is short-term (hours to days), climate is long-term (decades).

Try another

Question: Why does high humidity make a hot day feel even hotter?

Answer: Our bodies cool themselves by sweating. When sweat evaporates from the skin, it absorbs heat and cools us down. But when humidity is high, the air already contains a lot of water vapour, so sweat evaporates much more slowly. With less evaporation, less heat is removed from the body, and we feel hotter and more uncomfortable than the thermometer reading alone would suggest.
