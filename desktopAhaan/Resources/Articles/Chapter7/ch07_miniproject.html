Two Mini Projects for Chapter 7

Hands-on activities you can do today — with materials from the kitchen, not the lab. Each project takes about an hour and turns chapter ideas into something you can see and touch.

≈ 60 min total · hands-on

Reading is not the same as doing. These mini projects ask you to use your hands — predict, observe, and write down what surprises you. Try at least one before the next chapter test.

🌡️ Backyard weather station — record real climate

~43200 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • A simple thermometer (room thermometer is fine)
  • A measuring jar (rain gauge — anything with a flat top and straight sides)
  • A small notebook + pencil
  • 30 days of patience

Steps

  1. Pick a fixed time daily (8 am works) to record measurements.
  2. Each day: read and write the temperature.
  3. Each day after rain: measure how much fell in the jar (depth in mm) and empty.
  4. After 30 days, plot temperature on graph paper as a wavy line over the month.
  5. Calculate the average temperature (sum all 30 numbers / 30) and total rainfall.
  6. Compare with the previous year's average from IMD or weather.com.

👀 What you should see

30 daily data points forming a temperature graph. A monthly rainfall total. Your numbers — averaged over many months — would be 'your local climate'.

Why it works: Climate is just weather averaged over time. By collecting 30 days of weather, you've taken one step toward measuring climate. Real climate scientists collect 30+ YEARS of such data per location, across thousands of stations worldwide. Your backyard data is a microcosm of how IMD predicts the monsoon for hundreds of millions of farmers.

❄️ Insulation race — what keeps heat in best

~45 min · one-sitting project

What you'll need

  • 3 identical small glass jars with lids
  • Hot water (just-boiled)
  • Three insulating materials: cotton wool, newspaper, aluminium foil
  • A thermometer
  • Sticky tape
  • 30 minutes of timing

Steps

  1. Wrap one jar in cotton wool (mimics fur), one in newspaper (mimics insulation), one in foil (mimics shiny radiation shield).
  2. Pour hot water into each. Lid all three. Note temperature.
  3. Wait 30 minutes.
  4. Open and read temperature in each.
  5. Which lost the LEAST heat? That insulator wins.

👀 What you should see

Typically: cotton wool retains heat best (it traps air in small pockets), newspaper close second, foil worst (foil is reflective but a poor insulator when in contact with hot glass).

Why it works: Animals use exactly this kind of insulation race. Polar bears have hollow fur fibres that trap air (best insulator). Sea otters have the densest fur per cm² of any mammal (1 million hairs!) — like wearing thousands of jars of cotton wool. Penguins layer down feathers AND a top windbreak layer (similar to newspaper + foil combined). Every animal's coat is the result of generations of insulation racing.

📝 How to use this

Before you start, predict what will happen. Write your prediction down. Then do the project. Compare. If your prediction was wrong, write down why — that's where real learning happens.
