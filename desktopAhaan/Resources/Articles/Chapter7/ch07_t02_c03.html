Migration — the long-distance solution

Migration is the seasonal long-distance travel by animals to escape harsh climate or find food and breeding grounds.

≈ 2 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

When winter brings frozen rivers and no food, some animals don't tough it out — they leave. Birds fly thousands of kilometres to warmer places where insects are still around. Siberian cranes fly all the way from Russia to Bharatpur in Rajasthan, then back when spring returns. Some butterflies migrate. Whales migrate. Even some insects do. They navigate using the Sun, stars, magnetic fields of the Earth, and memory of landmarks. It's one of the most amazing behaviours in nature.

The textbook version

Migration is the seasonal movement of animals from one region to another to avoid harsh climatic conditions or to find food, water, and breeding grounds. Birds are the most famous migrators — many species fly thousands of kilometres twice a year. Examples: Siberian cranes migrating from Siberia to Bharatpur, India in winter; Arctic terns flying from the Arctic to Antarctic and back, the longest migration on Earth (~70,000 km roundtrip). Other migratory animals include whales, salmon, butterflies (Monarch), and certain bats. Migration is triggered by changes in temperature, day length, and food availability.

Why does this happen?

When local conditions become impossible, an animal can either tough it out (with adaptations) or leave (with migration). Migrants are usually fast-moving species that can cover distance — birds, whales, butterflies. Slow-moving species (bears, foxes) generally adapt locally.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Bharatpur Bird Sanctuary in winter — Each November, thousands of migratory birds — Siberian cranes, painted storks, ducks — arrive at Keoladeo National Park (Bharatpur). They feed all winter, then fly home in March. [Animals]
  • Monarch butterflies — Monarchs in North America migrate over 4,000 km from Canada to Mexico each autumn. Remarkably, no single butterfly makes the round trip — it takes 4-5 generations. [Animals]
  • Salmon returning to spawn — Salmon born in freshwater streams swim out to the ocean, live there for years, then navigate back to the exact stream of their birth to lay eggs. Smell + magnetic sense + memory together guide them. [Animals]

Going deeper

Migration navigation remains one of biology's deepest puzzles. Birds use multiple cues: the Sun's position (with internal clock compensation), the polarisation pattern of light at twilight, stars at night, Earth's magnetic field (detected by iron-bearing cells in their beaks), and remembered landmarks. The Arctic tern's 70,000 km annual flight is among the most extreme journeys in nature — over its lifetime, an Arctic tern flies the equivalent of three trips to the Moon. Climate change is disrupting migration patterns: birds arriving at breeding grounds before insects hatch, fish missing spawning windows, butterflies running into shifted weather. Conservation increasingly involves protecting whole migration corridors, not just nesting sites.

Beyond the book

Indian flamingos at Bhuj's Banni grasslands and Pulicat Lake migrate seasonally too. The Bombay Natural History Society has been ringing migratory birds since the 1920s — small numbered metal bands on legs let them track which Russian wetland a Kashmiri-spotted bird came from. Citizen-science apps like eBird now do this digitally; you can contribute to migration research with just a phone.
