One Curious Person Behind Chapter 7

The scientist most tied to weather, climate and adaptations of animals to climate — their question, their experiment, and what changed because of them.

Every chapter has a person behind it — someone who first asked the right question, ran the right experiment, or wrote down the answer. This is a one-page portrait of the scientist most tied to this chapter's content.

Sir Gilbert Walker

1868–1958 · British (working in India)

🎧 In one line

Discovered the El Niño-Southern Oscillation by studying Indian monsoons — built the foundation of modern climate science from Shimla.

In 1904, Sir Gilbert Walker became Director-General of the Indian Meteorological Department, headquartered in Shimla. India had just suffered the catastrophic Bengal Famine of 1873-74 and three more failed-monsoon famines. Walker's job: predict the monsoon. He had no satellites, no computers, just rain gauge data from Indian railway stations. Over 20 years, he built mathematical models comparing pressure at Tahiti versus Darwin, sea temperature in the Pacific versus the Indian Ocean. He found patterns: when pressure in the Pacific was high, the monsoon failed. This was the Walker Circulation — what we now call El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), the dominant climate cycle on Earth. Walker's mathematical climate science was rejected for decades — too statistical, not enough physics. By the 1960s satellite data confirmed everything he said. India fed his daughter's daughter's tools, and the Indian Meteorological Department in Pune now runs ensemble climate models that descend directly from Walker's pen-and-graph beginnings in Shimla.
