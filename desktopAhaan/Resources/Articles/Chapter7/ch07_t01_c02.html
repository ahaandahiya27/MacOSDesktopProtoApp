Measuring Weather — Instruments and Data

The tools that turn the invisible atmosphere into numbers we can study

~7 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Before we can predict the weather, we must first measure it — and measuring something as vast and restless as the atmosphere is no small feat. Meteorologists use a toolkit that ranges from centuries-old glass tubes to billion-dollar satellites orbiting 36,000 kilometres above the equator. Each instrument captures one piece of the puzzle: temperature, pressure, humidity, wind, rainfall. Together, they paint a detailed portrait of the atmosphere at any moment. Let us meet the instruments that make modern weather science possible.

What this concept means

Weather measurement — or meteorological observation — is the process of recording atmospheric conditions using standardised instruments at regular intervals. These readings are fed into computer models that simulate the atmosphere and produce forecasts. Without accurate, consistent measurements, forecasting would be impossible.

Thermometer: Measures temperature. A traditional mercury or alcohol thermometer works because liquids expand when heated and contract when cooled. The liquid rises in a narrow glass tube, and a calibrated scale tells us the temperature. Modern digital thermometers use electronic sensors called thermistors, which change their electrical resistance as temperature changes. Weather stations typically record both the maximum and minimum temperature each day using special thermometers designed for that purpose.

Barometer: Measures atmospheric pressure. Evangelista Torricelli invented the mercury barometer in 1643. A column of mercury in a glass tube, inverted over a dish of mercury, rises or falls as air pressure changes. Standard sea-level pressure supports a mercury column about 760 millimetres tall. Modern aneroid barometers use a sealed metal capsule that flexes with pressure changes, moving a needle on a dial. Falling pressure often signals approaching rain; rising pressure suggests clearing skies.

Hygrometer: Measures humidity. One common type, the wet-and-dry-bulb hygrometer (also called a psychrometer), uses two thermometers side by side. One bulb is wrapped in a wet muslin cloth. Evaporation from the wet cloth cools that thermometer, and the difference between the two readings reveals the relative humidity. A large difference means dry air; a small difference means humid air.

Anemometer: Measures wind speed. The classic cup anemometer has three or four small cups mounted on arms that spin around a vertical axis. The faster the cups spin, the stronger the wind. A wind vane (or weather vane) alongside it shows the direction from which the wind blows.

Rain gauge: Measures precipitation. It is a simple cylindrical container placed in an open area. After a rainfall event, the depth of water collected is measured in millimetres. Tipping-bucket rain gauges automate this by funnelling rain into a small bucket that tips each time it fills, sending an electronic pulse to a recorder.

Weather satellites orbit the Earth and capture images of cloud patterns, measure sea-surface temperatures and track storm movements. Geostationary satellites hover over one spot (such as INSAT over the Indian Ocean), providing continuous images. Polar-orbiting satellites circle from pole to pole, scanning the entire Earth twice a day with high-resolution instruments.

Doppler radar sends out pulses of microwave energy that bounce off raindrops and return to the receiver. By measuring the shift in frequency (the Doppler effect), the radar can determine not just where rain is falling but how fast and in what direction the rain-bearing clouds are moving. This is crucial for detecting severe thunderstorms, tornadoes and cyclones.

Why it works this way

Every weather instrument exploits a physical property. Thermometers rely on thermal expansion. Barometers use the balance between air pressure and the weight of a mercury column. Hygrometers exploit evaporative cooling. Anemometers convert wind kinetic energy into rotation. Rain gauges simply collect and measure fallen water. Satellites use electromagnetic radiation — visible light, infrared, microwave — to sense the atmosphere from afar. The genius of meteorology is combining all these different measurements into a single, coherent picture of the atmosphere's state.

Did you know?

Every day, about 10,000 land weather stations, 7,000 ships, 3,000 aircraft, 1,000 upper-air sounding stations (which launch weather balloons), and dozens of satellites feed data into global weather models. The sheer volume of data is staggering — hundreds of millions of observations per day — and it takes supercomputers running trillions of calculations per second to turn them into a forecast.

Real-world applications

  1. Daily forecasts — Instrument data feeds into numerical weather prediction models that produce the 5-day forecasts on your phone.
  2. Cyclone tracking — Doppler radar and satellites allow the IMD to track cyclones and issue warnings 48 to 72 hours in advance.
  3. Aviation meteorology — Automated weather stations at airports report visibility, wind and pressure every few minutes for safe take-offs and landings.
  4. Flood forecasting — Rain gauge networks in river catchments help predict how much water will flow downstream.
  5. Agricultural advisories — Agro-meteorological stations combine weather data with soil moisture sensors to advise farmers on irrigation timing.
  6. Climate records — Decades of instrument data form the climate record, allowing scientists to detect long-term trends such as global warming.
  7. Air quality monitoring — Weather instruments measure wind speed and mixing height, which determine how quickly pollution disperses.
  8. Renewable energy — Wind-speed data from anemometers determines where to build wind farms for maximum electricity generation.
  9. Marine safety — Buoy-mounted instruments measure wave height, wind and pressure on the open ocean, warning ships of dangerous conditions.
  10. Research — Upper-air soundings using weather balloons provide vertical profiles of temperature, humidity and wind, critical for understanding storm dynamics.

In your kitchen right now

Your kitchen thermometer — the one you use to check if a cake is done or if the fridge is cold enough — works on the same principle as a weather thermometer. A sensor responds to temperature and displays a number. The difference is that a weather station thermometer is housed in a white louvred box called a Stevenson screen, which allows air to flow through while shielding the sensor from direct sunlight and rain, ensuring accurate readings.

Try this at home

Build a simple barometer. Stretch a piece of balloon rubber over the mouth of a glass jar and secure it tightly with a rubber band. Glue a drinking straw horizontally so one end rests on the centre of the rubber membrane and the other end points outward like a needle. Tape a piece of paper marked with a scale behind the straw tip. When air pressure rises, it pushes the membrane down, tilting the straw tip upwards. When pressure falls, the trapped air inside pushes the membrane up, tilting the straw down. Check it against a real weather report and watch the correlation.

Check your understanding

Question: A wet-and-dry-bulb hygrometer shows the wet bulb at 22 degrees Celsius and the dry bulb at 28 degrees Celsius. Is the air relatively dry or relatively humid?

Answer: The air is relatively dry. A 6-degree difference between the wet and dry bulbs indicates that evaporation is happening rapidly (cooling the wet bulb significantly), which only occurs when the air has low humidity. If the air were very humid, evaporation would be slow, and the two readings would be close together.

Try another

Question: Why is a rain gauge placed in an open area, away from trees and buildings?

Answer: Trees and buildings can block or redirect rainfall, leading to inaccurate measurements. A tree canopy might intercept rain before it reaches the gauge, while a building could create a wind shadow that diverts rain away from or towards the gauge. An open, level site ensures the gauge catches a representative sample of rainfall for the area.
