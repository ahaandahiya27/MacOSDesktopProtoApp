Clouds, Rain and Storms

How water vapour transforms into towering clouds, gentle drizzle and violent tempests

~8 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Look up at the sky on a partly cloudy day. You will see an ever-shifting gallery of shapes — flat grey sheets, puffy white castles, wispy feathers high above. Each type of cloud tells a story about what the atmosphere is doing at that altitude. Some clouds bring nothing more than shade; others unleash torrential rain, hail, lightning and even tornadoes. Understanding clouds is the key to understanding precipitation and storms, two of the most powerful forces in weather. Let us climb into the sky and find out how clouds form, why rain falls, and what drives the fury of a storm.

What this concept means

Cloud formation begins when the Sun heats the Earth's surface, causing water to evaporate from oceans, lakes, rivers, soil and even plant leaves (a process called transpiration). This invisible water vapour rises with warm air. As the air rises, it expands because atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude. Expansion cools the air. When the air cools to its dew point — the temperature at which it can no longer hold all its water vapour — condensation occurs. Tiny water droplets form around microscopic particles called condensation nuclei (dust, pollen, sea salt, soot). Billions of these droplets, each far too small and light to fall, together form a visible cloud.

Types of clouds: Clouds are classified by altitude and shape. High-altitude clouds (above 6,000 metres) include cirrus (thin, wispy, made of ice crystals), cirrostratus (thin sheets that produce halos around the Sun) and cirrocumulus (small white puffs in rows). Mid-altitude clouds (2,000 to 6,000 metres) include altostratus (grey sheets) and altocumulus (white or grey patches). Low-altitude clouds (below 2,000 metres) include stratus (flat, grey, blanket-like), stratocumulus (lumpy grey rolls) and nimbostratus (thick, dark, rain-bearing layers). Then there are the vertical giants: cumulus clouds, which can grow from low altitudes up through the entire troposphere, and cumulonimbus, the thunderstorm cloud, which can tower to 15 kilometres or more.

How rain forms: Cloud droplets are tiny — about 10 to 20 micrometres across, hundreds of times smaller than a raindrop. For rain to fall, droplets must grow large enough for gravity to overcome air resistance. This happens in two main ways. In warm clouds (tropical regions), droplets collide and merge — a process called coalescence. Larger droplets fall faster, sweeping up smaller ones, growing until they are heavy enough to fall as rain. In cold clouds (and much of the mid-latitudes), the Bergeron process dominates: ice crystals and supercooled water droplets coexist, and ice crystals grow at the expense of the droplets because the air is more saturated with respect to ice. The ice crystals eventually become heavy enough to fall, melting into raindrops as they pass through warmer air below.

Storms: A storm is any disturbed state of the atmosphere with strong winds, often accompanied by rain, snow, thunder or lightning. Thunderstorms form when powerful updrafts carry moist air to great heights inside cumulonimbus clouds. The rapid movement of water droplets and ice crystals inside the cloud generates static electricity. When the charge difference between regions becomes large enough, a massive electrical discharge occurs — lightning — which superheats the air to about 30,000 degrees Celsius, causing it to expand explosively and produce the sound we call thunder. Tropical cyclones (called hurricanes in the Atlantic and typhoons in the Pacific) are much larger storm systems powered by warm ocean water, with organised wind circulation around a calm centre called the eye.

Why it works this way

The atmosphere is a heat engine. The Sun pumps energy in at the surface, and the atmosphere redistributes that energy through convection, radiation and the phase changes of water. When water evaporates, it absorbs latent heat from the surface, cooling it. When that vapour condenses into cloud droplets high in the atmosphere, it releases that stored heat, warming the surrounding air and making it more buoyant. This released energy is what fuels storms. A single thunderstorm can release energy equivalent to a small nuclear weapon. A tropical cyclone releases as much energy in one day as the entire United States uses in electricity in six months. The water cycle is therefore not just a gentle loop — it is the engine that drives the most powerful weather events on the planet.

Did you know?

A typical cumulonimbus thunderstorm cloud contains about 300,000 tonnes of water. If all that water fell as rain at once, it would fill roughly 120 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Lightning strikes the Earth about 100 times every second — roughly 8 million times a day. Despite its fearsome reputation, each bolt lasts only about one-fifth of a second.

Real-world applications

  1. Weather forecasting — Cloud types visible on satellite images help forecasters predict rain, storms and clearing weather hours in advance.
  2. Aviation — Pilots avoid cumulonimbus clouds because they contain severe turbulence, hail and lightning that can damage aircraft.
  3. Agriculture — Farmers depend on rain forecasts to schedule irrigation and protect harvests from unexpected downpours or hail.
  4. Flood management — Radar-tracked storm cells allow authorities to predict flash floods and issue timely warnings.
  5. Hydroelectric power — Rainfall patterns determine reservoir levels, directly affecting electricity generation at dams.
  6. Lightning protection — Tall buildings use lightning rods, and outdoor event organisers monitor lightning radar to protect crowds.
  7. Cloud seeding — In some regions, silver iodide particles are released into clouds to encourage rain, a technique used during water shortages.
  8. Solar energy — Cloud cover forecasts help solar farm operators predict how much electricity they can supply to the grid.
  9. Sailing and marine navigation — Sailors read clouds to anticipate wind shifts and approaching squalls.
  10. Climate science — The type, altitude and coverage of clouds influence how much sunlight reaches the surface and how much heat escapes to space, making clouds a critical factor in climate models.

In your kitchen right now

When you open a pressure cooker after it cools, a small puff of steam escapes and immediately turns into a tiny cloud of white droplets. That is condensation — exactly what happens when moist air rises and cools in the atmosphere. The white cloud you see is not steam (which is invisible) but millions of tiny liquid water droplets, just like a real cloud in the sky.

Try this at home

Make a cloud in a bottle. Fill a clear plastic bottle one-third full with warm water. Light a match, blow it out, and quickly drop it into the bottle. Immediately squeeze the bottle hard with both hands, then release. When you release, the air inside expands and cools, and the smoke particles act as condensation nuclei — a cloud forms inside the bottle. Squeeze again and the cloud disappears as the air warms and can hold more vapour. You are controlling cloud formation with your hands.

Check your understanding

Question: Why are condensation nuclei necessary for cloud formation?

Answer: Water vapour molecules find it very difficult to stick together and form droplets on their own in clean air — they need a tiny surface to cling to. Condensation nuclei (dust, pollen, sea salt, soot) provide that surface. Without them, air could become supersaturated — holding more vapour than it normally should — without any cloud forming. In the real atmosphere, there are always plenty of particles, so clouds form readily once the air cools to its dew point.

Try another

Question: Explain why thunder is heard after lightning is seen, even though they occur at the same time.

Answer: Light travels at about 300,000 kilometres per second, while sound travels at only about 340 metres per second (roughly one kilometre every three seconds). Since light is nearly a million times faster than sound, the flash of lightning reaches your eyes almost instantly, but the sound of thunder takes several seconds to arrive. By counting the seconds between the flash and the thunder and dividing by three, you can estimate the distance of the lightning strike in kilometres.
