Conservation — what we can do

Protecting wildlife combines national parks (legal protection), breeding programmes (saving species near extinction), and small daily actions (saving energy, planting trees, supporting tribal communities).

≈ 3 min read · for curious 12-year-olds

Saving wildlife isn't only a government job. Every action matters. India has 104 NATIONAL PARKS and 566 SANCTUARIES where animals are legally protected. PROJECT TIGER (1973) saved the Bengal tiger from extinction — numbers grew from 1,800 to 3,000+. PROJECT ELEPHANT, GANGETIC DOLPHIN RESERVES, and many more species-specific programmes followed. Captive breeding has saved species near extinction. But government action alone isn't enough — you can help too: save electricity (every kWh saved = less CO₂), plant a tree (each absorbs ~20 kg of CO₂ per year), eat less meat (livestock is a major emitter), avoid single-use plastic, support tribal communities who protect forests. Small choices, multiplied by a billion Indians, become huge.

The textbook version

Wildlife conservation strategies combine multiple approaches: (1) Legal protection — India's Wildlife Protection Act 1972 created National Parks (104, full protection), Wildlife Sanctuaries (566, partial), Tiger Reserves (54), Biosphere Reserves (18 under UNESCO MAB). (2) Species-specific programmes — Project Tiger (1973), Project Elephant (1992), Crocodile Conservation Project, Olive Ridley Turtle Conservation. (3) Captive breeding for critically endangered species — Asiatic lion at Gir, vultures at Pinjore, Indian gharial at Lucknow. (4) Habitat restoration — reforestation, wetland revival, coral-reef restoration. (5) Community-based conservation — Joint Forest Management, Van Panchayats, community-conserved areas. (6) Personal action — energy conservation, reducing meat/dairy, plastic reduction, tree planting, supporting eco-tourism, supporting tribal communities. International frameworks include CBD, CITES, IUCN Red List, Ramsar Convention (wetlands), CMS (migratory species).

Why does this happen?

Conservation works when many levers are pulled together: legal protection stops killing; breeding programmes rebuild populations; habitat restoration gives them somewhere to live; community engagement aligns local people's livelihoods with wildlife survival; personal action reduces the global pressure of climate change and pollution.

Where you see this in the real world

  • Project Tiger (1973) — Started with 9 reserves and ~1800 tigers; now 54 reserves and ~3000 tigers — globally recognised conservation success. [Animals]
  • Gir Asiatic lions — From <100 lions in the early 1900s to ~700 today — single-population dependence is now the main risk. [Animals]
  • Eat less meat — climate impact — Livestock production accounts for ~15% of global greenhouse-gas emissions; eating less meat reduces this footprint. [Kitchen]

Going deeper

Conservation biology has matured into a quantitative interdisciplinary science combining ecology, genetics, economics, and policy. Population Viability Analysis (PVA) predicts species extinction risks. Landscape connectivity (wildlife corridors, road underpasses, dams with fish ladders) reduces population fragmentation. Genetic-rescue interventions (Florida panther outbreeding 1995) save inbred populations. Economic valuation (ecosystem services, payments for ecosystem services, REDD+) creates incentives for conservation. India's recent Vulture Conservation Action Plan and Cheetah Reintroduction (2022) demonstrate active conservation engineering. Climate adaptation planning is being mainstreamed into national park management.

Beyond the book

Class 11 / 12 Biology 'Biodiversity and Conservation' is a NEET-heavy chapter. UPSC Environment paper requires deep knowledge of Indian protected-area network and species-specific programmes. JEE Earth Sciences and Class 12 cover conservation genetics and ecological economics. Personal-level actions feature in UPSC Ethics paper and Class 9 / 10 Sustainable Development units.
