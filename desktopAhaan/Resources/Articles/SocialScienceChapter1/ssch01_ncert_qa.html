Q1. What, in your opinion, are two important geographical features of India? Why do you think they are important? (NCERT page 24)

Two very important features are the Himalayas and the Northern Plains. The Himalayas are important because they act as a natural barrier protecting the north, block cold winds, trap the monsoon to give India rain, and store snow that feeds great rivers all year. The Northern Plains are important because their flat, fertile, river-fed land feeds and supports a huge population and made it easy to build cities, farms and transport networks. (Other reasonable choices, such as the peninsular plateau's minerals or the long coastline's ports, are also valid if justified.)

Q2. India has been called a 'mini-continent'. Based on what you've read, why do you think this is so? (NCERT page 25)

India is called a mini-continent because, within a single country, it contains an enormous range of landforms and climates — snowy Himalayas, the cold desert of Ladakh, the fertile Gangetic Plains, the hot Thar Desert, the ancient Aravallis, the Deccan Plateau, long coastlines with deltas, and island groups. Such variety of relief, climate, soils, plants, animals and cultures is usually found across a whole continent, not one country, so India is described as a 'mini-continent'.

Q3. Why is the southern part of India referred to as a peninsular plateau? (NCERT page 25)

Because it is both a plateau and a peninsula. It is a plateau — a raised area of land with a more or less flat top and steep sides — and it is a peninsula because it is surrounded by water on three sides: the Arabian Sea to the west, the Bay of Bengal to the east, and the Indian Ocean to the south. A plateau that is also a peninsula is called a peninsular plateau.

Q4. Despite having such different regions (mountains, deserts, plains, coasts), India remains one country. How do you think our geography has helped unite people? (NCERT page 25)

Geography has united India in several ways. Great rivers such as the Ganga, Brahmaputra and the peninsular rivers link mountains, plains and coasts and have carried people, goods and ideas between regions for thousands of years. The monsoon gives almost the whole country a shared rhythm of seasons. Land and sea trade routes connected distant regions, exchanging foods, crafts and beliefs. So while the regions look very different, these connections wove them into a single civilisation — an example of 'unity in diversity'.
