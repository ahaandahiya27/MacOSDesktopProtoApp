Orographic rainfall: why mountains make rain [Class 9]

When moist winds meet a mountain or range of hills, they are forced to rise. As air rises it expands and cools, and cooler air can hold less water vapour, so the vapour condenses into clouds and falls as rain on the windward (wind-facing) side. This is called 'orographic' or 'relief' rainfall. It explains why the Western Ghats soak the west coast, why the Khasi hills of the Northeast are among the wettest places on Earth, and why the far side of a range (the 'rain shadow') stays dry — which is exactly why Ladakh, behind the Himalayas, is a desert. One range can therefore create both a rainforest and a desert, only a short distance apart.

You'll want to know first: Understanding that the Himalayas and Ghats force winds upward.

How geographers know India is still moving [Class 10]

We can actually measure India creeping into Asia. Networks of GPS stations fixed to the ground record their positions to within millimetres, year after year, and they show the Indian plate moving north-northeast at roughly 5 cm a year. Geologists combine this with the age of rock layers, the pattern of earthquakes along the Himalayan front, and marine fossils found high in the mountains to reconstruct the whole collision. This is a great example of how a claim in your textbook ('the Himalayas are still rising') is not a guess but a measured, testable fact — the heart of how science works.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the Himalayas formed by the India–Eurasia collision.

Seashells on the roof of the world [Class 9]

Climb high into the Himalayas and you can find fossils of ammonites and other sea creatures embedded in the rock, kilometres above the nearest ocean. How did sea animals end up on the world's tallest mountains? Before the Himalayas existed, a wide sea called the Tethys lay between India and Asia. As India drifted north and slammed into Asia, the floor of that vanished sea was squeezed, folded and pushed UP to form the Himalayas, carrying its buried sea-life with it. Those fossils are direct evidence that today's snowy peaks were once an ocean bed — one of the clearest proofs of continental drift you can hold in your hand.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the Himalayas rose from the India–Eurasia collision.

Why the Northern Plains are kilometres deep [Class 10]

The Northern Plains look flat and ordinary, but beneath your feet the river-laid soil (alluvium) goes down for kilometres before it reaches solid rock. For millions of years the Himalayan rivers have ground down the young, rising mountains and dumped the sediment into the trough in front of them, filling it like sand poured into a tray. Geographers even sort this alluvium into 'bhangar' (older, higher, slightly clayey land) and 'khadar' (newer, lower flood-plain soil renewed each monsoon). This colossal depth of fine, mineral-rich soil is exactly why the plains are so fertile and can feed so many people — but it also means earthquakes shake harder here, because loose soil wobbles more than firm rock.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the Northern Plains were built by the Himalayan rivers.

Why southern India's rivers run the 'wrong' way [Class 10]

Look at a map of the peninsula and a puzzle appears: its biggest rivers — the Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri — begin in the Western Ghats, only a short distance from the Arabian Sea, yet they flow the other way, right across India to empty into the Bay of Bengal in the east. Why? The plateau is gently tilted. The Western Ghats stand high and steep along the west coast, so the whole tableland slopes downwards towards the east. Water always runs downhill, so these rivers travel east for hundreds of kilometres, slowing as they reach the flat coast and dropping their silt to build the wide, fertile deltas of the East Coast. The short rivers on the western slope, by contrast, have no room to wander — they tumble straight down the steep Ghats and reach the Arabian Sea quickly, which is why the West Coast has swift rivers and few big deltas. The tilt of the land, set when the plateau formed, still decides which way the water goes today.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the peninsular plateau is bordered by the high Western Ghats and sends most of its rivers east.

Two kinds of Indian river: snow-fed and rain-fed [Class 10]

India's rivers come in two families that behave very differently. The northern rivers — the Ganga, Indus and Brahmaputra — are born in the Himalayas and are fed by melting snow and glaciers as well as the monsoon. Because the snow keeps melting even in the dry season, these rivers flow all year round; geographers call them 'perennial'. The peninsular rivers — like the Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri — have no glaciers behind them and depend almost entirely on the monsoon rains. So they swell into torrents during the rainy months and shrink to thin streams, or even dry up, in summer; these are 'seasonal' or rain-fed rivers. This one difference shapes life across India: the snow-fed north can rely on river water for farming and cities through the whole year, while much of the peninsula had to invent tanks, reservoirs and careful water storage to bridge the long dry season — an idea you meet again in the story of Indian farming.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the Himalayan snow and glaciers feed the Ganga, Indus and Brahmaputra.
