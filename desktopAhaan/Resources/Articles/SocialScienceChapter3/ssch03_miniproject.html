🌡️ Two-week weather diary versus climate (about 45 min)

What you need

  • A notebook and pen
  • An outdoor thermometer (or a weather app/newspaper)
  • A simple chart or graph paper

Steps

  1. Each day for two weeks, note the date, the highest and lowest temperature, and whether it was sunny, cloudy, rainy or windy.
  2. At the end of each day, write one line describing the day's weather.
  3. After two weeks, plot the daily high temperatures on a simple graph.
  4. Look at how much the weather changed from day to day — circle the biggest jumps.
  5. Now find your city's usual (average) temperatures for this month online or in an atlas — that is part of its climate.
  6. Compare your two weeks of changing 'weather' with the steady 'climate' average.
  7. Write a short note explaining, in your own words, the difference between weather and climate using your data.

👀 What you should see

You will see the daily weather bouncing up and down a lot, while the long-term climate average stays much steadier — making the difference between the two crystal clear.

🧠 Why it works

By recording real, changing weather and then comparing it with the long-term average, you turn an abstract definition into something you measured yourself. Seeing your own zig-zag graph next to a flat climate line makes the timescale difference unforgettable.

💡 Sun-angle and the latitude effect (about 30 min)

What you need

  • A torch or flashlight
  • A sheet of graph paper
  • A pencil
  • A dark room

Steps

  1. Tape the graph paper flat on a table in a dark room.
  2. Hold the torch straight down, about 30 cm above the paper, and switch it on.
  3. Trace the outline of the bright circle of light and count how many squares it covers — this is like the Sun at the Equator.
  4. Now tilt the torch to a steep slant, keeping it the same height, and switch it on again.
  5. Trace the new, stretched oval of light and count how many squares it now covers.
  6. Compare: the slanted light covers more squares but is dimmer on each square.
  7. Write how this models why the Equator (straight rays) is hotter than the poles (slanting rays).

👀 What you should see

The straight-down beam makes a small, bright patch, while the slanted beam spreads into a larger, dimmer patch — the same total light spread over more area.

🧠 Why it works

The torch is a stand-in for the Sun and the paper for the Earth's surface. Spreading the same light over more squares when tilted demonstrates exactly why slanting rays near the poles deliver less heat per area than the straight rays at the Equator.
