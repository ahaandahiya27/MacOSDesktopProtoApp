Orographic rainfall: why mountains make rain [Class 9]

When moist winds meet a mountain or range of hills, they are forced to rise. As air rises it expands and cools, and cooler air can hold less water vapour, so the vapour condenses into clouds and falls as rain on the windward (wind-facing) side. This is called 'orographic' or 'relief' rainfall. It explains why the western slopes of the Western Ghats are drenched while the Deccan behind them lies in a drier 'rain shadow', and why the hills of Meghalaya make Mawsynram the rainiest place on Earth. One range can therefore create both a soaking-wet coast and a much drier interior only a short distance apart — the same idea explains why the cold desert of Ladakh sits dry behind the Himalayas.

You'll want to know first: Knowing that the Western Ghats and other hills force monsoon winds upward.

Temperature range: how to measure a climate's 'evenness' [Class 8]

Geographers describe how 'extreme' or 'even' a climate is using the temperature range — the difference between the maximum and minimum temperature. A small range means an even, moderate climate; a large range means hot summers and cold winters. The chapter's example makes this concrete: Mumbai's range is about 14°C (max ~32°C, min ~18°C) because the sea moderates it, while Nagpur's range reaches about 34°C (max ~44°C, min ~10°C) because it is far inland. You can compute a place's range for a day, a month or a year, and comparing ranges is a quick way to tell maritime climates from continental ones — a skill you will use throughout geography.

You'll want to know first: Knowing the sea moderates coastal temperatures.
